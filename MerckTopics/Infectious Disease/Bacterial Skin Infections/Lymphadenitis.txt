Lymphadenitis is a feature of many bacterial, viral, fungal, and protozoal infections.

Focal lymphadenitis is prominent in the following:

Streptococcal infection

Tuberculosis or nontuberculous mycobacterial infection (particularly scrofula)

Tularemia

Plague

Cat-scratch disease

Primary syphilis

Lymphogranuloma venereum

Chancroid

Genital herpes simplex

Multifocal lymphadenitis may occur in patients with the following:

Infectious mononucleosis

Cytomegalovirus infection

Toxoplasmosis

Brucellosis

Secondary syphilis

Disseminated histoplasmosis

Cutaneous Tuberculosis (Scrofuloderma)

Other, noninfectious causes of lymphadenitis include autoimmune disease (eg, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis), cancers (eg, lymphoma, leukemia), drug reactions (eg, to allopurinol or phenytoin), Castleman disease, and Kawasaki disease (1).

Symptoms and Signs of Lymphadenitis

Lymphadenitis typically causes focal erythema, pain, tenderness, and lymph node enlargement. Pain and tenderness typically distinguish lymphadenitis from lymphadenopathy. With some infections, the overlying skin is inflamed, occasionally with cellulitis. Abscesses may form, and penetration to the skin produces draining sinuses. Fever is common.

Diagnosis of Lymphadenitis

History and physical examination

Aspiration and culture or excisional biopsy in selected cases

The diagnosis of lymphadenitis is primarily clinical, based on suggestive history of the underlying disorder and physical examination findings of lymph node enlargement with features suggesting infection (eg, erythema, pain, sometimes purulent drainage). Enlarged lymph nodes that do not cause pain, tenderness, or erythema may raise concern for other disorders such as lymphomas.

Tissue diagnosis may be required when the etiology remains uncertain, there is concern for cancer, or if unilateral, firm, large, fixed, or matted nodes are present (1). Fine-needle aspiration and core needle biopsy are minimally invasive options; however, excisional biopsy may be required (eg, for patients with scrofula, cancer, or persistent, recalcitrant, or recurrent lesions) (2, 3). Cultures and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) technique (or other similar molecular diagnostic methods) can accurately identify the causative organism in some cases (eg, tularemia, tuberculosis).

Treatment of Lymphadenitis

Treatment of underlying cause

The treatment of lymphadenitis is directed at the underlying cause.

Many patients with infectious lymphadenitis may respond to outpatient therapy with oral antibiotics. Empiric therapy is typically directed at Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes or at atypical mycobacteria. Antifungals or antiparasitics are administered depending on the suspected etiology. The use of warm compresses may be provide symptomatic pain relief, and NSAIDs can also be administered.

However, many patients also go on to form abscesses, which require surgical drainage in addition to antibiotics. In children, IV antibiotics are commonly needed, and IV antibiotics may also be required in patients with a severe bacterial infection.

Lymphadenitis usually resolves with timely treatment, but residual, persistent, nontender lymphadenopathy is common.
