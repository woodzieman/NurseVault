The list of potential pathogens in patients with compromised immune system defenses is highly varied. Pathogens include those that cause community-acquired pneumonia as well as atypical or otherwise unusual pathogens (ie, the pathogen spectrum is broader than in immunocompetent patients) (1). More than one pathogen may be involved. Likely pathogens depend on the type of underlying defect in immune system defenses (see table Pneumonia in Immunocompromised Patients). However, respiratory symptoms and changes on chest imaging in immunocompromised patients may be due to various processes other than (or in addition to) infection, such as pulmonary hemorrhage, pulmonary edema, radiation injury, pulmonary toxicity due to cytotoxic drugs or immunotherapy, and tumor infiltrates.

Symptoms and Signs of Pneumonia in Immunocompromised Patients

Symptoms and signs may be the same as those that occur with community-acquired pneumonia in immunocompetent patients. Symptoms may include malaise, chills, fever, rigor, cough, dyspnea, and chest pain. However, immunocompromised patients may have no fever or respiratory signs and are less likely to have purulent sputum if they are neutropenic. In some patients, the only sign is fever. Symptoms and signs may become more prominent with reconstitution of the immune system and the host response to the pathogen. Disseminated infections are not uncommon; therefore, the clinical assessment should include looking for extra-pulmonary manifestations in the skin, central nervous system, and other organ systems.

Pearls & Pitfalls

Have a high index of suspicion for pneumonia in patients who are immunocompromised because symptoms can be atypical or muted.

Diagnosis of Pneumonia in Immunocompromised Patients

Chest imaging

Assessment of oxygenation

Sputum induction or bronchoscopy to obtain lower respiratory samples

Blood cultures and molecular assays

Pathogens predicted based on symptoms, radiographic changes, and type of immunodeficiency

The diagnosis of pneumonia in immunocompromised patients is generally suspected first based on clinical presentation and confirmed based on a stepwise diagnostic approach, beginning with rapid noninvasive tests (eg, imaging, expectorated sputum testing) and progressing to more definitive and sometimes invasive procedures (eg, blood culture, bronchoscopic tissue or sputum examination, polymerase chain reaction [PCR]) when initial testing is unrevealing (1, 2).

Chest imaging and assessment of oxygenation (usually by pulse oximetry) are done in immunocompromised patients with respiratory symptoms or signs, or fever. If an infiltrate or hypoxemia is present on a chest radiograph more specific diagnostic assessment should be performed. Chest radiographs may be normal in Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, but hypoxia or hypoxemia (an increased alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient on arterial blood gas testing) is usually present. If clinical suspicion of pneumonia is high and the chest radiograph is unrevealing, a chest CT scan should be performed (as lung infiltrates may not be apparent on chest radiography in immunocompromised patients).

Pathogen identification

Sputum testing and blood cultures are typically performed. Sputum testing should include Gram stain, mycobacterial and fungal stains and cultures, and sometimes testing for viruses (eg, PCR for cytomegalovirus DNA in a patient who has had a transplant or in a patient with HIV). If signs, symptoms, or risk factors for Aspergillus infection are present, serum galactomannan assay should be done.

It is important to aggressively pursue a microbiological diagnosis with induced sputum, bronchoscopy, or both, especially in patients with severe defects in immune function or failure to respond to broad-spectrum antibiotics. Lung biopsy should be considered when these tests do not provide a specific diagnosis and empiric therapy is not effective.

Molecular diagnostic techniques such as nucleic acid amplification tests (which includes PCR) play a critical role in pathogen identification; they are used to detect pathogen-specific nucleic acids or antigens.

Likely pathogens can also often be predicted based on symptoms, radiographic changes, and the type of immunodeficiency. In patients with acute symptoms, the differential diagnosis includes bacterial infection, hemorrhage, pulmonary edema, a leukocyte agglutinin reaction to transfusion of blood products, and pulmonary emboli. An indolent time course is more suggestive of a fungal or mycobacterial infection, an opportunistic viral infection, P. jirovecii pneumonia, a tumor, a cytotoxic drug reaction, or radiation injury.

Radiographs showing a localized opacity or consolidation usually indicate an infection involving bacteria (including Nocardia), mycobacteria, or fungi.

A diffuse interstitial pattern is more likely to represent a viral infection, P. jirovecii pneumonia, drug or radiation injury, or pulmonary edema.

Diffuse nodular lesions suggest mycobacteria, Nocardia species, fungi, or a tumor.

Cavitary disease suggests mycobacteria, Nocardia species, fungi, or bacteria, particularly S. aureus or anaerobes.

In organ or bone marrow transplantation recipients with bilateral interstitial pneumonia, the usual cause is cytomegalovirus, or the disease is idiopathic.

A pleural-based consolidation is suggestive of Aspergillus infection.

In patients with advanced HIV, bilateral pneumonia is usually P. jirovecii pneumonia. Many patients with HIV infection have P. jirovecii pneumonia as the initial advanced HIV-defining diagnosis, especially if they are not receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART). Patients with HIV infection who are not on antiretroviral therapy are at high risk for developing this pneumonia if prophylaxis is not given. Patients with HIV infection become vulnerable to P. jirovecii pneumonia when the CD4+ T cell count is < 200 cells/microliter.

Treatment of Pneumonia in Immunocompromised Patients

Broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy

The antimicrobial therapy depends on the underlying immune system defect and the risk factors for specific pathogens (1, 2). Consultation with an infectious diseases specialist is usually indicated. In patients with neutropenia, empiric treatment depends on its underlying cause, radiographic findings, and severity of illness.

Generally, broad-spectrum antibiotics that are effective against gram-negative bacilli (especially Pseudomonas species), Staphylococcus aureus (including methicillin-resistant S. aureus [MRSA]), and anaerobes are needed, as for hospital-acquired pneumonia. If patients with conditions other than HIV infection do not improve with 5 days of antibiotic therapy, antifungal therapy is frequently added empirically.

Prevention of Pneumonia in Immunocompromised Patients

Therapies to correct the underlying immune system defect or enhance its function are indicated for the prevention of pneumonia in immunocompromised patients. For example, patients with chemotherapy-induced neutropenia should receive granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF, or filgrastim), and patients with hypogammaglobulinemia due to an inherited or acquired disease (eg, multiple myeloma, leukemia) should receive IV immune globulin.

Patients with HIV and CD4+ T cell count < 200 cells/microliter should receive daily prophylactic therapy with sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim or other appropriate therapy (see prevention of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia).

Vaccination is critical in such patients. For example, patients at risk of pneumonia with encapsulated bacteria (eg, hypogammaglobulinemia, asplenia) should receive vaccinations against pneumococcus and H. influenzae.

Key Points

Consider typical as well as unusual pathogens in immunocompromised patients who have pneumonia.

If patients have hypoxemia or an abnormal chest radiographs, perform further testing, including obtaining lower respiratory samples, either from induced sputum or bronchoscopically.

Begin with broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy.
