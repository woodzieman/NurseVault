=== Delusional Parasitosis (Merck Manual Professional) ===
Section: Dermatology > Parasitic Skin Infections
Source: https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/parasitic-skin-infections/delusional-parasitosis
Retrieved: 2026-09-25

Patients with delusional parasitosis have an unshakable yet erroneous belief that they are infested with insects, worms, mites, lice, or other organisms. They often provide vivid descriptions of how the organisms enter their skin and move around their bodies and bring samples of hair, skin, and debris such as dried scabs, dust, and lint on slides or in containers (the matchbox sign) to prove that the infestation is real. The condition is considered a somatic type of delusional disorder. Patients may have other psychiatric or physical disorders (eg, organic brain disorders, toxic psychosis) (1).

Diagnosis of Delusional Parasitosis

Primarily history and physical examination

Rarely, skin scrapings

Diagnosis of delusional parasitosis is suspected by history and clinical examination. Evaluation requires ruling out true infestations and other physiologic disease by physical examination and judicious testing, such as skin scrapings and other tests as clinically indicated.

Treatment of Delusional Parasitosis

Psychological support and possibly antipsychotic medications

It is important to establish an empathetic and supportive relationship with the patient. Although often rejected by patients, the most effective treatment is with antipsychotic medications, typically second-generation antipsychotics, because of their lower rate of extrapyramidal symptoms (1). Some patients who also present with features consistent with obsessive compulsive disorder may benefit from a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) (eg, sertraline), but more data are needed to support routine use of this approach (2). Typically, the patient seeks confirmation that the medication treats the infestation itself, and any suggestion that the treatment is for something else is met with resistance, rejection, or both. Thus, effective treatment often requires diplomacy and the maintenance of a delicate balance between offering proper treatment and respecting the patient’s right to know.
