Mastocytosis is a group of disorders characterized by proliferation of mast cells and infiltration of the skin, other organs, or both. (See also Overview of Allergic and Atopic Disorders.)

In many cases, the etiology of mastocytosis involves an activating mutation (most commonly, D816V) in the KIT gene coding for the stem cell factor receptor c-kit, which is present on mast cells. D816V KIT mutations are present in approximately 80 to 95% of adults and in 30% of children with systemic mastocytosis (1). The result is autophosphorylation of the receptor, which causes uncontrolled clonal mast cell proliferation.

Pathology is characterized mainly by the release of mast cell mediators, including histamine, heparin, leukotrienes, and various inflammatory cytokines. Histamine causes many symptoms, including gastric symptoms, but other mediators also contribute. Significant organ infiltration may cause organ dysfunction.

Mast cell activation and subsequent mediator release may be triggered by:

Physical touch

Exercise

Alcohol

Medications (eg, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs], opioids)

Insect stings

Foods

Classification of Mastocytosis

Mastocytosis may be cutaneous or systemic.

Cutaneous mastocytosis

Cutaneous mastocytosis typically occurs in children. Specific manifestations include maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis (formerly known as urticaria pigmentosa), diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis, or mastocytoma (1).

Most patients present with maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis, a local or diffusely distributed salmon or brown maculopapular rash (caused by multiple small mast cell collections); this presentation is called the monomorphic variant. Sometimes, rashes can be of different shapes and sizes, and nodular lesions and plaques can additionally develop; this is called the polymorphic variant.

Less common are diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis, which is skin infiltration without discrete lesions, and mastocytoma, which is a large (1 to 5 cm) solitary collection of mast cells.

Cutaneous forms rarely progress to systemic disease in children but may do so in adults.

Systemic mastocytosis

Systemic mastocytosis most commonly occurs in adults and is characterized by multifocal bone marrow lesions; it often involves other organs, most commonly skin, lymph nodes, liver, spleen, and/or gastrointestinal (GI) tract.

Systemic mastocytosis is classified as:

Indolent mastocytosis, with no organ dysfunction and a good prognosis

Smoldering mastocytosis, with a high mast cell burden, but limited impacts on organ function

Aggressive mastocytosis, characterized by impaired organ function

Mastocytosis associated with other hematologic disorders (eg, myeloproliferative disorders, myelodysplasia, lymphoma)

Mast cell leukemia, with > 20% mast cells in bone marrow, no skin lesions, multiorgan failure, and a poor prognosis

Mast cell activation syndrome

Mast cell activation syndrome is a term used to describe a constellation of clinical features characterized by increased and inappropriate activation of mast cells with mediator release usually without clonal proliferation or organ infiltration by mast cells (2). The syndrome was originally diagnosed only when mediator release was idiopathic, but it has since been expanded to include release triggered by allergen-specific IgE, certain medications, or physical factors (also called secondary mast cell activation syndrome). Genetic causes are suspected, particularly KIT or aberrant CD25 mutations but not proved (also called primary mast cell activation syndrome). Most cases do not involve clonal proliferation of mast cells but are due to a lower threshold for mast cells to degranulate. Mast cell activation syndrome has frequently been associated with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, although the nature of the connection is unclear.

The manifestations of mast cell activation syndrome are frequently similar to those of patients with systemic mastocytosis; they include tachycardia, syncope, urticaria, flushing, nausea, vomiting, and brain fog.

It is unclear whether mast cell activation syndrome can progress to systemic mastocytosis or another form of mast cell disease and, if so, how many patients are affected.

Symptoms and Signs of Mastocytosis

Skin involvement is often pruritic in mastocytosis across the spectrum of manifestations. Triggers of mast cell activation and subsequent mediator release may worsen pruritus.

Stroking or rubbing skin lesions causes urticaria and erythema around the lesion (Darier sign); this reaction differs from dermatographism, which involves normal skin.

Systemic symptoms can occur with any form. The most common is flushing; the most dramatic are anaphylactoid and anaphylactic reactions with syncope and shock.

Other symptoms include epigastric pain due to peptic ulcer disease, nausea (because histamine stimulates gastric acid production), vomiting, chronic diarrhea, arthralgias, bone pain, and neuropsychiatric changes (eg, irritability, depression, mood lability). Hepatic and splenic infiltration may cause portal hypertension with resultant ascites.

Diagnosis of Mastocytosis

History and physical examination

Bone marrow biopsy

Serum tryptase levels (baseline and during symptoms if possible)

Systemic mastocytosis

The diagnosis of mastocytosis is suggested by clinical presentation. However, similar symptoms can be caused by many other disorders such as carcinoid syndrome, vipoma, gastrinoma (Zollinger-Ellison syndrome), and chronic urticaria.

Diagnostic confirmation usually requires bone marrow biopsy and measurement of serum tryptase levels (a marker of mast cell degranulation). Skin biopsy can be done to check for mast cells, but this test does not replace the need for a bone marrow biopsy to classify the diagnosis and staging.

Diagnosis of mastocytosis is confirmed when one major criterion and at least one minor criterion or ≥ 3 (of 4) minor criteria are met.

The major criterion is:

The presence of multifocal, dense aggregates of > 15 mast cells in bone marrow (preferred) or other extracutaneous organs (except the GI tract, lymph nodes, liver, or spleen)

The 4 minor criteria are:

Atypical morphology or spindle shapes in > 25% of the mast cells in bone marrow biopsy sections or aspirate

A KIT mutation at codon 816 (commonly Asp816Val) in bone marrow, peripheral blood, or other tissue

Bone marrow or other extracutaneous mast cells expressing the surface markers CD2, CD25, or both

Baseline serum tryptase levels > 20 ng/mL (> 20 mcg/L); values > 11.4 ng/mL (11.4 mcg/L) are considered elevated in most diagnostic laboratories

The baseline serum level of tryptase is elevated in systemic mastocytosis but is typically normal in cutaneous mastocytosis and mast cell activation syndrome. Advanced forms of mastocytosis can cause cytopenias because of bone marrow dysfunction.

Mast cell activation syndrome

The diagnosis of mast cell activation syndrome requires meeting all 3 criteria (1):

Symptoms consistent with mast cell degranulation and release of mast cell mediators

Laboratory evidence of an increase in mast cell mediators in the serum or urine such as an increase in serum tryptase during an event of 20% + 2 ng/mL above baseline or an increase in urinary metabolites such as N-methylhistamine, prostaglandin D2, or leukotriene E4 (2)

Positive clinical response to anti-mast cell therapy

Although the diagnostic criteria are clearly defined, many patients that are labeled as having or suspected to have mast cell activation syndrome do not actually meet the full criteria for diagnosis. This has led to a higher likelihood of overdiagnosis with a consequent lack of clarity regarding the true impact of the disease (3).

If the diagnosis is uncertain, levels of mast cell mediators and their metabolites (eg, 24-hour N-methylhistamine, prostaglandin D2, leukotriene E4) may be measured in plasma and urine; elevated levels support the diagnosis of mast cell disease but not necessarily systemic mastocytosis.

Most patients with mast cell activation typically have a normal bone marrow biopsy if it is done. However, in selected patients, bone densitometry may show early findings of osteoporosis (4).

Colonoscopy with biopsy may show slightly increased mast cell numbers but they are usually morphologically distinct (eg, scattered, single round or ovoid cells in the lamina propria without aggregates, clustering, or spindle-shaped) from systemic mastocytosis.

Treatment of Mastocytosis

For cutaneous mastocytosis, H1 blockers and possibly psoralen plus ultraviolet light or topical glucocorticoids

For systemic mastocytosis and mast cell activation syndrome, H1 and H2 blockers, cromolyn, ketotifen, montelukast, and aspirin

For aggressive forms, midostaurin, avapritinib, interferon alfa-2b, or glucocorticoids

Cutaneous mastocytosis

H1 blockers are effective for symptoms. Children with cutaneous forms require no additional treatment because most cases resolve spontaneously.

Adults with cutaneous forms may be treated with psoralen plus ultraviolet light or with topical glucocorticoids once or twice a day.

Mastocytoma usually involutes spontaneously and requires no treatment.

Systemic mastocytosis

Management of systemic mastocytosis focuses on symptom control and preventing anaphylaxis (1). Specific treatments generally include combinations of H1 and H2 blockers, aspirin, and mast cell stabilizers, while aggressive forms require immunomodulatory and/or targeted therapies. Management of anaphylactic reactions includes parenteral epinephrine, inhaled beta-agonists for wheezing, and IV fluid replacement for hypotension.

H1 and H2 blockers should be administered to all patients with systemic mastocytosis.

A self-injecting epinephrine syringe (autoinjector) should be prescribed for patients to use in the event of anaphylaxis.

Aspirin controls flushing but may enhance leukotriene production, thereby contributing to other mast cell–related symptoms; it should not be given to children because of the risk of Reye syndrome.

Mast cell stabilizers may also aid in symptom relief. Cromolyn may help by preventing mast cell degranulation. Ketotifen may also be effective. No treatment can reduce the number of tissue mast cells.

Omalizumab is an anti-IgE antibody that is sometimes used for moderate to severe asthma and chronic urticaria; it has sometimes been used in patients with mastocytosis or mast cell activation syndrome to try to prevent anaphylaxis.

Immunomodulatoryand/ortargeted therapies (eg, kinase inhibitors, interferons, glucocorticoids, chemotherapeutic agents) may be required in patients with an aggressive form of mastocytosis, which is characterized by increasing mast cell accumulation in different organs. These forms of mastocytosis lead to organ dysfunction. The multikinase inhibitors midostaurin or avapritinib (2) can be used to help control end-organ damage, cytopenias, and mast cell accumulation in bone marrow (3). Avapritinib can also be used for symptomatic management in indolent mastocytosis (4). Imatinib (a tyrosine kinase inhibitor) may be useful when treating adults with aggressive systemic mastocytosis but is ineffective in patients with the D816V KIT mutation. Interferon alfa-2b induces regression of bone lesions. Glucocorticoids may be required as adjunctive treatment for severe cases. Cytotoxic agents (eg, daunomycin, etoposide, 6-mercaptopurine) may be indicated for treatment of mast cell leukemia.

Mast cell activation syndrome

Treatment is directed at preventing mast cell mediator release (eg, with cromolyn and/or ketotifen) (5, 6). Additionally, regimens of H1 and H2 blockers to block histamine, aspirin to block prostaglandins, and montelukast to block leukotrienes may aid in symptom relief.

Known triggers should be avoided.

Key Points

Patients with cutaneous mastocytosis, usually children, typically present with a diffuse salmon or brown, often pruritic maculopapular rash.

Systemic mastocytosis causes multifocal bone marrow lesions, usually in adults, but often affects other organs.

All types can cause systemic symptoms (most commonly, flushing but sometimes anaphylactoid reactions).

For cutaneous mastocytosis, use H1 blockers to relieve symptoms, and in adults, consider treatment with psoralen plus ultraviolet light or topical glucocorticoids.

For systemic mastocytosis, use H1 and H2 blockers and sometimes cromolyn, and for aggressive mastocytosis, consider midostaurin, avapritinib, interferon alfa-2b, systemic glucocorticoids.

For mast cell activation syndrome, target mediators released during activation with antihistamines, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and mast cell stabilizers.

Make sure all patients with mastocytosis carry a prefilled, self-injecting epinephrine syringe.
