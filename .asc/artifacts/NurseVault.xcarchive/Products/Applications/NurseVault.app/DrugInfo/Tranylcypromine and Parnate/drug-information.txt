Drug Class: Tranylcypromine is a traditional, nonselective, oral antidepressant of the monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI) class.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 60 mg/day PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): agitation, akinesia, anxiety, ataxia, confusion, dizziness, drowsiness, headache, insomnia, mania, memory impairment, myoclonia, paresthesias, restlessness, weakness, acne vulgaris, alopecia, rash (unspecified), urticaria, appetite stimulation, peripheral edema, weight gain, ejaculation dysfunction, impotence (erectile dysfunction), increased urinary frequency, orgasm dysfunction, urinary incontinence, urinary retention, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatitis

In an Overdose: It has been suggested that plasma may be of value in the management of hypotension due to overdosage of MAOIs.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Tranylcypromine is contraindicated in patients with known hypersensitivity to tranylcypromine. Tranylcypromine is contraindicated in patients receiving other MAOI therapy or other drugs that possess MAOI-like activity (see Drug Interactions).

In patients receiving contraindicated drugs known to interact with MAOIs, the interacting drug should be discontinued for at least 1 to 2 weeks before initiating tranylcypromine therapy. Initiate at 20 mg/day PO, given in divided doses, in the morning and at lunch. May titrate weekly by 10 mg/day PO to efficacy and tolerance. Max: 60 mg/day PO, given in divided doses. American Psychiatric Association guidelines suggest other agents for first line therapy along with cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and for refractory cases, the guidelines recommend switching or combining these same treatments. Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) are considered for further refractory cases based on empirical evidence and balancing the potential severity of drug and food interactions and side effects. Patients with co-morbid panic disorder and social anxiety (n = 36) were randomly assigned to 12 weeks of low dose (30 mg/day) or high dose (60 mg/day) tranylcypromine. The primary measure for the number of panic attacks was the Sheehan Panic and Anticipatory Anxiety Scale. After 12 weeks of treatment, both groups were similarly effective; panic attacks were reduced 69.6% from baseline in the 30 mg/day PO group and 74.8% in the 60 mg/day group. Side effect incidence was higher in the 60 mg/day group (88.2%) vs. the 30 mg/day PO group (73.7%), a statistically significant difference. The most common side effects included orthostatic dizziness, decreased libido, insomnia, headache, constipation, somnolence, nausea, dizziness, and dry mouth.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Tranylcypromine is contraindicated in patients with known hypersensitivity to tranylcypromine.
 
Tranylcypromine is contraindicated in patients receiving other MAOI therapy or other drugs that possess MAOI-like activity (see Drug Interactions). Tranylcypromine is contraindicated when the patient is taking certain medications known to interact with MAO inhibitors (see Drug Interactions). Tranylcypromine is also contraindicated in combination with alcohol, excessive caffeine, or certain foods or beverages known to interact with MAO inhibitors (see Drug Interactions for ethanol and food). Alcoholic beverages, such as beer (including reduced-alcohol and alcohol-free beer); wine (red and white varieties); sherry; hard liquor; or liqueurs that contain tyramine, can precipitate hypertensive reactions if consumed by patients during MAOI therapy. Tranylcypromine should not be used in patients with active alcoholism. Ethanol intoxication should be avoided. Sudden and severe hypertensive episodes can also occur if foods containing high-pressor amines (e.g., tyramine or tryptophan) are consumed during therapy with tranylcypromine. Foods to avoid include: aged cheese; yeast extract; protein extract; soy sauce; fava bean or broad bean pods; smoked meats; pickled meats; chicken livers, smoked poultry; pickled poultry; smoked fish (lox, smoked salmon); pickled fish (pickled herring); fermented sausage (bologna, pepperoni, salami, summer sausage) or other fermented meat; bananas; avocados; and any over-ripe fruit. Following the discontinuation of tranylcypromine, dietary restrictions should continue for at least 2 weeks.
 
Tranylcypromine has not been systematically studied in animals or humans for its potential for substance abuse, tolerance, or physical dependence. Drug dependency has occurred in patients using supra-therapeutic doses of tranylcypromine; some of these patients had a history of substance abuse. Patients should be evaluated for a prior history of drug abuse and those patients should be followed closely to identify potential signs of misuse or abuse (e.g., development of tolerance, increments of dose, drug-seeking behavior). Because a withdrawal syndrome has been observed in some patients after the discontinuation of MAOIs, abrupt discontinuation of long-term treatment is not recommended, dosage should be reduced gradually when appropriate.
 
Tranylcypromine is contraindicated in patients with pheochromocytoma, since the associated tumor secretes pressor substances (e.g., norepinephrine) whose metabolism may be inhibited by tranylcypromine. Impaired metabolism of norepinephrine can increase blood pressure, and potentially cause a hypertensive crisis.
 
Tranylcypromine is contraindicated in patients with a history of hepatic disease (e.g., jaundice, hepatitis) or in patients with abnormal liver function tests. Hepatic coma could occur if a MAO inhibitor is administered to patients with cirrhosis. There is a low incidence of altered hepatic function or jaundice in patients treated with tranylcypromine. Periodic liver function tests should be performed during tranylcypromine therapy; tranylcypromine should be discontinued at the first sign of hepatic dysfunction.
 
Tranylcypromine can change the pattern of seizures and should be used with caution in patients with epilepsy or a preexisting seizure disorder. Because tranylcypromine lowers the convulsive threshold in some animal experiments, suitable precautions should be taken if patients with a seizure disorder are treated. Tranylcypromine appears to have varying effects in epileptic patients; some have a decrease in frequency of seizures, while others have more seizures.
 
Anticholinergic effects during therapy with tranylcypromine may result in drowsiness or dizziness and the patient should use caution while driving or operating machinery while taking tranylcypromine until the effects of the drug are known. Effects may be additive to existing anticholinergic medications or CNS depressants.
 
Tranylcypromine should be used with caution in asthma or bronchitis because of possible changes in the blockade of sympathetic neurotransmission and marked pressor effects from beta-adrenergic bronchodilators.
 
Tranylcypromine should be used with caution in diabetes mellitus; increased insulin sensitivity may occur. Requirements for insulin or oral hypoglycemics may be decreased.
 
Tranylcypromine should be used with caution in patients with hyperthyroidism because sensitivity to pressor amines may be further increased.
 
Patients taking tranylcypromine should not undergo elective surgery requiring general anesthesia. Patients should also inform their dentists of MAOI treatment prior to pursuing dental work. Also, they should not be given cocaine (can block reuptake of monoamines into the adrenergic nerve terminals) or local anesthetics containing sympathomimetic vasoconstrictors (e.g., epinephrine). Combined hypotensive effects can occur with tranylcypromine and spinal anesthesia. Tranylcypromine should be discontinued at least 10 days before elective surgery.
 
All effective antidepressants can transform depression into mania or hypomania in predisposed individuals. It is generally believed (though not established in controlled trials) that treating such an episode with an antidepressant alone may increase the likelihood of precipitation of a mixed/manic episode in patients at risk for bipolar disorder. If a patient develops manic symptoms, tranylcypromine should be withheld and appropriate therapy initiated to treat the manic symptoms. Additionally, depression may be the presenting symptom of a mixed/manic episode of bipolar disorder. Patients should be adequately screened for bipolar disorder prior to initiating an antidepressant. Such screening should include a detailed psychiatric history, including a family history of suicide, bipolar disorder, and depression. Patients with depression or comorbid depression in the setting of other psychiatric illness being treated with antidepressants should be observed for clinical worsening and suicidality, especially during the initial few months of drug therapy, or at times of dose changes. Tranylcypromine may aggravate coexisting symptoms in depressive patients, such as anxiety and agitation, especially at the beginning of treatment. Dosage reduction and slow dosage titration may help limit hyperstimulatory effects. Tranylcypromine should be used cautiously in hyperactive or agitated patients, as well as in patients with schizophrenia or psychosis because it can cause excessive stimulation. Caregivers should be advised to closely observe the patient on a daily basis and to communicate immediately with the prescriber the emergence of agitation, irritability, unusual changes in behavior, or suicidality. It should be noted that tranylcypromine is not approved for use in treating bipolar depression.
 
Tranylcypromine is not FDA approved for use in children. The safety and effectiveness of tranylcypromine has not been established in pediatric patients less than 16 years old. In October 2004, the FDA directed manufacturers of all antidepressants to include a boxed warning detailing the risk of suicide in pediatric patients. A causal role has been established for antidepressants in inducing suicidality in pediatric patients. The risk of suicidality for these drugs was identified in a pooled analysis of 24 placebo-controlled trials (n=4400) lasting up to 16 weeks in pediatric patients with major depressive disorder (MDD), obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), or other psychiatric disorders. The analysis showed a greater risk of suicidality during the first few months of treatment in those receiving antidepressants (SSRIs and others). The average risk of such events on drug was 4% and 2% for placebo; however, no suicides occurred in these trials. Pooled analysis of short-term clinical trials during early phase treatment of SSRIs and other antidepressants in young adults (18 to 24 years) also showed an increased risk of suicidal thinking and behavior. The clinical need for an antidepressant in children or young adults for any use must be weighed against the risk of increased suicidality; patients who are started on therapy should be observed closely for clinical worsening, suicidality, or unusual changes in behavior, particularly within the first few months of starting therapy or at the time of dose increase or decrease; such observation would generally include at least weekly face-to-face contact with patients during the first 4 weeks of treatment, then every other week visits for the next 4 weeks, then at 12 weeks, and as clinically indicated beyond 12 weeks; additional contact by telephone may be appropriate between visits. It is unknown if the suicidality risk in children or young adults extends to longer-term therapy (i.e., beyond several months). The possibility of a suicide attempt is inherent in patients with depressive symptoms, whether these occur in primary depression or in association with another primary psychiatric disorder. All patients with a history of suicidal ideation or behaviors and those with a prominence of suicidal ideation prior to treatment are considered at an increased risk for suicidal ideation or attempts, and should be closely monitored during treatment with tranylcypromine. In patients who exhibit changes in symptoms, worsening of depression or suicidality, a decision should be made to change or discontinue treatment. If discontinuing, medication should be tapered as rapidly as possible, but with recognition that abrupt discontinuation can also cause adverse symptoms. All antidepressants should be prescribed in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management in order to reduce the risk of overdose.
 
Tranylcypromine is contraindicated in patients with hypertension (including patients receiving antihypertensive agents), cardiac disease, or confirmed or suspected cerebrovascular defect (e.g., cerebrovascular disease, intracranial bleeding, increased intracranial pressure, recent head trauma, stroke), because of the possible adverse effects on blood pressure. MAOIs can also suppress chest pain that would otherwise serve as a warning of myocardial ischemia (e.g., angina) or acute myocardial infarction. Tranylcypromine is also contraindicated in patients with a history of headache because it can mask the headache associated with drug-related hypertensive reactions. Headaches during therapy may be the first symptom of a hypertensive reaction to an MAOI. Hypertensive crisis is the most serious side effect of MAOIs, and if palpitations, neck stiffness, chest pains, or headaches occur, tranylcypromine should be discontinued. Blood pressure should be routinely measured any time a patient receiving tranylcypromine complains of a headache, palpitations, diaphoresis, dizziness, neck stiffness or chest constriction, nausea, or vomiting. Tranylcypromine should be used with caution in the elderly because of the increased possibility of cerebrovascular disease. Analysis of short-term studies in geriatric patients aged 65 years and older showed a decline in the risk of suicidal thinking and behavior during early phase treatment of SSRIs and other antidepressants, contrary to the data obtained in children and young adults.
 
Parkinson's disease may be aggravated by tranylcypromine, because of the drug's induction of tremor, muscle spasm or myoclonic movement. Also, serious reactions have been reported when a non-selective MAOI was used in combination with anti-parkinsonian therapy. Use of a more selective MAOI, such as selegiline, may be indicated in patients with Parkinson's disease.
 
The use of metrizamide or iohexol (radiographic contrast administration) in patients receiving MAOIs can increase the risk of seizures by lowering the seizure threshold. MAOIs should be discontinued at least 48 hours before myelography and not resumed until 24 hours after the procedure.
 
Tranylcypromine is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. It is not known whether tranylcypromine can cause fetal harm when administered to a pregnant woman. Animals studies indicate that tranylcypromine crosses the placenta. Tranylcypromine should be used during pregnancy only when the benefit to the mother clearly outweighs the risk to the fetus.
 
Tranylcypromine is excreted in human milk; the effects of tranylcypromine on the nursing infant are unknown. Many drugs are excreted into breast milk and can pose a potential threat to the nursing infant. Tranylcypromine should be avoided in breast-feeding women except when clearly needed.

    General Information
    Tyramine ingestion (by means of tyramine-rich foods or beverages) is contraindicated with tranylcypromine due to the risk of hypertensive crisis with significant tyramine use. Specific dietary restrictions must be followed to avoid sudden and severe hypertensive episodes, including hypertensive crisis. Carefully review the needed dietary restrictions with any patient who is initiating treatment with tranylcypromine. Following the discontinuation of tranylcypromine, dietary restrictions should continue for at least 2 weeks to reduce the risk of hypertensive crisis. Discontinue tranylcypromine, other drugs, foods or beverages suspected to contribute to the hypertensive crisis immediately.
    Headline
    cardiovascular disease, headache
    General Information
    Careful evaluation of the benefits and risks of tranylcypromine treatment is essential for people with cardiovascular disease, including hypertension or cerebrovascular disease, or in people who have a history of headache, as a headache may be a prodromal symptom of a hypertensive crisis. Blood pressure should be monitored closely in all patients receiving tranylcypromine. If symptoms (e.g., palpitations, headache) develop, patients should stop treatment and seek immediate medical care. Severe hypertension (greater than 180/120 mmHg) with evidence of organ dysfunction requires immediate blood pressure reduction. Discontinue tranylcypromine, other drugs, foods or beverages suspected to contribute to the hypertensive crisis immediately. Fever should be managed with external cooling, and additional measures to control the causes of hyperthermia (psychomotor agitation, increased neuromuscular activity, persistent seizure activity) may be required. Additionally, hypotension, including postural hypotension, has been observed during therapy with tranylcypromine. Postural hypotension is especially common at doses above 30 mg/day and may result in syncope. Symptoms of postural hypotension are seen most commonly, but not exclusively, in patients with pre-existing hypertension.

    Headline
    adolescents, children, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    The safety and effectiveness of tranylcypromine have not been established in pediatric patients less than 18 years of age. A boxed warning in the product label describes the risk of suicidality and suicidal ideation in children, adolescents, and young adults receiving antidepressants. In a pooled analysis of placebo-controlled trials of antidepressants (n = 4,500 pediatrics and 77,000 adults), there was an increased risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors in patients 24 years of age and younger receiving an antidepressant versus placebo, with considerable variation in the risk of suicidality among drugs. The difference in absolute risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors across different indications was highest in those with major depression. The need for an antidepressant in children, adolescents, or young adults for any use must be weighed against the risk of suicidality; it is unknown if this risk extends to long-term use. All patients should be monitored for symptom worsening or suicidality, especially at treatment initiation or after dose changes. Caregivers and/or patients should immediately notify the prescriber of changes in behavior or suicidal ideation. A change to the treatment regimen or discontinuation of tranylcypromine may be necessary in patients with emerging suicidality or worsening depression.

    General Information
    Abuse of tranylcypromine has been reported, with some patients having a history of a prior substance abuse disorder. The potential for abuse and increased risk for serious adverse reactions with higher doses should be considered when determining whether to use tranylcypromine in patients with a history of a substance abuse disorder.

In people with bipolar disorder, treating a depressive episode with tranylcypromine or another antidepressant may precipitate a mixed/manic episode. Additionally, tranylcypromine therapy may aggravate coexisting symptoms in depression, such as anxiety and agitation.

    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Dosage selection of antidepressant MAOIs should be cautious in the geriatric adult, usually starting at the low end of the dosing range. Published data for MAOIs are insufficient in older adults, but the incidence of side effects such as orthostatic hypotension due to antidepressant treatment in geriatric adults is usually greater than that seen in younger adults. MAOIs are generally third-line treatments for adult depression. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates antidepressant use in long-term care facilities. When an antidepressant is being used to manage behavior, stabilize mood, or treat a psychiatric disorder, tapering as outlined in the OBRA guidelines should be attempted unless clinically contraindicated. Dosages and durations of treatment used should align with prescribing labels, published literature recommendations, and expert guidelines.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Use tranylcypromine during pregnancy when the benefit to the pregnant individual outweighs the potential risk to the fetus. There are limited reports of placental infarction and congenital anomalies associated with tranylcypromine use during human pregnancy. However, these reports do not inform of the presence or absence of a drug-associated risk. Animal studies indicate that tranylcypromine crosses the placenta and can cause a dose-dependent decrease in uterine blood flow. An increased risk of malformations was found in the Collaborative Perinatal Project which monitored 21 mother-child pairs with exposure to a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI) during the first trimester of pregnancy (tranylcypromine pairs = 13). Limitations of this data include the small sample size, absence of malformation descriptions, and inclusion of isoniazid as an MAOI agent. Teratogenicity was not observed in 2 separate cases of in utero exposure to a MAOI. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant individuals exposed to tranylcypromine; information about the registry can be obtained at womensmentalhealth.org/research/pregnancyregistry or by calling 1-866-961-2388.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use of tranylcypromine with great caution during breast-feeding due to a lack of safety data. Tranylcypromine is present in human milk. There are no data on the effects of tranylcypromine on the breast-fed child or its effects on milk production. It is generally advised to consider alternatives to tranylcypromine during breast-feeding. Due to individual variability in response to antidepressants, continued use of the current antidepressant regimen may be considered. However, if initiating treatment in a lactating individual, sertraline, paroxetine, or nortriptyline are preferred. Data indicate these drugs yield low or undetectable infant serum levels.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Tranylcypromine is a potent inhibitor of monoamine oxidase, an enzyme that has wide distribution throughout the body. Administration of tranylcypromine can therefore be expected to produce diverse effects. Most adverse effects subside with continued dosage, or respond to dosage reduction. The most important reaction associated with MAO inhibitors is the occurrence of hypertensive crises, which have sometimes been fatal. Such crises may occur from the coadministration of MAOIs and certain drugs and foods (see Drug Interactions). These crises are characterized by some or all of the following symptoms: occipital headache which may radiate frontally, palpitations, neck stiffness or soreness, nausea/vomiting, diaphoresis (sometimes with fever and sometimes with cold, clammy skin or chills), and photophobia. Either sinus tachycardia or sinus bradycardia may occur, and may be accompanied by palpitations or constricting chest pain (angina) and dilated pupils (mydriasis). Intracranial bleeding, sometimes fatal, has been reported in association with the increased blood pressure. Rare instances have been reported in which hypertension was accompanied by twitching or myoclonic fibrillation of skeletal muscles with hyperpyrexia, sometimes progressing to generalized rigidity and coma. Blood pressure should be followed closely during tranylcypromine treatment to detect any pressor response. Discontinue therapy immediately if palpitations or frequent headaches occur as these symptoms may be prodromal of a hypertensive crisis. Headache tends to abate as blood pressure is lowered. If a hypertensive crisis occurs, tranylcypromine should be discontinued, and therapy to lower blood pressure should be instituted immediately. Although there has been no systematic study of the treatment of hypertensive crises with MAOIs, phentolamine has been used and is recommended at a dosage of 5 mg IV, given slowly to avoid hypotensive effects. Fever should be managed by external cooling. Other symptomatic and supportive measure may be necessary in some cases.Orthostatic hypotension is common side effect of MAOIs, and is most likely to occur in those patients with preexisting hypertension or in those patients receiving dosages of trancypromine > 30 mg/day PO, and may result in syncope. If a tendency toward hypotension is detected during routine monitoring of initial tranylcypromine therapy, the dose should be increased more gradually. Dose reduction may counteract hypotensive effects, but if orthostatic hypotension worsens or persists, the MAOI should be withdrawn. Blood pressure usually returns rapidly to pretreatment levels upon discontinuation of the drug. Postural hypotension can be alleviated by having the patient lie down until the blood pressure returns to normal. Dizziness is often worse if the patient changes positions too rapidly. It has been suggested that plasma may be of value in the management of hypotension due to overdosage of MAOIs. Peripheral edema may progress to overt congestive heart failure in severe circumstances. Persistent edema may be a sign of impaired water excretion due to SIADH, which has been reported infrequently with tranylcypromine.Anticholinergic effects during therapy with tranylcypromine may result in a number of adverse reactions such as drowsiness, confusion, constipation, blurred vision or mydriasis, xerostomia (dry mouth), sweating, urinary retention, weakness, or headache. Patients with these symptoms should have their blood pressure monitored to rule out potential hypertensive crisis, as some of these symptoms can be warning signs of hypertensive events. Most true anticholinergic effects can be relieved by lowering the dosage or by giving suitable concomitant medication. Anorexia has been reported with tranylcypromine, but appetite stimulation and weight gain may occur in some patients.Sexual dysfunction, which may include orgasm dysfunction (anorgasmia or delayed orgasm) in both males and females, ejaculation dysfunction (retarded or no ejaculation), priapism, and possible impotence (erectile dysfunction), can occur during therapy with tranylcypromine. Urinary retention has been reported.Shaking or tremor can result from action of MAOIs on the vasomotor centers. Other CNS effects of tranylcypromine include myoclonia, hyperreflexia, twitching, fatigue, restlessness, and insomnia (or other sleep disturbance). Nystagmus, paresthesias, numbness, tinnitus, and euphoria have been reported infrequently. In rare instances, hallucinations have been reported with high dosages, but have disappeared upon dose reduction or discontinuation. Depression may obscure hypomania, which may emerge as the depression is lifted. Tranylcypromine can also exacerbate preexisting agitation. Periodic liver function tests are recommended, especially during long-term therapy; use of the drug should be discontinued at the first sign of hepatic dysfunction or jaundice. Hematological adverse reactions that have been reported with tranylcypromine therapy include anemia, leukopenia, agranulocytosis and thrombocytopenia.Tranylcypromine lowers the convulsive threshold in some animal experiments. Although a causal link between the emergence of such symptoms and either the worsening of depression and/or the emergence of suicidal impulses has not been established, there is concern that such symptoms may represent precursors to emerging suicidality. Consideration should be given to changing the therapeutic regimen, including possibly discontinuing the medication, in patients whose depression is persistently worse or who are experiencing emergent suicidality or symptoms that might be precursors to worsening depression or suicidal ideation. Changing or stopping the therapeutic regimen may be especially important if these symptoms are severe, abrupt in onset, or were not part of the patients presenting symptoms. Many of these adverse symptoms have been reported in clinical trials and have not always been associated with suicidality. Rapid discontinuation of many antidepressants may also result in some of these adverse CNS events.

In a pooled analysis of placebo-controlled trials of antidepressants (n = 4,500 pediatrics and 77,000 adults), there was an increased risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors in patients 24 years of age and younger receiving an antidepressant versus placebo, with considerable variation in the risk of suicidality among drugs.     Tremor has been reported during treatment with tranylcypromine. Shaking or tremor can result from action of monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) on the vasomotor centers.     neuroleptic malignant syndrome-like symptoms, serotonin syndrome
    General Information
    The development of a potentially life-threatening serotonin syndrome has been reported with MAOIs when used concomitantly with other serotonergic drugs. Such drugs include SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclic antidepressants, triptans, fentanyl, lithium, tramadol, tryptophan, buspirone, St. John's wort, S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM-e), and other MAOIs used to treat nonpsychiatric disorders (such as linezolid or intravenous methylene blue). The concomitant use, or use in rapid succession, of tranylcypromine with other serotonergic drugs is contraindicated. However, there may be circumstances when treatment with other serotonergic substances (such as linezolid or IV methylene blue) is necessary and cannot be delayed. In such cases, tranylcypromine must be discontinued as soon as possible before initiating treatment with the other agent. Manifestations of the serotonin syndrome may include mental status changes (e.g., agitation, hallucinations, delirium, coma), autonomic instability (e.g., tachycardia, labile blood pressure, dizziness, diaphoresis, flushing, hyperthermia; with possible rapid fluctuations of vital signs), neuromuscular symptoms (e.g., tremor, rigidity, myoclonus, hyper-reflexia, incoordination), seizures, and/or gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., nausea, vomiting, diarrhea). Fatal outcome of serotonin syndrome has been reported, including in patients who had been treated with tranylcypromine. In some cases of an interaction between tranylcypromine and SSRIs or SNRIs, the features of the syndrome resembled neuroleptic malignant syndrome, thus resulting in neuroleptic malignant syndrome-like symptoms. Treatment with tranylcypromine and any concomitant serotonergic agents should be discontinued immediately if the above events occur, and supportive symptomatic treatment should be initiated.

Description
  Text
  Tranylcypromine is a traditional, nonselective, oral antidepressant of the monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI) class. Due to the dietary restrictions required for MAOI use, as well as their side effect profile and the potential for serious drug-drug and drug-food interactions, MAOIs are primarily considered an option for major depressive disorder (MDD) in adults refractory to treatment with other antidepressants (e.g., third-line treatment), and they may be considered in some patients with combined anxiety/depression resistant to other treatments. All product labels for antidepressants, including traditional MAOI antidepressants, contain a boxed warning related to an increased risk of suicidality in children, adolescents, and young adults during the initial stages of therapy when treating depression or other conditions; therefore, the necessity of pharmacologic therapy versus the potential risks should be carefully considered in these populations.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Tranylcypromine binds irreversibly to monoamine oxidase (MAO). Like other irreversible inhibitors, the MAO enzyme activity cannot be restored until the body replaces the enzyme through new enzyme synthesis, which takes a period of time after drug discontinuation. When tranylcypromine is withdrawn, MAO activity is recovered in 3—5 days, although the drug is excreted in 24 hours. Depression is believed to result from dysregulation of CNS neurotransmitter systems. Antidepressant activity arises from the increased availability of monoamines, resulting from the inhibition of the enzyme MAO. Reduction of MAO activity results in an increased concentration of neurotransmitters, such as epinephrine, norepinephrine, and dopamine, at various storage sites in the central nervous system and sympathetic nervous system.
 
Tranylcypromine is a nonselective MAOI that desensitizes alpha- and beta-adrenergic and serotonin receptors. The delay of up to 4 weeks in therapeutic response to MAOI drugs may be accounted for by alterations in receptor characteristics rather than by increased neurotransmitter concentration. Inhibition of MAO in the GI tract and liver can result in systemic absorption of large amounts of tyramine, such as from ingestion of foods high in tyramine content. Consequently, severe hypertension may occur from a massive displacement of norepinephrine by tyramine from adrenergic storage sites. The exact mechanism by which MAOIs produce hypotension is unknown, but it has been proposed to be the result of actions at alpha- or beta-adrenergic receptors by false neurotransmitters which also aid in causing hypertensive crisis. It has been suggested that slow accumulation of these false neurotransmitters from MAO inhibition (e.g. octopamine produced from tyramine) occurs in neurons. It is also thought that octopamine may displace norepinephrine at these sites, simultaneously releasing octopamine, and causing a blockade of sympathetic neurotransmission. This is in contrast to the large release of norepinephrine which occurs from more rapid absorption of tyramine from the GI tract, causing a hypertensive crisis. MAOIs also interfere with the hepatic metabolism of many drugs.

  Hepatic Impairment

  Tranylcypromine is contraindicated in patients with severe renal impairment. In patients with mild to moderate renal impairment, tranylcypromine should be used with caution due to the potential for accumulation of active metabolites.
