Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Because of the long half-life of cariprazine and its active metabolites, changes in dose or discontinuation may not be fully reflected in plasma concentrations or clinical response for several weeks. Following discontinuation of cariprazine, plasma concentrations of cariprazine and its active metabolites decline by approximately 50% in about 1 week; however, changes in clinical symptoms may not be immediately apparent. Monitor patient for clinical response and adverse reactions for several weeks after each dosage change or discontinuation. There are no systematic data to specifically address switching patients from cariprazine to other antipsychotics or regarding concurrent administration with other antipsychotics.

    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, obesity
    General Information
    Cariprazine, like other atypical antipsychotic agents, may cause metabolic changes. Metabolic changes are of particular concern in patients with pre-existing risk factors, such as diabetes mellitus and/or obesity. Increased blood sugar, worsening or new-onset diabetes, dyslipidemia, and weight gain may occur during therapy. While all medications in the atypical antipsychotic class have been shown to produce some metabolic changes, each drug has its own specific risk profile. Metabolic changes may increase cardiovascular or cerebrovascular risk over time. Hyperglycemia, sometimes associated with diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar coma, or death, has been reported in people treated with atypical antipsychotics. Monitor weight and for symptoms of hyperglycemia. Regularly monitor patients with established diabetes mellitus for changes in weight and worsening of glucose control during atypical antipsychotic treatment. In those with risk factors for diabetes mellitus (e.g., obesity, family history of diabetes), perform fasting blood glucose testing at the beginning of treatment. In patients who develop symptoms of hyperglycemia during treatment, order fasting blood glucose testing. In some cases, hyperglycemia has resolved when the antipsychotic is discontinued. However, some patients require continuation of antidiabetic agents despite discontinuation of the suspect drug.
    Headline
    hyperlipidemia
    General Information
    Cariprazine, like other atypical antipsychotic agents, may cause metabolic changes. Metabolic changes are of particular concern in those with pre-existing risk factors, such as hyperlipidemia (e.g., hypercholesterolemia or hypertriglyceridemia). While all medications in the atypical antipsychotic class have been shown to produce some metabolic changes, each drug has its own specific risk profile. Dyslipidemia (increased cholesterol and/or triglycerides) may occur during therapy. Metabolic changes may increase cardiovascular or cerebrovascular risk over time. Clinical monitoring of serum lipid profiles is recommended during cariprazine treatment. Inform all patients of the importance of physical activity and maintaining a nutritionally balanced diet during treatment.
    Headline
    cardiovascular disease, hypovolemia
    General Information
    Cariprazine should be used with caution in patients with known cardiovascular disease (e.g., history of myocardial infarction or ischemic heart disease, heart failure or conduction abnormalities) or conditions which would predispose patients to hypotension (dehydration, hypovolemia, and treatment with antihypertensive medications). Cariprazine can cause orthostatic hypotension and, in rare cases, syncope; risks may be increased in patients with underlying risk factors. Orthostatic hypotension from antipsychotic use may lead to falls with the potential for fractures and other injuries; a fall risk assessment should be completed and reassessed as needed in patients with conditions, diseases, or concurrent medication use that could exacerbate orthostasis.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Cariprazine may increase the risk of suicidal ideation and behaviors in children, adolescents, and young adults receiving antidepressants. In a pooled analysis of placebo-controlled trials of antidepressants (n = 4,500 pediatrics and 77,000 adults), there was an increased risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors in patients 24 years and younger receiving an antidepressant versus placebo, with considerable variation in the risk of suicidality among drugs. The difference in absolute risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors across different indications was highest in those with major depression. The need for an antidepressant in children, adolescents, or young adults for any use must be weighed against the risk of suicidality; it is unknown if this risk extends to long-term use. All patients should be monitored for symptom worsening or suicidality, especially at treatment initiation or after dose changes. Caregivers and/or patients should immediately notify the prescriber of changes in behavior or suicidal ideation. A change to the treatment regimen or discontinuation of cariprazine may be necessary in patients with emerging suicidality or worsening depression. The Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) list includes cariprazine as a potentially inappropriate medication for use in children and adolescents and use should be avoided. If avoidance is not possible, use cariprazine with caution and appropriate monitoring.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Antipsychotics, including cariprazine, may cause somnolence, postural hypotension, motor and sensory instability, which may lead to falls and, consequently, fractures or other injuries. For people with diseases, conditions, or medications that could exacerbate these effects, complete fall risk assessments when initiating antipsychotic treatment and periodically during long-term antipsychotic therapy. Cariprazine may have the potential to impair judgment, thinking, or motor skills; advise patients about driving or operating machinery or other activities requiring coordination and concentration until they are reasonably certain that cariprazine does not affect them adversely. Given the primary CNS effects of cariprazine, use caution during coadministration with other CNS depressants and alcohol. Ethanol ingestion may further impair cognitive and motor skills and patients should be advised to avoid use of alcoholic beverages when taking cariprazine.
    Headline
    leukopenia
    General Information
    Use cariprazine with caution in those with a history of leukopenia. Hematologic effects including leukopenia, neutropenia, and agranulocytosis have been associated with antipsychotic use. A history of drug-induced leukopenia or neutropenia or pre-existing low white blood cell (WBC) count may increase the likelihood of recurrence during treatment with an antipsychotic medication. Patients with a history of clinically significant low WBC count or drug-induced leukopenia/neutropenia should have frequent complete blood count (CBC) assessments during the first few months of treatment. Consider discontinuation of the antipsychotic if a clinically significant decline in WBC occurs in the absence of an identifiable cause. Monitor patients with clinically significant neutropenia closely for fever and infection; institute appropriate medical intervention if necessary. Cariprazine should be discontinued in patients with severe neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count less than 1,000/mm3), with ongoing medical care until the symptoms resolve.
    Headline
    seizures
    General Information
    As with other antipsychotic drugs, cariprazine should be used with caution in patients with a history of seizures, a seizure disorder, or with conditions that may lower the seizure threshold. Conditions that lower the seizure threshold may be more prevalent in a population who are 65 years or older.
    Headline
    conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature
    General Information
    Disruption of the body's ability to reduce core body temperature has been attributed to antipsychotic agents. Patients receiving cariprazine should be advised of conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature (e.g., exercising strenuously, exposure to extreme heat, receiving concomitant medication with anticholinergic activity, or being subject to dehydration).
    Headline
    Parkinson disease
    General Information
    Avoid use of cariprazine in people with Parkinson disease unless the benefit clearly outweighs the risk of worsening of motor symptoms. Atypical antipsychotics can worsen the motor symptoms of Parkinson disease and can also lead to the development of extrapyramidal symptoms.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Cariprazine and its metabolites are extensively metabolized in the liver. Cariprazine is not recommended for use in severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure because the drug has not been evaluated in this patient population. No dosage adjustments are required for patients with mild to moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A or B).
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Cariprazine should be used with caution in patients with renal impairment. Cariprazine is not recommended for use in those with severe renal impairment (CrCl less than 30 mL/minute), including renal failure, because the drug has not been evaluated in this patient population. No adjustment is necessary in mild to moderate renal impairment (CrCl 30 mL/minute or more). 60164
    Headline
    dysphagia, increased risk for aspiration
    General Information
    People with dysphagia or who are at increased risk for aspiration should be closely monitored while receiving cariprazine. Antipsychotic use, including cariprazine, has been associated with esophageal dysmotility and aspiration of gastric contents. Aspiration pneumonia is a common cause of morbidity and mortality in older adults, in particular those with advanced Alzheimer disease.
    Headline
    dementia with psychotic disturbance, geriatric
    General Information
    Antipsychotics, including cariprazine, pose a risk of increased mortality when used for dementia with psychotic disturbance in geriatric adults. Most of the deaths are cardiovascular (e.g., heart failure, sudden death) or infectious (e.g., pneumonia) in nature. A higher incidence of stroke and transient ischemic attack, including fatal stroke, has also been reported. Initiate cariprazine at the lower end of the dosage range and titrate carefully in the older adult. The Beers Criteria label antipsychotics as potentially inappropriate medications for geriatric adults, except for specific indications like agitation in Alzheimer disease, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder adjunct therapy, Parkinson disease psychosis, or short-term use as part of an antiemetic regimen during chemotherapy. Avoid use in delirium, dementia, or non-psychotic Parkinson disease. Reserve antipsychotic use for when non-pharmacologic strategies have failed, are not possible, or the patient poses a danger to self or others. Due to the potential for hyponatremia and SIADH, monitor sodium levels. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates antipsychotic use in long-term care facilities and requires documented clinical justification. Taper antipsychotics unless clinically contraindicated, following guidelines for indications, dosages, and duration. OBRA limits "as needed" use to 14 days in long-term care facilities, with ongoing use requiring evaluation by the prescriber.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no available data regarding cariprazine use during pregnancy to be informative of any drug-associated risks for birth defects or miscarriage. Based on animal data, cariprazine may cause fetal harm. Administration of cariprazine to pregnant rats caused malformations, lower pup survival, and developmental delays in the offspring at drug exposures that were less than the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD, i.e., 6 mg/day). However, cariprazine was not teratogenic in rabbits at doses up to 4.6 times the MRHD. The major active metabolite of cariprazine, DDCAR, has been detected in adults up to 12 weeks after discontinuation of cariprazine. It is not clear if antipsychotics, via their effect on prolactin, would affect labor or delivery. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant patients exposed to cariprazine; information about the registry can be obtained at womensmentalhealth.org/clinical-and-research-programs/pregnancyregistry or by calling 1-866-961-2388.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    The health benefits of breast-feeding should be considered along with the mother's clinical need for cariprazine and any potential adverse effects on the breast-fed infant from cariprazine or the underlying maternal condition. Lactation studies have not been conducted to assess the presence of cariprazine in human milk, the effects on the breast-fed infant, or the effects on milk production; however, the drug is excreted in the milk of lactating rats. Also, antipsychotics may cause hyperprolactinemia and galactorrhea, and thus may interfere with proper lactation. Because there is a lack of experience with cariprazine during breast-feeding, other agents may be preferred while nursing a newborn or preterm infant. Alternate medications for consideration include atypical agents such as olanzapine or quetiapine. However, due to individual variability in response to antipsychotics, it may be prudent to continue the woman's existing regimen if ongoing treatment is deemed necessary during breast-feeding. Data related to the safety of antipsychotics during breast-feeding are limited and chronic administration of any antipsychotic during lactation should be avoided if possible. If an antipsychotic is used, closely monitor the nursing infant for excessive drowsiness, lethargy, and developmental delays. Combination treatment with antipsychotics may increase the risk of these adverse events.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Consider pregnancy testing in people who may become pregnant prior to initiation of an antipsychotic, as there are limited controlled data regarding use of these agents during gestation. Ideally, health care providers should use pre-conceptual planning to help such individuals taking atypical antipsychotics balance the risk for illness relapse with the potential reproductive risk of continuing medication while pregnant.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero during the third trimester of pregnancy are at risk for extrapyramidal and/or withdrawal symptoms following delivery. There have been reports of agitation, hypertonia, hypotonia, tremor, somnolence, respiratory distress and feeding disorders in these neonates. These complications have varied in severity and have ranged from self-limited to those requiring intensive care unit support and prolonged hospitalization. Neonates exhibiting signs or symptoms of extrapyramidal effects or withdrawal should be carefully monitored. The major active metabolite of cariprazine, DDCAR, has been detected in adults up to 12 weeks after discontinuation of cariprazine; neonatal data are not available. The knowledge about long-term neurobehavioral effects in offspring is limited for all antipsychotic agents, including cariprazine, and requires further investigation.

Acarbose: (Moderate) Atypical antipsychotic therapy may aggravate diabetes mellitus and cause metabolic changes such as hyperglycemia. Monitor patients on antidiabetic agents for worsening glycemic control. The atypical antipsychotics have been associated with metabolic changes, including hyperglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar, hyperglycemic states, and diabetic coma. Aggravation of diabetes mellitus has been reported. Possible mechanisms include atypical antipsychotic-induced insulin resistance or direct beta-cell inhibition.

Fluconazole: (Major) A cariprazine dosage reduction may be required during concomitant use with fluconazole. Cariprazine dosage modification recommendations vary by indication, previous exposure to cariprazine, and age. When initiating cariprazine in adults receiving fluconazole, reduce the cariprazine starting dose to 0.75 mg PO once daily; in adults with schizophrenia or bipolar mania, the cariprazine dose may be increased to 1.5 mg PO once daily, if needed, based on clinical response. Avoid initiating cariprazine in pediatric patients receiving fluconazole. When adding fluconazole to a stable dose of cariprazine, reduce the cariprazine dosage: from 1.5 mg or 3 mg once daily to 0.75 mg once daily, from 4.5 or 6 mg once daily to 1.5 mg once daily. Concomitant use increases cariprazine exposure and the risk for cariprazine-related adverse effects. Cariprazine is a CYP3A substrate and fluconazole is a moderate CYP3A inhibitor. Concomitant use with another moderate CYP3A inhibitor increased cariprazine overall exposure by 1.5-fold.

Nefazodone: (Major) A cariprazine dosage reduction may be required during concomitant use with nefazodone. Cariprazine dosage modification recommendations vary by indication, previous exposure to cariprazine, and age. When initiating cariprazine in adults receiving nefazodone, reduce the cariprazine starting dose to 0.5 mg PO once daily; in adults with schizophrenia or bipolar mania, the cariprazine dose may be increased to 0.75 mg PO once daily, if needed, based on clinical response. Avoid initiating cariprazine in pediatric patients receiving nefazodone. When adding nefazodone to a stable dose of cariprazine, reduce the cariprazine dosage: from 1.5 mg or 3 mg once daily to 0.5 mg once daily, from 4.5 or 6 mg once daily to 0.75 mg once daily. Concomitant use increases cariprazine exposure and the risk for cariprazine-related adverse effects. Cariprazine is a CYP3A substrate and nefazodone is a strong CYP3A inhibitor. Concomitant use with another strong CYP3A inhibitor increased cariprazine overall exposure by 2-fold.

    Headline
    tardive dyskinesia
    General Information
    Tardive dyskinesia (TD) is a syndrome of potentially irreversible, involuntary, dyskinetic movements that may develop during treatment with antipsychotic drugs. TD was reported in less than 1% of adult patients receiving 3 mg/day of cariprazine in short-term clinical trials for bipolar depression. Involuntary orofacial movements (tongue, mouth, jaw, eyelids, or face) or choreoathetoid movements in the extremities may occur. Factors associated with an increased risk of TD include an age above 55 years, female gender, white or African ethnicity, presence of a mood disorder, intellectual disability, CNS injury, prior or current akathisia, significant parkinsonism, or acute dystonic reaction. The rate of TD in adults treated with a first generation antipsychotic appears to be at least 3 times that observed with second generation antipsychotics like cariprazine. It is believed that the likelihood of developing TD increases with prolonged treatment and cumulative doses. Less commonly, it can also develop after short periods of time and with low dosages or may go undetected until the medication is stopped. The use of antipsychotics may mask signs and symptoms of TD; however, TD may also remit partially or completely if the antipsychotic is withdrawn. The effect that symptomatic suppression has upon the long-term course of TD is unknown. In people who require chronic treatment with cariprazine, the lowest possible dose and the shortest duration of treatment should be used. All patients receiving antipsychotics (including cariprazine) should undergo routine monitoring for movement disorders (e.g., AIMS or DISCUS assessment) at 3- to 6-month intervals. If signs or symptoms of TD develop, discontinuation of cariprazine therapy should be considered.

    diabetes mellitus, diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperglycemia
    General Information
    Atypical antipsychotics, including cariprazine, have caused metabolic changes, including hyperglycemia and diabetes mellitus. Aggravation of pre-existing diabetes mellitus may also occur. Atypical antipsychotics have been associated with extreme cases of hyperglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar coma, and death. Although it is possible that patients with schizophrenia may have an increased risk of diabetes mellitus, epidemiological studies suggest an increased risk of hyperglycemia-related adverse effects during atypical antipsychotic use. Atypical antipsychotics may have effects on glucose metabolism that are independent of their effect on weight. One study noted that patients taking atypical agents were 9% more likely to have a new diagnosis of diabetes mellitus than patients taking older therapies. During short-term clinical trials with cariprazine in adult patients with schizophrenia or bipolar mania, the mean change in fasting glucose from normal (less than 100 mg/dL) to high (at least 126 mg/dL) and borderline (100 to 125 mg/dL) were similar in patients treated with cariprazine and placebo. In long-term, open-label studies of patients with schizophrenia or bipolar mania, 4% of patients with normal hemoglobin A1C at baseline developed an A1C of at least 6.5%. Changes in fasting glucose were also noted in 6-week, placebo-controlled trials of adult patients with major depressive disorder, with shifts in fasting glucose from normal (at least 100 mg/dL) to high (at least 126 mg/dL) occurring in 2% (cariprazine 1.5 mg/day) and 3.2% (cariprazine 3 mg/day) of patients on concomitant antidepressants compared to 1.3% of patients on placebo. An 8-week, placebo-controlled trial of adults with major depressive disorder found changes in fasting glucose from baseline to the end of the trial to be similar among the cariprazine and placebo plus antidepressant therapy groups. However, serum insulin levels increased by 12 pmol/L in the cariprazine 1 mg/day to 2 mg/day and 20 pmol/L in the cariprazine 2 mg/day to 4.5 mg/day group compared to an increase of 8.5 pmol/L in the placebo arm. In pediatric patients receiving cariprazine for bipolar I disorder with manic or mixed episodes, 4.6% of individuals experienced a shift from normal baseline fasting serum glucose to high concentrations. Discontinuation of cariprazine should be considered if symptoms are severe. In some cases, continued antidiabetic treatment has been necessary despite discontinuation of the causative antipsychotic. Patients should be monitored for adverse reactions for several weeks after treatment initiation and after each dose increase since plasma levels of cariprazine and its active metabolites accumulate over time. The incidence of adverse reactions in short-term trials may not reflect the rates after longer term exposure.

Description
  Text
  Cariprazine is an oral atypical antipsychotic. Cariprazine is indicated for the treatment of schizophrenia, the acute treatment of manic or mixed episodes associated with bipolar I disorder, the treatment of depressive episodes associated with bipolar I disorder, and as an adjunctive therapy to antidepressants for the treatment of major depressive disorder. Adverse events may first appear several weeks after initiating treatment or increasing the dose of cariprazine, due to a prolonged time to reach steady-state resulting from the long half-lives of cariprazine (2 to 4 days), a metabolite DCAR (1 to 2 days), and another major active metabolite DDCAR (1 to 3 weeks). Because cariprazine is a primary substrate of CYP3A4, drug interactions may occur with potent inhibitors and inducers of CYP3A4. As with all atypical antipsychotics, the label includes a boxed warning regarding an increased mortality risk in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis and increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors in pediatric and young adult patients. Cariprazine was FDA-approved in 2015.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The exact mechanism of cariprazine in treating psychiatric disorders has not been determined. However, it is thought that the efficacy of the drug is the result of partial agonist activity at central dopamine D-2 and serotonin 5-HT1A receptors, and antagonist activity at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors. In vitro data indicate that the two major metabolites of cariprazine, desmethyl cariprazine and didesmethyl cariprazine, have receptor binding profiles similar to the parent drug and are pharmacologically equipotent to the parent compound. Cariprazine is a partial agonist at D-2 and D-3 with high binding affinity, an antagonist at 5-HT2B with high binding affinity, an antagonist at 5-HT2A with moderate binding affinity, and it also acts as moderate antagonist at H-1 receptors. There is a lower binding affinity at the serotonin 5-HT2C and alpha-1 adrenergic receptors. There is no appreciable affinity for muscarinic receptors.
 
Dopamine and serotonin mediate various effects in different portions of the brain. According to one hypothesis, a dopamine excess in the mesolimbic tract is thought to be responsible for the positive symptoms of schizophrenia. In the mesocortical tract, a reduction in dopamine activity may be responsible for the negative symptoms of schizophrenia. Reduced dopamine activity in the nigrostriatal tract may be related to decreased metabolic activity in the basal ganglia. Central serotonin hyperactivity may be associated with dopamine hypoactivity in the nigrostriatal and mesocortical tracts. Antipsychotics with a high affinity for serotonin receptors are thought to be more effective for treating the negative symptoms of schizophrenia than those with dopaminergic modulation as a primary mechanism.
 
The tuburoinfundibular tract controls neuroendocrine and hypothalamic function (e.g., prolactin release). Antipsychotic-mediated dopamine receptor blockade in the tuberoinfundibular tract increases prolactin release, which can lead to adverse effects such as amenorrhea, gynecomastia, galactorrhea, decreased libido, and impotence.
 
At a dose that was three times the human maximum recommended dose, cariprazine did not prolong the QTc interval to clinically relevant extent.

Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4
Cariprazine is extensively metabolized by CYP3A4 and, to a lesser extent, by CYP2D6 to DCAR and DDCAR, the two major active metabolites. DCAR is further metabolized into DDCAR by CYP3A4 and CYP2D6. DDCAR is then metabolized by CYP3A4 to a hydroxylated metabolite. Clinically relevant drug interactions are expected with potent CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers. Despite some involvement of CYP2D6 in metabolite formation, CYP2D6 inhibitors are not expected to interact with cariprazine. Cariprazine and its major active metabolites do not induce CYP1A2 and CYP3A4 and are weak inhibitors of CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 in vitro. Cariprazine is also a weak inhibitor of CYP2C19, CYP2A6, and CYP2E1 in vitro. Cariprazine and its major active metabolites are not substrates of P-gp, OATP1B1, OATP1B3, or BCRP. In vitro, cariprazine and its major active metabolites were poor or non-inhibitors of OATP1B1, OATP1B3, BCRP, OCT2, OAT1, and OAT3. The major active metabolites were also poor or non-inhibitors of P-gp; however, cariprazine may inhibit P-gp based on theoretical gastrointestinal concentrations at high doses observed in vitro.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Mild to moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh score 5 to 9): No dosage adjustment is needed.
Severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh score 10 to 15): Do not use; cariprazine has not been evaluated in this patient population.
  Renal Impairment
  CrCl 30 mL/minute or more: No dosage adjustment is needed.
CrCl less than 30 mL/minute: Do not use; cariprazine has not been evaluated in this patient population.
