Drug Class: Deferasirox is a first in class, tridentate, oral, iron-chelating agent used in the treatment of chronic iron overload secondary to multiple RBC transfusions (i.e., transfusional iron overload) and non-transfusion dependent thalassemia syndromes.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 28 mg/kg/day PO for Jadenu; 40 mg/kg/day PO for Exjade.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): cholelithiasis, gi bleeding, gi perforation, pancreatitis, peptic ulcer, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatic failure, hepatitis, hepatotoxicity, fanconi syndrome, glycosuria, hematuria, interstitial nephritis, nephrotoxicity, proteinuria, renal failure (unspecified), cataracts, hearing loss, optic neuritis, visual impairment, anxiety, dizziness, edema, fatigue, insomnia, agranulocytosis, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Deferasirox is contraindicated in patients with hypersensitivity to deferasirox or any of its components. Serious hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis and angioedema, have been reported during treatment with deferasirox; a majority of cases occurred within the first month of treatment.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Deferasirox is contraindicated in patients with hypersensitivity to deferasirox or any of its components. Serious hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis and angioedema, have been reported during treatment with deferasirox; a majority of cases occurred within the first month of treatment. Discontinue deferasirox, and institute appropriate medical interventions, for severe reactions.
 
The clinical benefits and associated risks of deferasirox therapy should be considered when deciding to remove accumulated iron.
 
Use caution when administering to patients with renal disease, renal failure, or renal impairment of any kind. In clinical trials, deferasirox treated patients experienced dose-dependent increases in serum creatinine (SCr), with most of the elevations remaining within normal limits and not requiring treatment discontinuation; however, there have been post-marketing reports of sometimes fatal acute renal failure. Most reported fatalities occurred in patients with multiple comorbidities and who were in an advanced stage of their hematological disorder. Prior to initiating treatment with deferasirox, obtain two SCr assessments to establish a reliable pre-treatment baseline and then obtain monthly SCr measurements. For patients with the following conditions, monitor SCr weekly for the first month of treatment and weekly for one month following any dose modifications, then monitor monthly: patients at increased risk of complications; patients with preexisting renal conditions; age > 65 years; those with comorbid disease states; or those receiving other drugs that decrease renal function. For any elevations in SCr, consider dose reduction (see Renal Impairment Dosage) or treatment interruption or discontinuation. Once the SCr has returned to within the normal range, therapy may be reinitiated at a lower dose followed by gradual dose escalation, if the clinical benefit is expected to outweigh potential risks.
 
Use caution when administering to patients with pre-existing proteinuria, as intermittent proteinuria (urine protein/creatinine ratio > 0.6 mg/mg) occurred in 18.6% of deferasirox treated patients compared to 7.2% of deferoxamine-treated patients in clinical studies. Although treatment discontinuation due to proteinuria was not noted in clinical trials, in patients followed up to one year, close monitoring is recommended. The clinical significance and mechanism of the proteinuria are uncertain.
 
Caution is warranted when administering deferasirox to patients with hepatic disease. In one study, four patients discontinued deferasirox because of hepatic abnormalities (drug-induced hepatitis and increased serum transaminases). In addition, hepatic failure, sometimes fatal, has been reported during post-marketing surveillance. Most of these occurred in patients > 55 years of age. Additionally, many reports have occurred in patients with significant comorbidities including liver cirrhosis and multi-organ failure. Monitor serum transaminases and bilirubin before the initiation of treatment, every 2 weeks during the first month, and monthly thereafter. If there are unexplained, persistent, or progressive increases in serum transaminase levels, interrupt or discontinue treatment. It should also be noted that hepatic disease can dramatically alter enterohepatic recirculation, and may lead to a decrease in efficacy.
 
Use caution when administering deferasirox to patients with hearing impairment or visual disturbance, as treatment is associated with the development of both. Patients taking deferasirox should undergo auditory and ophthalmologic testing (including slit lamp examinations and dilated funduscopy) before initiation of therapy and annually thereafter. If changes are noted, dose reduction or interruption should be considered.
 
In clinical trials, deferasirox testing did not include sufficient numbers of subjects aged 65 and over to determine whether they respond differently from younger subjects. In general, caution should be used in elderly patients due to the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function, and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy. Elderly patients may be at greater risk of developing acute renal failure and hepatic failure. Elderly patients > 65 years may have a more pronounced reaction to this medicine and need smaller doses.
 
The safety and efficacy of deferasirox has not been established in infants or children < 2 years. Of the patients receiving deferasirox in clinical trial, 292 were pediatric patients between the ages of 2 and 16 years of age. The safety and efficacy of deferasirox was similar to that of adult patients, and younger pediatric patients responded similarly to older pediatric patients. The recommended starting dose and dosing modifications are the same for children and adults.
 
Deferasirox is classified in FDA Pregnancy Risk Category B. Pregnant rats were used in reproduction studies at oral doses up to 100 mg/kg per day and in pregnant rabbits at oral doses up to 50 mg/kg per day (about 0.8 times the recommended human oral dose based on body surface area for both animal models). There was no evidence of impaired fertility or harm to the fetus due to deferasirox in these studies. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women. Animal reproduction studies are not always predictive of human response, and deferasirox should be used during pregnancy only if clearly needed.
 
Because it is not known whether deferasirox is excreted in human milk, use caution when administering to a breast-feeding woman. When deferasirox was administered to lactating rats at 10 mg/kg dose (about 0.08 times the recommended human oral dose based on body surface area), deferasirox and its metabolites were found in rat milk.
 
Deferasirox has been associated with dizziness and patients should be warned to use caution when driving or operating machinery.
 
Cytopenias, including agranulocytosis, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia, sometimes fatal, have been reported during clinical trials and post-marketing with use of deferasirox. The relationship of these cytopenias to treatment with deferasirox is uncertain. Most of the patients in whom the cytopenias were reported had preexisting hematologic disorders that are frequently associated with bone marrow failure. Regularly monitor blood counts in patients treated with deferasirox; consider interrupting treatment in any patient who develops an unexplained cytopenia.
 
Because upper gastrointestinal ulceration and hemorrhage have been reported in patients receiving deferasirox, use with caution in patients taking therapies known to have ulcerogenic or hemorrhagic potential including non-steroidal antiinflammatory drug therapy, corticosteroid therapy, anticoagulant therapy, and oral bisphosphonate therapy. Clinicians should be alert for signs and symptoms of gastrointestinal ulceration or hemorrhage; if a serious adverse event is suspected, clinicians should promptly evaluate the patient and initiate appropriate treatment.
 
Adverse event information collected from a company-sponsored global safety database suggests that there may be a greater risk of kidney failure, gastrointestinal hemorrhage (potentially fatal), and deaths in patients with hematological disease, specifically myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), who receive deferasirox compared to non-MDS patients. Many of these patients are over the age of 60 and these events are not uncommon in people with MDS. The number of deaths reported seems to be fewer in young people and patients with other chronic anemias. Further analysis is needed to determine if deferasirox poses an increased risk for these adverse events in patients with MDS or if these events are due to other factors.

    Headline
    renal disease, renal failure
    General Information
    Deferasirox can cause acute kidney injury, including renal failure, which may be fatal, particularly in individuals with comorbidities or in advanced stages of their hematologic disorder. The drug is contraindicated in individuals with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) less than 40 mL/minute/1.73 m2. For those with eGFR between 40 and 60 mL/minute/1.73 m2, reduce the initial dose and monitor renal function frequently; exercise particular caution in pediatric patients within this eGFR range. If treatment is required, use the minimum effective dose and individualize dose titration based on improvement in renal function. Pre-existing renal disease and concomitant use of other nephrotoxic drugs may increase the risk of acute kidney injury in all individuals. Acute illnesses associated with volume depletion and overchelation may further increase the risk of nephrotoxicity in pediatric patients. In pediatric patients, small decreases in eGFR can markedly increase deferasirox exposure, particularly in younger children (younger than 7 years) with smaller body surface areas. Interrupt deferasirox therapy during acute illnesses that may cause volume depletion (e.g., vomiting, diarrhea, or prolonged decreased oral intake). Evaluate renal function in all individuals before initiating therapy or increasing the dose. Obtain serum creatinine in duplicate to establish an accurate baseline and calculate eGFR using an appropriate prediction equation. Assess serum electrolytes and urinalysis to evaluate renal tubular function. Monitor renal function weekly during the first month of therapy and at least monthly thereafter. For individuals with baseline renal impairment or increased risk of acute renal failure, including those receiving concomitant nephrotoxic drugs and pediatric patients with volume depletion or overchelation, monitor renal function more frequently. Reduce the dose or interrupt therapy if renal function worsens, and discontinue deferasirox in individuals who develop severe renal failure.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class A, Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Deferasirox can cause hepatic injury, hepatic failure, and death. Hepatic toxicity appears to be more common in individuals older than 55 years of age and in those with significant comorbidities, including liver cirrhosis and multi-organ failure. Fatal hepatotoxicity has also occurred in pediatric patients, usually in association with acute renal failure in those at risk for overchelation during a volume-depleting event. Avoid deferasirox in individuals with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C). Reduce the starting dose in individuals with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B) and closely monitor those with mild (Child-Pugh class A) or moderate (Child-Pugh class B) hepatic impairment for efficacy and adverse reactions that may require dose titration. Monitor liver function tests (AST, ALT, and bilirubin) before initiating treatment, every 2 weeks during the first month, and monthly thereafter. Monitor liver function more frequently in pediatric patients with volume depletion and in those receiving deferasirox oral granules or tablets in the dosage range of 14 to 28 mg/kg/day or deferasirox tablets for oral suspension in the dosage range of 20 to 40 mg/kg/day when body iron burden is approaching normal. Interrupt therapy in pediatric patients when acute liver failure is suspected and during volume depletion. Use the lowest effective dose to achieve and maintain a low iron burden, and consider dose modification or treatment interruption for severe or persistent transaminase elevations.
    Headline
    hematological disease, thrombocytopenia
    General Information
    Deferasirox is contraindicated in individuals with thrombocytopenia and platelet counts less than 50,000/mm3. Agranulocytosis, neutropenia, worsening anemia, and thrombocytopenia, including fatal events, have been reported with deferasirox use. Preexisting hematological disease may increase the risk for these events. Monitor complete blood counts (CBC) monthly in all individuals receiving deferasirox. Interrupt treatment in any individual who develops an unexplained cytopenia until the cause is determined.
    Headline
    ECOG performance status grade 3 or higher, hematologic malignancy, high-risk myelodysplastic syndrome, myelodysplastic syndrome
    General Information
    Deferasirox is contraindicated in individuals with an advanced hematologic malignancy, high-risk myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), or poor performance status (e.g., ECOG performance status grade 3 or higher). Gastrointestinal hemorrhage, including fatal cases, was observed more frequently in individuals with advanced hematologic malignancies. Controlled clinical trials have not been conducted in individuals with high-risk MDS, and this population is not expected to benefit from chelation therapy. However, data from 1 clinical trial suggest that individuals with low- to intermediate-risk MDS and transfusional iron overload may benefit from deferasirox therapy.
    Headline
    GI bleeding
    General Information
    Treatment with deferasirox has been associated with serious and sometimes fatal gastrointestinal (GI) adverse reactions, including GI bleeding, ulceration, and perforation, particularly in individuals 65 years and older with advanced hematologic malignancy and/or low platelet counts. Nonfatal upper GI irritation, ulceration, and hemorrhage have also been reported, including in pediatric patients. Monitor for signs and symptoms of GI ulceration or hemorrhage during therapy, and promptly initiate further evaluation and treatment if a serious GI adverse reaction is suspected. The risk of GI hemorrhage may be increased when deferasirox is administered with drugs that have ulcerogenic or hemorrhagic potential, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids, oral bisphosphonates, or anticoagulants.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, diarrhea, hypovolemia, inadequate oral intake, vomiting
    General Information
    Deferasirox has been associated with serious and sometimes fatal adverse reactions, including acute renal and hepatic failure, in children and adolescents. These events have most often occurred during acute illnesses causing hypovolemia (e.g., diarrhea, vomiting, or prolonged decreased or inadequate oral intake) or with higher doses when body iron burden is near or within the normal range. Interrupt deferasirox therapy in those with volume depletion and resume only after renal function and fluid status have normalized. Monitor liver and renal function more frequently during episodes of volume depletion and in individuals receiving deferasirox oral granules or tablets in the dosage range of 14 to 28 mg/kg/day or deferasirox tablets for oral suspension in the dosage range of 20 to 40 mg/kg/day when body iron burden is approaching normal. Use the lowest effective dose necessary to achieve and maintain a low iron burden.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Dizziness may occur with deferasirox. Advise individuals to avoid driving or operating machinery or performing other activities requiring coordination and concentration until they know how deferasirox affects their ability to perform these activities.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Use cautious dose selection for deferasirox in geriatric adults, starting at the low end of the dosing range. Geriatric adults experienced a higher frequency of serious and fatal adverse reactions than younger individuals in clinical trials with deferasirox. Monitor geriatric adults for early signs or symptoms of adverse reactions that may require a dose adjustment.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Deferasirox may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy, based on data from animal studies. Advise pregnant people of the potential fetal risk. In animal studies, the administration of deferasirox resulted in decreased offspring viability and an increase in renal anomalies in male offspring at doses equal to or less than the recommended human doses, based on body surface area comparisons. Use deferasirox during pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential fetal risk.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking deferasirox, due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in the breastfed child. There are no data available on the presence of deferasirox or its metabolites in human milk, their effects on the breastfed child, or their effects on milk production. Deferasirox and its metabolites are present in rat milk.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  In clinical trials, 700 patients were treated with deferasirox in studies lasting 48 weeks. The most frequently occurring adverse events were gastrointestinal in nature and included diarrhea, nausea/vomiting, and abdominal pain; all were reported to be mild in severity. These symptoms were deemed drug related, and were possibly due to the dense oral suspension administered. In 5% included fever, headache (with no association to the dose level), cough, naso-pharyngitis, and influenza. Adverse events that led to discontinuation of treatment included glycosuria, Henoch Schonlein purpura, hyperactivity, and insomnia.
 
In a primary efficacy study including 296 deferasirox treated patients, four patients discontinued deferasirox because of elevated hepatic enzymes and other hepatic abnormalities including drug-induced hepatitis (in 2 patients) and increased serum transaminases (in 2 additional patients). In addition, during post-marketing surveillance, hepatic failure, sometimes fatal, has been reported. A consideration to drug interruption should be given in those patients that have persistent or severe elevations in hepatic enzymes.
 
There have been post-marketing reports of acute renal failure (unspecified), sometimes fatal, with deferasirox treatment. Most reported fatalities occurred in patients with multiple comorbidities and who were in an advanced stage of hematological disease. In addition, renal tubulopathy (Fanconi syndrome) has been reported in 0.6 mg/mg) occurred in 18.6% of deferasirox treated patients compared to 7.2% of deferoxamine treated patients. One patient was discontinued from deferasirox in clinical trials due to glycosuria/proteinuria. The clinical significance and mechanism of proteinuria are uncertain.
 
Auditory disturbances (high frequency hearing loss, decreased hearing) and visual impairment (lens opacities, cataracts, elevations in intraocular pressure, and retinal disorders) have been reported at a frequency of < 1% with deferasirox. One patient who developed early cataract discontinued treatment in clinical trials.
 
Uncommon adverse reactions (reported at rates of 0.1—1% in clinical trials) included gastritis, edema, sleep disorder, pigmentation disorder, dizziness, anxiety, maculopathy, cholelithiasis, pyrexia, fatigue, and pharyngolaryngeal pain.
 
Cytopenias, including agranulocytosis, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia, sometimes fatal, have been reported during clinical trials and post-marketing with use of deferasirox. The relationship of the cytopenias to treatment with deferasirox is uncertain; most of the patients in whom the cytopenias were reported had preexisting hematologic disorders that are frequently associated with bone marrow failure. Blood counts should be regularly monitored during treatment with deferasirox (see Contraindications/Precautions). Conversely, in one clinical trial certain hematological parameters were abnormally high, such as platelet counts, eosinophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils, with an order of magnitude of 1.2 to 1.5. These findings are suspected to be caused by the underlying disease and by frequent blood sampling during the study.
 
Treatment with deferasirox would be expected to achieve near prevention of iron accumulation and prevention of cardiotoxic effects in thalassemia, and long-term studies to assess these predictions are in progress. Researchers do, however, note that currently approved doses are ineffective in achieving negative iron balance in most patients, suggesting that only some of the iron mobilized by deferasirox from the liver is excreted in the feces, with the remainder possibly being redistributed to the heart and other organs. Iron loading of the heart by deferasirox is also suspected because of its lipophilicity, long half-life in plasma (12—16 h), and the transfer of iron from its iron complex to transferrin.
 
There have been post-marketing reports of leukocytoclastic vasculitis, possibly related to deferasirox use.
 
Serious hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis (anaphylactoid reactions) and angioedema, have been reported during treatment with deferasirox; a majority of cases occurred within the first month of treatment. Discontinue deferasirox treatment, and institute appropriate medical interventions, for severe reactions.

    General Information
    Fatal GI bleeding has been reported in patients receiving deferasirox. These events were more common in elderly patients with advanced hematological malignancies and/or low platelet counts. In clinical trials, 700 adult and pediatric patients were treated with deferasirox in studies lasting 48 weeks. The most frequently occurring adverse events were gastrointestinal in nature and included diarrhea (11.8% to 19.7%), nausea (10.5% to 22.7%), vomiting (10.1% to 21.2%), and abdominal pain (21.3% to 28%); all were reported to be mild in severity. These symptoms were deemed dose-related, and were possibly due to the dense oral suspension administered. In a cohort-based analysis of deferasirox pediatric pooled clinical trials, the frequency of gastrointestinal reactions was increased among pediatric patients who received Exjade doses more than 25 mg/kg/day when serum ferritin was less than 1,000 mcg/L. Although controlled clinical trials in patients with myelodysplastic syndrome and chronic iron overload due to blood transfusions have not been conducted, 627 patients with myelodysplastic syndrome across 5 uncontrolled trials were compared to patients with sickle cell disease (n = 132; Study 1) and beta-thalassemia (n = 296; Study 3); diarrhea (47% vs. 20% vs. 12%) and nausea (26% vs. 23% vs. 11%) were found to occur more frequently in patients with myelodysplastic syndrome than sickle cell or beta-thalassemia in this analysis. In patients with non-transfusion-dependent thalassemia syndromes, nausea was reported in 2% to 6% of patients, and diarrhea was reported in 5% to 6% of patients. Other gastrointestinal events reported by drug recipients (0.1% to 1%) included non-fatal GI bleeding, upper GI irritation, peptic ulcer (gastric, duodenal, multiple ulcerations), pancreatitis associated with cholelithiasis, and gastritis. Pancreatitis, with or without underlying biliary conditions, has also been reported in postmarketing use. Description
  Text
  Deferasirox is a first in class, tridentate, oral, iron-chelating agent used in the treatment of chronic iron overload secondary to multiple RBC transfusions (i.e., transfusional iron overload) and non-transfusion dependent thalassemia syndromes. Iron overload is a serious complication caused by frequent blood transfusions in patients with blood transfusion-dependent anemias, such as β-thalassemia, sickle cell disease, other rare anemias, and myeloproliferative disorders. Deferasirox has been developed as an alternative to deferoxamine, the standard treatment of chronic iron overload, which is administered parenterally. In patients with chronic iron toxicity, chelation treatments are effective for rapid iron removal, maintenance of low iron stores, prevention of heart and other organ damage caused by iron overload, and ultimately improved survival. A large number of patients that require iron chelation therapy with deferoxamine often do not receive adequate therapy due to noncompliance. Because deferasirox is administered orally once daily, it will likely be a more acceptable regimen for chronic use, thus enhancing the management of chronic iron toxicity. Additionally, the quality of life of patients who require lifelong iron-chelating therapy may be considerably improved. Despite the benefits, health care providers are urged to consider the risks versus benefits of prescribing deferasirox. To highlight these risks, the product labeling contains a Black Box Warning regarding potential for renal failure, hepatic failure, and gastrointestinal hemorrhages. In some cases these adverse events have resulted in fatalities, particularly in elderly and pediatric patients.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Deferasirox is an orally active iron chelator that binds selectively to Fe3+. It is a tridentate ligand that requires two molecules of itself to form a stable complex with each iron atom. The efficiency of chelation of this compound is defined as the ratio of the amount of iron actually excreted to the amount of iron that could have been theoretically bound. In one clinical study, this capacity was calculated to be 20.5% in patients receiving a 20 mg/kg oral dose; one oral dose of deferasirox appears to be four to five times more effective than parenterally administered deferoxamine in promoting the excretion of chelatable iron from hepatocellular iron stores. This suggests that deferasirox chelates excess iron that enters the reticuloendothelial system as insoluble ferritin rather than iron required for enzyme activity. This may explain the lack of enzyme-related adverse effects seen with deferasirox treatment. At recommended doses, deferasirox is able to prevent net iron accumulation in most patients transfused with 12—15 ml packed red blood cells/kg/day.
 
Deferasirox is highly selective for iron, while it does not appear to promote dietary absorption of iron. Although deferasirox has very low affinity for zinc and copper, there are variable decreases in the serum concentration of these trace metals after the administration of deferasirox. These transient decreases may, however, be clinically irrelevant. The iron complex of deferasirox appears to be inert and is excreted to a large extent in the feces, rather than being redistributed.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Deferasirox is well absorbed and circulates in the plasma for several hours after once daily dosing. Maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) is reached in about 1.5 to 4 hours. After administration of a single dose, the Cmax and AUC of deferasirox increase in an approximate linear fashion under steady-state conditions. Deferasirox tablets for oral suspension have an absolute bioavailability of 70%, while the bioavailability of the tablet and sprinkle granules is 36% and 52% more, respectively. The bioavailability of deferasirox oral suspension is variably increased when taken with a meal; therefore, this formulation must be taken on an empty stomach 30 minutes before eating. Exposure (AUC) and Cmax of the oral tablets when administered with a low-fat meal are similar to fasting conditions; however, when administered with a high-fat meal the AUC and Cmax are increased by 18% and 29%, respectively. The AUC and Cmax of the granule formulation after a low-fat meal or soft foods were also similar to that under fasting conditions. When administered with a high-fat meal, the AUC of the granules increased by 18% with no changes in Cmax compared to fasting condition. The oral tablets and granules may be administered either on an empty stomach or with a light meal (less than 250 calories and less than 7% fat). After multiple dosing, exposure to deferasirox increases by an accumulation factor of 1.3 to 2.3.

  Text
  Deferasirox is administered orally. It is highly (about 99%) protein bound, binding almost exclusively to serum albumin. Only 5% is confined to the blood cells in humans. The volume of distribution at steady state (Vss) is 14.37 +/- 2.69 L in adults. The main metabolic pathway is glucuronidation, with subsequent biliary excretion. Deconjugation of glucuronidates in the intestine and subsequent reabsorption (enterohepatic recycling) is likely to occur. Glucuronidation is carried out by UGT1A1 and to a lesser extent by UGT1A3. CYP450-catalyzed (oxidative) metabolism appears to be minor in humans (about 8%). No evidence for induction or inhibition of enzymes at therapeutic doses has been observed. Deferasirox and metabolites are primarily (84% of the dose) excreted in the feces. Renal excretion of deferasirox and metabolites is minimal (8% of the dose). The mean elimination half-life ranged from 8 to 16 hours following oral administration. In clinical evaluation, the total body iron elimination rate (range 7.7 to 28.5 mg iron/day) in patients treated with deferasirox was similar to values in patients receiving deferoxamine.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4, CYP2C8, CYP1A2, CYP2A6, CYP2D6, CYP2C19, UGT1A1, UGT1A3
UGT1A1 is the primary enzyme involved in glucuronidation of deferasirox, and UGT1A3 is involved to a lesser extent. If a concomitantly administered drug undergoes selective metabolism by UGT1A1, competition for drug metabolism will exist, possibly resulting in the reduced clearance of deferasirox. During in vivo studies, deferasirox inhibited the actions of CYP1A2 and CYP2C8, and was an inducer of CYP3A4. In vitro studies showed inhibition of CYP2A6, CYP2D6, and CYP2C19.
