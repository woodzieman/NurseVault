Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Avoid milnacipran use in any patient with a known or suspected milnacipran hypersensitivity reaction or a hypersensitivity reaction to any other ingredient in the formulation.
 
Milnacipran is contraindicated for use in patients with uncontrolled closed-angle glaucoma; milnacipran was associated with an increased risk of mydriasis during clinical trials. The drug should be used with caution in patients who have increased intraocular pressure or controlled closed-angle glaucoma.
 
Due to the effects of milnacipran on serotonin and norepinephrine, the drug is contraindicated in patients receiving MAOI therapy. Milnacipran should not be administered within 14 days of taking an MAOI. An MAOI should not be started for at least 5 days after discontinuing milnacipran (see Drug Interactions).
 
Patients should be instructed to use caution when driving or operating machinery until the full effects of milnacipran are known. Although milnacipran appears to have limited effects on cognitive function and psychomotor performance, the manufacturer states that milnacipran may affect mental and physical capabilities in some patients.
 
In general, abrupt discontinuation should be avoided due to the potential for withdrawal symptoms (see Adverse Reactions). If intolerable symptoms occur after a dosage reduction or upon treatment discontinuation, then resuming the previously prescribed dose may be considered. Subsequently, dosage reductions may continue but at a more gradual rate.
 
The use of milnacipran is not recommended in patients with renal failure (CrCl < 5 ml/min). Caution is advised when prescribing milnacipran to patients with renal disease associated with moderate to severe renal impairment. Dosage reductions are recommended for patients with severe renal impairment (see Dosage).
 
Caution is advised when prescribing milnacipran to patients with severe hepatic disease or impairment; however, dosage reductions are not necessary. In general, milnacipran should not be administered to patients with significant alcohol use (e.g., alcoholism) or chronic liver disease. During clinical trials, elevated liver enzymes occurred more frequently in milnacipran-treated patients than those receiving placebo. Milnacipran should be discontinued if jaundice or other signs of liver dysfunction become evident.
 
Patients with cardiac disease or hypertension should be monitored closely if milnacipran therapy is initiated. Milnacipran has caused elevations in blood pressure and hypertension in patients who were previously non-hypertensive (see Adverse Reactions). Sustained increases in systolic and diastolic blood pressure and hypertensive emergencies have also occurred. Evaluate blood pressure prior to initiating treatment and periodically thereafter during maintenance therapy. Dose reduction or discontinuation of milnacipran may be necessary in patients who experience sustained increases in blood pressure.
 
Tachyarrhythmias should be treated prior to treatment initiation because the use of milnacipran in patients with pre-existing cardiac arrhythmias has not been systematically evaluated. Milnacipran has caused small increases in heart rate (e.g., 7—8 beats/min) during clinical studies; therefore, it is advisable to evaluate heart rate prior to initiating treatment and periodically thereafter during maintenance therapy. Dose reduction or discontinuation of milnacipran may be necessary in patients who experience sustained increases in heart rate.
 
Caution is advised when prescribing milnacipran to patients with seizures or a seizure disorder. Although seizures were not reported during clinical trials for fibromyalgia, seizures have occurred infrequently during use of the drug for other indications.
 
Serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) may cause hyponatremia, which is frequently the result of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). In some cases, serum sodium levels less than 110 mmol/L have been reported; however, the adverse effect appeared reversible upon discontinuation of the causative SNRI. Elderly patients, those receiving diuretics or prone to dehydration, and those who are otherwise volume depleted (e.g., hypovolemia) appear to be at greatest risk. Hyponatremia may manifest as headache, difficulty concentrating, memory impairment, confusion, weakness, and unsteadiness which may result in falls. Severe manifestations include hallucinations, syncope, seizure, coma, respiratory arrest, and death. Symptomatic hyponatremia may require discontinuation of the SNRI, as well as implementation of the appropriate medical interventions.
 
Milnacipran is in a class of drugs known to affect urethral resistance. Use milnacipran with caution in patients with pre-existing urinary retention, prostatic hypertrophy, prostatitis, urinary tract obstruction, or lower urinary tract obstructive disorders. Dysuria was an infrequently reported adverse effect in milnacipran clinical trials. Some cases with other serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors have required hospitalization and/or catheterization.
 
Milnacipran is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. Milnacipran should be used in pregnancy only where the benefit to the mother clearly outweighs any potential risk to the fetus. If the clinician and patient decide to continue milnacipran during pregnancy, discontinuation symptoms should be considered in the newborn at birth. Some neonates exposed to serotonergic antidepressants late in the third trimester have developed complications requiring prolonged hospitalization, respiratory support, and tube feeding. Such complications can arise immediately upon delivery. Features are consistent with either a direct toxic effect of serotonergic agents or, possibly, a neonatal abstinence syndrome. It should be noted that, in some cases, the clinical picture is consistent with serotonin syndrome. When treating a pregnant woman with an SNRI or other serotonergic agent during the third trimester, the physician should carefully consider the potential risks and benefits of treatment. If clinically feasible, gradual tapering of the medication prior to delivery may be considered. The effects of milnacipran during labor and delivery are unknown. Results from animal studies showed an increased incidence in fetal mortality and skeletal variation (i.e., extra single rib) when milnacipran was administered to pregnant animals. In addition, postpartum weight loss and decreased viability occurred when milnacipran was administered to rats during late gestation.
 
It is not known if milnacipran is excreted in human breast milk; however, animal data indicate that milnacipran is excreted into maternal milk. According to the manufacturer, because of the potential adverse effects on a nursing infant, either milnacipran or breast-feeding should be discontinued. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
Milnacipran is not approved for use in children below 17 years of age. In October 2004, the FDA directed manufacturers of all antidepressants to include a boxed warning detailing the risk of suicide in pediatric patients. A causal role has been established for antidepressants in inducing suicidality in pediatric patients. The risk of suicidality for these drugs was identified in a pooled analysis of 24 placebo-controlled trials (n=4400) lasting up to 16 weeks in pediatric patients with major depressive disorder (MDD), obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), or other psychiatric disorders. The analysis showed a greater risk of suicidality during the first few months of treatment in those receiving antidepressants (SSRIs and others). The average risk of such events was 4% on drug therapy and 2% for placebo; however, no suicides occurred in these trials. Pooled analysis of short-term clinical trials during early phase treatment of SSRIs and other antidepressants in young adults (18 to 24 years) also showed an increased risk of suicidal thinking and behavior. The clinical need for an antidepressant in children or young adults for any use must be weighed against the risk of increased suicidality; patients who are started on therapy should be observed closely for clinical worsening, suicidality, or unusual changes in behavior, particularly within the first few months of starting therapy or at the time of dose increase or decrease; such observation would generally include at least weekly face-to-face contact with patients during the first 4 weeks of treatment, then every other week visits for the next 4 weeks, then at 12 weeks, and as clinically indicated beyond 12 weeks; additional contact by telephone may be appropriate between visits. It is unknown if the suicidality risk in children and young adults extends to longer-term therapy (i.e., beyond several months). The possibility of a suicide attempt is inherent in patients with depressive symptoms, whether these occur in primary depression or in association with another primary disorder such as OCD. All patients with a history of suicidal ideation or behaviors and those with a prominence of suicidal ideation prior to treatment are considered at an increased risk for suicidal ideation or attempts, and should be closely monitored during treatment with milnacipran. In patients who exhibit changes in symptoms, worsening of depression or emergent suicidality, a decision should be made to change or discontinue treatment. If discontinuing, the medication should be tapered as rapidly as possible, but with recognition that abrupt discontinuation can also cause adverse symptoms. All antidepressants should be prescribed in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management in order to reduce the risk of overdose.
 
All effective antidepressants can transform depression into mania in predisposed individuals. It is generally believed that treating such an episode with an antidepressant alone may increase the likelihood of precipitation of a mixed/manic episode in patients at risk for bipolar disorder. If a patient develops manic symptoms, milnacipran should be withheld, and appropriate therapy initiated to treat the manic symptoms. Additionally, depression may be the presenting symptom of a mixed/manic episode of bipolar disorder. Patients should be adequately screened for bipolar disorder prior to initiating an antidepressant. Such screening should include a detailed psychiatric history, including a family history of suicide, bipolar disorder, and depression. Patients with depression or comorbid depression in the setting of other psychiatric illness being treated with antidepressants should be observed for clinical worsening and suicidality, especially during the initial few months of a course of drug therapy, or at times of dose changes. Caregivers should be advised to closely observe the patient on a daily basis and to communicate immediately with the prescriber the emergence of agitation, irritability, unusual changes in behavior, or suicidality. Analysis of short-term studies in geriatric patients aged 65 years and older showed a decline in the risk of suicidal thinking and behavior during early phase treatment with SSRIs and other antidepressants, contrary to the data obtained in children and young adults.
 
Monitor patients taking a serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) for signs and symptoms of bleeding. Platelet aggregation may be impaired by SNRIs due to platelet serotonin depletion, possibly increasing the risk of a bleeding complication (e.g., gastrointestinal bleeding, ecchymoses, epistaxis, hematomas, petechiae, hemorrhage). Concurrent use of anticoagulant therapy, thrombolytic therapy, or other medications that enhance bleeding potential may increase this risk (see Drug Interactions). Patients taking milnacipran should be instructed to promptly report any bleeding events to the practitioner.

    adolescents, children, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Safety and effectiveness of milnacipran in a fibromyalgia pediatric population below 18 years of age have not been established. Milnacipran is a selective serotonin and norepinephrine re-uptake inhibitor (SNRI) similar to some antidepressants. In a pooled analysis of placebo-controlled trials of antidepressants (n = 4,500 pediatrics and 77,000 adults), there was an increased risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors in children, adolescents, and young adults 24 years of age and younger receiving an antidepressant versus placebo, with considerable variation in the risk of suicidality among drugs. The difference in absolute risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors across different indications was highest in those with major depression. No suicides occurred in any of the pediatric trials. Nevertheless, the need for an antidepressant in children, adolescents, or young adults for any use must be weighed against the risk of suicidality; it is unknown if this risk extends to long-term use. All patients should be monitored for symptom worsening or suicidality, especially at treatment initiation or after dose changes. Caregivers and/or patients should immediately notify the prescriber of changes in behavior or suicidal ideation. A change to the treatment regimen or discontinuation of milnacipran may be necessary in people with emerging suicidality or worsening depression.
    Headline
    bipolar disorder
    General Information
    Milnacipran is a selective serotonin and norepinephrine re-uptake inhibitor (SNRI) similar to some antidepressants. Activation of mania, hypomania, or a mixed episode may occur with medications used to treat depression, especially in people predisposed to bipolar disorder. A major depressive episode may be the initial presentation of bipolar disorder. Prior to initiating treatment with milnacipran, people with depressive symptoms should be screened for risk factors for bipolar disorder, including a detailed personal psychiatric history and family history of bipolar disorder, suicide, and/or depression. If an individual taking milnacipran develops symptoms consistent with mania or hypomania, the medication should be discontinued, and appropriate therapy should be initiated.
    Headline
    cardiovascular disease
    General Information
    Individuals receiving milnacipran should have regular monitoring of heart rate and blood pressure, as the drug may increase blood pressure and heart rate. Caution should be exercised when using milnacipran in people with pre-existing cardiovascular disease such as hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, or other conditions that might be compromised by increases in blood pressure or heart rate. Pre-existing hypertension, tachyarrhythmias, or other cardiac conditions should be controlled prior to initiating treatment. Cases of elevated blood pressure requiring immediate treatment have been reported with milnacipran. Sustained blood pressure increases could have adverse consequences. For individuals who experience a sustained increase in blood pressure or heart rate while receiving milnacipran, either dosage reduction or discontinuation should be considered.

    Headline
    hypovolemia
    General Information
    Serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), including milnacipran, are associated with significant hyponatremia resulting from the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Older adults, people taking diuretics, and those who have hypovolemia appear to be at greatest risk. Hyponatremia during SNRI use has resulted in serum sodium levels less than 110 mmol/L in some cases. However, the adverse effect appears reversible upon discontinuation of the causative SNRI. Symptomatic hyponatremia may require discontinuation of milnacipran, as well as implementation of appropriate medical interventions.
    Headline
    closed-angle glaucoma, narrow iridocorneal angles
    General Information
    The pupillary dilation that can occur with many antidepressants, including milnacipran, may precipitate a closed-angle glaucoma attack in people with anatomically narrow iridocorneal angles who do not have a patent iridectomy. Avoid the use of antidepressants, including milnacipran, in individuals with anatomically narrow iridocorneal angles.
    Headline
    prostatic hypertrophy, urinary tract obstruction
    General Information
    Use caution when administering milnacipran to people with a history of dysuria, notably in male patients with prostatic hypertrophy, prostatitis, and other lower urinary tract obstruction disorders. The noradrenergic effect of serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) can affect urethral resistance. Males are more prone to genitourinary adverse effects, such as dysuria or urinary retention, and may experience testicular pain or ejaculation disorders. Symptoms of urinary hesitancy, urinary retention, or dysuria which develop during treatment with milnacipran may be a drug-related event, and discontinuation or other appropriate medical intervention should be considered.

    General Information
    Because it is possible that milnacipran may aggravate pre-existing hepatic disease, milnacipran should not be prescribed to patients with substantial alcohol use (ethanol ingestion) or evidence of chronic liver disease. There have been cases of increased liver enzymes and reports of severe liver injury, including fulminant hepatitis with milnacipran from postmarketing experience.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Renal excretion is a major route of elimination of milnacipran. Caution is advised when prescribing milnacipran to people with moderate (CrCl: 30 to 49 mL/minute) renal impairment. Dosage adjustments are recommended in people with severe (CrCl: 5 to 29 mL/minute) renal impairment.  Milnacipran is not recommended in people with renal failure (CrCl less than 5 mL/minute) or end-stage renal disease (ESRD).

    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero during the third trimester may experience poor neonatal adaptation, resulting in complications such as prolonged hospitalization, respiratory support, and tube feeding upon delivery. Symptoms of poor neonatal adaptation include respiratory distress, cyanosis, apnea, seizures, temperature instability, feeding difficulty, vomiting, hypoglycemia, hypotonia, hypertonia, hyperreflexia, tremor, jitteriness, irritability, and constant crying. These features are consistent with direct SNRI toxicity, serotonin syndrome, and/or drug discontinuation syndrome.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use milnacipran with caution during breast-feeding. Milnacipran is present in human milk. Monitor infants exposed to milnacipran through breast milk for agitation, irritability, poor feeding, and poor weight gain. In a pharmacokinetic study, milnacipran 50 mg PO was administered as a single dose to 8 lactating individuals who were at least 12 weeks postpartum and weaning their infants. Based on peak plasma concentrations, the maximum daily infant dose of milnacipran from breast milk (assuming mean milk consumption of 150 mL/kg/day) was estimated to be 5% of the maternal dose. In most patients, peak concentrations of milnacipran in breast milk were noted within 4 hours after the maternal dose. The milnacipran milk/plasma AUC ratio was 1.85 +/- 0.38. Galactorrhea has been reported during milnacipran therapy; interference with lactation may be possible. Although duloxetine may be considered as an alternative to milnacipran in the treatment of fibromyalgia, safety of duloxetine during breast-feeding has not been established.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    An age-related decline in renal function may reduce milnacipran clearance, and renal function should be assessed before determining dosage in the older adult. Geriatric adults have an increased risk of developing clinically significant hyponatremia, a known side effect of serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs). The Beers Criteria state that SNRIs are potentially inappropriate medications in older adults with a history of falls or fractures; SNRIs can produce ataxia, impaired psychomotor function, syncope, and additional falls. If an SNRI must be used, consider reducing the use of other CNS-active medications and implement strategies to reduce fall risk. SNRIs may also cause hyponatremia and SIADH; closely monitor sodium concentrations when initiating treatment or changing doses in older adults. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates antidepressant use in long-term care facilities. When used to manage behavior, stabilize mood, or treat a psychiatric disorder, tapering as outlined in the OBRA guidelines should be attempted unless clinically contraindicated. Dosages and durations of treatment used should align with prescribing labels, published literature recommendations, and expert guidelines.

    obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    Data are insufficient to evaluate a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse maternal or fetal outcomes with milnacipran use during pregnancy. Animal studies have shown an increased incidence in fetal mortality and skeletal variation (i.e., extra single rib), decreased body weight, and decreased viability with milnacipran administration during late gestation. Data from observational studies report that exposure to SNRIs, particularly in the month before obstetric delivery, is associated with a less than 2-fold increase in the risk of postpartum hemorrhage.

Benzoic Acid; Hyoscyamine; Methenamine; Methylene Blue; Phenyl Salicylate: (Contraindicated) Concurrent use of methylene blue and serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) (e.g., venlafaxine, duloxetine, desvenlafaxine, milnacipran) should generally be avoided due to the potential for serotonin syndrome. Results from an in vitro study indicate that methylene blue is a potent, reversible inhibitor of the monoamine oxidase type A enzyme (MAO-A). MAO-A is responsible for the metabolism of serotonin; therefore, concurrent use of an MAO-A inhibitor with serotonergic agents such as SNRIs may result in a clinically significant interaction. Cases of serotonin syndrome have been reported, primarily following administration of standard infusions of methylene blue (1 to 8 mg/kg) as a visualizing agent in parathyroid surgery, in patients receiving selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, SNRIs, or clomipramine. It is not known if patients receiving other serotonergic psychiatric agents with intravenous methylene blue are at a comparable risk or if methylene blue administered by other routes (e.g., orally, local injection) or in doses less than 1 mg/kg IV can produce a similar outcome. One case report suggests that serotonin toxicity may have occurred post-operatively following administration of standard infusions of methylene blue in a patient receiving duloxetine. The patient experienced disorientation, a mildly elevated temperature, tachycardia, elevated blood pressure, mild agitation, and nystagmus. In a separate case, a patient who had been receiving venlafaxine developed expressive aphasia, confusion, and disinhibition following a methylene blue infusion. The authors concluded that methylene blue toxicity had occurred; however, they did not exclude the possibility of a drug interaction based upon previous reports of an interaction between injectable methylene blue and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). Published interaction reports between intravenously administered methylene blue and serotonergic psychiatric agents have documented symptoms including lethargy, confusion, delirium, agitation, aggression, obtundation, myoclonus, expressive aphasia, hypertonia, pyrexia, elevated blood pressure, seizures, and/or coma. Signs and symptoms of serotonin syndrome include fever, diaphoresis, shivering, myoclonus, tremor, tachycardia, diarrhea, nausea, headache, incoordination, mental status changes (e.g., agitation, confusion), hyperreflexia, seizures, and coma. If emergent treatment with methylene blue is required in a patient receiving an SNRI, the SNRI must be stopped immediately and the patient should be monitored for symptoms of CNS toxicity for two weeks or until 24 hours after the last dose of methylene blue, whichever comes first. During non-emergent use of methylene blue, the SNRI should be stopped at least 2 weeks prior to methylene blue treatment, but also taking into consideration the half-life of the SNRI being discontinued. Hyoscyamine; Methenamine; Methylene Blue; Phenyl Salicylate; Sodium Biphosphate: (Contraindicated) Concurrent use of methylene blue and serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) (e.g., venlafaxine, duloxetine, desvenlafaxine, milnacipran) should generally be avoided due to the potential for serotonin syndrome. Results from an in vitro study indicate that methylene blue is a potent, reversible inhibitor of the monoamine oxidase type A enzyme (MAO-A). MAO-A is responsible for the metabolism of serotonin; therefore, concurrent use of an MAO-A inhibitor with serotonergic agents such as SNRIs may result in a clinically significant interaction. Cases of serotonin syndrome have been reported, primarily following administration of standard infusions of methylene blue (1 to 8 mg/kg) as a visualizing agent in parathyroid surgery, in patients receiving selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, SNRIs, or clomipramine. It is not known if patients receiving other serotonergic psychiatric agents with intravenous methylene blue are at a comparable risk or if methylene blue administered by other routes (e.g., orally, local injection) or in doses less than 1 mg/kg IV can produce a similar outcome. One case report suggests that serotonin toxicity may have occurred post-operatively following administration of standard infusions of methylene blue in a patient receiving duloxetine. The patient experienced disorientation, a mildly elevated temperature, tachycardia, elevated blood pressure, mild agitation, and nystagmus. In a separate case, a patient who had been receiving venlafaxine developed expressive aphasia, confusion, and disinhibition following a methylene blue infusion. The authors concluded that methylene blue toxicity had occurred; however, they did not exclude the possibility of a drug interaction based upon previous reports of an interaction between injectable methylene blue and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). Published interaction reports between intravenously administered methylene blue and serotonergic psychiatric agents have documented symptoms including lethargy, confusion, delirium, agitation, aggression, obtundation, myoclonus, expressive aphasia, hypertonia, pyrexia, elevated blood pressure, seizures, and/or coma. Signs and symptoms of serotonin syndrome include fever, diaphoresis, shivering, myoclonus, tremor, tachycardia, diarrhea, nausea, headache, incoordination, mental status changes (e.g., agitation, confusion), hyperreflexia, seizures, and coma. Serotonin syndrome, in its most severe form, can resemble neuroleptic malignant syndrome. If serotonin syndrome is suspected, vortioxetine and concurrent serotonergic agents should be discontinued.
Warfarin: (Moderate) Advise patients of the increased bleeding risk associated with the concomitant use of milnacipran and warfarin. Carefully monitor patients receiving warfarin therapy if milnacipran is initiated or discontinued. Case reports and epidemiological studies have demonstrated an association between use of drugs that interfere with serotonin reuptake and gastrointestinal bleeding. At steady-state, milnacipran did not affect the INR or pharmacokinetics of a single dose of warfarin 25 mg.
ZOLMitriptan: (Moderate) Because of the potential risk and severity of serotonin syndrome, caution should be observed when administering milnacipran with other drugs that have serotonergic properties such as serotonin-receptor agonists (e.g., almotriptan, eletriptan, frovatriptan, naratriptan, rizatriptan, sumatriptan, zolmitriptan). Serotonin syndrome has been reported during concurrent use of serotonin-receptor agonists and serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors. Some patients had used the combination previously without incident when serotonin syndrome occurred. Inform patients taking this combination of the possible increased risk and monitor for the emergence of serotonin syndrome particularly during treatment initiation and dose increases. Discontinue serotonergic agents and initiate symptomatic treatment if serotonin syndrome occurs.
Zolpidem: (Moderate) Disorientation, delusions, or hallucinations have been reported rarely during co-administration of zolpidem and some SNRI-type antidepressants. The duration of the visual hallucinations has ranged from 30 minutes to 7 hours. The interaction is thought to be pharmacodynamic in nature; therefore, a similar reaction is possible with levomilnacipran or milnacipran. Data from a clinical study in which SSRI-treated patients were given immediate-release zolpidem revealed that four of the seven discontinuations during double-blind treatment with zolpidem (n = 95) were associated with impaired concentration, continuing or aggravated depression, and manic reaction.

  General Information
  As with other drugs that affect serotonin, gastrointestinal side effects can be common, especially at treatment onset. Nausea, the most common adverse effect, appears to be dose-related and is more common during initiation of treatment. During clinical trials for fibromyalgia, nausea/vomiting occurred in 37%/7% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 20%/2% of those on placebo. Nausea/vomiting led to discontinuation in 6%/1% of patients treated with milnacipran versus 1%/0% of those treated with placebo. During clinical trials for fibromyalgia, abdominal pain occurred in 3% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 2% of those on placebo, and constipation occurred in 16% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 4% of those on placebo. Constipation led to discontinuation in 1% of patients treated with milnacipran versus 0% of those treated with placebo. Other gastrointestinal effects reported in at least 1% of patients during clinical trials for fibromyalgia included diarrhea, dyspepsia, gastroesophageal reflux, flatulence, dysgeusia, and abdominal distension.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia, insomnia occurred in 12% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 10% of those on placebo.
 
Although seizures were not reported during clinical trials for fibromyalgia, seizures and loss of consciousness have been reported during post-marketing use of the drug worldwide. Caution is advised when prescribing milnacipran to patients with seizures or a seizure disorder.
 
Similar to other serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), milnacipran may increase blood pressure, resulting in hypertension or hypertensive crisis in some cases. Sustained increases in systolic and diastolic blood pressure have also occurred. During fibromyalgia controlled trials using up to 600 mg/day of milnacipran, mean increases in supine blood pressure were up to 8.1 mm Hg in the placebo group and up to 10 mm Hg in the active treatment group. In the same study, the mean increase in diastolic blood pressure was up to 4.6 mm Hg in the placebo group and up to 11.5 mm Hg in the active treatment group. During studies enrolling fibromyalgia patients without hypertension at baseline, approximately twice as many patients in the milnacipran group developed hypertension at the end of the study (19.5%) compared with the placebo patients (7.2%). Among fibromyalgia patients with hypertension at baseline, 1% of placebo patients had a minimum of 15 mm Hg increase in systolic pressure at study end compared to 7% of those receiving 100 mg/day of milnacipran and 2% of those receiving 200 mg/day of milnacipran. Similarly, hypertensive patients at baseline were more likely to experience diastolic increases of greater than 10 mm Hg when receiving milnacipran 100 mg/day (8%) or 200 mg (6%) than those on placebo (3%). In clinical trials for fibromyalgia overall, hypertension occurred in 5% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 2% of those on placebo.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia, palpitations occurred in 7% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 2% of those on placebo. Palpitations led to discontinuation in 3% of patients treated with milnacipran versus 1% of those treated with placebo. In addition, during clinical trials for fibromyalgia, increased heart rate occurred in 6% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 1% of those on placebo. Small increases in heart rate (e.g., 7—8 beats/min) occurred with milnacipran compared to placebo. Increases in pulse of at least 20 bpm were observed more frequently in those receiving milnacipran 100—200 mg/day (8%) versus placebo (0.3%). Increased heart rate led to discontinuation in 1% of patients treated with milnacipran versus 0% of those treated with placebo. Sinus tachycardia was reported in 2% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 1% of those on placebo. Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) has been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran worldwide; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia in patients receiving 100 mg/day of milnacipran, chest pain (unspecified) occurred in 3% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 2% of those on placebo. The percentage of patients who experienced chest pain or chest discomfort in the group receiving 200 mg/day of milnacipran was similar to placebo. Chest discomfort occurred in 2% of those receiving milnacipran 100 mg/day versus 1% of those on placebo.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia, headache occurred in 18% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 14% of those on placebo. Migraine occurred in 5% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 3% of those on placebo. Tension headache occurred in 2% of patients receiving milnacipran 100 mg/day versus 1% of those on placebo. The percentage of patients experiencing tension headache in the group receiving 200 mg/day of milnacipran was similar to placebo. Headache (unspecified) led to discontinuation in 2% of patients treated with milnacipran versus 0% of those treated with placebo.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia, xerostomia occurred 5% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 2% of those on placebo.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia, hot flashes occurred in 12% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 2% of those on placebo. Flushing was reported in 3% of milnacipran-treated patients compared to 1% of those on placebo. Additionally, hyperhidrosis occurred in 9% of patients during fibromyalgia clinical trials receiving milnacipran versus 2% of those on placebo. Hyperhidrosis led to discontinuation in 1% of patients treated with milnacipran versus 0% of those treated with placebo. Furthermore, both night sweats and pyrexia were reported in at least 1% of patients during clinical trials for fibromyalgia. Chills occurred in 2% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 0% of those on placebo.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia, dizziness occurred in 10% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 6% of those on placebo. Dizziness led to discontinuation in 1% of patients treated with milnacipran versus 0.5% of those treated with placebo. Drowsiness, fatigue, confusion, and falling were reported in at least 1% of patients during clinical trials for fibromyalgia. Although milnacipran appears to have limited effects on cognitive function and psychomotor performance, patients should be instructed to use caution when driving or operating machinery until the full effects of the drug are known.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia in patients receiving 200 mg/day of milnacipran, paresthesias occurred in 3% of patients on milnacipran versus 2% of those on placebo. The percentage of patients experiencing paresthesias or hypoesthesia in the group receiving 100 mg/day of milnacipran was similar to placebo. Hypoesthesia was reported in 2% of those receiving milnacipran 200 mg/day versus 1% of those on placebo.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia, tremor occurred in 2% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 1% of those on placebo.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia in patients receiving 100 mg/day of milnacipran, anxiety occurred in 5% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 4% of those on placebo. Anxiety was reported in 3% of those receiving milnacipran 200 mg/day. Irritability was reported in at least 1% of patients during clinical trials for fibromyalgia.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia, dyspnea occurred in 2% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 1% of those on placebo.
 
Delirium and hallucinations have been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequencies are unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
Serotonin syndrome has been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established. Signs and symptoms of serotonin syndrome include nausea/vomiting, diaphoresis (sweating), mental status changes, myoclonus, incoordination, shivering, fever, restlessness, hyperreflexia, agitation, and tremor. Serotonin syndrome is considered a medical emergency. Appropriate medical treatment should be promptly instituted if serotonin syndrome is suspected or confirmed.
 
Suicidal ideation has been reported in patients participating in clinical trials with antidepressants, including milnacipran. During clinical trials for fibromyalgia in patients with a history of depressive disorders, suicidal ideation occurred in 0.5% of patients on placebo, 0% of patients on milnacipran 100 mg/day, and 1.3% of patients on milnacipran 200 mg/day. No completed suicides occurred in any patients during clinical trials. Depression and stress were reported in at least 1% of patients during clinical trials. Symptoms of aggressiveness, akathisia (psychomotor restlessness), agitation, anxiety, insomnia, irritability, hostility, mania, hypomania, impulsivity, and panic attacks have been reported in adult and pediatric patients being treated with antidepressants for major depressive disorder as well as for other indications, both psychiatric and nonpsychiatric. Although a causal link between the emergence of such symptoms and either the worsening of depression and/or the emergence of suicidal impulses has not been established, there is concern that such symptoms may represent precursors to emerging suicidality. Consideration should be given to changing the therapeutic regimen, including possibly discontinuing the medication, in patients whose depression is persistently worse or who are experiencing emergent suicidality or symptoms that might be precursors to worsening depression or suicidal ideation. Changing or stopping the therapeutic regimen may be especially important if these symptoms are severe, abrupt in onset, or were not part of the patients presenting symptoms. Many of these adverse symptoms have been reported in clinical trials and have not always been associated with suicidality. Rapid discontinuation of many antidepressants may also result in some of these adverse CNS events.
 
Platelet dysfunction (i.e., impaired platelet aggregation) may occur during treatment with serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) due to platelet serotonin depletion, possibly increasing the risk of a bleeding complication (e.g., gastrointestinal bleeding, ecchymoses, epistaxis, hematomas, petechiae, hemorrhage). An increased risk of bleeding complications is possible in patients receiving antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications concurrently with milnacipran (see Drug Interactions).
 
Hyponatremia is considered a class effect of serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), with some cases possibly due to the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) secretion. Serum sodium concentrations lower than 110 mmol/L have been reported with other SNRIs. Hyponatremia has been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established. Although specific risk factors have not been identified, most cases have occurred in the elderly, those taking diuretics, or in patients with hypovolemia. Signs and symptoms of hyponatremia include headache, difficulty concentrating, memory impairment, confusion, weakness, and unsteadiness. More severe cases may cause hallucinations, syncope, seizures, coma, respiratory arrest, and death. Discontinuation of milnacipran may be necessary in those with symptomatic hyponatremia.
 
Milnacipran was associated with an increased risk of mydriasis during clinical trials. Blurred vision was reported in 2% of milnacipran-treated patients compared to 1% of those receiving placebo. Accommodation disorder has been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia in patients receiving 100 mg/day of milnacipran, upper respiratory tract infection occurred in 7% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 6% of those on placebo. The percentage of patients experiencing an upper respiratory tract infection in the group receiving 200 mg/day of milnacipran was similar to placebo.
 
During clinical trials for fibromyalgia in patients receiving 200 mg/day of milnacipran, rash (unspecified) occurred in 4% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 2% of those on placebo. The percentage of patients experiencing rash in the group receiving 100 mg/day of milnacipran was 3%. Pruritus occurred in 3% of patients receiving milnacipran 100 mg/day and 2% of those receiving milnacipran 200 mg/day or placebo. Erythema multiforme and Stevens-Johnson syndrome have been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequencies are unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
A discontinuation syndrome, described by the manufacturer as a result of physiological dependence, may occur following abrupt or rapid discontinuation of milnacipran. The most commonly reported withdrawal symptoms include dysphoric mood, irritability, agitation, dizziness, sensory disturbances, anxiety, confusion, headache, lethargy, emotional lability, insomnia, hypomania, tinnitus, and seizures. While these adverse events are usually transient, some may be severe. Gradual tapering is recommended during discontinuation of milnacipran to decrease or prevent the occurrence of withdrawal symptoms. Even with gradual tapering, self-limited adverse events may occur. If intolerable symptoms occur following a decrease in the dose or upon discontinuation of treatment, then resuming the previously prescribed dose may be considered. Subsequently, the clinician may continue decreasing the dose but at a more gradual rate.
 
During clinical trials, elevated hepatic enzymes occurred more frequently in milnacipran-treated patients than those receiving placebo. Elevated ALTs occurred in 6% of patients receiving 100 mg/day of milnacipran, 7% of patients receiving 200 mg/day of milnacipran, and 3% of those on placebo. One patient experienced an increased ALT between 5—10 times the upper limit of normal. Elevated ASTs occurred in 3% of patients receiving 100 mg/day of milnacipran, 5% of patients receiving 200 mg/day of milnacipran, and 2% of those on placebo. Post-marketing reports from countries other than the US indicate that cases of increased liver enzymes and severe liver injury, including fulminant hepatitis, have occurred. Milnacipran should be discontinued if jaundice or other forms of liver dysfunction become evident.
 
The effects of milnacipran on body weight are uncertain. During clinical trials for fibromyalgia in patients receiving 100—200 mg/day of milnacipran or placebo, a mean weight loss of 0.8 kg occurred in the active treatment groups versus a 0.2 kg mean weight loss in the placebo group. Conversely, weight gain (unspecified) was reported in at least 1% of patients during clinical trials for fibromyalgia. During clinical trials for fibromyalgia, decreased appetite occurred in 2% of patients receiving milnacipran versus 0% of those on placebo. Anorexia has been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
Milnacipran is in a class of drugs known to affect urethral resistance. Dysuria was reported in 1% of milnacipran-treated patients compared to 0.5% of those receiving placebo in clinical trials. Some cases with other serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors have required hospitalization and/or catheterization. Other GU-related complications reported in at least 1% of patients during clinical trials for fibromyalgia included urinary tract infection and cystitis. In addition, the following genitourinary effects occurred in at least 2% of male patients receiving milnacipran during clinical trials and with an incidence greater than placebo: dysuria, ejaculation dysfunction (e.g., ejaculation disorder, ejaculation failure), impotence (erectile dysfunction), libido decrease, prostatitis, scrotal pain, testicular pain, testicular swelling, urinary hesitation, urinary retention, urethral pain, and decreased urine flow. Sexual dysfunction and reproductive system disorders are well known and common adverse effects associated with serotonergic agents, and may frequently be underreported by patients to their health care provider as patients may be hesitant to discuss this topic.
 
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) has been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is characterized by hyperthermia, severe extrapyramidal dysfunction, alterations in consciousness, altered mental status, and autonomic instability. Several predisposing factors are thought to contribute to the development of NMS including heat stress, physical exhaustion, dehydration, and organic brain disease. Milnacipran should be immediately discontinued and appropriate supportive therapy initiated as soon as symptoms of NMS are discovered.
 
Peripheral edema was reported in at least 1% of patients during clinical trials for fibromyalgia.
 
Hypercholesterolemia was reported in at least 1% of patients during clinical trials for fibromyalgia.
 
Hematologic and lymphatic effects that have been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran include leukopenia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia. The frequencies are unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
Hyperprolactinemia has been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
Rhabdomyolysis has been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
Acute renal failure (unspecified) has been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
Galactorrhea has been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
Pseudoparkinsonism has been reported during post-marketing use of milnacipran; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.

    Serotonin syndrome has been reported during use of serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) alone, during concurrent use of other medications known to increase serotonin levels, or during SNRI overdose.     General Information
    A neonatal abstinence syndrome has been reported in infants exposed to serotonergic agents in utero. Description
  Text
  Milnacipran is an oral serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) antidepressant used for the management of fibromyalgia in the United States. Milnacipran is approved in some other countries for major depressive disorder. There is no consensus for a single medication as first-line therapy for fibromyalgia because high quality, direct comparator studies are lacking and meaningful conclusions cannot be made. In addition, no single medication appears to be fully effective for treating the entire spectrum of fibromyalgia symptoms, which commonly include pain, sleep disturbances, depressed mood, and fatigue. A twice daily dosing regimen is one disadvantage of milnacipran. Conversely, a major advantage of milnacipran is minimal hepatic metabolism and lack of CYP-mediated kinetic interactions. Similar to other SNRIs, milnacipran has been associated with increases in blood pressure and heart rate and caution is recommended when conditions are present that may be adversely affected by increased blood pressure or heart rate. Product labels for all antidepressants contain a boxed warning related to an increased risk of suicidality in children, adolescents, and young adults during the initial stages of therapy or during dose changes when treating depression or other conditions; therefore, the necessity of pharmacologic therapy versus the potential risks should be carefully considered in these populations.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Milnacipran is a serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) which inhibits the central reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine. Milnacipran is a racemic mixture with two pharmacologically active enantiomers. Although the exact mechanism by which milnacipran improves the symptoms of fibromyalgia is unknown, the neurotransmitter norepinephrine is considered important in the treatment of pain. Milnacipran demonstrate a 3-fold greater potency for norepinephrine reuptake inhibition than serotonin reuptake inhibition. All SNRIs inhibit serotonin uptake in human platelets, which has been associated with an increased risk of bleeding adverse events. In addition, SNRIs can increase blood pressure and heart rate; cases of elevated blood pressure requiring immediate treatment have been reported. Milnacipran does not directly affect the reuptake of dopamine or inhibit monoamine oxidase, and has no significant affinity for noradrenergic, muscarinic, beta-adrenergic, dopamine, opiate, benzodiazepine, GABA, or histaminergic receptor sites. There is no significant affinity for calcium, potassium, sodium, or chloride channels and no clinically relevant effect on the QTc interval.

  Hepatic Impairment

  Renal Impairment
  The following recommendations apply to adult patients:
