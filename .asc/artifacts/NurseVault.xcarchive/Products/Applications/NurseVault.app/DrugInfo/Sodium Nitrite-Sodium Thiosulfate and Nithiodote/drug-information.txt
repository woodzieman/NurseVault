Drug Class: Sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate are indicated for sequential administration to treat serious or life-threatening acute cyanide poisoning.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 300 mg (10 mL) per dose IV sodium nitrite; 12.5 g (50 mL) per dose IV sodium thiosulfate.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): arrhythmia exacerbation, coma, hypotension, methemoglobinemia, palpitations, sinus tachycardia, syncope, blurred vision, confusion, dizziness, headache, seizures, anxiety, cyanosis, diaphoresis, fatigue, injection site reaction, paresthesias, urticaria, weakness

In an Overdose: DO NOT administer concurrently in the same IV line with other cyanide antidotes, hydroxocobalamin, or blood products (whole blood, packed red cells, platelet concentrate or fresh frozen plasma).

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Sodium thiosulfate may contain trace impurities of sodium sulfite.

      Route Age Groups
        Heading
        Adults
        Dosage Description
        300 mg (10 mL) sodium nitrite IV at a rate of 2.5 to 5 mL/minute administered as early as possible after diagnosis. In patients with anemia, the sodium nitrite dose should be reduced proportionately to the hemoglobin concentration. Immediately after the sodium nitrite injection, administer 12.5 g (50 mL) of sodium thiosulfate by slow IV injection. Monitor blood pressure during the injections; decrease injection rates if significant hypotension is noted. Closely monitor patients for 24 to 48 hours for recurrent symptoms of cyanide poisoning. If symptoms return, repeat treatment with both sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate at one-half the original doses. Expert advice from a Poison Control Center is available at 1-800-222-1222 or online; DO NOT delay treatment if cyanide poisoning is suspected.

        6 mg/kg (0.2 mL/kg or 6 to 8 mL/m2) sodium nitrite IV at a rate of 2.5 to 5 mL/minute; maximum dosage is 300 mg (10 mL). Give as early as possible after diagnosis.     Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Sodium thiosulfate may contain trace impurities of sodium sulfite. However, the presence of trace amounts of sulfites in this product should not deter use of the drug in emergency situations, even in individuals with sulfite hypersensitivity.
    Headline
    infants, methemoglobin reductase deficiency, methemoglobinemia, neonates, respiratory insufficiency
    General Information
    Treatment with sodium nitrite causes methemoglobin formation, which diminishes oxygen carrying capacity. Closely monitor to ensure the individual is adequately oxygenated during treatment. Consider alternative therapeutic approaches in individuals known to have diminished oxygen reserves (e.g., respiratory insufficiency, smoke inhalation, carbon monoxide poisoning) and those at higher risk of developing methemoglobinemia (e.g., congenital methemoglobin reductase deficiency), as they are at greater risk for potentially life-threatening adverse events related to the use of sodium nitrite. Use caution and ensure the reduced dosing guidelines are followed in pediatric patients younger than 6 months; neonates and infants (with lower methemoglobin reductase concentrations and presence of easily oxidized fetal hemoglobin) may be more susceptible than adults or older pediatric patients to severe methemoglobinemia when sodium nitrite is administered. Additionally, use sodium nitrite with caution in the presence of other medications that may cause methemoglobinemia, such as procaine or nitroprusside. Monitor serum methemoglobin concentrations using co-oximetry and administer oxygen during treatment with sodium nitrite whenever possible. It is generally recommended that methemoglobin concentrations be kept below 30%. If methemoglobin concentrations exceed 30%, discontinue use of sodium nitrite. For life-threatening methemoglobinemia, intravenous methylene blue and exchange transfusion have been reported in literature as treatments.
    Headline
    cardiac disease, hypotension, hypovolemia
    General Information
    Cautious use of sodium nitrite is recommended in individuals who may be susceptible to injury from vasodilation. Treatment with sodium nitrite has been associated with severe hypotension. Closely monitor the blood pressure to ensure adequate perfusion during treatment. If significant hypotension is noted, the rate of infusion should be decreased. Consider alternative therapeutic approaches in individuals known to have diminished cardiovascular reserves (e.g., substantial blood loss or hypovolemia, cardiac disease), as they are at greater risk for potentially life-threatening adverse events related to the use of sodium nitrite.
    Headline
    anemia
    General Information
    When possible, obtain baseline hemoglobin and hematocrit concentrations when initiating treatment with sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate. In individuals with known anemia, it is recommended that sodium nitrite be used with caution and at a dose that is reduced proportionately to the hemoglobin concentration. In comparison to persons with normal red blood cell (RBC) volumes, individuals with anemia will form more methemoglobin (as a percentage of total hemoglobin).
    Headline
    G6PD deficiency
    General Information
    The risk of hemolytic crisis is increased when sodium nitrite is administered to individuals with G6PD deficiency; therefore, consideration of an alternative therapeutic approach is recommended in these individuals. Monitor individuals with known or suspected G6PD deficiency for an acute drop in hematocrit concentration. Individuals with G6PD deficiency who receive sodium nitrite may require an exchange transfusion.
    Headline
    geriatric, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate are substantially excreted in the kidneys, and the risk of toxic reactions to these drugs may be increased in individuals with renal impairment or renal failure. Geriatric adults are more likely to have renal impairment; therefore, care should be taken in dose selection, and it may be useful to monitor renal function in these individuals.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Cyanide poisoning is a medical emergency in pregnancy, which can be fatal for the pregnant individual and fetus if untreated. Cyanide readily crosses the placenta. Do not withhold life-sustaining therapy; sodium nitrite with sequential use of sodium thiosulfate is recommended in pregnant individuals with known or suspected cyanide poisoning. There are no available data on the use of sodium nitrate during human pregnancy to establish a drug-associated risk for major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. However, sodium nitrite produces methemoglobin. Fetal hemoglobin is more easily oxidized to methemoglobin than adult hemoglobin. Additionally, the fetus has lower concentrations of methemoglobin reductase than an adult. If available, consider an alternative therapy not associated with methemoglobinemia. In animal studies, fetal mortality was reported when pregnant guinea pigs were given subcutaneous sodium nitrate at 1.7 times the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) when maternal and fetal methemoglobin levels were at their peak. In other studies, there was no evidence of malformations, but severe anemia, reduced growth, and increased pup mortality were reported when pregnant rats were treated with 4.7 times the MRHD of sodium nitrate via drinking water. Orally administered sodium thiosulfate at doses 0.2- to 0.9-times the human dose for cyanide poisoning was not embryotoxic or teratogenic when given during organogenesis to pregnant mice, rats, hamsters, and rabbits.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise individuals to avoid breast-feeding while receiving sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate for the treatment of cyanide poisoning. There are no available data on the presence of sodium nitrite or sodium thiosulfate in human milk, their effect on the breast-fed child, or their effect on milk production. However, cyanide and thiocyanate (which is formed when sodium thiosulfate combines with cyanide) are present in human milk; therefore, breast-feeding is not recommended during treatment for cyanide poisoning. It is not known when breast-feeding may be safely resumed after administration of sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate.

Description
  Text
  Sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate are indicated for sequential administration to treat serious or life-threatening acute cyanide poisoning. Sodium nitrite is an inorganic compound that oxidizes hemoglobin to form methemoglobin which then combines with cyanide to form the relatively nontoxic cyanomethemoglobin. Following the sodium nitrite injection, sodium thiosulfate is administered to enhance the endogenous detoxification of cyanide by serving as a sulfur donor for the conversion to thiocyanate. This drug combination is effective in treating cyanide toxicity especially if administered in a timely manner; the effect of combining sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate is much greater than using each alone. Healthcare providers are advised via a Black Box Warning that treatment with sodium nitrite has been associated with serious adverse reactions and death in humans, even at doses less than twice the recommended therapeutic dose. When the diagnosis of cyanide poisoning is uncertain, the potentially life-threatening risks associated with treatment should be weighted against the potential benefits. For expert advice, contact Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 or online.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate have different mechanisms of action which, when used in combination for the treatment of cyanide poisoning, are synergistic. Sodium nitrite reacts with hemoglobin to form methemoglobin, an oxidized form of hemoglobin incapable of oxygen transport but with high affinity for cyanide. Cyanide ions preferentially bind to methemoglobin over cytochrome a3 (a component of the cytochrome c oxidase complex in mitochondria) to form the nontoxic cyanomethemoglobin. This process displaces cyanide from cytochrome oxidase, allowing for resumption of aerobic metabolism. Following sodium nitrite dosing, sodium thiosulfate is immediately administered to aid in the conversion of cyanide to the relatively nontoxic thiocyanate by acting as a sulfur donor.

  Text
  Sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate are administered intravenously.
Sodium nitrite: Data on the pharmacokinetics of sodium nitrite are limited. Once in the systemic circulation, the drug rapidly reacts with hemoglobin to form methemoglobin. Approximately 40% of an administered dose is excreted unchanged in the urine, while the remaining 60% is metabolized to ammonia and related small molecules.
Sodium thiosulfate: Once in the systemic circulation, sodium thiosulfate is distributed into extracellular fluid. Most of the thiosulfate is oxidized to sulfate or incorporated into endogenous sulfur compounds. Approximately 20% to 50% of the thiosulfate dose is excreted unchanged in the urine. The serum thiosulfate half-life is reported to be 20 minutes following a 1 g intravenous dose; however, the half-life was reported as 182 minutes after the administration of a larger dose (150 mg/kg or 9 g for 60 kg body weight). After detoxification, thiocyanate is excreted in the urine at a rate inversely proportional to creatinine clearance; in healthy subjects, the volume of distribution and half-life of thiocyanate is 0.25 L/kg and 2.7 days, respectively.

Administration
  General Information
  For storage information, see the specific product information within the How Supplied section.
 
NOTE: When treating acute cyanide intoxication, sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate should be considered as adjunctive therapies to appropriate support of vital functions. Do not delay airway, ventilatory, and circulatory support or oxygenation in order to administer sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate.
NOTE: For expert advice, contact Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 or online.
  Route Specific Administrations
    Administration Details
      Name
      Intravenous Administration
      Description
      Withdraw dose of sodium nitrite into a suitable syringe, and withdraw the dose of sodium thiosulfate into a separate syringe.
Inject sodium nitrite intravenously at a rate of 2.5 to 5 mL/minute while monitoring blood pressure. Slow infusion rate if significant hypotension is noted.
Immediately after giving sodium nitrite, administer sodium thiosulfate by slow intravenous injection while monitoring blood pressure. Slow the injection rate if significant hypotension is noted.
Sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate may be administered sequentially through the same IV line, as no chemical incompatibilities have been reported.
DO NOT administer concurrently in the same IV line with other cyanide antidotes, hydroxocobalamin, or blood products (whole blood, packed red cells, platelet concentrate or fresh frozen plasma). However, sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate may be administered simultaneously with blood products if using a separate IV line (preferably on contralateral extremities, if using peripheral lines).
Observe patient closely for at least 24 to 48 hours. If signs of poisoning reappear, injections of sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate may be repeated at one-half of the original dose.
