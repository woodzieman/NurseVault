Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Lactulose contains galactose and is contraindicated in patients who require a galactose-free diet.Lactulose is relatively contraindicated prior to surgery, especially if the procedure requires electrocautery during proctoscopy or colonoscopy. Preoperative use of mannitol can generate a flammable gas (i.e., hydrogen gas), which can explode in the presence of a spark. Although this phenomenon has not been reported with lactulose, caution should be observed.Although very little lactulose is absorbed systemically, it should be used with caution in diabetes mellitus patients due to its sugar content (galactose six months should have serum electrolytes (potassium, chloride, carbon dioxide) measured periodically.Lactulose is classified as an FDA pregnancy risk category B drug. No evidence of teratogenicity has been found in animal reproduction studies. There are no adequate or well-controlled studies in humans regarding the use of lactulose during pregnancy. Lactulose should only be used during pregnancy if the potential therapeutic benefits justify its usage. It is not known whether lactulose is excreted in human milk. The manufacturer advises caution for the use of lactulose for breast-feeding women; however, lactulose is minimally absorbed systemically, so the risk to the infant is low.

    Headline
    galactosemia
    General Information
    Lactulose contains galactose and is contraindicated in patients with galactosemia who require a low galactose diet.
    Headline
    planned procedure
    General Information
    A theoretical hazard may exist for patients being treated with lactulose undergoing a planned procedure such as electrocautery procedures during proctoscopy or colonoscopy. Accumulation of hydrogen gas in significant concentration in the presence of an electrical spark may result in an explosive reaction. Although this complication has not been reported with lactulose, patients on lactulose therapy undergoing such procedures should have a thorough bowel cleansing with a non-fermentable solution. Insufflation of carbon dioxide as an additional safeguard may be pursued, but is considered to be a redundant measure.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus
    General Information
    Lactulose contains galactose and lactose (less than 0.3 g/10 g as a total sum) and should be used with caution in people with diabetes mellitus.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Geriatric adults or debilitated patients may be more likely to experience electrolyte and fluid imbalances with the chronic use of lactulose. Geriatric adults who receive lactulose chronically for more than 6 months should have serum electrolytes (potassium, chloride, carbon dioxide) measured periodically.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Osmotic laxatives, such as lactulose, can be administered safely during pregnancy. Lactulose is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, and fetal exposure is unlikely. Animal reproduction studies have revealed no evidence of fetal harm.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Lactulose is compatible with breast-feeding. Lactulose is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, and excretion into human milk in large amounts is unlikely.

Description
  Text
  Lactulose, a synthetic derivative of lactose, is a disaccharide sugar containing one molecule of galactose and one molecule of fructose. Lactulose is used in the treatment of hepatic encephalopathy and as a laxative. Clinical response in the treatment of hepatic encephalopathy is about equal to that of neomycin. Lactulose was approved by the FDA in 1976.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Lactulose is a synthetic product that cannot be hydrolyzed by any gastrointestinal enzyme. Therefore, oral doses of the drug reach the colon virtually unchanged. In the colon, normal bacterial flora degrade lactulose into lactic acid and small amounts of formic and acetic acid. This acidic environment ionizes ammonia in the colon to the ammonium ion. Once ionized, ammonia, now in the form of the ammonium ion, cannot diffuse across the colon membrane and is ultimately excreted in the stool. This process reduces blood ammonia levels by 25—50%, which usually has a beneficial effect on the patient's mental status. Lactulose also can inhibit nonbacterial, glutamine-dependent ammonia production in the intestinal wall.
 
Intracolonic breakdown of lactulose also increases osmotic pressure, causing fluid accumulation that softens the stool and distends the colon, enhancing peristalsis. Evidence suggests that reduction in luminal pH may enhance motility and secretion. Effects of lactulose occur only in the colon and are therefore motility-dependent. A normal bowel movement may not occur for 1—2 days.
