Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  When thyrotropin alfa-stimulated thyroglobulin (Tg) testing is performed in combination with radioiodine imaging there is still a risk of missing a diagnosis of thyroid cancer or underestimating the extent of disease. A newly detectable Tg level, a rising Tg level over time after thyrotropin alfa treatment, or a high index of suspicion of metastatic disease, even in the setting of a negative or low-stage thyrotropin alfa radioiodine scan, should prompt further evaluation(e.g., thyroid hormone withdrawal scan) to definitively establish the location and extent of thyroid cancer. However, in clinical trials, none of the patients with undetectable Tg levels (< 2.5 ng/ml) following thyrotropin alfa-stimulation had metastatic disease. Furthermore, antibodies to thyroglobulin may confound the assay and render the thyroglobulin concentrations noninterpretable. In these instances, patients should also be evaluated further, even with a negative or low-stage thyrotropin radioiodine scan.
 
Thyrotropin alfa may cause a transient but significant increase in serum thyroid hormone concentrations. Due to the cardiostimulatory effects of thyroid hormones, thyrotropin alfa should be used with caution in elderly patients with functioning thyroid tumors, those with preexisting cardiac disease and coronary artery disease, including angina, recent acute myocardial infarction and hypertension, and in patients with significant residual thyroid tissue. For patients for whom hyperthyroidism could have serious consequences (e.g., extensive metastatic disease, cardiac disease, elderly patients, or other significant comorbidities), hospitalization for administration of thyrotropin alfa and post-administration observation should be considered; rare cases of serious adverse effects (e.g., myocardial infarction, death) have been reported within 24 hours of thyrotropin administration in patients with serious comorbidities.
 
As a result of increased serum thyroid hormones, adrenal insufficiency should be corrected prior to administration of thyrotropin. Increased thyroid hormone levels in patients with uncontrolled adrenal insufficiency, diabetes mellitus or other endocrine disorders can unmasked or exacerbated these conditions.
 
Other possible etiologies of hypothyroidism (e.g., morphologic hypogonadism and nephroses) should be ruled out prior using thyrotropin. Patients with hypothyroidism secondary to hypopituitarism are likely to have suppressed adrenal function as well, which should be corrected prior to administering thyrotropin. Symptoms of hypopituitarism can be unmasked or exacerbated by the increase in thyroid hormones.
 
Thyrotropin alfa (Thyrogen) in produced in a modified Chinese hamster ovary cell line. Therefore, it should be used cautiously in patients with a known hamster protein hypersensitivity. Also of note, patients who have previously received bovine-derived thyroid stimulating hormone (Thytropar) or have had an adverse reaction to bovine TSH are at increased risk of hypersensitivity reactions during thyrotropin alfa therapy. There is a potential that these patients have developed antibodies against bovine-TSH that may cross-react with thyrotropin alfa, a recombinant TSH product.
 
Thyrotropin alfa elimination is significantly reduced in patients with renal failure on dialysis; elevated TSH concentrations are prolonged in these patients.
 
Thyrotropin alfa is classified pregnancy category C. No adequate or well-controlled reproductive studies have been done in humans or animals. Thyrotropin alfa should only be administered to a pregnant women if clearly needed.
 
It is not known if thyrotropin alfa is secreted in breast milk. Since many drugs and proteins are excreted in breast-milk, breast-feeding should only be continued during thyrotropin alfa therapy if the risk to the infant is outweighed by the benefit to the mother.
 
Safety and effectiveness in children < 16 years of age have not been established.
 
Thyrotropin alfa is for intramuscular administration only; it should not be given via intravenous administration. Severe reactions including nausea, vomiting, diaphoresis, hypotension and tachycardia have been reported in one patient following intravenous administration of 0.3 mg thyrotropin alfa. Intramuscular injections can cause injury at the injection site and should be used cautiously in patients with thrombocytopenia or coagulopathy (i.e., hemophilia).

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Thyrotropin alfa may cause a transient but significant increase in thyroid hormones leading to symptoms of hyperthyroidism. Adverse reactions to thyrotropin alfa are uncommon, and are similar in type and frequency regardless of indication for use. Headache (7.3%), nausea/vomiting (11.9%/2.9%), and fatigue (3.3%) were the most commonly reported adverse reactions. Reactions occurring in < 2% of patients include arthralgia, asthenia, chills, dizziness, fever, flu-like syndrome, myalgia, and paresthesias. Post-marketing surveillance indicates that patients may experience flu-like symptoms for 48 hours after drug administration. Patients with renal failure may experience prolonged elevated TSH concentrations secondary to decreased elimination of thyrotropin; accordingly, these patients may be at increased risk of side effects, including headache and nausea/vomiting. Patients who received thyrotropin alfa at doses higher than recommended (2.7—3.6 mg IM) experienced weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, vomiting, and hot flashes.Mild allergic reactions consisting of rash (unspecified), urticaria, pruritus, flushing, and respiratory difficulties requiring treatment occurred in < 1% of patients. In clinical trials, no patient developed antibodies to thyrotropin alfa, either after single or repeated use.Four of 55 (7.3%) patients with CNS metastases of thyroid cancer experienced acute hemiplegia or hemiparesis (paresis on one side of the body) 1—3 days after administration of thyrotropin alfa. The symptoms were attributed to local edema and/or focal hemorrhage at the site of cerebral or spinal metastases. One case of acute visual impairment and one case of laryngeal edema with respiratory distress, requiring tracheotomy, with onset of symptoms within 24 hours after thyrotropin alfa administration, have been reported in patients with metastases to the optic nerve and paratracheal areas, respectively. In addition, sudden rapid and painful enlargement of locally recurring papillary carcinoma, accompanied by dyspnea, stridor, or dysphonia, has been reported with 12—48 hours of thyrotropin alfa administration; glucocorticoid therapy provided rapid clinical improvement. Premedication with corticosteroids should be considered for patients in whom local tumor expansion may compromise vital anatomic structures.A fatal myocardial infarction was reported in a patient with a history of cardiac disease and spinal metastasis of thyroid cancer who received 4 injections of thyrotropin alfa over 6 days. The acute myocardial infarction occurred 24 hours after the last dose of thyrotropin alfa and was likely due to thyrotropin-induced hyperthyroidism. There have been reports of death in the 24 hours after thyrotropin administration, especially in patients with other serious comorbidities such as cardiac disease, metastatic disease, or other known serious illnesses. In patients at high-risk for serious complications from thyrotropin-induced hyperthyroidism, hospitalization for the injection and for post-injection monitoring should be considered.A patient who received thyrotropin alfa 0.3 mg IV experienced severe nausea, vomiting, diaphoresis, hypotension (blood pressure decrease form 115/66 mm Hg to 81/44 mm Hg) and sinus tachycardia (pulse increased from 75 to 117 beats per minute). Thyrotropin alfa should only be administered intramuscularly.

Description
  Text
  Thyrotropin alfa is an injectable thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) indicated as an adjunctive diagnostic tool for serum thyroglobulin testing with or without radioiodine imaging in the follow-up of patients with well-differentiated thyroid cancer who have previously undergone thyroidectomy and as adjunctive therapy for radioiodine ablation of thyroid tissue remnants in patients who have undergone a near-total or total thyroidectomy for well-differentiated thyroid cancer and who do not have evidence of distant metastatic thyroid cancer. Thyrotropin alfa is synthesized in a genetically modified Chinese hamster ovary cell line. Use of thyrotropin alfa before whole-body scanning avoids the need to withdraw the patient's exogenous thyroid supplements 2 weeks or more before scanning and placing the patient in a hypothyroid state. The utility of thyrotropin alfa in scanning for thyroid cancer was compared to standard scanning with withdrawal of thyroid hormone; thyrotropin alfa stimulated radioiodine uptake and resulted in positive scans that were as sensitive as those obtained after withdrawal of thyroid hormone in 71% of cases. In cases where thyrotropin alfa scans were not as sensitive, 6% of patients had more advanced tumor staging and 13% required radioiodine treatment. Patients given thyrotropin alfa appear to have improved quality of life compared to patients who discontinue their thyroid hormone therapy. Thyrotropin alfa also stimulates the production of thyroglobulin by residual thyroid tissue, which may increase the usefulness of thyroglobulin as a tumor marker in patients who have had thyroid ablation and are being treated with thyroid hormone. The effect of thryotropin alfa on thyroid cancer recurrence more than 5 years post-remnant ablation has not been evaluated.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Thyrotropin alfa acts similarly to native thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH). Binding of thyrotropin alfa to TSH receptors on normal thyroid tissue or on well differentiated thyroid cancer tissue stimulates iodine uptake and organification, and the synthesis and release of thyroglobulin (Tg), triiodothyronine (T3), and thyroxine (T4). The effect of TSH activation on thyroid cells is to increase uptake of radioiodine to allow scan detection or radioiodine killing of thyroid cells. TSH activation also leads to the release of thyroglobulin by thyroid cells, which functions as a tumor marker and is detected in blood specimens.

Reconstitution
Reconstitute the vial with 1.2 mL of Sterile Water for Injection, USP to yield a single-dose solution containing 0.9 mg/mL of thyrotropin alfa that delivers 1 mL (0.9 mg).

Withdraw 1 mL (0.9 mg) of the reconstituted solution and inject intramuscularly into the outer region of the gluteus maximus.
