Drug Class: Trastuzumab is a recombinant DNA-derived humanized IgG1 kappa monoclonal antibody against the HER2 protein, indicated for the treatment of HER2-positive breast cancer and gastric cancer.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: Loading Dose: 8 mg/kg IV.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): cardiomyopathy, heart failure, left ventricular dysfunction, arrhythmia exacerbation, palpitations, sinus tachycardia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ards), anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension, hypoxia, infusion-related reactions, cough, dyspnea, pleural effusion, pneumonitis, pulmonary edema, pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary toxicity, glomerulonephritis, nephrotic syndrome, renal failure, arthralgia, back pain, bone pain, muscle cramps, myalgia, infection

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Trastuzumab administration can result in severe infusion-related reactions including anaphylaxis, angioedema, and pulmonary toxicity (see Adverse Reactions). Pulmonary toxicity can occur as sequelae of infusion reactions. Emetic risk: Minimal

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Trastuzumab administration can result in severe infusion-related reactions including anaphylaxis, angioedema, and pulmonary toxicity (see Adverse Reactions). Pulmonary toxicity can occur as sequelae of infusion reactions. Patients with symptomatic intrinsic lung disease (i.e., asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)), or with extensive tumor involvement of the lungs resulting in dyspnea at rest (respiratory insufficiency) appear to have more severe toxicity such as dyspnea, interstitial pneumonitis, pulmonary infiltrates, pleural effusions, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, pulmonary insufficiency and hypoxia, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and pulmonary fibrosis. Some reactions have been fatal. In most cases, symptoms occur during or within 24 hours of administration. Trastuzumab infusion should be interrupted for patients experiencing dyspnea (e.g., acute bronchospasm) or clinically significant hypotension. Decrease the rate of infusion for mild or moderate infusion reactions. Monitor patients until signs and symptoms completely resolve. Discontinue trastuzumab for infusion reactions manifesting as anaphylaxis, angioedema, interstitial pneumonitis, or acute respiratory distress syndrome; strongly consider permanent discontinuation in all patients with severe infusion reactions, as no data exist in regard to the most appropriate method of identification of patients who may safely be retreated with trastuzumab. Most patients who experienced a severe infusion reaction were premedicated with antihistamines and/or corticosteroids before resumption of trastuzumab infusion. Some patients tolerated trastuzumab, but some patients had recurrent severe infusion reactions despite premedications.
 
Trastuzumab should be used with extreme caution in patients with cardiac disease, especially heart failure, cardiomyopathy, or ventricular dysfunction. Trastuzumab therapy can result in the development of left ventricular dysfunction and congestive heart failure (see Adverse Reactions). The incidence and severity of left ventricular cardiac dysfunction was highest in patients who received trastuzumab concurrently with anthracycline-containing chemotherapy regimens. Advanced age (elderly patients) may increase the probability of cardiac dysfunction. Patients with pre-existing cardiac disease, prior anthracycline use, or radiation therapy may have a decreased ability to tolerate trastuzumab therapy. All patients should undergo monitoring of their left ventricular heart function before and during therapy with trastuzumab. Monitoring should include a history; physical exam; and MUGA scan or echocardiogram. Monitoring may not identify all patients who will develop cardiac dysfunction. Discontinue trastuzumab in patients receiving adjuvant therapy and strongly consider discontinuation of trastuzumab in patients with metastatic breast cancer for a clinically significant decrease in left ventricular function. Patients who develop asymptomatic decreases in ejection fraction should be closely monitored for evidence of clinical deterioration. The safety of trastuzumab resumption in patients who have previously experienced trastuzumab-induced cardiac toxicity has not been adequately studied. Reintroduction in patients with trastuzumab-related cardiac toxicity has been retrospectively evaluated in 2 studies. In the first study of 38 patients who developed left ventricular dysfunction after anthracycline and during adjuvant trastuzumab therapy (20 were symptomatic), trastuzumab was reintroduced in 25 of the patients because of a considerable initial response. All 25 of these patients had stable left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and symptoms and were on maximum tolerated doses of angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors and beta blockers during trastuzumab reintroduction. After a median trastuzumab treatment of 8.4 months, 22 of the 25 patients had stable LVEF measurements and no CHF recurrence. Trastuzumab was permanently discontinued in 3 patients because of left ventricular dysfunction or CHF recurrence. In the second study of 49 patients with metastatic breast cancer who developed cardiac toxicity, trastuzumab was reintroduced in 26 of the patients (11 symptomatic and 15 asymptomatic) who had complete recovery of cardiac function after trastuzumab discontinuation. No additional cardiac toxicity occurred in 16 of the patients. The other 10 patients had a subsequent cardiac event, but 5 had complete recovery after trastuzumab discontinuation, and 5 maintained a slightly reduced LVEF without symptoms with continued trastuzumab receipt. In addition to the safety of trastuzumab resumption, the safety of trastuzumab continuation in patients who have previously experienced trastuzumab-induced cardiac toxicity has not been adequately studied. In one retrospective evaluation, trastuzumab was continued in 17 of 34 asymptomatic patients with metastatic breast cancer who received trastuzumab for at least a year (median of 21.3 months). Thirteen of the 17 patients had complete recovery without specific cardiac treatment, 2 had complete recovery with specific cardiac treatment, and 2 were not assessed but did not have cardiac symptoms.
 
For patients with known benzyl alcohol hypersensitivity, trastuzumab should be reconstituted with Sterile Water for Injection, USP. Discard any unused portion of the trastuzumab vial reconstituted with SWFI following single use.
 
Trastuzumab should be used cautiously in patients with hamster protein hypersensitivity (i.e., Chinese hamster ovary cell hypersensitivity), trastuzumab hypersensitivity, or hypersensitivity to other components of the product due to increased risk of severe allergic reactions.
 
Initial studies indicate a low level of immunogenicity of trastuzumab. Of 903 patients evaluated, human anti-human antibody (HAHA) was only detected in one patient who did not experience any allergic manifestations.
 
According to the manufacturer, trastuzumab or breast-feeding should be discontinued because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in nursing infants from trastuzumab. If trastuzumab is discontinued, consider the elimination half-life of trastuzumab. Trastuzumab excretion into human milk is unknown, but human IgG is excreted in human milk. Published data suggest that breast milk antibodies do not enter the neonatal and infant circulation in substantial amounts. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
Trastuzumab is a FDA pregnancy risk category D drug. Trastuzumab can cause fetal harm. The risk of oligohydramnios during the second and third trimesters appears to be increased. Post-marketing, oligohydramnios occurred in pregnant women who received trastuzumab alone or in combination with chemotherapy. In half of these women, the amniotic fluid index increased after trastuzumab cessation. In one case, oligohydramnios recurred with trastuzumab resumption; trastuzumab was restarted after the amniotic fluid index improved. Only use trastuzumab during pregnancy if the potential benefit to the mother justifies the potential risk to the fetus. Apprise a woman who is exposed to trastuzumab during pregnancy of the potential risk to the fetus. Registration in a pregnancy registry by calling 1—800—690—6720 is advised. Also, monitoring for oligohydramnios is recommended. If oligohydramnios occurs, fetal testing should be done that is appropriate for gestational age and is consistent with community standards of care. Additional intravenous hydration has been helpful when oligohydramnios has occurred after administration of other chemotherapy agents, but the effects of additional IV hydration with trastuzumab are unknown.
 
The safety and effectiveness of trastuzumab in children have not been established.

  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Trastuzumab can cause left ventricular cardiac dysfunction, arrhythmias, hypertension, disabling cardiac failure, cardiomyopathy, and cardiac death; older patients may have an increased incidence of cardiotoxicity. Trastuzumab may worsen or cause severe cardiomyopathy resulting in the development of ventricular dysfunction and congestive heart failure. Congestive heart failure due to trastuzumab therapy has been associated with disability, death, and mural wall thrombosis leading to stroke. Other symptoms of heart failure in patients treated with trastuzumab include dyspnea, increased cough, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, peripheral edema, S-3 gallop, and reduced ejection fraction. Sinus tachycardia has been reported in 5—12% of patients receiving trastuzumab, and pericardial effusion, myocardial infarction, syncope, or arrhythmia occurred in at least 1 of 958 patients who received trastuzumab in clinical trials. A 4—6 fold increase in the incidence of symptomatic myocardial dysfunction exists among patients receiving trastuzumab as monotherapy or as combination therapy as compared with patients not receiving trastuzumab. The greatest risk of subclinical or clinical cardiac failure occurs with concurrent trastuzumab and anthracycline therapy; the incidence and severity of left ventricular cardiac dysfunction was highest in patients who received trastuzumab concurrently with anthracycline-containing chemotherapy regimens. Severe cardiac dysfunction, classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class III or IV congestive heart failure (CHF), occurred in 5% of 213 patients treated with trastuzumab alone for metastatic breast cancer; most patients had received prior anthracyclines. In a different study, patients who received anthracyclines and cyclophosphamide with trastuzumab for metastatic breast cancer had an increased incidence of severe cardiac dysfunction (19% of 143) versus those receiving trastuzumab with paclitaxel (4% of 91) or paclitaxel alone (1% of 95). Baseline cardiac function assessment and frequent monitoring may help identify and limit cardiac toxicity; supportive medical management with CHF medications may be necessary. Among 32 patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy and who developed CHF, one patient died of cardiomyopathy, and all other patients received cardiac medication. Approximately half of the surviving patients had recovery to a normal LVEF, which was defined as a LEVF of at least 50%. Retrospective data are available in regard to cardiac toxicity related to trastuzumab use in both the metastatic and the adjuvant setting. In the metastatic setting, reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and/or symptoms of CHF occurred in 49 patients who received trastuzumab for at least a year (median of 21.3 months) and most of whom (85%) had prior exposure to anthracyclines. Of the 49 patients, 15 were symptomatic. The LVEF recovered in 11 of the 14 patients with trastuzumab discontinuation and with beta blocker with or without angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor initiation. Despite standard CHF therapy in the other 3 patients, 1 died of CHF, and 2 did not have LVEF recovery. The LVEF also recovered in 15 of the 17 asymptomatic patients with trastuzumab discontinuation plus or minus standard cardiac therapy; the other 2 patients did not have LVEF assessed but had no cardiac symptoms. In this retrospective analysis that included 124 patients without a cardiac event, the baseline LVEF was significantly associated with a cardiac event occurrence. The hazard ratio for cardiac event adjusted for trastuzumab use for baseline LVEF was 0.94 (95% CI, 0.91—0.98). Among the 49 patients with a cardiac event, the median baseline LVEF was 58 (range, 33—77) whereas the the median baseline LVEF was 61 (range, 50—87) among the 124 patients without a cardiac event. Also, the time from last anthracycline administration was almost statistically significantly associated with a cardiac event. The hazard ratio for cardiac event adjusted for trastuzumab use for the time from last anthracycline administration was 0.99 (95% CI, 0.97—1). Among patients who received trastuzumab for the adjuvant treatment of breast cancer, reduced LVEF occurred in 38 patients. All 38 had received prior anthracyclines, and the mean LVEF was 0.61 +/- 0.13 after anthracycline treatment and before trastuzumab initiation. After a median trastuzumab treatment duration of 4.5 months, the mean LVEF was 0.43 +/- 0.16, and 20 of the 38 patients were symptomatic. Most (n=31) patients were treated for CHF, and trastuzumab was discontinued in all but 1 patient. Approximately 1.5 months after trastuzumab discontinuation, the LVEF improved in all patients, and the mean LVEF was 0.55 +/- 0.11. No clinical progression of CHF and slight LVEF improvement occurred in the patient who remained on trastuzumab; she was started on standard CHF medications. Conduct a thorough cardiac assessment including history, physical examination, and determination of LVEF by echocardiogram or MUGA scan before the first trastuzumab dose. In clinical studies, a baseline LVEF measurement was obtained immediately before trastuzumab initiation, and LVEF measurements were obtained every 3 months during and upon trastuzumab completion. Further, LVEF measurements were obtained every 6 months for at least 2 years after trastuzumab completion. LVEF was measured at 4 week intervals if trastuzumab was withheld for significant left ventricular cardiac dysfunction. Assess LVEF in all patients before trastuzumab initiation and at regular intervals during treatment. If a 16% or greater absolute decrease in LVEF from pretreatment values occurs OR if the LVEF falls below institutional limits of normal AND is a 10% or greater absolute decrease from pretreatment values, withhold trastuzumab for at least 4 weeks. If the LVEF returns to normal limits AND the absolute decrease from baseline is 15% or less within 4—8 weeks, trastuzumab may be resumed.     cardiomyopathy, heart failure, left ventricular dysfunction
    General Information
    Trastuzumab is associated with cardiotoxicity ranging from asymptomatic declines in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) to symptomatic heart failure/cardiomyopathy and, rarely, cardiac death. Symptomatic myocardial dysfunction is increased about 4 to 6-fold with trastuzumab monotherapy or combination therapy versus regimens without trastuzumab, with the highest absolute risk when given with an anthracycline. In large adjuvant trials, clinically significant cardiac events were uncommon but measurable: in HERA (1 year of trastuzumab monotherapy), severe (NYHA III to IV) congestive heart failure (CHF) occurred in 0.8% of patients after a median 8-year follow-up, and mild symptomatic or asymptomatic left ventricular dysfunction in 4.6% of patients; early follow-up also showed higher rates of decreased ejection fraction (3.5% vs 0.6%) and CHF (2% vs 0.3%) versus observation; cardiac failure occurred in 0.5% of trastuzumab-treated patients. An LVEF of less than 50% was reported in 8.6% of trastuzumab-treated patients in the HERA trial, with at least a 10% decrease from baseline in 7% of patients, and at least a 16% decrease from baseline in 3.8% of patients; extending trastuzumab monotherapy to 2 years increased the rate of asymptomatic cardiac dysfunction compared to 1 year of trastuzumab (8.1% vs. 4.6%) and thus is not recommended. In NSABP B31, NCCTG N9831, and BCIRG006, an LVEF less than 50% was reported in 17% to 23.1% of patients who received AC (doxorubicin plus cyclophosphamide) followed by TH (taxane plus trastuzumab) compared with 9.5% to 11.7% of those who received AC followed by a taxane without trastuzumab and 8.5% of those who received non-anthracycline TCH (docetaxel, carboplatin, trastuzumab). Description
  Text
  Trastuzumab is a recombinant DNA-derived humanized IgG1 kappa monoclonal antibody against the HER2 protein, indicated for the treatment of HER2-positive breast cancer and gastric cancer. Due to differences in tumor histopathology including incomplete membrane staining and increased heterogenicity in HER2 expression in gastric cancers, detection of HER2 protein overexpression should be accomplished using FDA-approved tests for the specific tumor type. Do not substitute trastuzumab for or with ado-trastuzumab emtansine or fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan. Trastuzumab carries black box warnings for cardiomyopathy, infusion reactions/pulmonary toxicity, and embryofetal toxicity. Monitor left ventricular function in all patients. People who may become pregnant should have a negative pregnancy test documented prior to treatment and use effective contraception during treatment and for 7 months after discontinuation of therapy. Trastuzumab was originally approved by the FDA in September 1998.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Trastuzumab is a humanized IgG1 kappa monoclonal antibody that selectively binds with high affinity to the extracellular domain of HER2. Trastuzumab-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) preferentially acts on cancer cells that overexpress HER2 compared with cancer cells that do not overexpress HER2. The HER2 (or c-erbB2) proto-oncogene encodes a transmembrane receptor protein which is structurally related to the epidermal growth factor receptor. Trastuzumab has been shown, in both in vitro assays and in animals, to inhibit the proliferation of human tumor cells that overexpress HER2. The overexpression of HER2 in tumor cells is closely associated with increased angiogenesis and expression of vascular epidermal growth factor (VEGF); when the VEGF pathway is inhibited, tumor growth is suppressed. In cells treated with trastuzumab, the HER2 receptor is downregulated, cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p27 accumulates, and cell cycle arrest occurs. Trastuzumab also inhibits the constitutive HER2 cleavage/shedding mediated by metalloproteases, which may correlate with the clinical activity of trastuzumab.

    Description
    Population pharmacokinetic predictions have been performed in patients receiving trastuzumab on a 3-week cycle (8 mg/kg IV followed by 6mg/kg IV every 3 weeks) and on a weekly basis (4 mg/kg IV followed by 2 mg/kg IV once weekly). The predicted median Cmax for Cycle 1 was 174 mcg/mL (90% CI, 117 to 291 mcg/mL) for breast cancer patients on a 3-week cycle, 132 mcg/mL (90% CI, 84.2 to 225 mcg/mL) for metastatic gastric cancer patients on a 3-week cycle, and 88.3 mcg/mL (90% CI, 58 to 144 mcg/mL) for breast cancer patients on a weekly schedule of trastuzumab; the predicted median Cmin for Cycle 1 was 29.4 mcg/mL (90% CI, 5.8 to 59.5 mcg/mL), 23.1 mcg/mL (90% CI, 6.1 to 50.3 mcg/mL), and 37.5 mcg/mL (90% CI, 12.3 to 70.9 mcg/mL), respectively.
