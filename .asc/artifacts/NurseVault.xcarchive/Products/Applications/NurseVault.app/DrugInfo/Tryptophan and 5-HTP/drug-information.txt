    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate or well-controlled studies on the use of tryptophan during pregnancy. It is unknown if tryptophan has the potential to be transferred to the fetus, if it may cause fetal harm, or affect fertility. Limited data show that administration of tryptophan during pregnancy may be associated with altered fetal breathing activity. Because adequate, well-controlled studies are lacking, tryptophan should be used with caution during pregnancy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use tryptophan with caution during breast-feeding. While tryptophan is a normal component of human milk, there are no studies on the effects of exogenous administration during breast-feeding. However, limited data have shown that tryptophan administration has no effect on levels of tryptophan in human milk. L-tryptophan may be used by some individuals to improve sleep and reduce depressed mood during the postpartum period.

    General Information
    There are no adequate or well-controlled studies on the use of tryptophan during pregnancy. It is unknown if tryptophan has the potential to be transferred to the fetus, if it may cause fetal harm, or affect fertility. Limited data show that administration of tryptophan during pregnancy may be associated with altered fetal breathing activity. Because adequate, well-controlled studies are lacking, tryptophan should be used with caution during pregnancy.
  Breastfeeding
    General Information
    Use tryptophan with caution during breast-feeding. While tryptophan is a normal component of human milk, there are no studies on the effects of exogenous administration during breast-feeding. However, limited data have shown that tryptophan administration has no effect on levels of tryptophan in human milk. L-tryptophan may be used by some individuals to improve sleep and reduce depressed mood during the postpartum period.

Almotriptan: (Contraindicated) Combining medications that potentiate serotonin neurotransmission, such as serotonin-receptor agonists and tryptophan, could result in serotonin syndrome. Serotonin syndrome, while uncommon, can be serious and consists of symptoms such as mental status changes, diaphoresis, tremor, myoclonus, hyperreflexia, and fever.
Amitriptyline: (Major) The concomitant use of tryptophan with tricyclic antidepressants should be avoided. Since tryptophan is converted to serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine), the use of tryptophan in patients receiving drugs with serotonergic activity could lead to serotonin syndrome. Serotonin syndrome is characterized by rapid development of hyperthermia, hypertension, myoclonus, rigidity, autonomic instability, mental status changes (e.g., delirium or coma), and in rare cases, death. Serotonin syndrome, in its most severe form, can resemble neuroleptic malignant syndrome. If serotonin syndrome is suspected, the tricyclic antidepressant and concurrent serotonergic agents should be discontinued.

Since tryptophan is converted to serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine), the use of tryptophan in patients receiving venlafaxine could lead to serotonin excess and serotonin syndrome. Serotonin syndrome is characterized by rapid development of hyperthermia, hypertension, myoclonus, rigidity, autonomic instability, mental status changes (e.g., delirium or coma), and in rare cases, death. Serotonin syndrome, in its most severe form, can resemble neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
Vilazodone: (Major) Since tryptophan is converted to serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine), the use of tryptophan in patients receiving vilazodone could lead to serotonin syndrome. This combination is not recommended. Serotonin syndrome is characterized by rapid development of hyperthermia, hypertension, myoclonus, rigidity, autonomic instability, mental status changes (e.g., delirium or coma), and in rare cases, death. Serotonin syndrome, in its most severe form, can resemble neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
Vortioxetine: (Major) The concomitant use of tryptophan with vortioxetine should be avoided if possible. Since tryptophan is converted to serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine), the use of tryptophan in patients receiving vortioxetine could lead to serotonin syndrome. Serotonin syndrome is characterized by rapid development of hyperthermia, hypertension, myoclonus, rigidity, autonomic instability, mental status changes (e.g., delirium or coma), and in rare cases, death. Serotonin syndrome, in its most severe form, can resemble neuroleptic malignant syndrome. If serotonin syndrome is suspected, vortioxetine and concurrent serotonergic agents should be discontinued.
ZOLMitriptan: (Contraindicated) Combining medications that potentiate serotonin neurotransmission, such as serotonin-receptor agonists and tryptophan, could result in serotonin syndrome. Serotonin syndrome, while uncommon, can be serious and consists of symptoms such as mental status changes, diaphoresis, tremor, myoclonus, hyperreflexia, and fever.

    In the late 1980s, a severe adverse reaction known as eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome (EMS) was linked to the use of L-tryptophan supplements; some cases were fatal. EMS is rare, but affects multiple organ systems of the body including the muscles, skin, and lungs. The onset of the disorder is sudden and common symptoms include muscle pain or cramps (myalgia), muscle weakness (myasthenia), skin rash, dyspnea, and fatigue. Elevated eosinophils are present. With tryptophan supplements, EMS was ultimately attributed to one or more contaminants in bulk tryptophan powder from a Japanese manufacturer that was incorporated into marketed products. As a result, in 1989, the FDA issued a nationwide recall and ban of products containing tryptophan either as the sole or the major ingredient. In August 1998, researchers at the Mayo Clinic in Minnesota reported that the contaminant, known as "peak X", had been found in 5-HTP products still available on the market. Consumers should be counseled that care must be taken in the selection and consumption of dietary supplement products, which do not need to adhere to the same standards as FDA-approved drugs, including purity and potency standards, prior to marketing.

Description
  Text
  Tryptophan is an essential amino acid in the human diet and is found naturally in turkey, milk, fish, and many other protein-rich foods. Products are sold as tryptophan, L-tryptophan and 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP). 5-HTP is the molecular precursor for serotonin. Because serotonin is a neurotransmitter with complex pharmacology, tryptophan has been claimed to be useful for depressive symptoms, insomnia, and various mood disorders and may also be promoted to support weight management. There are limited data to support the supplement for any of these uses. Tryptophan is listed in the United States Pharmacopeia (USP) and is 'Generally Recognized As Safe' (GRAS) by the FDA.
 
NOTE: In the U.S., herbal products and dietary supplements are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for dietary supplements as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since supplements are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for these products and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Tryptophan is first converted to 5-hydroxytryptophan, then to 5-hydroxytryptamine (serotonin). The accumulation of serotonin systemically and within the central nervous system has a wide variety of complex actions. Fluctuations in serotonin levels have been linked to migraine headaches. Serotonin is known to produce vasoconstriction in many tissues and is important to platelet function. The neuropharmacology of serotonin is exceedingly complex but is known to be important for emotional health and sleep behaviors, and may play a role in the regulation of weight and appetite.
