  General Dosing Information
As with most antiepileptic drugs, withdraw zonisamide gradually because of the risk of increased seizure frequency and status epilepticus.
In the event of a serious adverse reaction, rapid discontinuation can be considered. In these situations, appropriate monitoring is recommended.

Contraindications And Precautions

  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Abrupt discontinuation of zonisamide therapy should not be undertaken. Zonisamide and other antiepileptic drugs should be withdrawn gradually to minimize the potential of increased seizure frequency.
 
Zonisamide is a sulfonamide and is contraindicated in patients with a history of sulfonamide hypersensitivity. Fatalities have occurred, although rarely, due to severe reactions to sulfonamides. Sensitization may recur when a sulfonamide is re-administered irrespective of the route of administration. If signs of serious reactions or hypersensitivity occur, zonisamide should be discontinued. Additionally, zonisamide should not be used in patients with carbonic anhydrase inhibitor hypersensitivity.
 
In January 2008, the FDA alerted healthcare professionals of an increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior in patients receiving anticonvulsants to treat epilepsy, psychiatric disorders, or other conditions (e.g., migraine, neuropathic pain). This alert followed an initial request by the FDA in March 2005 for manufacturers of marketed anticonvulsants to provide data from existing controlled clinical trials for analysis. Prior to this request, preliminary evidence had suggested a possible link between anticonvulsant use and suicidality. The primary analysis consisted of 199 placebo-controlled clinical studies with a total of 27,863 patients in drug treatment groups and 16,029 patients in placebo groups (>= 5 years of age). There were 4 completed suicides among patients in drug treatment groups versus none in the placebo groups. Patients receiving anticonvulsants had approximately twice the risk of suicidal behavior or ideation (0.43%) as patients receiving placebo (0.24%), corresponding to an estimated 2.1 per 1000 (95% CI: 0.7—4.2) more patients in the drug treatment groups who experienced suicidal behavior or ideation. The relative risk for suicidality was higher in patients with epilepsy compared to those with other conditions; however, the absolute risk differences were similar in trials for epilepsy and psychiatric indications. Age was not a determining factor. The increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior was observed between 1 and 24 weeks after therapy initiation. However, a longer duration of therapy should not preclude the possibility of an association to the drug since most studies included in the analysis did not continue beyond 24 weeks. Data were analyzed from drugs with adequately designed clinical trials including carbamazepine, felbamate, gabapentin, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine, pregabalin, tiagabine, topiramate, valproate, and zonisamide. However, this is considered to be a class effect. All patients beginning treatment with anticonvulsants or currently receiving such treatment should be closely monitored for emerging or worsening depression or suicidal thoughts/behavior. Patients and caregivers should be informed of the increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors and should be advised to immediately report the emergence or worsening of depression, the emergence of suicidal thoughts or behavior, thoughts of self-harm, or other unusual changes in mood or behavior. Anticonvulsants should be prescribed in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management in order to reduce the risk of overdose.
 
Zonisamide should be used cautiously in patients with hepatic disease. The drug is hepatically metabolized by cytochrome P450 enzymes.
 
Metabolic acidosis has occurred during use of zonisamide in some patients. Zonisamide-induced metabolic acidosis generally develops early during treatment and appears to be more common and severe in pediatric patients (see Adverse Reactions). The condition is more frequently associated with higher doses, but can occur at doses as low as 25 mg/day. Adverse effects on fetal development may occur in pregnant women with metabolic acidosis. The exact effects of zonisamide-induced metabolic acidosis on pregnant women, developing fetuses, or nursing infants are unknown. Conditions that may predispose patients to acidosis include diarrhea, ketogenic diet, severe pulmonary disease (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), emphysema, status asthmaticus), surgery, status epilepticus, kidney disease, or administration with other bicarbonate-lowering drugs. Measurement of baseline serum bicarbonate is recommended followed by periodic assessments during zonisamide therapy, even in the absence of symptoms. If metabolic acidosis occurs, consideration should be given to reducing the dose or discontinuing the drug. Alkali treatment (i.e., oral sodium bicarbonate) should be considered if zonisamide is continued in the presence of metabolic acidosis. Patients undergoing surgery who are being treated with zonisamide should have a careful history taken preoperatively. It is advisable to measure blood chemistries before surgery to screen for asymptomatic metabolic acidosis.
 
Zonisamide is classified as FDA pregnancy category C. Women of child bearing potential should be advised to use effective contraception while receiving zonisamide. Zonisamide has been associated with metabolic acidosis, a condition that may negatively affect fetal development (e.g., decreased fetal growth, decreased fetal oxygenation, or fetal death) and fetal tolerability to labor. The exact effect of zonisamide-induced metabolic acidosis on developing fetuses is unknown. Zonisamide showed teratogenesis when administered during the period of organogenesis in mice, rats, and dogs. It was also embryolethal in monkeys during this period. A variety of fetal abnormalities were observed in these animals at maternal plasma concentrations of zonisamide similar to or lower than therapeutic levels in humans. These findings suggest that zonisamide may present a significant risk to the fetus. Therefore, zonisamide should be used during pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus. Physicians are advised to recommend that pregnant patients receiving zonisamide enroll in the North American Antiepileptic Drug (NAAED) Pregnancy Registry to provide information about the effects of in utero exposure to the drug. Patients must call 1-888-233-2334 to enroll in the registry.
 
Metabolic acidosis has occurred during use of zonisamide in some patients; however, the exact effect of zonisamide-induced metabolic acidosis on nursing infants is unknown. It is not known whether zonisamide is excreted in human milk; however, significant secretion is possible. Because many drugs are excreted in human milk and because of the potential for adverse reactions in nursing infants from zonisamide, use of zonisamide during breast-feeding should be done cautiously, taking into account the importance of breast-feeding to the infant and the importance of the drug to the mother.
 
The safe and effective use of zonisamide in children under age 16 has not been established, although off label usage has been reported. Zonisamide has been associated with metabolic acidosis, a condition that may cause hyperventilation, fatigue, anorexia, cardiac arrhythmias, stupor, or unconsciousness. Zonisamide-induced metabolic acidosis generally develops early during treatment and appears to be more common and severe in children (see Adverse Reactions). Conditions that may predispose patients to acidosis include diarrhea, ketogenic diet, severe pulmonary disease (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), status asthmaticus), surgery, status epilepticus, kidney disease, or administration with other bicarbonate-lowering drugs. Measurement of baseline serum bicarbonate is recommended followed by periodic assessments during zonisamide therapy, even in the absence of symptoms. If metabolic acidosis occurs, consideration should be given to reducing the dose or discontinuing the drug. Alkali treatment (i.e., oral sodium bicarbonate) should be considered if zonisamide is continued in the presence of metabolic acidosis. Patients undergoing surgery who are being treated with zonisamide should have a careful history taken preoperatively. It is advisable to measure blood chemistries before surgery to screen for asymptomatic metabolic acidosis. Cases of oligohidrosis, hyperthermia and heat stroke have been reported (mainly in children 1.6 to 17 years of age) during clinical marketing of zonisamide. All patients with dehydration, hypovolemia or other predisposing factors to heat intolerance should have their condition corrected before using zonisamide. All patients, especially children, should be instructed to limit exposure to ambient temperature increase (i.e., elevated environmental temperatures) or other extremes that might aggravate temperature regulation. The manufacturer reports that the concurrent use of drugs that also predispose patients to heat intolerance (e.g., anticholinergic medications) should be used cautiously with zonisamide.
 
Zonisamide should be used cautiously in patients with renal impairment or renal disease. Zonisamide has been associated with metabolic acidosis and patients with renal disease may be at greater risk for this complication. Zonisamide is excreted by the kidneys after the drug is metabolized. Further, statistically significant increases (8%) from baseline in serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were reported. These increases persisted over time but were not progressive which was interpreted as a decrease in glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Decreases in GFR appeared within the first 4 weeks of treatment and returned to baseline within 2—3 weeks of drug discontinuation. It is not known if these decreases in GFR are reversible following long-term use of zonisamide. Zonisamide should be discontinued in patients who develop acute renal failure or a clinically significant sustained increase in the creatinine/BUN concentration. Although there were no episodes of unexplained acute renal failure in clinical development in the US, Europe, or Japan, zonisamide should not be used in patients with renal failure (estimated GFR < 50 ml/min) as there has been insufficient experience concerning drug dosing and toxicity. Zonisamide was also associated with nephrolithiasis in US clinical trials. In patients with predisposing risk factors, increased fluid intake and urine output may help reduce the risk of developing nephrolithiasis. Some patients who have developed nephrolithiasis while receiving zonisamide have passed the stone(s) and were able to continue on the drug. The stones that were analyzed consisted of calcium or urate salts. Nephrolithiasis was not reported in Japan where the drug has been marketed since 1989.
 
Zonisamide commonly causes somnolence, fatigue, dizziness and difficulty with concentration, particularly in the first month of therapy. Patients should be advised to use caution when driving or operating machinery, or performing other tasks that require mental alertness until they are aware of whether zonisamide adversely affects their mental and/or motor performance.

    mood disorder, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Monitor for worsening of depression, suicidal thoughts or behavior, or any unusual changes in behavior in individuals with mood disorder or suicidal ideation; antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), including zonisamide, increase the risk of suicidal thoughts or behavior in individuals taking these medications for any indication. Suicidal behavior and ideation may also occur after zonisamide discontinuation. When considering AED use, balance the risk of suicidal thoughts or behaviors with the risk of untreated illness. The relative risk for suicidal thoughts or behavior was higher in clinical trials in subjects with epilepsy than in clinical trials in subjects with psychiatric or other conditions, but the absolute risk differences were similar for the epilepsy and psychiatric indications. Epilepsy and many other illnesses for which AEDs are prescribed are themselves associated with morbidity and mortality and an increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behavior. If suicidal thoughts and behavior emerge during treatment, consider whether the emergence of these symptoms may be related to the illness being treated.

    hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction, inborn errors of metabolism
    General Information
    Hyperammonemia and encephalopathy have been reported with zonisamide use. Individuals with inborn errors of metabolism or hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction may be at an increased risk for hyperammonemia with or without encephalopathy, and this risk may be increased by zonisamide use. Additionally, the risks of hyperammonemia and various manifestations of encephalopathy may be increased in individuals treated with zonisamide and concomitantly receiving other medications that can cause hyperammonemia.

    General Information
    Use zonisamide with caution in individuals with renal impairment or renal failure. Because zonisamide is excreted by the kidneys, slower titration and more frequent monitoring may be needed in individuals with renal disease.     Headline
    diarrhea, ketogenic diet, pulmonary disease, status epilepticus
    General Information
    Zonisamide causes hyperchloremic, non-anion gap, metabolic acidosis. Conditions or therapies that predispose to acidosis, such as renal disease, severe pulmonary disease, status epilepticus, diarrhea, ketogenic diet, or specific drugs, may be additive to the bicarbonate lowering effects of zonisamide. Metabolic acidosis generally appears to be dose-dependent and can occur at doses as low as 25 mg/day. Measurement of baseline and periodic serum bicarbonate during zonisamide treatment is recommended.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature, infants, neonates
    General Information
    Neonates, infants, children, and adolescents appear to be at an increased risk for zonisamide-associated oligohidrosis and hyperthermia. Monitor individuals, especially pediatric populations, treated with zonisamide closely for evidence of decreased sweating or increased body temperature, especially in conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature, such as warm or hot weather. Use caution when zonisamide is prescribed with other medications that predispose individuals to heat-related disorders; these drugs include, but are not limited to, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors and anticholinergic medications. Additionally, zonisamide commonly causes metabolic acidosis in pediatric populations. Chronic untreated metabolic acidosis in pediatric populations may cause nephrolithiasis and/or nephrocalcinosis, osteoporosis and/or osteomalacia (potentially resulting in rickets), and may reduce growth rates. A reduction in growth rate may eventually decrease the maximal height achieved. The effect of zonisamide on growth and bone-related sequelae has not been systematically investigated.

    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Use cautious dose selection for zonisamide in geriatric adults, usually starting at the low end of the dosing range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function, and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy. According to the Beers Criteria, anticonvulsants are considered potentially inappropriate medications in geriatric adults with a history of falls or fractures; avoid in at-risk individuals, except for treating seizure and mood disorders, since anticonvulsants can produce ataxia, impaired psychomotor function, syncope, and additional falls. If zonisamide must be used, consider reducing the use of other CNS-active medications that increase the risk of falls and fractures and implement strategies to reduce fall risk.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on the use of zonisamide during human pregnancy are limited. Based on animal data, zonisamide may cause fetal harm. Data from the North American Antiepileptic Drug (NAAED) Pregnancy Registry have not identified a drug-associated risk of major birth defects (1.4%) in over 200 first trimester pregnancies exposed to zonisamide monotherapy. In contrast, data from the United Kingdom and Ireland Epilepsy Pregnancy Registry (UKIEPR) reported an increased rate of major birth defects (13%) in 26 first trimester pregnancies exposed to zonisamide monotherapy. Both registry studies have methodological limitations (e.g., small sample size, selection bias, inability to account for potential confounders). Both registries have reported increased rates of small for gestational age infants in zonisamide-exposed pregnancies compared to lamotrigine-exposed pregnancies and the unexposed general population, which may be associated with zonisamide-induced metabolic acidosis. Although there are no reports of metabolic acidosis with zonisamide use in pregnancy, metabolic acidosis is associated with decreased fetal growth, decreased fetal oxygenation, fetal death, and impaired ability of the fetus to tolerate labor. Monitor patients for metabolic acidosis during pregnancy and manage as in non-pregnant patients. In animal studies, zonisamide produced fetal malformations in multiple species and embryo-fetal death in monkeys and perinatal death in rats at maternal plasma concentrations similar to or lower than therapeutic concentrations in humans. Physiological changes during pregnancy may decrease zonisamide concentrations and reduce therapeutic effect; dose adjustments may be necessary. Pregnant patients taking zonisamide are encouraged to enroll in the NAAED Pregnancy Registry at www.aedpregnancyregistry.org or by calling 1-888-233-2334.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with zonisamide. Zonisamide may cause fetal harm. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking zonisamide and for 1 month after the last dose. Based on animal data, zonisamide may impair female fertility.

    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information

    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use zonisamide with caution during breast-feeding. Zonisamide is present in human milk with a milk-to-plasma ratio of 0.7 and 0.9. There are no data on the effects of zonisamide on the breastfed child or on milk production. Because zonisamide has been associated with metabolic acidosis and hyperthermia, monitor breastfed children for poor feeding, weight loss, excess sedation, decreased muscle tone, and elevated temperature. In a case report of an individual receiving zonisamide 300 mg/day while breast-feeding, the mean milk-to-plasma concentration ratio was 0.93 +/- 0.09 (range: 0.81 to 1.03) paired milk and plasma samples collected between days 3 and 30 postpartum, drawn 1.5 to 2.5 hours after zonisamide ingestion. No behavioral problems were observed in the breastfed infant. In a separate case report, an individual receiving zonisamide 400 mg/day, carbamazepine 1,000 mg/day, and clonazepam 1 mg/day throughout pregnancy and postpartum had zonisamide concentrations of 15.7 mcg/mL in serum and 14.4 mcg/mL in cord blood at delivery, representing a placental transfer rate of 92%. The zonisamide dose appeared unchanged during breast-feeding, and the infant's plasma zonisamide concentration declined from a concentration approximating that of the lactating individual at birth to 3.9 mcg/mL by day 24.

acetaZOLAMIDE: (Moderate) Monitor for the appearance or worsening of metabolic acidosis if zonisamide is given concomitantly with other carbonic anhydrase inhibitors. Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Since zonisamide is a sulfonamide and can be absorbed systemically, it can produce severe, possibly fatal, reactions such as toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS), fulminant hepatic necrosis, agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia and other blood dyscrasias. Once sensitization to sulfonamides has occurred, a recurrence can be precipitated by administration of sulfonamides by any route. The use of zonisamide should be discontinued if there is any sign of these reactions or a hypersensitivity reaction. In US and European randomized controlled trials, about 2.2% of zonisamide-treated patients discontinued treatment because of rash (unspecified) compared to none on placebo. Rash usually developed early in treatment, with 85% reported within 16 weeks in the US and European studies and 90% reported within two weeks in the Japanese studies. There was no apparent relationship between the dose and the occurrence of rash. Seven deaths from severe rash (i.e., SJS and TEN) were reported in the first 11 years of marketing in Japan. All of the patients were receiving other drugs concomitantly with zonisamide. In post-marketing reports in Japan, a total of 49 cases of SJS or TEN have been reported. There were no confirmed cases of SJS or TEN in the US, European, or Japanese development programs. Two confirmed cases of aplastic anemia and one confirmed case of agranulocytosis were reported in the first 11 years of use in Japan. There were no cases of aplastic anemia and two confirmed cases of agranulocytosis in the U.S., European, or Japanese development programs. There is inadequate information to assess the relationship, if any, between dose and duration of treatment and these events.
 
Anticonvulsants are thought to carry an increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior. An analysis by the FDA of previously gathered drug data showed that patients receiving anticonvulsants had approximately twice the risk of suicidal behavior or ideation (0.43%) as patients receiving placebo (0.24%). The relative risk for suicidality was higher in patients with epilepsy compared to those with other conditions. Age was not a determining factor. The increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior occurred between 1 and 24 weeks after therapy initiation. However, a longer duration of therapy should not preclude the possibility of an association to the drug since most studies included in the analysis did not continue beyond 24 weeks. During placebo-controlled trials with zonisamide, suicidal ideation was among the most frequently (>= 2% of patients) reported central nervous system (CNS) adverse reactions in zonisamide-treated patients. Suicide attempts were reported in 1.1% of zonisamide patients and 0.4% of placebo patients. During placebo-controlled trials with zonisamide, approximately 2.2% of zonisamide-treated patients discontinued therapy or were hospitalized for depression compared to 0.4% of placebo patients. Among all zonisamide-treated epilepsy patients, 1.4% were taken off zonisamide therapy and 1% were hospitalized because of reported depression or suicide attempts. All patients beginning treatment with anticonvulsants or currently receiving such treatment should be closely monitored for emerging or worsening suicidal thoughts/behavior or depression. Patients and caregivers should be informed of the increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors and should be advised to immediately report the emergence or worsening of depression, the emergence of suicidal thoughts or behavior, thoughts of self-harm, or other unusual changes in mood or behavior.
 
Metabolic acidosis has occurred during zonisamide treatment in some patients. Zonisamide-induced metabolic acidosis generally develops early during treatment and appears to be more common and severe in children. The condition is more frequently associated with higher doses, but can occur at doses as low as 25 mg/day. Signs and symptoms may include hyperventilation, fatigue, anorexia, cardiac arrhythmias, stupor, or unconsciousness; however some patients may be asymptomatic. Decreases in serum bicarbonate concentrations in adults have primarily been mild to moderate (i.e., average 2 mEq/L), although decreases as much as 10 mEq/L below baseline have occurred. During studies of epilepsy or migraine prophylaxis in adults, persistent decreases in serum bicarbonate to less than 20 mEq/L occurred in 21% and 43% of patients treated with zonisamide 25 mg/day and 300 mg/day, respectively; there was a 2% or less incidence of persistent abnormally low serum bicarbonate for all doses studied. However, in children 3—16 years of age with partial epilepsy, up to 90% experienced persistent decreases in serum bicarbonate to concentrations less than 20 mEq/L. The incidence of persistently low serum bicarbonate concentrations less than 17 mEq/L and with more than a 5 mEq/L decrease from baseline values of at least 20 mEq/L was as high as 18%.
 
Statistically significant increases (8%) from baseline in serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were reported. These increases persisted over time but were not progressive which was interpreted as a decrease in glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Decreases in GFR appeared within the first 4 weeks of treatment and returned to baseline within 2—3 weeks of drug discontinuation. It is not known if these decreases in GFR are reversible following long-term use of zonisamide. Although there were no episodes of unexplained acute renal failure in clinical development in the US, Europe, or Japan, zonisamide should be discontinued in patients who develop acute renal failure or a clinically significant sustained increase in the creatinine/BUN concentration (azotemia).

Decreases in GFR appeared within the first 4 weeks of zonisamide treatment and returned to baseline within 2 to 3 weeks of drug discontinuation. It is not known if these decreases in GFR are reversible after long-term use of zonisamide. Although there were no episodes of unexplained acute renal failure in clinical development in the US., Europe, or Japan, discontinue zonisamide in individuals who develop acute renal failure (unspecified) or a clinically significant sustained increase in the creatinine/BUN concentration (azotemia). Consider monitoring renal function periodically during zonisamide therapy.

    metabolic acidosis, nephrolithiasis, osteomalacia
    General Information
    As a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, zonisamide may cause metabolic acidosis. Zonisamide-induced metabolic acidosis generally develops early during treatment and appears. The condition is more frequently associated with higher doses, but can occur at doses as low as 25 mg/day. Signs and symptoms may include hyperventilation, fatigue, anorexia, cardiac arrhythmias, stupor, or unconsciousness; however some individuals may be asymptomatic. Decreases in serum bicarbonate concentrations in adults have primarily been mild to moderate (i.e., average 2 mEq/L), although decreases as much as 10 mEq/L below baseline have occurred. During studies of epilepsy in adults, persistent decreases in serum bicarbonate to less than 20 mEq/L occurred in 21% of subjects treated with zonisamide 25 mg/day or 100 mg/day, and 43% of subjects treated with 300 mg/day. In a placebo-controlled trial for prophylaxis of migraine, the incidence of a persistent treatment-emergent decrease in serum bicarbonate was 7% for subjects given placebo, 29% for subjects treated with 150 mg/day, and 34% for subjects treated with 300 mg/day. There was a 2% or less incidence of persistent abnormally low serum bicarbonate for all doses studied in these trials. In an open label trial of zonisamide as adjunct treatment of epilepsy in pediatric subjects 3 to 16 years of age, mean serum bicarbonate reductions ranged from about 2 to 4 mEq/L. In the same study, 52% of pediatric subjects who received doses up to 100 mg/day and up to 90% of those who received a wider range of doses up to 600 mg/day experienced persistent decreases in serum bicarbonate to concentrations less than 20 mEq/L. The incidence of persistently low serum bicarbonate concentrations less than 17 mEq/L and with more than a 5 mEq/L decrease from baseline values of at least 20 mEq/L was as high as 18%. Chronic, untreated metabolic acidosis may increase the risk for nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis, osteomalacia, and osteoporosis. During clinical development of zonisamide, possible or confirmed nephrolithiasis was reported in 4% of adults; the diagnosis was confirmed by passage of a stone or definitive sonographic detection in about 25% of these subjects. The stones consisted of calcium or urate salts. Nephrolithiasis was reported as an adverse effect in 3% of pediatric subjects and was detected by renal ultrasound in 8% of pediatric subjects who had at least 1 ultrasound performed prospectively during open-label treatment. In general, increased fluid intake and urine output can help reduce the risk of stone formation, particularly in those with predisposing risk factors, although it is uncertain if these measures reduce the risk of stone formation during zonisamide treatment. Zonisamide has also been associated with decreases in serum phosphorus and increases in serum alkaline phosphatase. These changes may be related to metabolic acidosis and subsequent osteomalacia. Chronic, untreated metabolic acidosis may be associated with reduced growth rates in children; however, the effects of zonisamide on growth rates in children have not been systematically evaluated. Baseline and periodic serum bicarbonate measurements are recommended during zonisamide treatment. If metabolic acidosis develops and persists, a reduction in dose or discontinuation of treatment may be necessary. If the decision is made to continue individuals on zonisamide in the face of persistent acidosis, consider alkali treatment.

Description
  Text
  Zonisamide is a sulfonamide anticonvulsant. It is a derivative of a series of compounds known as benzisoxazoles. The pharmacological profile is similar to that of carbamazepine and phenytoin; however, no comparative double-blind trials have been published. Zonisamide showed a significant reduction in seizure frequency as compared to placebo. No less than 28% of patients achieved a >= 50% reduction in seizure frequency during three multicenter, placebo-controlled, double blind, 3-month clinical trials (two in the US, one in Europe). Zonisamide appears to possess more than one mechanism of action which may make it effective in a broader range of seizures. Central nervous system adverse reactions (e.g., ataxia, psychosis, speech impairment, etc.) are common and can limit the usefulness of the drug in some patients. Zonisamide is approved for adjunctive treatment of partial seizures in adults 16 years and older with epilepsy. Although not approved for use in children in the US, the drug is often used 'off-label' in children; the drug is approved for use in children in Japan where it has been marketed since 1989. FDA approval for zonisamide was granted in March 2000.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The exact mechanism(s) by which zonisamide exerts its anticonvulsant effect is unknown. It appears that zonisamide exhibits a dual mechanism of action as an anticonvulsant. Zonisamide a) stops the spread of seizures and b) suppresses their focus. The drug may produce these effects by acting at sodium and calcium channels. In vitro pharmacological studies suggest that zonisamide blocks sodium channels and reduces voltage-dependent, transient inward currents (T-type calcium currents), thereby stabilizing neuronal membranes and suppressing neuronal hypersynchronization. It does not affect GABA pathways. Zonisamide also has weak carbonic anhydrase inhibiting activity, but this pharmacologic effect is not thought to be a major contributing factor in the antiseizure activity of zonisamide. It should be noted, however, that the carbonic anhydrase inhibiting effects of zonisamide may cause metabolic acidosis through renal bicarbonate loss (see Adverse Reactions). Augmentation of dopaminergic and serotonergic transmission occurs; however the clinical significance is unknown. In animal models, zonisamide has a profile of activity similar to carbamazepine and phenytoin and is more active against the tonic phase than the clonic phase.

  Text
  Zonisamide is administered orally. Once in the systemic circulation, it is extensively bound to erythrocytes, resulting in an 8-fold higher concentration in red blood cells (RBC) than in plasma. Zonisamide is approximately 40% bound to human plasma proteins. The apparent volume of distribution (V/F) of zonisamide is about 1.45 L/kg after a 400 mg oral dose. Protein binding is unaffected in the presence of therapeutic concentrations of carbamazepine, phenobarbital, or phenytoin. Dose proportional pharmacokinetics are observed in the range of 200 to 400 mg, but the Cmax and AUC increase disproportionately at 800 mg, possibly due to saturable binding to RBC. Once a stable dose is reached, steady state is achieved within 14 days. Zonisamide is metabolized by N-acetyl-transferases to form N-acetyl zonisamide and by CYP3A4 to form 2-sulfamoylacetylphenol (SMAP). Zonisamide is excreted primarily in the urine as parent drug and as the glucuronide of SMAP. After multiple dosing, approximately 62% and 3% of a dose were recovered in urine and feces, respectively, by day 10. Of the excreted dose, 35% was recovered as zonisamide, 15% as N-acetyl zonisamide, and 50% as the glucuronide of SMAP. Plasma clearance of zonisamide is approximately 0.3 to 0.35 mL/minute/kg in patients not receiving enzyme-inducing antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) and 0.5 mL/minute/kg in patients concurrently receiving enzyme-inducing AEDs. After single dose administration, renal clearance of zonisamide is approximately 3.5 mL/minute. The elimination half-life of zonisamide in plasma is approximately 63 hours. The elimination half-life of zonisamide in RBC is approximately 105 hours.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4, P-gp, UGT
Zonisamide is metabolized by acetylation and reduction. It undergoes acetylation to form N-acetyl zonisamide and reduction to form 2-sulfamoylacetyl phenol (SMAP), an open ring metabolite. Reduction to SMAP is mediated by the hepatic cytochrome P450 3A4 isozyme. Zonisamide is also metabolized by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT). In vitro data suggest that it does not inhibit or induce cytochrome P450 enzymes or UGT and is not likely to interfere with the hepatic metabolic clearance of drugs metabolized by these enzyme systems. Zonisamide does not induce its own metabolism. Zonisamide is a weak inhibitor of P-gp.
