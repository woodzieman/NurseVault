Drug Class: Heparin is a parenteral anticoagulant widely used in clinical medicine.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: Maintain the aPTT below levels that correlate with heparin concentrations of 0.4 units/mL (by protamine titration) or an anti-factor Xa concentration of 0.7 units/mL.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): adrenocortical insufficiency, anemia, bleeding, epistaxis, gi bleeding, hematemesis, hematoma, hematuria, hyphema, ocular hemorrhage, petechiae, prolonged bleeding time, purpura, retroperitoneal bleeding, vaginal bleeding, thrombocytopenia, thromboembolism, thrombosis, anaphylactic shock, anaphylactoid reactions, chills, fever, headache, lacrimation, pruritus, rhinitis, urticaria, alopecia, erythema, hypervolemia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Heparin in contraindicated in patients with heparin hypersensitivity and in patients with hypersensitivity to any component of the specific product formulation. Some heparin preparations contain sulfites, which can induce a life-threatening allergic response in some patients. Administer using a controlled infusion device. Do not admix with other drugs.

Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  Converting to Oral Anticoagulants
When converting from heparin to warfarin, continue therapeutic heparin therapy for several days after warfarin initiation until the INR (prothrombin time) is within a stable therapeutic range. At that point, heparin may be discontinued without tapering. NOTE: Heparin may prolong the one-stage prothrombin time. When heparin is given with warfarin, prothrombin time is most accurate at least 5 hours after the last IV dose or 24 hours after the last subcutaneous dose of heparin.
When converting from heparin to oral anticoagulants other than warfarin, stop the heparin infusion immediately after administering the first dose of oral anticoagulant. If the patient is receiving intermittent IV heparin, discontinue heparin and administer the oral anticoagulant 0 to 2 hours before the time that the next dose of heparin would have been administered.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Heparin in contraindicated in patients with heparin hypersensitivity and in patients with hypersensitivity to any component of the specific product formulation. Some heparin preparations contain sulfites, which can induce a life-threatening allergic response in some patients. Such heparin products should be used with extreme caution in patients with known sulfite hypersensitivity; sulfite sensitivity reactions tend to occur more frequently in patients with asthma. Heparin should not be used in patients with bovine protein hypersensitivity or porcine protein hypersensitivity. Some heparin preparations contain benzyl alcohol as a preservative; these formulations should be avoided in patients with benzyl alcohol hypersensitivity and in neonates. In February 2008, the FDA issued a MedWatch regarding an increase in the reports of serious adverse events including allergic or hypersensitivity-type reactions with the administration of higher bolus doses (5000—50,000 units). Symptoms include oral swelling, nausea/vomiting, diaphoresis, dyspnea, and cases of severe drops in blood pressure. Contaminants found in raw heparin imported from China are believed to be associated with these adverse events. In October 2009, the USP adopted new reference standards and manufacturing controls that will determine the potency of heparin and, unlike the previous testing method, detect contaminants. This new testing standard will match the World Health Organization's International Standard unit dose definition but will result in a 10% reduction in the potency of heparin in the US. Most manufacturers will place an "N" next to the lot number to differentiate heparin tested with the new USP reference standard (i.e., lower potency heparin). This change in heparin potency may have clinical significance in situations where loading doses are administered and an immediate anticoagulant effect is needed. The change in heparin potency is expected to be less clinically significant when administered subcutaneously because of the low and highly variable bioavailability of heparin when administered by this route. Healthcare providers should consider the change in heparin potency when deciding what dose to administer; higher doses may be required to achieve and maintain the desired anticoagulant effect in some patients. The FDA is working with heparin manufacturers to study the impact of this variation in potency.
 
Heparin should be used cautiously, if at all, in patients with a history of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and is contraindicated in patients with severe thrombocytopenia. Heparin may be used safely in patients with mild nonimmune thrombocytopenia (platelets > 100,000/mm3), which may remain stable or reverse with continued treatment. However, an alternative anticoagulant should be used in patients with a history of immune-mediated heparin-induced thrombocytopenia since these patients can develop thrombotic complications during heparin therapy (see Adverse Reactions). Nevertheless, since it may not be possible to differentiate asymptomatic, nonimmune thrombocytopenia from immune-mediated thrombocytopenia, the platelet count should be monitored closely. If the platelet count falls below 100,000/mm3 or recurrent thrombosis develops, heparin should be discontinued. Thrombosis in association with thrombocytopenia may indicate white-clot syndrome, which can lead to severe thromboembolic complications. HIT can occur up to several weeks after the discontinuation of heparin therapy, and patients presenting with thrombocytopenia or thrombosis after heparin discontinuation should also be evaluated for HIT.
 
Heparin is contraindicated in patients with uncontrollable bleeding (with the exception of bleeding associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation). Hemorrhage can occur at virtually any site in patients receiving heparin. Blood coagulation tests should be performed before and regularly during therapy. An unexplained fall in hematocrit, hypotension, or any other unexplained symptom should lead to serious consideration of a hemorrhagic event. Heparin should be used with extreme caution in a patient with conditions that might increase the risk of hemorrhage. These may include infective endocarditis; dissecting aortic aneurysm; peptic ulcer disease; diverticulitis; inflammatory bowel disease; coagulopathy; hemophilia; thrombocytopenia; menstruation; threatened abortion or other abnormal vaginal bleeding; severe hypertension; head trauma; major surgery or trauma, especially involving eye, brain, or spinal cord; during or immediately following a lumbar puncture or spinal anesthesia; tube drainage of stomach or small intestine; and increased capillary permeability. Heparin is frequently given immediately following thrombolytic therapy. Despite the potential drug-drug interaction between thrombolytics and agents used for oral anticoagulant therapy, heparin is frequently administered in combination with these agents (see Drug Interactions). Patients should be monitored closely for easy bruising or petechiae, which can precede signs of hemorrhage such as nosebleed, hematuria, or tarry stools.
 
In patients with acute intracranial bleeding, the American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) recommends the initial use of pneumatic compression devices for prevention of DVT and PE. In stable patients, low-dose subcutaneous heparin may be initiated as soon as the second day after the onset of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). This represents a Grade 2C recommendation by ACCP, and assumes a relatively low degree of risk aversion.
 
Increased resistance to heparin is frequently encountered in patients with fever, thrombosis, thrombophlebitis, infection with thrombosing tendencies, myocardial infarction, neoplastic disease, and postsurgical patients.
 
Heparin is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C in the manufacturer's product literature. However, heparin is classified as pregnancy risk category B by Brigg's et al. in Drugs in Pregnancy and Lactation. There are no reports linking heparin use during gestation with congenital defects. Although, there is no evidence of teratogenic effects, heparin should be used during pregnancy only when clearly needed. Heparin should be used with extreme caution near term because of the possibility of maternal postpartum hemorrhage. Heparin is not secreted in breast milk and can be administered safely to mothers who are breast-feeding.
 
Conflicting opinions exist regarding the effect of renal or hepatic failure on the elimination of heparin. Presently, most data indicate that neither renal nor hepatic dysfunction has any significant effect on heparin elimination. Patients acquired antithrombin deficiency ( 60 years, especially females, has been reported. Elderly patients may require lower doses of heparin.
 
Heparin is not intended for intramuscular administration. Severe large hematomas caused by accidental puncture of an IM vein may occur. Intramuscular injections of other medications should not be administered to patients receiving heparin. IM injections may cause bleeding, bruising, or hematomas.

    General Information
    Heparin is contraindicated in individuals in whom suitable blood coagulation tests (e.g., whole-blood clotting time, activated partial thromboplastin time [aPTT]) cannot be performed at appropriate intervals. This contraindication applies to full-dose heparin regimens only; monitoring of coagulation parameters is generally not required in individuals receiving low-dose heparin for prophylaxis.

If heparin resistance is suspected, consider measuring antithrombin concentrations. Monitor coagulation tests closely in such individuals; heparin dose adjustments based on anti-factor Xa concentrations and/or aPTT may be required.

    General Information
    Heparin is contraindicated in individuals with major bleeding, except when the bleeding is due to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Hemorrhage, including fatal events, has been reported in individuals receiving heparin, and bleeding may occur at virtually any site during therapy. Consider the possibility of hemorrhage in the setting of an unexplained fall in hematocrit, hypotension, or other unexplained clinical signs or symptoms. Use heparin with caution in conditions associated with an increased risk of hemorrhage, including subacute infective endocarditis, severe hypertension, during and immediately after lumbar puncture or spinal anesthesia, or major surgery, especially involving the brain, spinal cord, or eye. Caution is also advised in individuals with a bleeding disorder such as hemophilia, thrombocytopenia, or vascular purpuras; those with peptic ulcer disease, continuous gastrointestinal suction or drainage, or other conditions predisposing to stress-induced gastrointestinal bleeding; during menstruation; and in individuals with hepatic disease, severe renal disease, or an indwelling catheter. In individuals with hereditary antithrombin III deficiency receiving concurrent antithrombin III (human) therapy, the anticoagulant effect of heparin is enhanced; reduce the heparin dose during concomitant use to minimize bleeding risk.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Heparin can suppress adrenal secretion of aldosterone, leading to hyperkalemia, particularly in individuals with diabetes mellitus, chronic renal impairment or renal failure, preexisting metabolic acidosis, a raised plasma potassium (e.g., preexisting hyperkalemia), or taking potassium-sparing medications. The risk of hyperkalemia appears to increase with duration of therapy; however, it is usually reversible upon discontinuation of heparin. Measure plasma potassium in individuals at risk of hyperkalemia prior to initiation of heparin therapy and periodically in all individuals receiving heparin for more than 5 days or sooner if clinically indicated.
    Headline
    infants, neonates, premature neonates
    General Information
    Carefully examine all heparin products to ensure selection of the correct product and strength prior to administration. Medication errors involving incorrect vial strength have resulted in fatal hemorrhages in pediatric patients, particularly neonates. Use preservative-free heparin formulations (single-dose vials) in neonates and infants. Benzyl alcohol, used as a preservative in multiple-dose vials of heparin, has been associated with "gasping syndrome", a potentially fatal condition characterized by metabolic acidosis and central nervous system, respiratory, circulatory, and renal dysfunction in premature neonates and low-birth-weight infants. When administering multiple-dose vials of heparin in infants, consider the total daily metabolic load of benzyl alcohol from all sources. The minimum amount of benzyl alcohol that may cause serious adverse reactions is not known. When using heparin lock flush solutions to maintain catheter patency, avoid use of the 100 units/mL concentration in neonates or in infants who weigh less than 10 kg due to the risk of systemic anticoagulation. Use caution with the 10 units/mL concentration in premature infants weighing less than 1 kg who receive frequent flushes, as the cumulative dose may approximate a therapeutic dose over a 24-hour period.
    Headline
    females, geriatric
    General Information
    A higher incidence of bleeding has been reported in geriatric adults over 60 years of age, especially females. Lower doses of heparin may be indicated in these individuals.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no available data on the use of heparin during pregnancy to inform a drug-associated risk of major birth defects and miscarriage. In published reports, heparin exposure during human pregnancy did not result in an increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. No teratogenicity, but early embryo-fetal death was observed in animal reproduction studies with administration of heparin to pregnant rats and rabbits during organogenesis at doses approximately 10 times the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) of 45,000 units/day. In human studies using various heparin dosing regimens and routes of administration during pregnancy, obstetric deliveries have generally been uncomplicated, with no maternal or fetal bleeding. Consider the benefits and risks of heparin for the pregnant patient and the potential fetal risks when using heparin during pregnancy. If available, use preservative-free heparin injection during pregnancy. There are no known adverse fetal outcomes associated with exposure to the preservative benzyl alcohol via maternal administration; however, benzyl alcohol can cause serious harm and death when administered intravenously to neonates and infants.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Heparin is compatible with breast-feeding. Although there are no data on the presence of heparin in human milk, due to its large molecular weight, it is not likely to be excreted in human milk or absorbed by a breastfed child. Use preservative-free heparin formulations in lactating individuals; benzyl alcohol is likely to cross into human milk and may be orally absorbed by a nursing infant.

Fish Oil, Omega-3 Fatty Acids (Dietary Supplements): (Moderate) Drug interactions with fish oil, omega-3 fatty acids (Dietary Supplements) or fish oil, omega-3 fatty acids (FDA-approved) are unclear at this time. However, because fish oil, omega-3 fatty acids inhibit platelet aggregation, caution is advised when fish oils are used concurrently with anticoagulants, platelet inhibitors, or thrombolytic agents. Theoretically, the risk of bleeding may be increased, but some studies that combined these agents did not produce clinically significant bleeding events. In one placebo-controlled, randomized, double-blinded, parallel study, patients receiving stable, chronic warfarin therapy were administered various doses of fish oil supplements to determine the effect on INR determinations. Patients were randomized to receive a 4-week treatment period of either placebo or 3 or 6 grams of fish oil daily. Patients were followed on a twice-weekly basis for INR determinations and adverse reactions. There was no statistically significant difference in INRs between the placebo or treatment period within each group. There was also no difference in INRs found between groups. Description
  Text
  Heparin is a parenteral anticoagulant widely used in clinical medicine. It is a glycosaminoglycan composed of chains of alternating residues of D-glucosamine and an uronic acid; its molecular weight ranges from 5,000—30,000 daltons, with a mean of 15,000 daltons (about 50 monosaccharide chains). Commercially, heparin is derived from porcine or bovine tissue, and is available as the calcium or sodium salt. Full therapeutic doses of heparin prolong clotting time; however, bleeding time is usually unaffected by heparin. The response to heparin is variable since only about one-third of the molecules have anticoagulant activity, and its activity and clearance is dependent upon the length and size of the heparin molecules. The preferential clearance of larger molecules in vivo leads to accumulation of lower-molecular weight species that have a reduced ratio of antithrombin to antifactor Xa activity. Compared with low molecular weight heparins, unfractionated heparin produces a less predictable anticoagulant response due primarily to its reduced bioavailability after subcutaneous administration of low doses, its dose-dependent clearance, and differences among patients in the nonspecific binding of heparin to proteins and cells. Heparin was discovered in 1916 and was approved by the FDA in 1939.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Heparin exerts its anticoagulant action by accelerating the activity of antithrombin III (ATIII) to inactivate thrombin; however, heparin does not lyse existing clots. Approximately one-third of heparin molecules contain a unique pentasaccharide sequence with high-affinity binding to ATIII. The interaction of heparin with ATIII produces a conformational change in ATIII, which accelerates the ability of ATIII to inactivate thrombin (factor IIa), factor Xa, and factor IXa. Of these enzymes, thrombin is the most sensitive to inhibition by heparin/ATIII. Heparin catalyzes the ATIII inactivation of thrombin by acting as a template to which both thrombin and ATIII bind to form a ternary complex. The inactivation of factor Xa does not require the heparin/ATIII complex formation and occurs via binding of ATIII to factor Xa. Heparin molecules must be greater than 18 monosaccharides to bind to thrombin and ATIII simultaneously. Therefore, smaller heparin molecules (i.e., 50,000 International Units/day) to achieve an aPTT > 1.5-times control. Patients with acquired antithrombin deficiency (< 25% normal concentration) may not respond to heparin.

After subcutaneous administration, approximately 22% to 40% of the administered dose is absorbed systemically as determined by anti-factor Xa assay. Subcutaneous heparin concentrations reach steady-state between 4 and 10 hours.

  Text
  Heparin is given parenterally, either intravenously or subcutaneously. Heparin is highly bound to antithrombin, fibrinogens, globulins, serum proteases, and lipoproteins. The Vd is 0.07 L/kg. Heparin does not undergo enzymatic degradation. It is primarily cleared from the circulation by liver and reticuloendothelial cells mediated uptake into extravascular space. Heparin undergoes biphasic clearance, first by a rapid, saturable, zero-order process, then via slower first order elimination. In the zero-order phase, heparin is bound to the surface of cells (e.g., proteins, endothelial cells, macrophages) where it is internalized and depolymerized. Low doses of heparin are cleared mostly by a saturable, rapid, zero-order process. Slower first order elimination usually occurs with very high doses of heparin and is dependent on renal function. Plasma half-life is dose-dependent and ranges from 0.5 to 2 hours.

Do not use heparin products indicated for treatment or prophylaxis as catheter lock flush products. These formulations are supplied in vials containing various heparin concentrations, including highly concentrated solutions (e.g., 10,000 units/mL). Fatal hemorrhages have occurred due to medication errors when therapeutic heparin products were mistakenly used for catheter flushing. Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  It appears that hepatic impairment does not affect the elimination of heparin; however, patients with hepatic disease may have increased risk of bleeding during heparin therapy.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
