Drug Class: Lumateperone is an oral atypical antipsychotic that has a dual action as a serotonin 5-HT2A receptor antagonist and a dopamine modulator.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 42 mg/day PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): drowsiness, fatigue, insomnia, psychomotor impairment, akathisia, dyskinesia, dystonic reaction, gait disturbance, hypersalivation, pseudoparkinsonism, restlessness, tremor, agranulocytosis, anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, elevated hepatic enzymes, dizziness, headache, hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, qt prolongation, syncope, vertigo, contact dermatitis, pruritus, rash, urticaria, hypercholesterolemia, hyperlipidemia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Lumateperone, like other atypical antipsychotic agents, may cause metabolic changes.

    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, obesity
    General Information
    Lumateperone, like other atypical antipsychotic agents, may cause metabolic changes. Metabolic changes are of particular concern in patients with pre-existing risk factors, such as diabetes mellitus, obesity, and/or hyperlipidemia (e.g., hypercholesterolemia or hypertriglyceridemia). Increased blood sugar, worsening or new-onset diabetes, dyslipidemia, and weight gain may occur during therapy. While all medications in the atypical antipsychotic class have been shown to produce some metabolic changes, each drug has its own specific risk profile. Metabolic changes may increase cardiovascular or cerebrovascular risk over time. Hyperglycemia, sometimes associated with diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar coma, or death, has been reported in people treated with atypical antipsychotics. Monitor weight and for symptoms of hyperglycemia. Regularly monitor patients with established diabetes mellitus for changes in weight and worsening of glucose control during atypical antipsychotic treatment. In those with risk factors for diabetes mellitus (e.g., obesity, family history of diabetes), perform fasting blood glucose testing at the beginning of treatment. In patients who develop symptoms of hyperglycemia during treatment, order fasting blood glucose testing. In some cases, hyperglycemia has resolved when the antipsychotic is discontinued. However, some patients require continuation of antidiabetic agents despite discontinuation of the suspect drug. Weight gain and dyslipidemia (increased cholesterol and/or triglycerides) may also occur during therapy with lumateperone, and clinical monitoring of weight and serum lipid profiles is recommended during treatment. Inform all patients of the importance of physical activity and maintaining a nutritionally balanced diet during treatment.
    Headline
    cardiovascular disease, hypovolemia
    General Information
    Lumateperone should be used with caution in patients with known cardiovascular disease (e.g., history of myocardial infarction or ischemic heart disease, heart failure or conduction abnormalities) or conditions which would predispose patients to hypotension (dehydration, hypovolemia, and treatment with antihypertensive medications). Lumateperone can cause orthostatic hypotension and, in rare cases, syncope; risks may be increased in patients with underlying risk factors. Orthostatic hypotension from antipsychotic use may lead to falls with the potential for fractures and other injuries; a fall risk assessment should be completed and reassessed as needed in patients with conditions, diseases, or concurrent medication use that could exacerbate orthostasis.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Lumateperone is not currently approved for use in pediatric patients younger than 18 years; however, because lumateperone is approved for use as monotherapy or adjunct treatment of depressive episodes in bipolar I or II disorder in adults, a boxed warning in the product label describes the risk of suicidality and suicidal ideation in children, adolescents, and young adults receiving antidepressants. In a pooled analysis of placebo-controlled trials of antidepressants (n = 4,500 pediatrics and 77,000 adults), there was an increased risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors in patients 24 years and younger receiving an antidepressant versus placebo, with considerable variation in the risk of suicidality among drugs. The difference in absolute risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors across different indications was highest in those with major depression. The need for an antidepressant in children, adolescents, or young adults for any use must be weighed against the risk of suicidality; it is unknown if this risk extends to long-term use. All patients should be monitored for symptom worsening or suicidality, especially at treatment initiation or after dose changes. Caregivers and/or patients should immediately notify the prescriber of changes in behavior or suicidal ideation. A change to the treatment regimen or discontinuation of lumateperone may be necessary in patients with emerging suicidality or worsening depression. The Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) list includes lumateperone as a potentially inappropriate medication for use in children and adolescents and use should be avoided. If avoidance is not possible, use lumateperone with caution and appropriate monitoring.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Antipsychotics, including lumateperone, may cause somnolence, postural hypotension, motor and sensory instability, which may lead to falls and, consequently, fractures or other injuries. For people with diseases, conditions, or medications that could exacerbate these effects, complete fall risk assessments when initiating antipsychotic treatment and periodically during long-term antipsychotic therapy. Lumateperone may have the potential to impair judgment, thinking, or motor skills; advise patients about driving or operating machinery or other activities requiring coordination and concentration until they are reasonably certain that lumateperone does not affect them adversely. Given the primary CNS effects of lumateperone, use caution during coadministration with other CNS depressants and alcohol. Ethanol ingestion may further impair cognitive and motor skills and patients should be advised to avoid use of alcoholic beverages when taking lumateperone.
    Headline
    leukopenia
    General Information
    Use lumateperone with caution in those with a history of leukopenia. Hematologic effects including leukopenia, neutropenia, and agranulocytosis have been associated with antipsychotic use. A history of drug-induced leukopenia or neutropenia or pre-existing low white blood cell (WBC) count may increase the likelihood of recurrence during treatment with an antipsychotic medication. Patients with a history of clinically significant low WBC count or drug-induced leukopenia/neutropenia should have frequent complete blood count (CBC) assessments during the first few months of treatment. Consider discontinuation of the antipsychotic if a clinically significant decline in WBC occurs in the absence of an identifiable cause. Monitor patients with clinically significant neutropenia closely for fever and infection; institute appropriate medical intervention if necessary. Lumateperone should be discontinued in patients with severe neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count less than 1,000/mm3), with ongoing medical care until the symptoms resolve.
    Headline
    seizures
    General Information
    As with other antipsychotic drugs, lumateperone should be used with caution in patients with a history of seizures, a seizure disorder, or with conditions that potentially lower the seizure threshold. Conditions that lower the seizure threshold may be more prevalent in a population 65 years of age or older.
    Headline
    conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature
    General Information
    Disruption of the body's ability to reduce core body temperature has been attributed to antipsychotic agents. Patients receiving lumateperone should be advised of conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature (e.g., exercising strenuously, exposure to extreme heat, receiving concomitant medication with anticholinergic activity, or being subject to dehydration).
    Headline
    Parkinson disease
    General Information
    Avoid use of lumateperone in people with Parkinson disease unless the benefit clearly outweighs the risk of worsening of motor symptoms. Atypical antipsychotics can worsen the motor symptoms of Parkinson disease and can also lead to the development of extrapyramidal symptoms.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Lumateperone is extensively metabolized in the liver and systemic exposure is increased in people with hepaticimpairment. People with moderate (Child-Pugh class B) to severe (Child-Pugh class C) hepatic impairment or hepatic failure should receive a reduced dosage to reduce the risk of adverse effects. No dosage adjustment is recommended in patients with mild hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A).
    Headline
    dysphagia, increased risk for aspiration
    General Information
    People with dysphagia or who are at increased risk for aspiration should be closely monitored while receiving lumateperone. Antipsychotic use, including lumateperone, has been associated with esophageal dysmotility and aspiration of gastric contents. Aspiration pneumonia is a common cause of morbidity and mortality in older adults, in particular those with advanced Alzheimer disease.
    Headline
    dementia with psychotic disturbance, geriatric
    General Information
    Antipsychotics, including lumateperone, pose a risk of increased mortality when used for dementia with psychotic disturbance in geriatric adults. Most of the deaths are cardiovascular (e.g., heart failure, sudden death) or infectious (e.g., pneumonia) in nature. A higher incidence of stroke and transient ischemic attack, including fatal stroke, has also been reported. Initiate lumateperone at the lower end of the dosage range and titrate carefully in the older adult. The Beers Criteria label antipsychotics as potentially inappropriate medications for geriatric adults, except for specific indications like agitation in Alzheimer disease, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder adjunct therapy, Parkinson disease psychosis, or short-term use as part of an antiemetic regimen during chemotherapy. Avoid use in delirium, dementia, or non-psychotic Parkinson disease. Reserve antipsychotic use for when non-pharmacologic strategies have failed, are not possible, or the patient poses a danger to self or others. Due to the potential for hyponatremia and SIADH, monitor sodium levels. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates antipsychotic use in long-term care facilities and requires documented clinical justification. Taper antipsychotics unless clinically contraindicated, following guidelines for indications, dosages, and duration. OBRA limits "as needed" use to 14 days in long-term care facilities, with ongoing use requiring evaluation by the prescriber.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no available data regarding lumateperone use during pregnancy to be informative of any drug-associated risks for birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. The risks of untreated maternal schizophrenia or fetal exposure to antipsychotics during pregnancy should be considered. No malformations were observed in animal studies using lumateperone doses higher than the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD). It is not known if antipsychotics, through their effect on prolactin, would affect labor or delivery. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant individuals exposed to lumateperone; information about the registry can be obtained at https://womensmentalhealth.org/research/pregnancyregistry/ or by calling 1-866-961-2388.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Consider pregnancy testing in people who may become pregnant prior to initiation of an antipsychotic, as there are limited controlled data regarding use of these agents during gestation. Ideally, health care providers should use pre-conceptual planning to help such individuals taking atypical antipsychotics balance the risk for illness relapse with the potential risk of continuing medication while pregnant. Advise of the potential reproductive risk with lumateperone. Results from animal studies suggest that infertility may be possible during lumateperone administration. Advise people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others that lumateperone may impair fertility.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use lumateperone with caution during breast-feeding. Lumateperone and its metabolites are present in human breast milk in low amounts. There are no data on the effects of lumateperone on the breastfed infant or the effects on milk production. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding along with the clinical need for lumateperone and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from lumateperone or from the underlying maternal condition. In a clinical study of a 42 mg lumateperone dose in lactating women (n=17), lumateperone was detected in human milk at an estimated infant dose of 0.0004 mg/kg/day, with a relative infant dose of 0.06% of the maternal weight-adjusted dose. Several major circulating metabolites were detected in breast milk in low amounts. However, aniline metabolites (compounds associated with toxicity in animal studies) were not present in milk or maternal plasma at quantifiable levels. In general, data related to the safety of antipsychotics during breast-feeding are limited. It may be prudent to continue an existing antipsychotic regimen if ongoing treatment is deemed necessary during breast-feeding, since there is considerable individual variation in antipsychotic response. Alternate atypical antipsychotic medications for consideration during breast-feeding include olanzapine or quetiapine. If an antipsychotic is used, closely monitor the breastfed infant for excessive drowsiness, lethargy, and developmental delays. Combination treatment with antipsychotics may increase the risk of these adverse events.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero during the third trimester of pregnancy are at risk for extrapyramidal and/or withdrawal symptoms following delivery. There have been reports of agitation, hypertonia, hypotonia, tremor, somnolence, respiratory distress and feeding disorders in these neonates. These complications have varied in severity and have ranged from self-limited to those requiring intensive care unit support and prolonged hospitalization. Neonates exhibiting signs or symptoms of extrapyramidal effects or withdrawal should be carefully monitored. The knowledge about long-term neurobehavioral effects in offspring is limited for all antipsychotic agents and requires further investigation.

    akathisia, dyskinesia, dystonic reaction, gait disturbance, hypersalivation, pseudoparkinsonism, restlessness, tremor
    General Information
    Extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) are a class effect of antipsychotics, including lumateperone. Dystonic reaction usually occurs in susceptible individuals during the first few days of treatment. This effect is observed more commonly in males, younger age groups, and with high potency antipsychotics. Dystonic reactions may manifest as torticollis with or without throat tightness, difficulty swallowing or breathing, oculogyric crisis, trismus, or protrusion of the tongue. Pseudoparkinsonism may occur 1 to 2 weeks after initiation of antipsychotic therapy and is more common in elderly adults. Symptoms include bradykinesia, sialorrhea, abnormal glabellar reflex, hypokinesia, and psychomotor impairment. Akathisia may develop several days to weeks into therapy and may respond to dosage reduction or concomitant administration of a benzodiazepine or a beta-blocker. During short-term clinical trial evaluation of lumateperone in adults with schizophrenia, extrapyramidal symptoms were reported in more subjects receiving lumateperone than placebo (6.7% vs. 6.3%) and included akathisia, extrapyramidal disorder, muscle spasms, restlessness, musculoskeletal stiffness, dyskinesia, dystonic reaction, muscle twitching, tardive dyskinesia, tremor, drooling (hypersalivation), and involuntary muscle contractions. When evaluating lumateperone in adults with bipolar depression, extrapyramidal symptoms were reported in more subjects receiving lumateperone than placebo (1.3% to 4% vs. 1.1% to 2.3%) and included tremor, muscle spasms, akathisia, extrapyramidal disorder, gait disturbance, movement disorder, dyskinesia, and restlessness. Tremor was reported in 4% of subjects receiving lumateperone vs. less than 1% of subjects receiving placebo during clinical trials. The combined incidence of akathisia and restlessness was 1% in lumateperone-treated subjects vs. placebo (0.8%). The mean changes in baseline scores assessing EPS, akathisia, and tardive dyskinesia in subjects receiving lumateperone were as follows: the Simpson Angus Scale (0 to 0.1), the Barnes Akathisia Rating Scale (-0.1 to 0), and the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (0 to 0.1).

    General Information
    A potentially fatal symptom complex referred to as neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) has been associated with antipsychotic administration. NMS is characterized by hyperpyrexia, muscle rigidity, altered mental status, and autonomic instability (e.g., irregular pulse or blood pressure, tachycardia, diaphoresis, cardiac dysrhythmia). NMS occurs more frequently in men, which is thought to be related to the higher likelihood of male versus female exposure to the causative agent. Risk factors for recurrent NMS include a personal history of NMS, increasing age, and certain medical co-morbidities (e.g., electrolyte imbalances, dehydration). Description
  Text
  Lumateperone is an oral atypical antipsychotic that has a dual action as a serotonin 5-HT2A receptor antagonist and a dopamine modulator. Lumateperone is indicated for the treatment of schizophrenia, depressive episodes associated with bipolar I or II disorder (bipolar depression), either as monotherapy or in combination with lithium or valproate treatment, and as an adjunct to antidepressants for major depressive disorder (MDD). Lumateperone demonstrated statistically significant efficacy across multiple psychiatric conditions. In schizophrenia treatment trials, lumateperone improved Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) total score vs. placebo, with placebo-subtracted differences of -5.8 (95% CI, -10.5 to -1.1) and -4.2 (95% CI, -7.8 to -0.6), and significantly delayed time to relapse in a randomized withdrawal maintenance study of clinically stable adults. Adults receiving lumateperone (42 mg/day) for maintenance treatment had a 63% lower relapse risk compared with placebo (HR, 0.37), with 84% relapse-free over 6 months. For bipolar depression, lumateperone significantly improved total scores on the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) as monotherapy and adjunctive therapy with lithium or valproate, with placebo-subtracted differences of -4.6 (95% CI, -6.3 to -2.8) and -2.4 (95% CI, -4.4 to -0.4), respectively. Significant improvement was also observed on the Clinical Global Impression-Bipolar-Severity of Illness (CGI-BP-S). For major depression, adjunctive lumateperone significantly improved MADRS total score vs. placebo plus antidepressant therapy, with placebo-subtracted differences of -4.9 (95% CI, -6.38 to -3.44) and -4.5 (95% CI, -6.03 to -3.02), with significant improvement also observed on CGI-S. One advantage of lumateperone over most atypical antipsychotics is that dose titration is not required to reach therapeutic levels. Similar to many other antipsychotics, clinically important CYP-mediated drug interactions may occur with lumateperone and agents that inhibit uridine 5'-diphospho-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) also mediate clinically important interactions with this drug. As with all antipsychotics, lumateperone carries a boxed warning for increased mortality in elderly adults with dementia-related psychosis. Since lumateperone is also used adjunctively for depression treatment, the label also carries a boxed warning for increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors, particularly in pediatric individuals and young adults. Lumateperone was FDA approved in 2019.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Lumateperone has a unique dual mechanism of action as an atypical antipsychotic. The exact mechanism of lumateperone in treating schizophrenia, depressive episodes associated with bipolar disorder, and major depressive disorder (MDD) is unknown. However, the mechanism is thought to occur through a combination of antagonism at central serotonin 5-HT2A and postsynaptic dopamine D2 receptors. Lumateperone has a high binding affinity for serotonin 5-HT2A receptors and a moderate binding affinity for the dopamine D1, D2, and D4 receptors, alpha-1A and alpha-1B receptors, and serotonin transporters. There is a low binding affinity for histaminergic and muscarinic receptors.
 
Dopamine and serotonin mediate various effects on different portions of the brain. According to one hypothesis, a dopamine excess in the mesolimbic tract is thought to be responsible for the positive symptoms of schizophrenia. In the mesocortical tract, a reduction in dopamine activity may be responsible for the negative symptoms of schizophrenia. Reduced dopamine activity in the nigrostriatal tract may be related to decreased metabolic activity in the basal ganglia. Central serotonin hyperactivity may be associated with dopamine hypoactivity in the nigrostriatal and mesocortical tracts. Antipsychotics with a high affinity for serotonin receptors are thought to be more effective for treating the negative symptoms of schizophrenia than those with dopaminergic modulation as a primary mechanism. The tuberoinfundibular tract controls neuroendocrine and hypothalamic function (e.g., prolactin release).
