Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Ferumoxytol is contraindicated in patients with known hypersensitivity to ferumoxytol or any of its components. Ferumoxytol can cause serious hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis and/or anaphylactoid reactions. Serious hypersensitivity reactions were reported in 0.2% (3/1726) of patients in pre-marketing clinical trials. Monitor patients for signs and symptoms of hypersensitivity for at least 30 minutes after administration. Only administer ferumoxytol in facilities with personnel and therapies readily available to treat hypersensitivity reactions should they occur.
 
Hypotension has been reported in patients receiving intravenous iron products, including ferumoxytol. Use caution when administering ferumoxytol to patients with pre-existing hypotension or in those receiving hemodialysis. For patients receiving dialysis, administer ferumoxytol after at least one hour of hemodialysis has been completed and only when blood pressure is stable. In pre-marketing clinical trials, hypotension was reported in 1.9% (33/1726) of patients, 3 of which had serious hypotensive reactions. Blood pressure should be closely monitored after ferumoxytol administration.
 
The use of ferumoxytol is contraindicated in patients with evidence of iron overload and in patients with anemia not caused by iron deficiency. Ferumoxytol should be discontinued when target serum ferritin levels are attained. Unnecessary administration of iron may lead to iron overload and iron toxicity. Parenteral iron products should be used cautiously in patients receiving blood transfusions because iron overload may occur. Prolonged use of ferumoxytol should be restricted to avoid iron overload. Oral iron preparations should be discontinued prior to the use of ferumoxytol. Additionally, the type of anemia and the underlying cause or causes should be determined before starting therapy with ferumoxytol. Since the anemia may be a result of a systemic disturbance, such as recurrent blood loss, the underlying cause(s) should be corrected, if possible. Ferumoxytol is only useful for those anemia types where iron-deficiency co-exists. Exogenous administration of ferumoxytol will not alleviate hemolysis, and iron should only be administered to patients with hemolysis if an iron-deficiency is also present.
 
Unnecessary administration of ferumoxytol to patients with hemoglobinopathies may result in excessive storage of iron. Ferumoxytol is contraindicated in patients with hereditary/genetic hemochromatosis or hemochromatosis due to secondary iron overload (e.g., as in iron-loading anemias such as thalassemia or sideroblastic anemia). Hemochromatosis is a disease that causes the body to lose its ability to regulate the amount of iron that is absorbed, leading to excess iron absorption and tissue storage. Massive deposition of iron (hemosiderosis) in parenchymal tissues in these conditions may damage the liver, heart, pancreas and other tissues. Porphyria cutanea tarda (PCT) is sometimes associated with parenchymal iron deposits; patients with PCT should generally avoid iron therapies. Excess iron supplementation in PCT patients can contribute to hepatic uroporphinogen decarboxylase deficiency but the mechanism is not clear. Patients receiving ferumoxytol require periodic monitoring of hematologic and hematinic parameters to avoid iron overload. Hemosiderosis secondary to long-term iron therapy has primarily been reported in patients with renal failure receiving dialysis. Periodic monitoring of serum ferritin levels may be helpful in recognizing a progressive accumulation of iron resulting from impaired iron uptake from the reticuloendothelial system in patients with renal failure.
 
The administration of ferumoxytol may affect the diagnostic ability of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for up to 3 months; therefore, anticipated MRI studies should be conducted prior to ferumoxytol administration. If MRI studies are required within 3 months of ferumoxytol administration, use T1- or proton density-weighted magnetic resonance pulse sequences to minimize the effects of ferumoxytol. MRI using T2-weighted pulse sequences should not be performed earlier than 4 weeks after ferumoxytol administration. Maximum alteration of vascular MRI is anticipated to be evident for 1—2 days after ferumoxytol administration. Ferumoxytol will not interfere with X-ray, computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET), single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), ultrasound, or nuclear medicine imaging.
 
In the 24 hours after ferumoxytol administration, laboratory assays of iron status may be unreliable.
 
Some patients with chronic hepatic disease may also have hemochromatosis or moderate iron overload in hepatic tissues. Use ferumoxytol with caution in patients with hepatic disease. The liver is one of the main storage sites for iron, and advanced chronic liver disease may result in excess storage of iron in the liver.
 
In controlled clinical trials, no differences in safety or efficacy were noted between elderly and younger patients; however, greater sensitivity of some elderly patients to ferumoxytol therapy cannot be ruled out. In pre-marketing clinical trials, 330 patients >= 65 years of age were included.
 
Ferumoxytol is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. It is not known whether ferumoxytol can cause fetal harm when administered to pregnant women or can affect reproductive capacity. In animal trials (rats and rabbits), maternally toxic cumulative doses of 13 to 15 times the recommended human dose administered over 12 to 14 days during organogenesis resulted in decreased fetal weights and fetal malformations. Pregnant rats were given daily doses approximately 2 times the human dose on a mg/kg basis for 12 days during organogenesis. Ferumoxytol should be used during pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies any possible risk to the fetus.
 
According to the manufacturer, it is not known if ferumoxytol is excreted into human breast milk, and either breast-feeding should be discontinued or ferumoxytol administration should be avoided. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally administered drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
The safety and efficacy of ferumoxytol in children has not been established.

    General Information
    Magnetic resonance imaging obtained with ferumoxytol may demonstrate different size, intensity, and pattern of contrast signal in lesions compared to images obtained with gadolinium-based contrast. Be aware of the potential limited interpretability of changes in lesion contrast appearance if prior images were not obtained with ferumoxytol.
    Headline
    serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis
    General Information
    Fatal and serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis, presenting with cardiac or cardiopulmonary arrest, clinically significant hypotension, syncope, and unresponsiveness, have occurred in individuals receiving ferumoxytol. Hypersensitivity reactions have occurred in individuals in whom a previous dose was tolerated. Individuals with a history of multiple drug allergies may have a greater risk of anaphylaxis with parenteral iron products. Carefully consider the potential risks and benefits before administering ferumoxytol to these individuals. Only administer ferumoxytol as an intravenous infusion over at least 15 minutes and only when personnel and therapies are immediately available for the treatment of anaphylaxis and other hypersensitivity reactions. Monitor for signs and symptoms of hypersensitivity reactions, including blood pressure and pulse, during and after administration for at least 30 minutes and until clinically stable after completion of each infusion.
    Headline
    iron overload disorder
    General Information
    Do not administer ferumoxytol to individuals with iron overload disorder (e.g., hemochromatosis). Excessive therapy with iron products may lead to excess storage of iron and iatrogenic hemosiderosis. Monitor hematologic parameters (hemoglobin, ferritin, iron and transferrin saturation) periodically during ferumoxytol therapy.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    In controlled clinical trials, no differences in safety or efficacy were noted between geriatric and younger adult patients; however, greater sensitivity of some older adults to ferumoxytol therapy cannot be ruled out. Geriatric adults with multiple or serious comorbidities who experience hypersensitivity reactions and/or hypotension after ferumoxytol administration may be at increased risk for more severe outcomes; carefully consider the potential risks and benefits of administration in these individuals.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Data on the use of ferumoxytol during pregnancy are insufficient to inform a drug-associated risk of adverse developmental outcomes. Untreated iron deficiency in pregnancy is associated with adverse outcomes, including postpartum anemia, increased risk for preterm delivery, and low birth weight. However, serious adverse reactions, including circulatory failure, hypotension, in the context of an anaphylactic reaction, may occur in individuals administered parenteral iron products. This may have serious consequences in pregnancy, such as fetal bradycardia, especially during the second and third trimester. Advise pregnant individuals of the potential fetal risk. In animal studies, ferumoxytol administration to rats and rabbits during organogenesis did not result in adverse maternal or fetal outcomes at doses approximately 2 times the estimated human dose based on body surface area. At doses 6 times the estimated human daily dose, fetal malformations and decreased fetal weights were observed in the presence of maternal toxicity.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Ferumoxytol is compatible with breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of ferumoxytol in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Ferumoxytol is a polar, non-lipid soluble iron carbohydrate complex. After intravenous administration, it remains primarily in the intravascular space. It is unlikely to enter human milk or be orally bioavailable to the breastfed child. In addition, this medication has been safely administered intravenously to neonates as a magnetic resonance contrast agent.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Ferumoxytol administration caused anaphylaxis or anaphylactoid reactions in 0.2% (3/1726) of patients in pre-marketing clinical trials. In addition, other hypersensitivity reactions including pruritus, rash (unspecified), urticaria, and wheezing were reported in 3.7% (63/1726) of patients. In other pre-marketing evaluation, pruritis was reported in 1.2% of patients who received ferumoxytol (n = 605) compared to 0.4% of patients who received oral iron therapy (n = 280), and rash was reported in 1% of the ferumoxytol group compared to 0.4% of the oral iron group.
 
Hypotension may occur after the administration of ferumoxytol. In pre-marketing clinical studies, hypotension was reported in 1.9% (33/1726) of the study population; 3 patients developed severe hypotensive reactions. In other pre-marketing evaluation, hypotension was reported in 2.5% of patients who received ferumoxytol (n = 605) compared to 0.4% of patients who received oral iron therapy (n = 280). Patients undergoing hemodialysis may be at particular risk for hypotension (see Contraindications/Precautions). Carefully monitor all patients for hypotension after ferumoxytol administration.
 
When combining the results of 3 pre-marketing clinical studies (n = 605), gastrointestinal adverse events reported for ferumoxytol compared to oral iron, respectively, include abdominal pain (1.3% vs. 1.4%), nausea/vomiting (3.1%/1.5% vs. 7.5%/5%). Diarrhea (4%) and constipation (2.1%) also have been reported in patients receiving ferumoxytol.
 
When combining the results of the 3 pre-marketing clinical studies (n = 605), general adverse reactions reported for ferumoxytol compared to oral iron, respectively, include dizziness (2.6% vs. 1.8%), headache (1.8% vs. 2.1%), fever (pyrexia) (1 vs. 0.7%), back pain (1% vs. 0%), and muscle spasms (1% vs. 1.4%).
 
When combining the results of the 3 pre-marketing clinical studies (n = 605), the incidence of cardiovascular adverse reactions reported for ferumoxytol compared to oral iron, respectively, include peripheral edema (2% vs. 3.2%), edema (1.5% vs. 1.4%), and chest pain (unspecified) (1.3% vs. 0.7%). In addition, hypertension (1%) also has been reported in patients receiving ferumoxytol.
 
When combining the results of the 3 pre-marketing clinical studies (n = 605), the incidence of pulmonary adverse reactions reported for ferumoxytol compared to oral iron, respectively, include cough (1.3% vs. 1.4%) and dyspnea (1% vs. 1.1%).

    General Information
    The administration of ferumoxytol may result in diagnostic test interference with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for up to 3 months; therefore, conduct anticipated MRI studies (other than the intended studies for ferumoxytol brain imaging) prior to ferumoxytol administration. Description
  Text
  Ferumoxytol is an intravenous iron replacement product and an iron-based contrast agent indicated for the treatment of iron deficiency anemia in individuals who have an intolerance or unsatisfactory response to oral iron or in individuals with chronic kidney disease and for MRI of the brain in individuals with known or suspected malignant brain neoplasms, respectively. Fatal and serious hypersensitivity reactions have been reported with ferumoxytol; ferumoxytol is only for administration when personnel and therapies are immediately available for the treatment of anaphylaxis and other hypersensitivity reactions. Ferumoxytol may also cause clinically significant hypotension. Ferumoxytol was FDA-approved in June 2009.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Ferumoxytol is a supermagnetic iron oxide that is coated with a carbohydrate shell.
Ferumoxytol for Iron Deficiency Anemia
Ferumoxytol helps to isolate the bioactive iron from plasma components until the iron-carbohydrate complex enters the reticuloendothelial system macrophages of the liver, spleen, and bone marrow. The iron is released from the iron-carbohydrate complex within vesicles in the macrophages. Iron then enters the intracellular storage iron pool (e.g., ferritin) or is transferred to plasma transferrin for transport to erythroid precursor cells for incorporation into hemoglobin.
Ferumoxytol for Magnetic Resonance Imaging
Ferumoxytol develops a magnetic moment when placed in a magnetic field. The magnetic moment alters the relaxation rates of water protons in its vicinity in the body, leading to an increase in signal intensity and brightness of tissues on T1-weighted MRI. Disruption of the blood-brain barrier allows for distribution of ferumoxytol into lesions (e.g., neoplasms). Parenchymal enhancement is observed in the delayed phase, approximately 24 hours after the administration of ferumoxytol, due to slow extravasation of the large ferumoxytol molecules.
