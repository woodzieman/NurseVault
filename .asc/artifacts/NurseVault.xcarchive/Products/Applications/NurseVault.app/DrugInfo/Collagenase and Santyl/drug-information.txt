        Dosage Description
        0.58 mg injected into a Peyronie plaque. If more than 1 plaque is present, inject into the plaque causing the curvature. A treatment cycle consists of 2 injection procedures and 1 penile modeling procedure. The second of 2 injection procedures is performed 1 to 3 days after the first. The penile modeling procedure is performed 1 to 3 days after the second injection of the treatment cycle. The interval between treatment cycles is approximately 6 weeks. The treatment course consists of a maximum of 8 injection procedures and 4 modeling procedures (i.e., a maximum of 4 treatment cycles). The safety of more than 1 treatment course is unknown. If the curvature deformity is less than 15 degrees after the first, second, or third treatment cycle, or if the health care provider determines that further treatment is not clinically indicated, then the subsequent treatment cycles should not be administered. The safety and effectiveness of collagenase treatment of Peyronie disease was established in 2 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies in adult males (n = 832) with Peyronie disease with penile curvature deformity of at least 30 degrees. Participants were given up to 4 treatment cycles of collagenase or placebo and were then followed 52 weeks. Collagenase treatment significantly reduced penile curvature deformity and related bothersome effects compared with placebo.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Patients with hyperglycemia, malnutrition, immunosuppressant use, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), or certain cancers, such as leukemia are at a greater risk for wound infection due to immunosuppression. Patients with bone marrow suppression may have neutropenia or leukopenia. Patients with inadequate protein intake may have hypoalbuminemia. The elderly also often have a weakened immune system. In addition to suppressing the immune system, glucocorticoids halt the body's production of collagenase. Patients with a history of anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa may have nutrient deficiencies, such as zinc; these patients may also have an inadequate intake of protein and calories necessary for proper wound repair. Infection at the wound site may lead to a systemic infection (sepsis). Collagenase may increase the risk for bacteremia. However, eschar or necrotic tissue is a good medium to support bacterial growth. Wounds of patients with immunosuppression need to be monitored vigilantly for signs and symptoms of infection. Patients with a fever, chills, elevated white blood count, hypotension, confusion, purulent wound exudate, or odiforous wounds may need a systemic antibiotic.Certain wounds of patients with peripheral vascular disease, diabetes mellitus, or arteriosclerosis may have insufficient vascular supply. Wound beds need adequate blood supply to facilitate nutrition delivery, infection control, and waste removal. Collagenase application to a wound bed with inadequate blood perfusion may be moot. Steps to optimize circulation, such as external compression, surgery, exercise, and medication optimization may be considered, as appropriate.Collagenase does not have a pregnancy risk category assigned by the FDA. No well-controlled studies are available in pregnant women (therefore data most corresponds to FDA category C). Collagenase should only be given to a pregnant woman where clearly indicated.Topical collagenase has no specific precautions for use in breast-feeding women.

    General Information
    Administration of collagenase injections for the treatment of Peyronie disease (Xiaflex) has been associated with serious penile injury, including corporal rupture.     General Information
    It is recommended to avoid the use of injectable collagenase (Xiaflex) in patients with a bleeding disorder, including people who are receiving concomitant antiplatelet medications or anticoagulants (except for low-dose aspirin).     General Information
    Available data on the use of collagenase during pregnancy are insufficient to evaluate for drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse pregnancy outcomes. However, systemic concentrations are below the limit of quantification after subcutaneous injection and injection into a Dupuytren cord. Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The most common adverse effect of collagenase is slight transient erythema around the application site. Skin irritation may be minimized by wiping excess ointment from surrounding skin. Prolonged treatment with collagenase and cortisone has caused systemic hypersensitivity in some patients, but such reactions are uncommon. Of 92 patients that received collagenase on a daily or every-other-day basis, 1 developed a rash (unspecified). Patients who develop a rash, urticaria, anaphylactic shock, dyspnea, or anaphylactoid reactions from collagenase should not receive the product again.The use of collagenase may increase the risk for systemic infection. The risk for bacteremia from collagenase needs to be weighed against the fact that eschar or necrotic tissue is a good medium to support bacterial growth. Infection risk is greater for patients with diabetes mellitus, malnutrition, or immunosuppression. Wounds of patients with immunosuppression need to be monitored vigilantly for signs and symptoms of infection. If a topical antibiotic is applied to the site, the antibiotic should be applied before collagenase. The antibiotic should be chosen based on culture results. If the infection does not resolve, collagenase ointment should be withheld until culture results show infection resolution. Patients with a fever, chills, elevated white blood count, hypotension, confusion, purulent wound exudate, or odiferous wounds may need a systemic antibiotic.
  Adverse Reactions
    Headline
    anaphylactic shock, anaphylactoid reactions, dyspnea, erythema, pruritus, rash, skin irritation, urticaria
    General Information
    A common adverse effect of topical collagenase is slight transient erythema around the application site. Skin irritation may be minimized by wiping excess ointment from surrounding skin. Prolonged treatment with topical collagenase and cortisone has caused systemic hypersensitivity in some patients, but such reactions are uncommon. Of 92 patients who received collagenase on a daily or every other day basis, 1 developed a rash. Patients who develop a rash, urticaria, anaphylactic shock, dyspnea, or anaphylactoid reactions from topical collagenase should not receive the product again. In clinical trials with injectable collagenase, no severe allergic reactions were noted; however, a serious hypersensitivity reaction or anaphylaxis has been reported during postmarketing use of the drug. In a controlled trial of Peyronie's disease, more patients treated with injectable collagenase (4%) reported localized pruritus compared to placebo (1%) after up to 4 treatment cycles (up to 8 injections). The incidence of pruritus was similar after each injection regardless of the number of injections administered. The incidence of pruritus did increase after more collagenase injections. In controlled trials of Dupuytren's contracture, more patients treated with injectable collagenase (15%) experienced pruritus compared to those receiving placebo (1%); erythema was also reported with injectable collagenase (6%) more so than placebo (1%). Similarly, in clinical trials of women with cellulite, the incidence of pruritus and erythema were higher in those who received injectable collagenase (15% and 9%, respectively) than in the placebo group (1% and 5%, respectively). Injectable collagenase contains foreign proteins, and patients developed IgE-antidrug antibodies in greater proportions and higher titers with successive injections. Health care providers should be prepared to address severe allergic reactions after any collagenase injection.

    General Information
    In clinical trials for collagenase in the treatment of Peyronie's disease, reported localized adverse reactions included edema (55%) such as injection site swelling and penile edema, penile pain (45.4%), ecchymosis (14.5%), blood blister (4.5%), penile blister (3.3%), painful erection (2.9%), impotence (erectile dysfunction) (1.8%), skin discoloration (1.8%), procedural pain (1.6%), injection site vesicles (1.3%), dyspareunia (1.1%), penile irritation (1.1%), nodule (1.1%), and pelvic pain (1.1%). In clinical trials for collagenase in the treatment of Dupuytren's contracture, peripheral edema (mostly swelling of the injected hand) was a common adverse event, occurring in 73% to 77% of patients treated with collagenase. Other common adverse events were contusion (59% to 70%) or ecchymosis (5% to 70%), tenderness (24%), extremity pain (35% to 51%), injection site bleeding (6% to 38%), injection site swelling or edema (5% to 24%), and injection site reaction (14% to 35%) such as erythema, inflammation, irritation, pain, and warmth. Blood blister was reported in 12% of collagenase-treated patients. In women who received collagenase for treatment of cellulite, injections site bruising (defined as bruising, hematoma, and bleeding) was reported by 84% of drug recipients. Other common injection site reactions included pain (48%), nodule or mass (33%), edema or induration (8%), skin discoloration (8%), and warmth (3%).

Description
  Text
  Collagenase enzymatically degrades collagen and is available as an injection and as a topical ointment. The 2 commercially available injectable collagenase clostridium histolyticum products are administered by different routes, approved for different indications, and are not substitutable. The first injectable product is QWO, which is administered subcutaneously and is approved to treat moderate to severe cellulitis in the buttocks of adult women. The second injectable product is Xiaflex, which is administered via intralesional injection and is approved for the treatment of Dupuytren's contracture with a palpable cord as well as for the treatment of Peyronie's disease with a palpable plaque and curvature deformity of at least 30 degrees at the start of therapy. Due to the possible risk of serious penile injury, Xiaflex for the treatment of Peyronie's disease is only available through a restricted Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) program. The topical product is approved for the chemical debridement of acute or chronic dermal ulcers and severely burned areas. Topical collagenase should only be used on wounds associated with necrotic material. Although chemical debridement is a slower process than sharp (surgical) debridement, patients who have contraindications to surgery, whose wounds contain a relatively small amount of eschar, or who have a wound base of eschar mixed with granulation tissue might benefit from enzyme application. Topical collagenase may be used alone to break down the eschar, after sharp debridement, or in conjunction with mechanical debridement.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Collagenase digests collagen in collagen deposits. Lysis of collagen deposits occurs, as collagenases hydrolyze collagen in its native triple-helical conformation under physiological conditions. The exact mechanism by which collagenase treats moderate to severe cellulite is unknown.
 
A Dupuytren's cord is comprised mostly of collagen, and injection of collagenase into the cord may result in enzymatic disruption of the cord. In vitro data suggest that the AUX-I and AUX-II collagenases work synergistically to provide hydrolyzing activity towards collagen. The relative contributions of the individual collagenases (AUX-I or AUX-II) to the efficacy of collagenase in the treatment of Dupuytren's contracture are unknown.
 
The curvature deformity of Peyronie's disease is caused by a collagen plaque. Intralesional injection of collagenase into the penile plaque may enzymatically disrupt the plaque and reduce signs and symptoms. In vitro data suggest collagenase targets Types I and III collagen found in explanted tissues containing Peyronie's plaques. At higher doses and longer incubation times, non-fibrillar Type IV collagen is affected causing collagen lysis in small veins. However, in vitro and in vivo studies have not demonstrated structural damage to arteries, nerves, or large veins that contain Type IV collagen. In addition, collagen fragments derived from plaque disruption have been shown to generate increased vascular permeability, inflammatory responses, and regenerative changes; however, the effects of these changes have not been studied.
 
Collagenase also digests collagen in necrotic tissue. Moist, necrotic tissue provides a medium for infection, initiates an inflammatory response, places a phagocytic demand on the wound, and retards wound healing. As collagen composes roughly 75% of dry tissue weight and provides the framework to hold necrotic cells to the tissue bed, collagen removal by collagenase facilitates granulation tissue formation. Granulation is necessary for proper epithelialization. Human collagenases are produced by macrophages, fibroblasts, and keratinocytes, which are all necessary for wound healing. Unlike mammalian collagenase, Clostridial collagenase is secreted as an active enzyme and splits collagen into small peptides. The small peptides appear to enhance chemotaxis of macrophages, monocytes, fibroblasts, neutrophils, and keratinocytes. In vitro data suggest that Clostridial collagenase and the small peptides promote epithelization by enhancing keratinocyte migration to the wound.

Reconfirm the cord to be injected. The chosen injection site should be the area where the contracting cord is maximally separated from the underlying flexor tendons and where the skin is not intimately adhered to the cord.

Withdraw the needle tip from the cord and reposition it in a slightly more distal location (approximately 2 to 3 mm) to the initial injection in the cord and inject another one-third of the dose. Withdraw the needle tip from the cord and reposition it a third time proximal to the initial injection (approximately 2 to 3 mm) and inject the final portion of the dose into the cord.

Instruct the patient not to attempt to disrupt the injected cord by self-manipulation and to return the next day for follow-up and a finger extension procedure, if needed.
Inject only one cord at a time. If a patient has other palpable cords with contractures of MP or PIP joints, these cords may be injected with collagenase in a sequential order.
 
Follow-up:
If a contracture remains the day after the injection, perform a passive finger extension procedure to facilitate cord disruption. Put the patient's wrist is in the flexed position and apply moderate stretching pressure to the injected cord by extending the finger for approximately 10 to 20 seconds. For cords affecting the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint, perform the finger extension procedure when the metacarpophalangeal (MP) joint is in the flexed position. Avoid direct pressure on the injection site, as it will likely be tender. Local anesthesia may be used.
If the first finger extension procedure does not result in cord disruption, a second and third attempt can be performed at 5 to 10-minute intervals. However, no more than 3 attempts are recommended to disrupt a cord.
If the cord has not been disrupted after 3 attempts, a follow-up visit may be scheduled in approximately 4 weeks. If, at that subsequent visit, the contracted cord persists, an additional collagenase injection with finger extension procedures may be performed.
After the finger extension procedure(s), fit the patient with a splint and provide instructions for use at bedtime for up to 4 months to maintain finger extension. Also, instruct the patient to perform finger extension and flexion exercises several times a day for several months.

Site preparation:
Assess the need for pain control during wound manipulation. Enzyme application is usually not pain-inducing because tissue is not removed mechanically as with surgical debridement or wet-to-dry dressings. The process of washing and rinsing the wound could cause discomfort.
There is no need to remove all collagenase ointment from the wound, if it has been applied previously. Gently wash the site with a normal saline soaked sterile gauze pad. Do not scrub the site. Wash several times if povidone iodine, acidic detergents, or cleansers with heavy metal ions (e.g., mercury, silver) have been used previously on the site. Examples of such items include silver sulfadiazine, silver nitrate, aluminum acetate, Burow's solution, and povidone-iodine. Hydrogen peroxide and Dakin's solution do not interact with collagenase. After the wash, rinse the wound with normal saline.
If appropriate, remove loose necrotic debris with forceps and scissors.
If clinically indicated, thick eschar may be crosshatched using a #10 blade to expose more surface area to collagenase.
If an infection is present, surgical debridement may be needed initially; systemic antibiotics are of limited value for necrotic tissue, as it is avascular. If a topical antibiotic is applied to the site, the antibiotic is applied before collagenase. Antibiotic therapy should be chosen based on culture results. If the infection does not resolve, collagenase ointment should be withheld until culture results show infection resolution.
