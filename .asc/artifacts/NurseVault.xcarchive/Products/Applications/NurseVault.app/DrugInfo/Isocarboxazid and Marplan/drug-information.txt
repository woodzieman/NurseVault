Drug Class: Isocarboxazid is a traditional, nonselective, oral antidepressant of the monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI) class.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 60 mg/day PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): orthostatic hypotension, palpitations, syncope, agitation, akathisia, amblyopia, anxiety, ataxia, dizziness, drowsiness, euphoria, hallucinations, headache, insomnia, mania, memory impairment, myoclonia, neuritis, paresthesias, tongue discoloration, hyperhidrosis, photosensitivity, telangiectasia, dysuria, ejaculation dysfunction, impotence (erectile dysfunction), increased urinary frequency, orgasm dysfunction, urinary incontinence, urinary retention

In an Overdose: It has been suggested that plasma may be of value in the management of hypotension due to overdosage of isocarboxazid.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Isocarboxazid is contraindicated in patients with known hypersensitivity to isocarboxazid. Isocarboxazid is contraindicated in patients receiving other MAOI therapy or other drugs that possess MAOI-like activity (see Drug Interactions).

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Isocarboxazid is contraindicated in patients with known hypersensitivity to isocarboxazid.
 
Isocarboxazid is contraindicated in patients receiving other MAOI therapy or other drugs that possess MAOI-like activity (see Drug Interactions). Isocarboxazid is contraindicated when the patient is taking certain medications known to interact with MAO inhibitors (see Drug Interactions). Isocarboxazid is also contraindicated in combination with alcohol, excessive caffeine, or certain foods or beverages known to interact with MAO inhibitors (see Drug Interactions for ethanol and food). Alcoholic beverages, such as beer (including reduced-alcohol and alcohol-free beer), wine (red and white varieties), sherry, hard liquor, or liqueurs that contain tyramine can precipitate hypertensive reactions if consumed by patients during MAOI therapy. Therefore, isocarboxazid should be avoided in patients with active alcoholism. Ethanol intoxication should be avoided. Sudden and severe hypertensive episodes can also occur if foods containing high-pressor amines (e.g., tyramine or tryptophan) are consumed during therapy with isocarboxazid. Foods to avoid include: aged cheese; yeast extract; protein extract; soy sauce; fava bean or broad bean pods; smoked meats; pickled meats; chicken livers, smoked poultry; pickled poultry; smoked fish (lox, smoked salmon); pickled fish (pickled herring); fermented sausage (bologna, pepperoni, salami, summer sausage) or other fermented meat; bananas; avocados; and any over-ripe fruit. Following the discontinuation of isocarboxazid, dietary restrictions should continue for at least 2 weeks.
 
Isocarboxazid is contraindicated in patients with hypertension (including patients receiving antihypertensive agents), cardiac disease, or confirmed or suspected cerebrovascular defect (e.g., cerebrovascular disease, intracranial bleeding, increased intracranial pressure, recent head trauma, stroke), because of the possible adverse effects on blood pressure. MAOIs can also suppress chest pain that would otherwise serve as a warning of myocardial ischemia (e.g., angina) or acute myocardial infarction. Isocarboxazid is also contraindicated in patients with a history of headache because it can mask the headache associated with drug-related hypertensive reactions. Headaches during therapy may be the first symptom of a hypertensive reaction to an MAOI. Hypertensive crisis is the most serious side effect of MAOIs, and if palpitations, neck stiffness, chest pains, or headaches occur, isocarboxazid should be discontinued. Blood pressure should be routinely measured any time a patient receiving isocarboxazid complains of a headache, palpitations, diaphoresis, dizziness, neck stiffness or chest constriction, nausea, or vomiting.
 
Isocarboxazid should be used with caution in the elderly because of the increased possibility of cerebrovascular disease. Analysis of short-term studies in geriatric patients aged 65 years and older showed a decline in the risk of suicidal thinking and behavior during early phase treatment with SSRIs and other antidepressants, contrary to the data obtained in pediatrics and young adults.
 
Isocarboxazid is contraindicated in patients with pheochromocytoma, since the associated tumor secretes pressor substances (e.g., norepinephrine) whose metabolism may be inhibited by isocarboxazid. Impaired metabolism of norepinephrine can increase blood pressure, and potentially cause a hypertensive crisis.
 
Isocarboxazid is contraindicated in patients with a history of hepatic disease (e.g., jaundice, hepatitis) or in patients with abnormal liver function tests. Hepatic coma could occur if a MAO inhibitor is administered to patients with cirrhosis. There is a low incidence of altered hepatic function or jaundice in patients treated with isocarboxazid. Periodic liver function tests should be performed during isocarboxazid therapy; isocarboxazid should be discontinued at the first sign of hepatic dysfunction.
 
Isocarboxazid is contraindicated in patients with severe renal impairment or renal disease (e.g., renal failure, anuria). In patients with mild to moderate renal impairment, isocarboxazid should be used with caution because the reduction in renal excretion can increase the cumulative effects of the MAOI.
 
Isocarboxazid can change the pattern of seizures and should be used with caution in patients with epilepsy or a preexisting seizure disorder. Because isocarboxazid lowers the convulsive threshold in some animal experiments, suitable precautions should be taken if patients with a seizure disorder are treated. Isocarboxazid appears to have varying effects in epileptic patients; some have a decrease in frequency of seizures, while others have more seizures.
 
Anticholinergic effects during therapy with isocarboxazid may result in drowsiness or dizziness and the patient should use caution while driving or operating machinery while taking isocarboxazid until the effects of the drug are known. Effects may be additive to existing anticholinergic medications or CNS depressants.
 
Isocarboxazid should be used with caution in asthma or bronchitis because of possible changes in the blockade of sympathetic neurotransmission and marked pressor effects from beta-adrenergic bronchodilators.
 
Isocarboxazid should be used with caution in diabetes mellitus; increased insulin sensitivity may occur. Requirements for insulin or oral hypoglycemics may be decreased.
 
Isocarboxazid should be used with caution in patients with hyperthyroidism because sensitivity to pressor amines may be further increased.
 
Patients taking isocarboxazid should not undergo elective surgery requiring general anesthesia. Patients should also inform their dentists of MAOI treatment prior to pursuing dental work. Also, they should not be given cocaine (can block reuptake of monoamines into the adrenergic nerve terminals) or local anesthetics containing sympathomimetic vasoconstrictors (e.g., epinephrine). Combined hypotensive effects can occur with isocarboxazid and spinal anesthesia. Isocarboxazid should be discontinued at least 10 days before elective surgery.
 
All effective antidepressants can transform depression into mania or hypomania in predisposed individuals. It is generally believed (though not established in controlled trials) that treating such an episode with an antidepressant alone may increase the likelihood of precipitation of a mixed/manic episode in patients at risk for bipolar disorder. If a patient develops manic symptoms, isocarboxazid should be withheld and appropriate therapy initiated to treat the manic symptoms. Additionally, depression may be the presenting symptom of a mixed/manic episode of bipolar disorder. Patients should be adequately screened for bipolar disorder prior to initiating an antidepressant. Such screening should include a detailed psychiatric history, including a family history of suicide, bipolar disorder, and depression. Patients with depression or comorbid depression in the setting of other psychiatric illness being treated with antidepressants should be observed for clinical worsening and suicidality, especially during the initial few months of drug therapy, or at times of dose changes. Isocarboxazid may aggravate coexisting symptoms in depressive patients, such as anxiety and agitation, especially at the beginning of treatment. Dosage reduction and slow dosage titration may help limit hyperstimulatory effects. Isocarboxazid should be used cautiously in hyperactive or agitated patients, as well as in patients with schizophrenia or psychosis because it can cause excessive stimulation. Caregivers should be advised to closely observe the patient on a daily basis and to communicate immediately with the prescriber the emergence of agitation, irritability, unusual changes in behavior, or suicidality. It should be noted that isocarboxazid is not approved for use in treating bipolar depression.
 
Isocarboxazid has not been systematically studied in animals or humans for its potential for substance abuse, tolerance, or physical dependence. Drug dependency has occurred in patients using supra-therapeutic doses of isocarboxazid; some of these patients had a history of substance abuse. Patients should be evaluated for a prior history of drug abuse and those patients should be followed closely to identify potential signs of misuse or abuse (e.g., development of tolerance, increments of dose, drug-seeking behavior). Because a withdrawal syndrome has been observed in some patients after the discontinuation of MAOIs, abrupt discontinuation of long-term treatment is not recommended, dosage should be reduced gradually when appropriate.
 
Isocarboxazid is not FDA approved for use in children. The safety and effectiveness of isocarboxazid has not been established in pediatric patients less than 16 years old. In October 2004, the FDA directed manufacturers of all antidepressants to include a boxed warning detailing the risk of suicide in pediatric patients. A causal role has been established for antidepressants in inducing suicidality in pediatric patients. The risk of suicidality for these drugs was identified in a pooled analysis of 24 placebo-controlled trials (n=4400) lasting up to 16 weeks in pediatric patients with major depressive disorder (MDD), obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), or other psychiatric disorders. The analysis showed a greater risk of suicidality during the first few months of treatment in those receiving antidepressants (SSRIs and others). The average risk of such events on drug was 4% and 2% for placebo; however, no suicides occurred in these trials. Pooled analysis of short-term clinical trials during early phase treatment with SSRIs and other antidepressants in young adults (18 to 24 years) also showed an increased risk of suicidal thinking and behavior. The clinical need for an antidepressant in children or young adults for any use must be weighed against the risk of increased suicidality; patients who are started on therapy should be observed closely for clinical worsening, suicidality, or unusual changes in behavior, particularly within the first few months of starting therapy or at the time of dose increase or decrease; such observation would generally include at least weekly face-to-face contact with patients during the first 4 weeks of treatment, then every other week visits for the next 4 weeks, then at 12 weeks, and as clinically indicated beyond 12 weeks; additional contact by telephone may be appropriate between visits. It is unknown if the suicidality risk in children and young adults extends to longer-term therapy (i.e., beyond several months). The possibility of a suicide attempt is inherent in patients with depressive symptoms, whether these occur in primary depression or in association with another primary psychiatric disorder. All patients with a history of suicidal ideation or behaviors and those with a prominence of suicidal ideation prior to treatment are considered at an increased risk for suicidal ideation or attempts, and should be closely monitored during treatment with isocarboxazid. In patients who exhibit changes in symptoms, worsening of depression or emergent suicidality, a decision should be made to change or discontinue treatment. If discontinuing, medication should be tapered as rapidly as possible, but with recognition that abrupt discontinuation can also cause adverse symptoms. All antidepressants should be prescribed in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management in order to reduce the risk of overdose.
 
Parkinson's disease may be aggravated by isocarboxazid, because of the drug's induction of tremor, muscle spasm or myoclonic movement. Also, serious reactions have been reported when a non-selective MAOI was used in combination with anti-parkinsonian therapy. Use of a more selective MAOI, such as selegiline, may be indicated in patients with Parkinson's disease.
 
The use of metrizamide or iohexol (radiographic contrast administration) in patients receiving MAOIs can increase the risk of seizures by lowering the seizure threshold. MAOIs should be discontinued at least 48 hours before myelography and not resumed until 24 hours after the procedure.
 
Isocarboxazid is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. It is not known whether isocarboxazid can cause fetal harm when administered to a pregnant woman. Isocarboxazid should be used during pregnancy only when the benefit to the mother clearly outweighs the risk to the fetus.
 
Isocarboxazid is excreted in human milk; the effects of isocarboxazid on the nursing infant are unknown. Many drugs are excreted into breast milk and can pose a potential threat to the nursing infant. Isocarboxazid should be avoided in breast-feeding women except when clearly needed.

    Headline
    cardiovascular disease, headache
    General Information
    Isocarboxazid is contraindicated in any individual with a confirmed or suspected cerebrovascular defect, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, or history of headache. MAO inhibitors (MAOIs) can suppress anginal pain that would otherwise serve as a warning of myocardial ischemia. The most important reaction associated with MAOIs is the occurrence of hypertensive crisis. Blood pressure should be followed closely in all patients taking isocarboxazid to detect any pressor response. Isocarboxazid should be discontinued immediately if palpitations or frequent headaches occur during treatment as these symptoms may be prodromal of a hypertensive crisis. If a hypertensive crisis occurs, isocarboxazid should be discontinued, and therapy to lower blood pressure should be instituted immediately. Hypotension has also been observed during isocarboxazid therapy; symptoms of postural hypotension are seen most commonly, but not exclusively, in patients with pre-existing hypertension. Blood pressure usually returns rapidly to pre-treatment levels upon discontinuation of the drug. Dosage increases should be made more gradually in patients showing a tendency toward hypotension at the beginning of isocarboxazid therapy. Postural hypotension may be relieved by having the patient lie down until blood pressure returns to normal.

    Headline
    adolescents, children, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Safety and effectiveness of isocarboxazid have not been established in pediatric patients less than 16 years old. A boxed warning in the product label describes the risk of suicidality and suicidal ideation in children, adolescents, and young adults receiving antidepressants. In a pooled analysis of placebo-controlled trials of antidepressants (n = 4,500 pediatrics and 77,000 adults), there was an increased risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors in patients 24 years of age and younger receiving an antidepressant versus placebo, with considerable variation in the risk of suicidality among drugs. The difference in absolute risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors across different indications was highest in those with major depression. The need for an antidepressant in children, adolescents, or young adults for any use must be weighed against the risk of suicidality; it is unknown if this risk extends to long-term use. All patients should be monitored for symptom worsening or suicidality, especially at treatment initiation or after dose changes. Caregivers and/or patients should immediately notify the prescriber of changes in behavior or suicidal ideation. A change to the treatment regimen or discontinuation of isocarboxazid may be necessary in patients with emerging suicidality or worsening depression.

    geriatric
    General Information
    In general, dosage selection of antidepressant MAOIs for a geriatric adult should be cautious, usually starting at the low end of the dosing range, since older adults may be more likely to have underlying conditions or other drug therapy that might interact with traditional, nonselective MAOI therapy. Published data for MAOIs are insufficient in aged people, but the incidence of side effects such as orthostatic hypotension in geriatric adults with depression appears to be similar to that reported with tricyclic antidepressants; MAOIs are generally third-line treatments for adult depression. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates the use of antidepressants in residents of long-term care facilities (LTCFs). When an antidepressant is being used to manage behavior, stabilize mood, or treat a psychiatric disorder, the facility should attempt to taper the medication as outlined in the OBRA guidelines, unless a taper is clinically contraindicated. Dosages and durations of treatment used in the geriatric adult should be in accordance with prescribing labels, published literature recommendations, and expert guidelines.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    It is not known whether isocarboxazid can cause embryo-fetal harm when used during pregnancy. Reproductive toxicity has not been adequately evaluated in animals. However, other monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) have demonstrated animal risk. An increased risk of malformations was reported in the Collaborative Perinatal Project, which monitored 21 patient-child pairs with exposure to a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI) during the first trimester of pregnancy (isocarboxazid pair = 1). Limitations of this data include the small sample size, absence of malformation descriptions, and inclusion of isoniazid as an MAOI agent. Teratogenicity was not observed in 2 separate cases of in utero exposure to an MAOI. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant patients exposed to psychiatric medications; information about the registry can be obtained at http://womensmentalhealth.org/clinical-and-researchprograms/pregnancyregistry/antidepressants or by calling 1-844-405-6185.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use isocarboxazid with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of isocarboxazid or its metabolites in human milk, their effects on the breastfed child, or their effects on milk production. Due to the lack of data on isocarboxazid, other antidepressants are generally preferred for use during breast-feeding. Due to individual variability in response to antidepressants, it may be prudent to continue the existing regimen if ongoing treatment is deemed necessary during breast-feeding. Alternatives, such as sertraline, nortriptyline, or paroxetine, may be preferred, based on a pooled analysis showing undetectable or low drug concentrations in infant serum when used by a breast-feeding patient. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The most important reaction associated with MAO inhibitors is the occurrence of hypertensive crises, which have sometimes been fatal, resulting from the coadministration of MAOIs and certain drugs and foods (see Drug Interactions). These crises are characterized by some or all of the following symptoms: occipital headache which may radiate frontally, palpitations, neck stiffness or soreness, nausea/vomiting, diaphoresis (sometimes with fever and sometimes with cold, clammy skin), and photophobia. Either sinus tachycardia or sinus bradycardia may be present, and associated palpitations or constricting chest pain (angina) and dilated pupils (mydriasis) may occur. Intracranial bleeding, sometimes fatal, has been reported in association with the increased blood pressure. Blood pressure should be followed closely in patients taking isocarboxazid to detect any pressor response. Therapy should be discontinued immediately if palpitations or frequent headaches occur during isocarboxazid therapy as these symptoms may be prodromal of a hypertensive crisis. If a hypertensive crisis occurs, isocarboxazid should be discontinued, and therapy to lower blood pressure should be instituted immediately. Although there has been no systematic study of the treatment of hypertensive crises with MAOIs, phentolamine has been used and is recommended at a dosage of 5 mg IV, given slowly to avoid hypotensive effects. Fever should be managed by external cooling. Other symptomatic and supportive measure may be necessary in some cases.Orthostatic hypotension is common side effect of MAOIs, and is most likely to occur in patient with preexisting hypertension. If a tendency toward hypotension is detected during routine monitoring of initial isocarboxazid therapy, the dose should be increased more gradually. Dose reduction may counteract hypotensive effects, but if orthostatic hypotension persists or worsens, the MAOI should be withdrawn. Blood pressure usually returns rapidly to pretreatment levels upon discontinuation of the drug. Postural hypotension can be alleviated by lying down until the blood pressure returns to normal. Dizziness is often worse if the patient changes positions too rapidly. It has been suggested that plasma may be of value in the management of hypotension due to overdosage of isocarboxazid. Administration of pressor amines such as levarterenol may be of limited value; and their effects may be potentiated by isocarboxazid.Peripheral edema, swelling of the feet and legs, may be only a transient adverse reaction to isocarboxazid, subsiding within a week. Peripheral edema may progress to overt congestive heart failure. Persistent edema may be a sign of impaired water excretion due to SIADH, which has been reported infrequently with isocarboxazid.Anticholinergic effects during therapy with isocarboxazid may result in a number of adverse reactions such as drowsiness, confusion, constipation, blurred vision or mydriasis, xerostomia (dry mouth), sweating, urinary retention, weakness, or headache. Patients with these symptoms should have their blood pressure monitored to rule out potential hypertensive crisis.Sexual dysfunction, which may include orgasm dysfunction (anorgasmia or delayed orgasm) in both males and females; ejaculation dysfunction (retarded or no ejaculation), priapism, and possible impotence (erectile dysfunction), can occur during therapy with isocarboxazid.Appetite stimulation and weight gain may occur during therapy with isocarboxazid.Diarrhea may also occur during therapy with isocarboxazid, but is also a possible withdrawal symptom.Shaking or tremor can result from action of MAOIs on the vasomotor centers. Other CNS effects of isocarboxazid include myoclonia, hyperreflexia, twitching, fatigue, and insomnia (or other sleep disturbance). Nystagmus, paresthesias, and euphoria and restlessness have been reported infrequently. In rare instances, hallucinations have been reported with high dosages, but have disappeared upon dose reduction or discontinuation. All effective antidepressants can precipitate mania in predisposed individuals suffering from depression. If mania occurs, the antidepressant should be held and appropriate therapy to treat the manic symptoms initiated. Depression may obscure hypomania, which may emerge as the depression is lifted. Description
  Text
  Isocarboxazid is a traditional, nonselective, oral antidepressant of the monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI) class. Due to the dietary restrictions required for MAOI use, as well as their side effect profile and the potential for serious drug-drug and drug-food interactions, MAOIs are primarily considered an option for major depressive disorder (MDD) in adults refractory to treatment with other antidepressants (e.g., third-line treatment), and they may be considered in some patients with combined anxiety/depression resistant to other treatments. All product labels for antidepressants, including traditional MAOI antidepressants, contain a boxed warning related to an increased risk of suicidality in children, adolescents, and young adults during the initial stages of therapy when treating depression or other conditions; therefore, the necessity of pharmacologic therapy versus the potential risks should be carefully considered in these populations.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Isocarboxazid is an oral, non-selective monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI) with a hydrazine structure. Isocarboxazid, similar to phenelzine, tranylcypromine, and pargyline, inhibits monoamine oxidase (MAO) by irreversible binding. Like other irreversible inhibitors, the MAO enzyme activity cannot be restored until the body replaces the enzyme through new enzyme synthesis, which takes a period of time after drug discontinuation, usually requiring up to 2 weeks. The basis for the antidepressant activity following MAO inhibition is primarily attributed to the increased concentrations of biogenic amines (e.g., neurotransmitters such as epinephrine, norepinephrine, and dopamine) at various storage sites in the central nervous system and sympathetic nervous system. Increased brain amines produce central nervous system stimulation and subsequent therapeutic effects. Since MAO is a complex enzyme system and is widely distributed throughout the body, it is unknown whether MAO inhibitors exert their antidepressant effects via MAO inhibition alone or in conjunction with other pharmacologic actions.
 
Isocarboxazid is a nonselective MAOI that desensitizes alpha- and beta-adrenergic and serotonin receptors. The delay in therapeutic response to MAOI drugs, up to 4 weeks, may be accounted for by alterations in receptor characteristics, rather than increased neurotransmitter concentrations. In vivo and in vitro studies demonstrate inhibition of MAO in the brain, heart, and liver. MAOI drugs also have a variable hypotensive action, probably due to central inhibition of the vasomotor centers. Inhibition of MAO in the GI tract and liver may result in systemic absorption of large amount of tyramine, which may result in severe hypertension because of massive release of norepinephrine. MAOIs also interfere with the hepatic metabolism of many drugs.
