Drug Class: Cyclophosphamide is a bifunctional alkylating agent related to mechlorethamine (nitrogen mustard).

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC), oral; Common adult doses: Oncology: The maximum dose varies and is indication- and protocol-specific.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): anemia, bone marrow suppression, leukopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, immunosuppression, infection, influenza, sepsis, bladder spasm, cystitis, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, renal failure, renal tubular necrosis, cardiac tamponade, cardiomyopathy, cardiotoxicity, heart failure, myocarditis, pericardial effusion, pericarditis, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, bradycardia, cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction, palpitations, qt prolongation, supraventricular tachycardia (svt), ventricular fibrillation

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Hypersensitivity and anaphylactic reactions (e.g., acute bronchospasm, hypotension, or urticaria) have been reported with cyclophosphamide; death has also been reported in association with anaphylactic reactions. Possible cross-sensitivity with other alkylating agents has been reported. Emetic risk: Pediatrics: Extravasation risk: Nonvesicant Hazardous drug: NIOSH 2024 List: Table 1

      Routes
        Heading
        Intravenous dosage
        Route Age Groups
          Heading
          Adults
          Dosage Description
          250 mg/m2/day on days 1 to 3 in combination with fludarabine 25 mg/m2/day on days 1 to 3 and rituximab 375 mg/m2 IV on day 0 (the day prior to fludarabine and cyclophosphamide (FC) on cycle 1, then 500 mg/m2 IV on day 1 on cycles 2 to 6 repeated every 28 days (R-FC) for 6 cycles has been studied in randomized, phase III trials. Grade 3 or 4 neutropenia and leukopenia occurred significantly more often with R-FC therapy. The median PFS time (primary endpoint) was 30.6 months with R-FC compared with 20.6 months with FC (HR = 0.65; 95% CI, 0.51 to 0.82; p < 0.001) in another multinational, randomized, phase III trial in 552 CLL patients who had relapsed or refractory disease following 1 prior line of therapy. All patients in this study received tumor lysis and antibiotic/antiviral prophylaxis. At a median follow-up time of 25 months, the median OS was not significantly different between treatment arms (R-FC, median time not reached; FC, 52 months). There were more treatment-related deaths reported with R-FC therapy (19 vs. 14 deaths).

        Dosage Description
        Although FDA-approved for AML and CML, cyclophosphamide does not have extensive activity in primary myeloid malignancies and has generally been replaced with more effective agents (e.g., cytarabine/anthracyclines for AML and hydroxyurea/interferon alfa for CML). Cyclophosphamide may be used as part of conditioning regimens prior to bone marrow transplantation (BMT) in patients with AML or CML. Common doses of cyclophosphamide for conditioning regimens include 120 to 200 mg/kg IV divided as 60 mg/kg/day IV for 2 days or 50 mg/kg/day IV for 4 days or 3.6 to 6 g/m2 IV divided over 3 to 4 days. Doses greater than 120 mg/kg over 2 days (i.e., more than 60 mg/kg/day for 2 days) are associated with an increased risk of severe cardiac toxicity.

        Heading
        Adults, Adolescents, and Children
        Dosage Description
        600 mg/m2/day IV on days 1 and 2, or on days 1, 2, and 3 in combination with bleomycin and dactinomycin (BCD regimen) has been incorporated into multiple treatment protocols for osteogenic sarcoma. In the POG-8651 protocol, 106 patients (younger than 30 years old) with previously untreated nonmetastatic high-grade osteogenic sarcoma were randomized to receive multiagent chemotherapy either before or after surgical resection. Multiagent chemotherapy consisted of 3 days of BCD each cycle in sequence with doxorubicin and cisplatin, and high-dose methotrexate. Event-free survival (EFS), the primary endpoint, was not significantly different between the treatment arms and reached 69% at 5 years in the postoperative group. In a comparison of the Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer Center T-10 and T-12 protocols, 73 patients (ages 4.6 to 36.4 years) with previously untreated, high-grade osteogenic sarcoma received BCD on days 1 and 2 of each cycle as part of a multiagent chemotherapy regimen in sequence with doxorubicin and cisplatin, high-dose methotrexate, and surgical resection. The 5-year EFS was 78% and 73% in the T-12 and T-10 protocols, respectively. The use of BCD alone has also been studied in 8 pediatric patients (ages 9.1 to 16.4 years old) with previously treated metastatic osteogenic sarcoma. Patients received 1 to 5 courses of BCD. No tumor regression could be measured for any of the patients, and progressive tumor enlargement was demonstrated in 2 patients.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Hypersensitivity and anaphylactic reactions (e.g., acute bronchospasm, hypotension, or urticaria) have been reported with cyclophosphamide; death has also been reported in association with anaphylactic reactions. Possible cross-sensitivity with other alkylating agents has been reported. Cyclophosphamide is contraindicated in patients who have demonstrated a previous hypersensitivity to it.
 
Cyclophosphamide should be used cautiously in patients who have had previous myelosuppressive therapy such as chemotherapy or radiation therapy. Severe bone marrow suppression, including neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, is a relative contraindication to cyclophosphamide depending upon the disease. Patients with aggressive leukemia or lymphoma may require treatment with cyclophosphamide despite severe bone marrow suppression. Continued use of cyclophosphamide is contraindicated in patients with severely depressed bone marrow function. Only clinicians experienced in the use of cancer or immune suppression therapy should use cyclophosphamide for their patients. The hematological status (leukocyte and platelet counts) of the patient should be monitored closely during cyclophosphamide therapy; leukocyte counts as high as 3000—4000/mm3 can be maintained during therapy. Increased susceptibility to infection and possible development of secondary neoplastic disease, especially bladder cancer, myeloproliferative, or lymphoma/lymphoproliferative malignancies or disorders may result from immunosuppression. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with chemotherapy. Cyclophosphamide receipt may not be appropriate for patients who have or who develop a viral, bacterial, fungal, protozoan, or helminthic infection; cyclophosphamide receipt interruption, dose reduction, or initiation delay may be needed.
 
Myelosuppressive effects of cyclophosphamide can increase the risk of infection or bleeding; therefore, dental work should be delayed until blood counts have returned to normal. Patients, especially those with dental disease, should be instructed in proper oral hygiene, including caution in use of regular toothbrushes, dental floss, and toothpicks.
 
Intramuscular injections should not be administered to patients with platelet counts 50 years of age may be at increased risk of cardiotoxicity. Acute cardiac toxicity has been reported with high doses of cyclophosphamide or when the drug is used as part of an intensive, combination chemotherapy regimen (see Adverse Reactions).
 
Vaccination during chemotherapy or radiation therapy should be avoided because the antibody response is suboptimal. When chemotherapy is being planned, vaccination should precede the initiation of chemotherapy by >= 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment, urinary tract obstruction
    General Information
    Cyclophosphamide is contraindicated in individuals with urinary tract obstruction; use with caution, if at all, in individuals with an active urinary tract infection (UTI). Use cyclophosphamide with caution in patients with severe renal impairment (CrCl 10 to 24 mL/minute) or renal failure; monitor these patients for signs and symptoms of increased toxicity. Urotoxicity (e.g., bladder ulceration, necrosis, fibrosis, contracture, secondary cancer) which may be fatal can occur with short-term or long-term use and may require an interruption of cyclophosphamide therapy or cystectomy. Regularly monitor urinary sediment for the presence of erythrocytes and other signs of urotoxicity and/or nephrotoxicity. Aggressive hydration with forced diuresis and frequent bladder emptying can reduce the frequency and severity of bladder toxicity; mesna has been used to prevent severe bladder toxicity. Discontinue cyclophosphamide if severe hemorrhagic cystitis occurs.
    Headline
    drug-induced bone marrow suppression, radiation therapy
    General Information
    Do not start therapy with cyclophosphamide until patients have recovered from drug-induced bone marrow suppression including neutropenia (ANC 1,500 cells/mm3 or less) and thrombocytopenia (platelets less than 50,000 cells/mm3). Cyclophosphamide can cause severe myelosuppression and bone marrow failure, which may be worse in patients receiving concomitant chemotherapy and/or radiation therapy. Monitor complete blood counts at baseline and during treatment. An interruption of therapy and/or dose reduction or discontinuation of therapy may be necessary if hematologic toxicity occurs. Consider primary and secondary prophylaxis with a granulocyte colony-stimulating factor in patients who have an increased risk of neutropenic complications; antimicrobial prophylaxis may also be indicated.
    Headline
    infection
    General Information
    Use cyclophosphamide with caution in patients with an active infection; treatment can result in myelosuppression which may increase the severity of an infection and impede recovery.
    Headline
    cardiac disease, geriatric
    General Information
    Use cyclophosphamide with caution in patients with a history of cardiac disease and other risk factors for cardiotoxicity including heart failure, coronary artery disease, uncontrolled hypertension, thoracic radiation therapy, and previous treatment with other cardiotoxic agents. Geriatric patients (patients 65 years or older) and patients receiving high doses of cyclophosphamide are also at increased risk for cardiotoxicity. Cardiotoxicity including myocarditis, myopericarditis, pericardial effusion, cardiac tamponade, and congestive heart failure have been reported with cyclophosphamide use. Cardiac arrhythmias including supraventricular arrhythmias (e.g., atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter) and ventricular arrhythmias (e.g., QT prolongation associated with ventricular tachyarrhythmia) have also occurred following cyclophosphamide-containing therapy. Monitor patients with risk factors for cardiotoxicity and with pre-existing cardiac disease.
    Headline
    pulmonary disease
    General Information
    Use cyclophosphamide with caution in patients who have a history of pre-existing pulmonary disease; pneumonitis/interstitial lung disease, pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary veno-occlusive disease, and other forms of pulmonary toxicity have been reported in patients treated with cyclophosphamide. Monitor patients for new or worsening respiratory symptoms including fever, cough, or dyspnea.
    Headline
    abdominal radiation therapy, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C; total bilirubin greater than 3 times the upper limit of normal (ULN) and any AST) or hepatic failure may have decreased efficacy of cyclophosphamide due to reduced conversion to its active 4-hyroxyl metabolite; monitor these patients for treatment efficacy. When administering IV cyclophosphamide, consider the alcohol content of the specific product in patients with hepatic impairment. Additionally, patients with hepatic impairment including hepatic failure (as well as those who have received prior abdominal radiation therapy and those with a low performance status) may be at increased risk for veno-occlusive disease in patients receiving cyclophosphamide.
    Headline
    major surgery, planned procedure
    General Information
    Notify the anesthesiologist if a patient has been treated with cyclophosphamide within 10 days of a planned procedure or major surgery requiring general anesthesia. Cyclophosphamide causes a marked and persistent inhibition of cholinesterase activity; prolonged apnea may occur with concurrent depolarizing muscle relaxants (e.g., succinylcholine). Cyclophosphamide may also cause impaired wound healing.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Advise patients to use caution when driving or operating machinery or performing other dangerous duties or activities requiring coordination and concentration immediately after a cyclophosphamide infusion. Cyclophosphamide solution for injection contains ethanol to dissolve the active ingredients; take this into account for patients in whom ethanol ingestion should be avoided or minimized, including pediatric patients. The amount of alcohol varies between products; consult individual product labeling for alcohol content. Monitor patients for signs of alcohol intoxication during and after treatment.
    Headline
    children, infants, neonates
    General Information
    Some formulations of cyclophosphamide solution for injection are not indicated for use in neonates, infants, or children due to the alcohol and propylene glycol content; other cyclophosphamide products may have a different amount of alcohol or no alcohol. Check the specific product labeling to verify the appropriateness of use in pediatric patients. The systemic exposure to alcohol may be increased due to alcohol and propylene glycol being metabolized by the same enzymes, which may be particularly significant in pediatric patients.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Cyclophosphamide may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy, based on its mechanism of action and published reports in humans and animals. Human data indicate that first-trimester exposure to cyclophosphamide has been associated with miscarriage and congenital malformations involving the skeleton, palate, limbs, and eyes. Fetal growth restriction and neonatal toxicity, including leukopenia, anemia, pancytopenia, severe bone marrow hypoplasia, and gastroenteritis, have also been reported following in utero exposure. In animal studies (mice, rats, rabbits, and monkeys), administration during organogenesis at doses at or below those used clinically (based on body surface area) produced multiple malformations, including neural tube defects, limb and digit abnormalities, other skeletal anomalies, cleft lip and palate, and reduced skeletal ossification. Advise pregnant patients of the potential risk to the fetus. The American Society of Clinical Oncology provides additional information on the use of certain medications for the management of cancer during pregnancy.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with cyclophosphamide. Cyclophosphamide can cause fetal harm. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking cyclophosphamide and for at least 1 year after the last dose. For those that can cause pregnancy in others, contraception should be used during cyclophosphamide treatment and for 4 months after the last dose. Cyclophosphamide may cause male and female infertility, because of its interference with oogenesis and spermatogenesis. Treatment may cause amenorrhea (transient or permanent) associated with decreased estrogen and increased gonadotropins. Menstrual cycles often resume within a few months after treatment discontinuation, but the risk of premature menopause increases with age. Oligomenorrhea has also been reported. Animal data suggest that oocytes exposed during follicular development may remain affected for an extended period, potentially increasing the risk of failed pregnancy or malformations after treatment cessation. The exact duration of follicular development in humans is not known, but it may be longer than 12 months. Cyclophosphamide may also cause oligospermia or azoospermia, typically with increased gonadotropins and normal testosterone levels. These effects may be reversible, although recovery of spermatogenesis can take several years. The probability of male sterility increases if treatment lasts beyond 90 days. In pediatric patients, secondary sexual development generally occurs normally in both sexes. However, ovarian fibrosis and loss of germ cells have been reported in prepubescent females after prolonged exposure, and those who retain ovarian function may have an increased risk of premature menopause. Prepubescent males may develop oligospermia or azoospermia and, in some cases, testicular atrophy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking cyclophosphamide and for 1 week after the last dose, due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in the breastfed child. Cyclophosphamide is present in human milk. Neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, low hemoglobin, and diarrhea have been reported in children breastfed by people receiving treatment with cyclophosphamide.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Hematologic toxicity is one of the major and dose-limiting adverse effects of cyclophosphamide. It can manifest as leukopenia, neutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia, or pancytopenia. The neutrophil nadir usually occurs 8 to 14 days from treatment and recovers by day 18 to 25. Anemia and thrombocytopenia occur less commonly than neutropenia. Cyclophosphamide is considered relatively "platelet-sparing" and significant thrombocytopenia is uncommon, but does occur with high doses of cyclophosphamide (> 30 mg/kg). Cyclophosphamide does exhibit some stem-cell sparing effects and cumulative hematologic effects are rarely if ever seen with continued dosing. Cyclophosphamide also causes significant immunosuppression with lymphopenia and suppression of B-cell function. It has been suggested that most cytotoxic cyclophosphamide regimens do not uniformly produce profound immunosuppression and recovery of immune function is rapid. However, continuous dosing is more likely to lead to severe lymphopenia and immunosuppression. Due to myelosuppressive and immunosuppressive effects, patients treated with cyclophosphamide are at risk for serious, and sometimes fatal, infection and sepsis, which may involve bacterial, fungal, helminthic, protozoan, or viral infections. Fever without documented infection may occur in neutropenic patients. Asthenia and malaise may occur in conjunction with hematologic effects.Hemorrhagic cystitis can develop as a result of cyclophosphamide therapy, and rarely it can be severe and even fatal. Fibrosis of the bladder, sometimes extensive, also may develop with or without accompanying cystitis. Atypical bladder cells may appear in the urine. Hematuria usually resolves in a few days after cyclophosphamide treatment has stopped, but it may persist. The urinary effects of cyclophosphamide are dependent upon the dose and duration of cyclophosphamide therapy; reactions may be noted after one large, single dose or as a result of long-term, low-dose oral therapy. Medical and/or surgical supportive therapy may be required to treat protracted cases of severe hemorrhagic cystitis. Discontinuation of cyclophosphamide therapy is usually needed in cases of severe hemorrhagic cystitis. In a study of patients receiving oral cyclophosphamide 2 mg/kg/day with prednisone, 50% developed non-glomerular hematuria. The median time to development of hematuria was 37 months; 34 months in smokers and 56 months in nonsmokers. Non-glomerular hematuria was gross in 44% and microscopic in 56% and can develop years after cyclophosphamide therapy is discontinued. The median dose before the development of hematuria was 124 g. Bladder toxicity from cyclophosphamide is attributed to the build-up of a toxic metabolite, acrolein, in the bladder. Acrolein seeks nucleophilic binding sites such as the bladder mucosa. Preventive measures include ensuring adequate hydration, frequent voiding, and the administration of mesna. Hemorrhagic ureteritis and renal tubular necrosis have been reported and usually resolve following cessation of cyclophosphamide therapy.Infertility due to gonadal suppression can occur in men or women receiving cyclophosphamide. Cyclophosphamide interferes with oogenesis and spermatogenesis. Cyclophosphamide-induced sterility may be irreversible in some patients. Menstural irregularity or amenorrhea associated with decreased estrogen and increased gonadotropin secretion develops in the majority of women treated with cyclophosphamide. Affected patients generally resume regular menses within a few months of stopping therapy. Girls treated with cyclophosphamide during prepubescence generally develop secondary sexual characteristics normally, usually have regular menses, and have been able to conceive. Ovarian fibrosis with apparent complete loss of germ cells after prolonged cyclophosphamide during late prepubescence has been reported. Men treated with cyclophosphamide may develop oligospermia or azoospermia associated with increased gonadotropin but normal testosterone secretion. Sexual potency and libido are not affected. Boys treated with cyclophosphamide during prepubescence generally develop secondary sexual characteristics normally, but may have oligospermia or azoospermia and increased gonadotropin secretion. Some degree of testicular atrophy may occur. Cyclophosphamide-induced azoospermia is reversible in some patients, although the reversibility may not occur for several years after the cessation of therapy. Men temporarily rendered sterile by cyclophosphamide have subsequently fathered normal children.A syndrome resembling SIADH causing hyponatremia and water/fluid retention can occur in patients receiving moderate-to-high doses of cyclophosphamide, usually doses > 50 mg/kg. No changes in ADH have been reported, therefore, this reaction may be related to a renal tubular effect. The syndrome is characterized by decreased urine output noted within 6—8 hours along with significant weight gain, increased urine osmolality, and decreased serum osmolality. The syndrome is usually self-limited and resolves within 12—16 hours. However, altered mental status and seizures related to hyponatremia have occurred. Furosemide has been used to correct hyponatremia despite restriction of free water. This adverse effect can complicate the hydration therapy being given to treat or prevent hemorrhagic cystitis.Teratogenesis is a serious concern if cyclophosphamide is administered during pregnancy. The teratogenic action seems to be due to direct cytotoxicity to the developing fetus. Both normal and malformed newborns have been reported following cyclophosphamide therapy, even in the first trimester. However, there appears to be an increased risk of malformations if the drug is given during the first trimester. In one case, multiple fetal anomalies occurred when the mother received only two doses of cyclophosphamide very early during the first trimester. In another case, a normal infant was delivered to a mother who had received cyclophosphamide-containing chemotherapy during the third trimester. Pancytopenia developed in an infant exposed to cyclophosphamide and five other antineoplastics during the third trimester.Alkylating agents, alone or in combination with other agents or treatment modalities, have been associated with the development of secondary malignancy, including leukemia, lymphoma, and solid tumors (e.g., skin cancers and reticulum cell sarcomas). The most common secondary malignancies associated with cyclophosphamide are bladder cancer and myeloproliferative or lymphoproliferative malignancies. Secondary malignancies are most commonly detected in patients treated for primary myeloproliferative or lymphoproliferative malignancies or nonmalignant disease in which immune processes are involved. In some cases, the secondary malignancy developed several years after cyclophosphamide treatment had been discontinued. Bladder cancer develops in 5% of patients treated with cyclophosphamide, a 31-fold increase in incidence compared to the general population. Bladder cancer generally occurs in patients who have had some degree of hemorrhagic cystitis. In 6 of 7 patients who developed bladder cancer, the total cumulative dose exceeded 100 g, and the cumulative duration of treatment exceeded 2.7 years. The possibility of cyclophosphamide-induced malignancy should be considered in any benefit-risk assessment for the use of the drug.Cyclophosphamide has been associated with severe cardiotoxicity characterized by rapid-onset congestive heart failure, which can be intractable and fatal within 10—14 days. Acute cardiac toxicity has been reported with doses as low as 2.4 g/m2 to as high as 26 g/m2. The syndrome is more common in patients receiving doses > 100 mg/kg during a 48 hour period and most often in patients receiving total doses > 200 mg/kg as part of bone marrow transplant preparative regimens. In some cases with high doses of cyclophosphamide, severe and sometimes fatal congestive heart failure has occurred with the first dose. The hearts of patients who have died of this complication are dilated and show patchy transmural hemorrhage and pericardial effusion. Hemopericardium has occurred secondary to hemorrhagic myocarditis and myocardial necrosis. Pericarditis has been reported independent of any hemopericardium. No residual cardiac abnormalities appear to be present in patients surviving episodes of apparent cyclophosphamide-induced cardiotoxicity. There is no evidence of cumulative effects to the heart with repeated or low doses of cyclophosphamide. Elderly patients or patients > 50 years of age and patients who have been previously treated with anthracyclines may be at increased risk.Adverse GI reactions associated with cyclophosphamide therapy include anorexia and nausea/vomiting. The incidence and severity of nausea/vomiting is dose-related and may not begin until 8 hours after cyclophosphamide administration. Antiemetic therapy should be given prophylactically and for several days following cyclophosphamide. Less frequently reported GI reactions include abdominal pain or discomfort and diarrhea. Rarely reported adverse GI reactions include jaundice (hyperbilirubinemia), mucositis including esophagitis and stomatitis, hemorrhagic colitis (typhlitis), drug-induced hepatitis, and hepatic necrosis. Prior therapy with azathioprine has been associated with hepatic toxicity during cyclophosphamide treatment. Dose-related hepatotoxicity usually consists of elevated hepatic enzymes. The incidence is 33% with cyclophosphamide doses < 400 mg/m2 and 77% with higher doses. The use of high-dose cyclophosphamide alone or in combination with other agents for bone marrow transplantation has been associated with hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD).Alopecia occurs commonly in patients treated with cyclophosphamide Almost all patients have some thinning of the hair. Approximately 50% of patients develop complete alopecia. Hair can be expected to grow back after treatment or even during treatment with cyclophosphamide, but it may be a different color or texture (usually more curly). Skin rash (unspecified), urticaria, skin hyperpigmentation, and nail discoloration occur occasionally in patients receiving the drug. Cyclophosphamide may cause impaired wound healing. An injection site reaction consisting of phlebitis may occur; however, cyclophosphamide is not a vesicant. Anaphylactoid reactions including anaphylactic shock and death have occurred during cyclophosphamide therapy. Potential cross-sensitivity with other alkylating agents may occur. Hypersensitivity reactions are thought to be induced by IgE-mediated histamine release. The phosphoramide mustard metabolite is thought to be responsible for the reactions. Very rare cases of Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis have been reported during post-marketing surveillance; due to the spontaneous nature, a definitive causal relationship to cyclophosphamide has not been established.Occasionally, cyclophosphamide administration has been associated with rhinitis and associated nose and throat irritation. Symptoms are self-limiting resolving in 1—3 days, although some patients require treatment with corticosteroids and antihistamines.Interstitial pneumonitis, which can be fatal, has been reported in patients receiving low doses of cyclophosphamide for prolonged periods of time. The onset is usually insidious. Interstitial pulmonary fibrosis has been reported in patients receiving high-dose cyclophosphamide over a prolonged period. Alkylating metabolites and acrolein may be responsible for cyclophosphamide pulmonary toxicity. Although the incidence of cyclophosphamide-induced pulmonary toxicity is low, it has been associated with a 50% mortality rate.Transient, reversible blurred vision has been reported during high-dose cyclophosphamide therapy.Cyclophosphamide-induced cellular destruction can result in tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) which includes hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, and hypocalcemia. Uric acid nephropathy, acute renal failure due renal tubular obstruction, metabolic acidosis, and nephrolithiasis can also occur. These problems are more severe when a highly chemosensitive disease is treated (i.e., leukemia or lymphoma). Aggressive alkalinization of the urine and use of allopurinol can prevent urate nephropathy; close monitoring of serum electrolytes and renal function is recommended.

    General Information

    Headline
    amenorrhea, azoospermia, gonadal suppression, infertility, oligospermia, testicular atrophy
    General Information
    Cyclophosphamide interferes with oogenesis and spermatogenesis and may cause infertility due to gonadal suppression in both sexes. Prepubescent children treated with cyclophosphamide typically develop secondary sexual characteristics normally. The risk for sustained amenorrhea in patients with lupus receiving monthly intermediate-dose cyclophosphamide is 12% for women younger than 25 years old and is greater than 50% for women over 30 years old. Description
  Text
  Cyclophosphamide is a bifunctional alkylating agent related to mechlorethamine (nitrogen mustard). It is indicated for the treatment of adult and pediatric patients with various malignant diseases including acute lymphocytic leukemia, breast cancer, malignant lymphomas, ovarian cancer, multiple myeloma, leukemias, mycosis fungoides, neuroblastoma, and retinoblastoma. It is also indicated for the treatment of biopsy proven minimal change nephrotic syndrome in pediatrics patients who failed to adequately respond to or are unable to tolerate adrenocorticosteroid therapy. The safety and effectiveness of cyclophosphamide for the treatment of nephrotic syndrome in adults or other renal disease has not been established. The use of cyclophosphamide is contraindicated in patients with urinary outflow obstruction. Cyclophosphamide was approved by the FDA in 1959.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Cyclophosphamide is a synthetic alkylating agent that is chemically related to the nitrogen mustards. It is an inactive prodrug that requires activation to release the active metabolite, phosphoramide mustard. Hydroxylation of cyclophosphamide by the cytochrome P450 system generates 4-hydroxycyclophosphamide, which coexists in equilibrium with its tautomer, aldophosphamide. These metabolites are able to freely diffuse into cells, where aldophosphamide is decomposed into 2 compounds, phosphoramide mustard and acrolein. Phosphoramide mustard produces interstrand and intrastand DNA crosslinks, responsible for the cytotoxic properties of cyclophosphamide. Acrolein is the cause of hemorrhagic cystitis. Cyclophosphamide causes immunosuppression via lymphopenia (both B-cells and T-cells) and selective suppression of B-lymphocyte activity. Decreased immunoglobulin secretion has been described in patients treated with low-dose cyclophosphamide for autoimmune diseases. Cyclophosphamide also influences T-lymphocyte activity, although the exact mechanism has not been established. Cyclophosphamide can suppress some T-cell function (e.g., graft-versus-host response and delayed hypersensitivity). In addition, cyclophosphamide (or an activated analog, 4-hydroperoxycyclophosphamide) can enhance the immune response by inhibiting suppressor T-cells. Some studies have shown that cyclophosphamide can increase the efficacy of certain immunotherapy regimens by decreasing tumor-induced suppressor T-cells. Other data suggest that cyclophosphamide induces release of factors that enhance tumor-specific T-cells, specifically type I interferons.

  Special Populations
    Name
    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    The mean total body clearance of cyclophosphamide was 45 liters/kg (+/- 8.6 liters/kg) in patients with severe hepatic impairment (mean bilirubin 3.5 mg/dL and mean AST 90 international units/liter), which is a 40% decrease compared to those with normal hepatic function (mean bilirubin 0.5 mg/dL and mean AST 10 international units/liter). The elimination half-life was 12.5 hours (+/- 1 hour) in patients with severe hepatic impairment, representing a 64% increase from the control group. Patients with severe hepatic impairment have reduced conversion of cyclophosphamide to the active 4-hydroxyl metabolite, potentially reducing efficacy.
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    Systemic exposure to cyclophosphamide increases as renal function decreases. Following a 1-hour IV infusion, the mean dose-corrected AUC increased by 38% in patients with moderate renal impairment (CrCl 25 to 50 mL/min) and by 64% to 77% in patients with severe renal impairment (CrCl 10 to 24 mL/min) compared to the control group (CrCl 80 mL/min or higher). Cyclophosphamide is dialyzable. In four patients on long-term hemodialysis, the dialysis clearance of cyclophosphamide averaged 104 mL/min (calculated by arterial venous difference and actual drug recovery in dialysate); this is in the range of the metabolic clearance of 95 mL/min for cyclophosphamide. A mean of 37% of the administered dose was removed during a 4-hour hemodialysis period. The elimination half-life of cyclophosphamide was 3.3 hours during hemodialysis, which is a 49% reduction from that reported for uremic patients (6.5 hours). Cyclophosphamide exposure increased by 23% in patients receiving hemodialysis (CrCl less than 10 mL/min) compared to controls (CrCl 80 mL/min or higher).
  Text
  Cyclophosphamide is administered orally and intravenously. It is approximately 20% protein-bound, with no dose-dependent changes in protein binding; some metabolites are protein-bound to an extent greater than 60%. The cyclophosphamide volume of distribution approximates total body water and is approximately 30 to 50 liters; it has an elimination half-life of 3 to 12 hours, with total body clearance ranging from 4 to 5.6 liters/hour. Cyclophosphamide appears to induce its own metabolism, which results in an increase in the total clearance, increased formation of the active 4-hydroxycyclophosphamide metabolite, and shortened half-life values following repeated administration at 12- to 24-hour intervals. Cyclophosphamide is primarily excreted as metabolites, with 10% to 20% excreted as unchanged drug in the urine and 4% in the bile following IV administration.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes: CYP2A6, CYP2B6, CYP3A, CYP2C9, CYP2C19
Cyclophosphamide is a prodrug that is primarily (75%) biotransformed in the liver to both therapeutically active (N-hydroxylated) and therapeutically inactive but neurotoxic (N-dechlorethylated) metabolites. CYP2B6 is primarily responsible for metabolism to the active alkylating metabolites, 4-hydroxycyclophosphamide and aldophosphamide; CYP2A6, CYP3A, CYP2C9, and CYP2C19 also contribute to cyclophosphamide metabolism. Aldophosphamide and 4-hydroxycyclophosphamide undergo oxidation by aldehyde dehydrogenases to form inactive metabolites carboxyphosphamide and 4-ketocyclophosphamide, respectively. Aldophosphamide can also undergo beta-elimination to form active metabolites phosphoramide mustard and acrolein, which is urotoxic. This spontaneous conversion can be catalyzed by albumin and other proteins. The N-dechloroethylation process for cyclophosphamide appears catalyzed primarily by CYP3A4. Less than 5% of cyclophosphamide may be directly detoxified by side chain oxidation, leading to the formation of inactive metabolites 2-dechloroethylcyclophosphamide (neurotoxic). At high doses, the fraction of parent compound cleared by 4-hydroxylation is reduced, resulting in non-linear elimination of cyclophosphamide in patients.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Baseline Hepatic Impairment
Mild to moderate hepatic impairment: It appears no dosage adjustments are necessary.
Severe hepatic impairment (total bilirubin greater than 3 mg/dL and any AST): Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments are not available from the manufacturer; however, the conversion of cyclophosphamide to the active 4-hydroxyl metabolite may be reduced, potentially decreasing efficacy.
  Renal Impairment
  Baseline Renal Impairment
Mild to moderate renal impairment (CrCl 25 to 89 mL/min): It appears dosage adjustments are not necessary.
GFR of 30 mL/minute/1.73 m2 or less for the treatment of antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody associated vasculitis: Guidelines recommend a dose of 0.5 mg/kg/day PO or 2.5 mg/kg/dose IV.
Severe renal impairment (CrCl 10 to 24 mL/min): Monitor patients for signs and symptoms of toxicity. Some references recommend reducing the dose by 25%.
End stage renal disease (CrCl less than 10 mL/min): Specific recommendations are not available from the manufacturer. Some references recommend reducing the dose by 50%.
Hemodialysis: Different references have recommended reducing the dose by 25% to 50%. The amount of cyclophosphamide and its metabolites removed during hemodialysis varies depending upon the dialysis system used and the time dialyzed. Consider the use of a consistent interval between cyclophosphamide administration and hemodialysis to reduce variability in exposure.
