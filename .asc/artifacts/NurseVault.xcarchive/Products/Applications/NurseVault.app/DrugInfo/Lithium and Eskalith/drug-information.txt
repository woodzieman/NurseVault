Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Lithium is classified as pregnancy category D. It crosses the placental barrier freely and has been associated with fetal toxicity. Data from lithium birth registers suggest an increased incidence of neonatal goiter and an increase in cardiac anomalies, especially Ebstein's anomaly. In some cases, however, lithium therapy may be warranted during pregnancy, but patients should be warned about possible damage to the fetus. If possible, lithium should be withheld during the first trimester. Women of childbearing age who require lithium therapy should be counseled about becoming pregnant. Lithium is secreted into breast milk and is contraindicated in women who are breast-feeding. Symptoms of lithium toxicity, including ECG changes, have been observed in some breast-fed infants, whose mothers were taking lithium.Lithium therapy can cause hyponatremia by reducing resorption of sodium by the renal tubules. Lithium should be used with caution in hyponatremia. An adequate fluid intake and maintenance of salt balance are essential during lithium therapy. Lithium clearance depends on GFR. Lithium should be used with caution in patients with renal impairment or renal disease; dosage reductions are recommended. Reevaluation of treatment may be necessary depending on renal function. In addition urinary retention may delay lithium excretion and cause toxicity. Chronic lithium therapy may lead to a reduction in renal concentrating ability, with polyuria and polydipsia. Dehydration can lead to lithium toxicity. Lithium should be used with caution in patients requiring sodium restriction in their diet. Any factor leading to dehydration can affect lithium balance, such as protracted sweating or diarrhea, which may require supplemental fluid and salt intake or, alternatively, reduction or suspension of lithium therapy. Other factors that raise body temperature may lead to dehydration, such as fever, exercise, sauna or hot baths. A concurrent infection with elevated body temperature may also require reduced lithium dosage or temporary stoppage of therapy.Serum lithium concentrations are closely correlated with the toxicity of the drug; a concentration above 1.5 mmol/L is likely to produce some degree of toxicity and a concentration above 3 mmol/L is considered a medical emergency. Therefore, routine serum lithium concentration monitoring should be performed throughout treatment. Typically, concentrations are drawn during dosage adjustments once the new steady state is achieved (i.e., approximately 5 days), or whenever toxicity is suspected. Following stabilization, many patients only require follow-up monitoring every 3—6 months. The clinician and patient should be familiar with common factors which can alter lithium concentrations thereby producing toxicity including dehydration, concurrent use of some prescription and over-the-counter (OTC) medications, and change in sodium intake. The patient should be instructed to contact a health care provider for further evaluation if they experience potential symptoms of toxicity including nausea/vomiting, diarrhea, confusion, course tremor, delirium, hallucinations, muscle fasciculations, seizures, or ataxia. The clinician should ensure that facilities are available for prompt and accurate evaluation of lithium concentrations prior to initiating treatment with the drug.Lithium may be the preferred agent over many other mood stabilizers in some instances due to its relatively mild effects on cognition. However, fatigue and lethargy may occur during therapy with the drug. Patients beginning treatment with lithium should be instructed to avoid driving or operating machinery until the effects of the drug are known.Lithium can affect thyroid function. Lithium should be used cautiously in patients with thyroid disease, especially hypothyroidism, or in the elderly because these patients may be more susceptible to this adverse effect.Lithium should be used cautiously in patients with cardiac disease. Changes in the electrocardiogram can occur at therapeutic dosages (T wave depression or inversion), although these changes are almost always reversible (see Adverse Reactions). Rarely, sinus bradycardia, sinoatrial block, and AV block have been reported with use of therapeutic lithium dosages; patients with sick sinus syndrome may be at increased risk for these adverse events. More serious adverse cardiac effects such as intraventricular conduction defect, hypotension and cardiac arrhythmias are rare and are usually associated with severe, acute intoxication.Patients with psoriasis should receive lithium with caution. Lithium therapy is known to exacerbate psoriasis.Because lithium can cause seizures and/or tremors, it should be used cautiously in patients with a preexisting seizure disorder or parkinsonism. These conditions may mask the neurotoxicity of lithium.Lithium can produce mental status changes. Patients need to be informed about the possibility of reduced mental alertness affecting their ability to drive or use machinery safely, especially when lithium therapy is instigated.Lithium should be used cautiously in patients with organic brain syndrome or CNS impairment. Lithium has produced an encephalopathic syndrome (similar to NMS) in some patients receiving concomitant neuroleptic therapy (see Drug Interactions). Brain damage produced by this syndrome may be irreversible.Bipolar disorder has an inherent risk of suicidal ideation. Patients with a history of alcohol or substance abuse, a history of attempted suicide, or those who are socially isolated may be at greatest risk. High risk patients may require lithium treatment within a hospital environment or monitoring by family or friends.Lithium should be used with caution in patients with a history of leukemia. There have been rare reports of lithium-induced hematologic neoplasms, including acute leukemia; however, a causal relationship has not been established.

    Headline
    conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature, diarrhea, fever, hyponatremia, hypovolemia, renal failure, renal impairment, sodium restriction
    General Information
    Lithium toxicity can be fatal; close monitoring of lithium levels throughout treatment is essential. The risk of lithium toxicity is increased in patients with recent onset of a fever-causing illness, impaired renal function, volume depletion (hypovolemia) or dehydration, and changes in sodium or potassium concentrations (particularly hyponatremia). Patients should be encouraged to maintain a normal diet including salt and adequate fluid intake (e.g., 2,500 to 3,000 mL) during treatment. Lithium should be used cautiously in patients with pre-existing hyponatremia or patients requiring fluid or sodium restriction. Any factor leading to dehydration can affect lithium balance, such as protracted sweating or diarrhea. If such conditions occur, supplemental fluid and salt should be administered under careful medical supervision in addition to a reduction or suspension of lithium therapy until the condition is resolved. Other conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature may lead to dehydration, such as fever, exercise, sauna or hot baths. Concurrent infection with elevated body temperature may also result in reduced lithium dosage requirements or temporary discontinuation of therapy. Lithium clearance is dependent on the glomerular filtration rate; therefore, lithium should be used cautiously in patients with renal impairment or renal failure, and renal function should be monitored throughout treatment. The dosage should be modified depending on clinical response and the degree of renal impairment. In patients with mild to moderate renal impairment (CrCl 30 to 89 mL/minute), start with a dose lower than that of a patient with normal renal function and titrate slowly with frequent monitoring. Avoid use of lithium in patients with severe renal impairment (CrCl less than 30 mL/minute) or renal failure. Progressive or sudden changes in renal function, even those occurring within the normal range, indicate the need for re-evaluation of treatment.
    Headline
    serum lithium concentration monitoring
    General Information
    Clinicians should ensure laboratory facilities are available to promptly measure lithium levels before initiating therapy. Routine serum lithium concentration monitoring is essential, as lithium has a narrow therapeutic index. Lithium levels above 1.5 mEq/L are likely to cause some degree of toxicity; levels greater than 3 mEq/L are associated with severe toxicity, including confusion, seizures, coma, or death. Early signs of toxicity include fine tremor, lightheadedness, incoordination, and weakness, while moderate symptoms include giddiness, drowsiness, hyperreflexia, muscle twitching, blurred vision, tinnitus, and slurred speech. Toxicity may persist despite discontinuation and can involve other organs: cardiovascular (ECG changes, myocarditis), renal (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, renal failure), respiratory (dyspnea, pneumonia, respiratory failure), and gastrointestinal (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea). Lithium levels should not be permitted to exceed 2 mEq/L, and levels should be kept below 1.5 mEq/L if possible. After treatment initiation, levels should be obtained after 3 days of treatment, then regularly monitored and adjusted based on response and tolerability. Some patients, especially geriatric individuals, may show toxicity at therapeutic levels. Factors such as illness, dehydration, renal impairment, cardiac disease, medication interactions, and electrolyte shifts can alter lithium levels. Patients should be instructed to recognize early toxicity signs, such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, confusion, coarse tremor, hallucinations, seizures, or ataxia, and contact their healthcare provider immediately upon the development of any of these symptoms.
    Headline
    hypothyroidism
    General Information
    Lithium can affect thyroid function and should be used cautiously in patients with hypothyroidism. Because of the potential adverse effects of lithium on thyroid functioning and to rule out a mood disorder due to endocrine dysfunction, it is advisable to obtain a baseline thyroid panel prior to initiating lithium treatment, with periodic monitoring throughout lithium therapy (e.g., every 6 to 12 months). Ensure that patients with pre-existing hypothyroidism are adequately treated with thyroid supplementation, and adjust thyroid supplementation as necessary during lithium treatment. If hypothyroidism develops during lithium therapy, supplemental thyroid treatment may be used.
    Headline
    bradycardia, Brugada syndrome, cardiomyopathy, cardiovascular disease, congenital long QT syndrome, coronary artery disease, females, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, QT prolongation
    General Information
    Use lithium with caution in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g.,cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk. Changes in electrocardiogram (ECG) parameters can occur at therapeutic dosages of lithium (isoelectricity or rarely T-wave inversion, reversible flattening, QT prolongation). Lithium should be avoided in patients with a suspected or established diagnosis of Brugada syndrome, a cardiac disorder characterized by abnormal ECG findings and a risk of sudden death. There have been post-marketing reports of the unmasking of Brugada syndrome during lithium administration. Consultation with a cardiologist is recommended prior to initiation of lithium treatment in patients suspected of having Brugada syndrome, those with risk factors for Brugada syndrome (e.g., family history of Brugada syndrome, unexplained syncope, or a family history of sudden unexplained death before the age of 45 years), and patients who develop unexplained syncope or palpitations after starting lithium therapy. Caution is also advised in patients with significant cardiovascular disease, as this may increase the risk of lithium toxicity.
    Headline
    psoriasis
    General Information
    Patients with psoriasis should receive lithium with caution. Lithium therapy has been reported to induce or to exacerbate psoriasis in patients predisposed to this disease; the mechanism by which lithium can exacerbate plaque psoriasis is not known.
    Headline
    delirium, dementia
    General Information
    An encephalopathic syndrome, characterized by weakness, lethargy, fever, tremulousness and confusion, extrapyramidal symptoms, leukocytosis, and elevated serum enzymes, BUN and fasting blood glucose, has occurred in patients treated with combined therapy of lithium and an antipsychotic (most notably haloperidol). In some instances, the syndrome was followed by irreversible brain damage. Because of a possible causal relationship between these events and the concomitant administration of lithium and antipsychotics, patients receiving this combined treatment, or those with organic brain syndrome (such as dementia or delirium) or other CNS impairment should be monitored closely for early evidence of neurological toxicity and treatment should be discontinued promptly if such signs appear. This encephalopathic syndrome may be similar to or the same as neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
    Headline
    Parkinson disease, seizure disorder
    General Information
    Neurotoxic side effects of lithium, such as ataxia, tremor, and seizure have been well described in the literature. These conditions can mimic the symptoms of a seizure disorder or Parkinson disease. Elderly patients in particular may present with tremor that can be confused with Parkinson disease during chronic therapy. Careful examination of the patient may be needed to rule out lithium toxicity and to confirm suspected diagnoses when symptoms overlap.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Lithium may be the preferred agent over many other mood stabilizers in some instances due to its relatively mild effects on cognition. However, fatigue, lethargy, and sedation may occur during treatment. Patients should be instructed to avoid driving or operating machinery and performing other activities requiring coordination and concentration during treatment initiation and following dosage increases, until the patient knows how the drug affects them.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Consider starting with lower doses and titrating the lithium dosage slowly in geriatric adults while frequently monitoring serum lithium concentrations, renal function, and signs of lithium toxicity. Geriatric adults are more likely to have a greater frequency of concurrent conditions, renal impairment, or multiple medication use which may predispose this population to lithium toxicity. Older adults often respond to a reduced dosage of lithium, and may exhibit adverse effects and signs of toxicity at lithium serum levels ordinarily tolerated by younger patients. A benefit versus risk assessment is essential before administering lithium to those with significant renal or cardiovascular disease, severe debilitation or dehydration, sodium depletion, or to patients receiving diuretics, ACE inhibitors, or NSAIDs, since these increase the risk for lithium toxicity. Lithium should be avoided if the creatinine clearance less than 30 mL/minute, especially if the condition requires adherence to a low-sodium diet. Clinical studies of lithium did not include sufficient numbers of geriatric adults to determine whether they respond differently from younger adults. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates psychotropic use in long-term care facilities. When lithium is used to manage behavior, stabilize mood, or treat a psychiatric disorder, tapering as outlined in the OBRA guidelines should be attempted unless clinically contraindicated. Indications for use, dosage, and monitoring of therapy should align with prescribing labels, published literature recommendations, and expert guidelines.
    Headline
    obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    Lithium use during pregnancy is associated with potential adverse pregnancy outcomes and neonatal complications. However, because lithium is highly effective for some conditions, its use may be necessary to prevent relapse in a pregnant person. Lithium freely crosses the placenta. Early reports indicated a significantly increased risk of congenital cardiac malformations, particularly Ebstein anomaly, with lithium exposure during the first trimester; however, subsequent data indicate that the increased risk of cardiac malformations, including Ebstein anomaly, is likely to be small. 62732 If lithium is continued during pregnancy, consider fetal echocardiography between 16 and 20 weeks of gestation following first-trimester lithium exposure because of the potential risk of cardiac malformations. American Psychiatric Association (APA) guidelines recommend serum alpha-fetoprotein screening for neural tube defects before 20 weeks of gestation, with amniocentesis and targeted sonography performed if alpha-fetoprotein values are elevated in pregnant patients receiving lithium. Monitor serum lithium concentrations closely throughout pregnancy because concentrations change at different stages, and adjust the dosage as needed. During the first and second trimesters, pregnant patients may have increased lithium clearance and, consequently, lower serum concentrations. Lithium exposure near obstetric delivery may be associated with neonatal toxicity, including hypotonia or floppy-infant manifestations, respiratory or feeding difficulties, lower Apgar scores, and central nervous system or neuromuscular complications. FDA-approved labeling recommends decreasing the lithium dosage or discontinuing lithium therapy 2 to 3 days before the expected delivery date to reduce neonatal lithium concentrations and the risks of maternal and neonatal toxicity associated with rapid peripartum changes in vascular volume and renal lithium clearance. However, some experts note that evidence supporting routine discontinuation at delivery is lacking and recommend individualized management with careful monitoring of lithium concentrations and fluid status. Some data suggest that higher infant lithium concentrations are associated with lower Apgar scores, more central nervous system and neuromuscular complications, and longer hospital stays. Lithium may be restarted postpartum at the preconception dosage in medically stable patients, with close monitoring of serum lithium concentrations and individualized dosage adjustment.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with lithium. Lithium may cause fetal or neonatal harm. FDA-approved labeling recommends evaluating pregnancy status before initiating lithium. American Psychiatric Association (APA) treatment guidelines for bipolar disorder recommend pregnancy testing before lithium initiation and encourage effective contraception for female patients of childbearing age receiving pharmacologic treatment.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero during late pregnancy may experience lithium-induced toxicity, sometimes referred to as floppy baby syndrome. Manifestations may include hypotonia, respiratory distress syndrome, cyanosis, lethargy, feeding difficulties, depressed neonatal reflexes, apnea, and bradycardia. Monitor affected neonates and provide supportive care until lithium is excreted and clinical signs resolve; this may take up to 14 days. Rare reported neonatal effects after in utero lithium exposure include thyroid dysfunction or goiter, cardiac arrhythmias, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, jaundice, hypoglycemia, and polyuria. FDA-approved labeling recommends decreasing the lithium dosage or discontinuing treatment 2 to 3 days before the expected delivery date to reduce the risk of lithium toxicity in the neonate. However, some experts note that evidence supporting routine discontinuation at delivery is lacking and recommend individualized management with careful monitoring.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use lithium with caution during breast-feeding. Lithium concentrations in human milk are approximately 40% to 45% of the lactating person's plasma concentrations, and infant plasma concentrations may reach 30% to 40% of the lactating person's concentrations. Signs and symptoms of lithium toxicity, including hypertonia, hypothermia, cyanosis, and ECG changes, have been reported in breastfed children exposed to lithium through human milk. If breast-feeding is chosen, monitor the child for lithium toxicity. Consider regular monitoring of lithium concentrations, thyroid function, hydration, and urine output. Dehydration may increase the risk of toxicity. Discontinue breast-feeding if toxicity occurs. Rare adverse effects, including increased thyroid-stimulating hormone concentrations, have been reported in breastfed infants. Increased prolactin concentrations have been observed in lactating people taking lithium, but the effect on milk production is unknown. In a study of 11 lactating people receiving lithium 600 to 1,500 mg/day for bipolar disorder, the estimated infant dose through milk ranged from 0% to 30% of the weight-adjusted dose received by the lactating person.

Some atypical antipsychotics, including olanzapine, are indicated as adjunctive therapy to mood stabilizers such as lithium. However, it is advisable to monitor patients for neurotoxicity during co-administration. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) has been observed occasionally during concurrent use of lithium and either atypical or conventional antipsychotics. Additive extrapyramidal effects have also been noted. Early case reports described an encephalopathic syndrome consisting of delirium, tremulousness, dyskinesia, seizures, leukocytosis, weakness, hyperpyrexia, confusion, extrapyramidal symptoms, elevations in laboratory values (e.g., liver function tests, blood urea nitrogen, fasting blood sugar) and, in some cases, irreversible brain damage, during use of lithium and conventional antipsychotics, particularly haloperidol. Subsequent rare reports of NMS or NMS-like reactions have been described during co-administration of lithium and atypical antipsychotics (e.g., risperidone, olanzapine, clozapine). Following resolution of NMS, there are isolated instances of re-emergence of symptoms following re-initiation of lithium as monotherapy. Lithium may be a risk factor for antipsychotic-induced NMS; however, this hypothesis has not been confirmed. In many reported cases, confounding factors have been present (e.g., previous history of NMS, high dose therapy). The ability of antipsychotics alone to precipitate NMS and the rarity of the condition further complicate assessment of lithium as a risk factor. Olanzapine does not influence the pharmacokinetics of lithium.

  General Information
  NOTE: Lithium can be a toxic drug. Many adverse reactions are dose- and concentration-related. The increase in serum concentrations that can precipitate mild to moderate adverse reactions to those producing moderate to severe reactions is small, ranging from below 1.5 mEq/L to 2 mEq/L and above. Serum concentrations should be monitored in all patients receiving lithium therapy.Some adverse reactions to lithium occur regularly and at therapeutic serum concentrations. These include: fine hand tremor, xerostomia, dysgeusia, weight gain, polydipsia, polyuria, mild nausea/vomiting, impotence, libido decrease, nephrotic syndrome, and diarrhea. Gastrointestinal effects often resolve with continued therapy and can be alleviated by taking divided doses with meals. Administration of controlled-release preparations may also minimize gastrointestinal effects such as nausea/vomiting. If diarrhea or polyuria lead to dehydration, lithium toxicity is possible. Polydipsia and polyuria are more likely to occur during the first few weeks of treatment, and soon disappear. Polyuria may recur unexpectedly as nephrogenic diabetes insipidus , with constant thirst and a large output of very dilute urine. While nephrogenic diabetes insipidus may occur at any time during treatment, long-term lithium therapy (> 15 years) is a risk factor for its development, and long-term treatment may be more likely to be associated with irreversible cases. The incidence in patients treated long-term with lithium is estimated at roughly 10%. Chronic kidney disease or lowered creatinine clearance (CrCl < 60 ml/min), multiple daily doses (versus single-daily doses), and higher lithium trough concentrations may also be risk factors for the development of this syndrome; however, the mechanism by which lithium produces the syndrome has not been defined. If diabetes insipidus occurs, lithium should be discontinued if possible and the patient treated symptomatically.Adverse reactions that suggest lithium toxicity include: anorexia; visual impairment or disturbance; drowsiness; muscular weakness; fasciculations or myoclonia; ataxia; dysarthria or slurred speech; stupor or coma; tremor; confusion or impaired cognition; seizures; and arrhythmias. Serum lithium concentrations should not be allowed to rise above 2 mEq/L during the acute treatment phase. Serum concentrations above 3 mEq/L can produce adverse effects involving multiple organ systems.Benign, reversible leukocytosis is routinely noted during lithium therapy. This effect appears to be secondary to an increased number of circulating neutrophils. There have been rare reports of lithium-induced secondary malignancy, including acute leukemia; however, a causal relationship has not been established. Unless the white blood cell count rises to 100,000 cells/mm3, there is no need to discontinue lithium therapy.At therapeutic dosages, ECG abnormalities may occur in up to 30% of patients receiving lithium and are usually limited to reversible ST-T wave changes (T wave depression and rarely, T wave inversion). T wave depression may be accompanied by the development of U waves. These changes are thought to be due to intracellular myocardial potassium depletion by displacement with lithium. Development of conduction and rhythm abnormalities are rare at therapeutic dosages and may be secondary to underlying cardiac disease. Sinus bradycardia, sinoatrial block, and rarely, first-degree AV block have been reported with use of lithium at therapeutic dosages. Cardiovascular effects from lithium intoxication may include T wave inversion and/or flattening, intraventricular conduction defect, ventricular arrhythmias, hypotension, and, rarely, cardiovascular collapse. In almost all reported cases, dysrhythmias, hypotension, and circulatory collapse occurred in the setting of severe, acute intoxication in patients with a clinical course complicated by a prolonged comatose state.Approximately 4% of patients develop goiter while taking lithium. Hypothyroidism has been reported, and preexisting hypothyroidism is a relative contraindication to lithium therapy. There may be an early and transient decrease in serum-bound iodine and free thyroxine, which returns to normal in a few weeks. A few patients may go on to develop a diffuse enlargement of the thyroid. Careful monitoring of thyroid function is necessary. Signs of hypothyroidism may be detected; they include: dry rough skin, alopecia, hoarseness, mania, mental depression, sensitivity to cold, swelling of the feet and lower legs, swelling of the neck.Teratogenesis is a serious concern with lithium. Its use during the first trimester has been associated with an increased incidence of congenital defects of the cardiovascular system, including the rare Ebstein's anomaly. Neural tube defects were reported in two infants out of 105 live births in 138 pregnancies. Since it is an inorganic ion, lithium can freely cross the placenta, however, because lithium is highly effective for some conditions, in some cases, its use may be necessary. If at all possible, its use during the first trimester should be avoided. Its use near term may produce reversible symptoms of lithium toxicity in the neonate.Pseudotumor cerebri has also been reported in patients receiving lithium.Lithium enhances the uptake of tryptophan, increases the synthesis of serotonin, and may also enhance the release of serotonin in the CNS. In rare cases, lithium may cause symptoms consistent with the serotonin syndrome when used with other medications (e.g., SSRIs) that enhance serotonin activity within the CNS. Lithium has been reported to increase 5-hydroxytryptamine metabolites in the cerebrospinal fluid and may interact pharmacodynamically with SSRIs resulting in serotonin syndrome.Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS), (e.g., high fever, muscle rigidity, altered consciousness, tachycardia, autonomic dysfunction, rhabdomyolysis, acute renal failure, respiratory failure) has been reported in a patient with acute lithium toxicity due to overdose. However, the last lithium level in the patient prior to NMS onset was 0.5 mEq/L. It is suggested that elevated lithium levels in the brain tissue resulted in NMS. The patient was also taking fluoxetine, but this agent was not involved in the overdose. The authors speculate that fluoxetine may have influenced the serotonin-dopamine balance in the brain to possibly augment the development of NMS. NMS has also been described in patients taking concomitant lithium and neuroleptics or tricyclic antidepressants .

Extrapyramidal symptoms such as dystonic reaction, cogwheel rigidity, and choreoathetoid movements (choreoathetosis) are infrequent, and seizures are rare. Pseudotumor cerebri (benign intracranial hypertension and papilledema) has been reported; however, the exact frequency is unknown. If a patient presents with severe headache, changes in vision, and papilledema, it is advisable to evaluate the patient for the possibility of pseudotumor cerebri. If undetected, this condition may result in enlargement of the blind spot, constriction of visual fields, and blindness due to optic atrophy. Lithium should be discontinued, if possible if this syndrome occurs. CNS and neuromuscular effects that may be early signs of lithium toxicity (less than 2 mEq/L) include drowsiness, myasthenia, coarse hand tremor, and incoordination. Ataxia and tinnitus may become evident at higher concentrations (2 to 3 mEq/L).

Description
  Text
  Lithium is a monovalent cation similar to sodium and potassium. For clinical use, it is administered orally as the carbonate and the more water-soluble citrate salts. Immediate-release lithium is indicated for the treatment of manic episodes, and as maintenance treatment for Bipolar I Disorder in adults and pediatric patients 7 years and older; clinically, the drug has been used in children as young as 6 years of age. Extended-release lithium is indicated for the treatment of Bipolar I Disorder in adults and pediatric patients 12 years and older. Lithium is useful as an adjunct for refractory depression and for reducing the risk of suicide and all-cause mortality in patients with mood disorders and is used off-label for these purposes. While other clinical uses for lithium have been described, the drug is rarely used off-label for non-psychiatric medical purposes due to its narrow therapeutic range, lack of sufficient trials for these uses, and the propensity for lithium toxicity. Lithium toxicity is closely related to serum lithium concentrations and can occur at doses close to therapeutic concentrations. A boxed warning requires that facilities for prompt and accurate determinations of lithium concentrations be readily available during use of the drug. Due to risk for toxicity, requires close monitoring of serum lithium concentrations, electrolytes, and renal function. Common conditions that can cause lithium toxicity include urinary retention, hyponatremia, dehydration, and infection. The use of lithium dates back to 1841, and lithium chloride was used as a salt substitute during the 1940's. After several cases of lithium toxicity were associated with indiscriminate use, lithium use fell out of favor until 1949 when it was discovered that lithium was beneficial in the management of mania. However, lithium prescription products were initially approved by the FDA in 1970.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Lithium competes at cellular sites with sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium ions. Lithium competes with these ions at intracellular binding sites, at protein surfaces, at carrier binding sites, and at transport sites. At the cell membrane, lithium readily passes through sodium channels, and high concentrations can block potassium channels. Although the mechanism of the antimanic and antidepressant action in the CNS is not known, evidence suggests that the drug interferes with the synthesis, storage, release, and reuptake of monoamine neurotransmitters. Lithium enhances the uptake of tryptophan, increases the synthesis of serotonin, and may also enhance the release of serotonin in the CNS. Lithium does not possess sedative, depressant, or euphoriant effects. Onset of the acute antimanic effect is usually seen in 5 to 7 days, and the full therapeutic effect is established in 10 to 21 days.
 
Lithium administration increases renal sodium and potassium clearance. These effects are attenuated by a compensatory increase in aldosterone after 2 to 3 days. Lithium does not affect sodium reabsorption in either the ascending limb of the loop of Henle or in the distal tubule. A decrease in renal concentrating ability occurs in 30% to 50% of patients while receiving lithium; it often produces a mild nephrogenic diabetes insipidus manifested as polyuria. Lithium-induced diabetes insipidus is thought to be due to inhibition of vasopressin-induced adenylate cyclase activity in the medullary collecting tubule of the nephron. Since lithium is more toxic and a less reliable agent than demeclocycline, lithium should be considered a last choice for the treatment of SIADH.
 
Lithium enhances granulocyte production via stimulation of monocyte colony stimulating factor production. Lithium produces an increase in the total neutrophil pool and each of its components in the bone marrow and circulation. Leukocytosis peaks within 7 to 10 days of initiating therapy and the WBC count will return to baseline 7 to 10 days after discontinuing lithium.
 
The actions of lithium on the heart generally give rise to adverse effects. The most common electrocardiogram (ECG) changes include flattening or inversion of the T-waves. This manifestation is thought to be due to lithium-induced inhibition of potassium cellular reuptake leading to intracellular hypokalemia. Because lithium displaces potassium, an extracellular hyperkalemia is seen and, since the intracellular:extracellular potassium balance is shifted, cardiac arrest is possible at lower than usual degrees of hyperkalemia.

  Text
  Lithium salts are administered orally. Lithium carbonate is most commonly used because it has a longer shelf-life and contains more lithium on a weight basis than do other salts. Lithium has negligible protein binding (15%) and the distribution space of lithium approximates that of total body water. The kinetics of lithium distribution into the brain remain largely anecdotal and there is large inter-patient variability. A review of studies using magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) in humans indicated that initial brain lithium concentrations were about half those in serum, occasionally rising to 75% to 80%. Brain concentrations varied significantly over 48 hours, mirroring serum concentrations. Ten days after the last dose, lithium was not detectable in the brain. Because lithium may take up to 24 hours to distribute into brain tissue, occurrence of acute toxicity symptoms may be delayed. Lithium is concentrated into bile by a factor of 2 to 10 compared with blood concentrations. There appears to be long-term retention in the bone, from where it is eliminated over several months after discontinuation of lithium. In adults, the half-life is 18 to 36 hours. The half-life may be increased or decreased during acute episodes of mania or depression. Lithium is not metabolized and more than 95% of a dose is eliminated by the kidneys. The amount eliminated through sweat, saliva, and feces is negligible under normal circumstances. Many factors can affect lithium clearance including hyponatremia or hypernatremia, dehydration, and diuretic use. Both creatinine clearance (CrCl) and body weight are important factors in predicting lithium clearance. Lithium is freely filtered by renal glomeruli, but it also undergoes significant renal tubular reabsorption (about 80%). Renal impairment, such as a decrease in the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) will reduce lithium elimination. It was once thought that tubular reabsorption occurred only in the proximal tubule but interaction studies with HCTZ and furosemide revealed substantial lithium reabsorption also occurs in the ascending limb of the loop of Henle.
 
A serum lithium concentration between 0.6 mEq/L and 1 mEq/L is the usual range for maintenance treatment with immediate-release formulations. For sustained-release preparations, a range between 0.6 mEq/L to 1.2 mEq/L is recommended by the manufacturers. The manufacturers of the sustained-release products recommend lithium concentrations of 0.8 mEq/L to 1.5 mEq/L for the treatment of acute mania, while the manufacturers of the immediate-release formulations recommend a range of 0.8 mEq/L to 1.2 mEq/L for acute mania. The dosage should be adjusted according to efficacy and tolerability while ensuring that lithium serum concentrations are maintained in the therapeutic range. Toxicity is likely in most patients when levels exceed 1.5 mEq/L, although symptoms of lithium toxicity can appear in some patients with serum concentrations of 1 mEq/L or less. The narrow therapeutic ratio and inter-patient variations make individual monitoring and dosage adjustment essential.

    Maximum dosage not well defined. Individualize dosage to patient disease and response, and lithium concentrations; avoid concentration above 1.5 mEq/L. For most adults, a total daily dosage of 1,200 to 1,800 mg/day PO (in divided doses) will provide appropriate lithium concentrations.

    Children
    Max Dosage
    7 to 12 years: Maximum dosage not well defined. Individualize dosage to patient disease and response, and lithium concentrations; avoid concentration above 1.5 mEq/L. In children weighing 20 to 30 kg, a total daily dosage up to 1,500 mg/day PO (in divided doses) for acute treatment and 1,200 mg/day PO (in divided doses) for maintenance therapy will provide appropriate lithium concentrations for most patients. In children weighing more than 30 kg, a total daily dosage up to 1,800 mg/day PO (in divided doses) for acute or maintenance therapy will provide appropriate lithium concentrations for most patients.
6 years: Safety and efficacy have not been established. Used off label; dosing must be individualized. One consensus guideline notes that approximate weight-based doses of 10 to 30 mg/kg/day PO (in divided doses) are needed to provide appropriate lithium concentrations. Avoid lithium concentration above 1.5 mEq/L.
Less than 6 years: Safety and efficacy have not been established.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific recommendations for dosage adjustment in patients with hepatic impairment are not available, use caution. Dosage adjustment will be needed if renal dysfunction is also present. Many factors can affect renal lithium clearance including hyponatremia or hypernatremia, dehydration, and diuretic use, some conditions may be present in the patient with hepatic impairment.
  Renal Impairment
  CrCl at least 90 mL/min: No dosage adjustments are necessary.
CrCl 30 mL/min to 89 mL/min: Start with a dosage that is less than in patients with normal renal function and titrate slowly with frequent monitoring for lithium toxicity.
CrCl less than 30 mL/min: Avoid use.
