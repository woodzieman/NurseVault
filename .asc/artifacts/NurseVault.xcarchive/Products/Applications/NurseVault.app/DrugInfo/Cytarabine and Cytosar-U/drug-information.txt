          20 mg/m2 subcutaneously once daily on days 1 to 10 repeated every 28 days starting on day 1 of cycle 1 in combination with oral venetoclax. Eligible patients (median age, 76 years; range, 36 to 93 years) in this trial were aged 75 years or older (approximately 58%) and/or had coexisting conditions that prevented the use of intensive chemotherapy (i.e., standard induction therapy). Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  NOTE: Cytarabine Liposomal, ARA-C, is discussed in a separate monograph.
 
Cytarabine should be used only by clinicians experienced in myelosuppressive cancer chemotherapy. Patients receiving cytarabine, especially for induction, should be under close medical supervision in facilities with laboratory and supportive resources sufficient to monitor drug tolerance and to protect and maintain a patient compromised by drug toxicity. Treatment with cytarabine may result in severe and prolonged bone marrow suppression, which is the main toxic effect. Anemia, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia may occur. Other toxicity includes nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, oral ulceration, and hepatic dysfunction. Frequent monitoring of complete blood counts (CBC), electrolytes, liver function, and bone marrow are necessary. Patients with an active infection should be treated prior to receiving cytarabine, when possible. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with chemotherapy. Consider the possible benefit and the known toxic effects of cytarabine and be familiar with the cytarabine prescribing information before deciding to treat a patient with cytarabine.
 
Myelosuppressive effects of cytarabine can increase the risk of infection or bleeding; therefore, dental work should be delayed until blood counts have returned to normal. Patients, especially those with dental disease, should be instructed in proper oral hygiene, including caution in use of regular toothbrushes, dental floss, and toothpicks.
 
Intramuscular injections should not be administered to patients with platelet counts = 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    Headline
    drug-induced bone marrow suppression, serious adverse effects
    General Information
    Cytarabine may cause serious adverse effects. The main toxic effect is bone marrow suppression with leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia. Use cytarabine with caution in patients with pre-existing drug-induced bone marrow suppression. Frequently monitor complete blood cell counts during therapy, including daily leucocyte and platelet counts during induction therapy. Bone marrow examinations should be performed frequently after blasts have disappeared from the peripheral blood.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Cytarabine, ARA-C is a FDA pregnancy risk category D drug. Cytarabine is a known teratogen and should not be used during the first trimester of pregnancy. Deformities of the head and limbs are most common. Use in the second and third trimesters is safer but constitutes a considerable risk. Females of childbearing potential should be cautioned to avoid getting pregnant. If pregnancy occurs during cytarabine treatment, women should be counseled regarding risks and options for the pregnancy. Reports of pancytopenia, leukopenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia, sepsis and death have occurred during the neonatal period to infants exposed to cytarabine in utero. Infants whose mothers have received cytarabine during pregnancy should be closely observed.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Use cytarabine with caution in patients with renal impairment including renal failure. A higher incidence of neurologic/CNS toxicity after high-dose cytarabine treatment may occur in patients with renal impairment; consider a reduced dose in these patients.

  General Information
  NOTE: Cytarabine Liposomal, ARA-C, is discussed in a separate monograph.One case of anaphylaxis that resulted in acute cardiopulmonary arrest and required resuscitation occurred immediately after the intravenous administration of cytarabine injection. Monitor patients for anaphylactoid reactions such as anaphylactic shock.The toxicity associated with cytarabine is dose and schedule dependent. Cytarabine commonly causes profound bone marrow suppression and pancytopenia when used to treat acute leukemia. Patients will develop leukopenia, neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia on approximately day 7, with resolution by day 21—28. The primary cell lines affected by cytarabine are platelet and granulocyte lines. There is little effect on lymphocyte count although decreases in cell-mediated immunity may be noted.Severe nausea/vomiting occurs with high-dose cytarabine (100% of patients); however, nausea and vomiting is usually mild with lower dose cytarabine therapy. Anorexia occurs commonly. Other GI effects include severe stomatitis, diarrhea, ileus, and GI bleeding. Mucositis including stomatitis, pharyngitis and esophagitis may be severe in patients receiving longer than 5 days of continuous infusion cytarabine. Typhlitis has been associated with cytarabine in the treatment of acute leukemias. Patients develop lower abdominal pain, diarrhea, and bacteremia.Cytarabine syndrome can occur within 6 to 12 hours of treatment. The symptoms include bone pain, fever, myalgia, rash (unspecified), and fatigue.Hepatotoxicity has been reported in patients receiving cytarabine alone or in combination with other chemotherapeutic agents. Reversible intrahepatic cholestasis occurs frequently in patients during induction therapy but is rarely severe. Pancreatitis may occur and has been associated with prior L-asparaginase exposure. High-dose cytarabine is associated with hepatotoxicity similar to hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD) and includes passive liver congestion, jaundice and elevated hepatic enzymes including serum bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and transaminases.Dermatologic effects of cytarabine include a generalized maculopapular rash (30—50% of patients) and palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia (hand and foot syndrome). Patients may complain of a burning sensation on the hands and feet. The erythema can progress to bulla formation and desquamation. Fever often accompanies the skin rash.Ocular adverse effects associated with high-dose cytarabine therapy include a chemical keratoconjunctivitis, photophobia, pain, burning, blurred vision, and excessive tearing. Punctate lesions on the cornea can be seen. This syndrome is actually self-limiting, but prophylactic administration of dexamethasone ophthalmic drops usually prevents this problem and increases patient comfort.Due to cytarabine's toxicity to endothelial cells of the pulmonary vasculature, patients can develop pulmonary edema, respiratory depression and failure, and frank acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). This reaction can be quite serious but is not common. The clinical syndrome is characterized by an acute onset of tachypnea and ARDS with rapid deterioration of pulmonary function requiring intubation. Chest X-rays in patients are consistent with extensive parenchymal changes, pulmonary edema and diffuse infiltrates.Peripheral neuropathy has been associated with both high-dose cytarabine and conventional dose therapy and seems to correlate with the cumulative dose of cytarabine. Symptoms have included sensory neuropathy in a stocking-glove distribution to progressive (similar to a Guillain-Barre syndrome) ascending polyneuropathy. Onset is within a few hours to 2 weeks after the last dose. High-dose cytarabine has been associated with cerebellar toxicity. Patients may demonstrate ataxia, nystagmus, dysarthria (slurred speech), tremor, and diplopia. Neurologic reactions occur in as many as 20% of patients. They are more common with higher doses, in patients over age 50, and in patients with a creatinine clearance < 60 ml/min. In most cases, this toxicity is reversible but if cytarabine is continued without dose reduction, severe, irreversible and possibly fatal effects may occur. Cerebral dysfunction is characterized by generalized encephalopathy, coma, confusion, memory loss, disorientation, impaired cognition, psychosis and frontal lobe release signs have been described. Seizures, either generalized or psychomotor, have occurred in patients receiving high-dose cytarabine. Seizures most commonly occur during or shortly after infusions of cytarabine.Intrathecal administration of cytarabine can cause nausea/vomiting, transient headache, and fever; nausea, vomiting, and fever are the most frequently reported reactions, which are mild and self-limiting. Diluents of cytarabine that contain preservatives have been implicated in the development of neurotoxicity in some but not all patients receiving intrathecal injections. Blindness (visual impairment) has occurred in two patients in remission who had received combination chemotherapy (systemically), prophylactic CNS irradiation, and intrathecal cytarabine. Five children developed necrotizing leukoencephalopathy after receiving CNS irradiation and intrathecal methotrexate, hydrocortisone sodium succinate, and cytarabine. Two children developed delayed progressive ascending paralysis (fatal in one) after receiving cytarabine intrathecally and intravenously in combination with other drugs. Paraplegia has also been reported. An increased risk of spinal cord toxicity exists if cytarabine is given both intravenously and intrathecally within a few days; a risk:benefit analysis is recommended if concurrent drug administration by both routes is contemplated.Teratogenesis has been associated with cytarabine especially if it is used during the first trimester. Deformities of the head and limbs are common. Chromosomal abnormalities have been noted as well. Use in the second and third trimester still carries risk but is safer than use earlier in the pregnancy. Premature delivery is common. Reports of pancytopenia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, sepsis and death have occurred during the neonatal period.Cytarabine-induced cellular destruction can result in tumor lysis syndrome (TLS), which includes hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, and hypocalcemia. Uric acid nephropathy, acute renal failure due renal tubular obstruction, metabolic acidosis, and nephrolithiasis can also occur. These problems are more severe when a highly chemosensitive disease is treated (i.e., leukemia or lymphoma). Aggressive alkalinization of the urine and use of allopurinol can prevent urate nephropathy; close monitoring of serum electrolytes and renal function is recommended.

In most cases, this toxicity is reversible but if cytarabine is continued without dose reduction, severe, irreversible and possibly fatal effects may occur. Cerebral dysfunction is characterized by generalized encephalopathy, coma, confusion, memory loss (memory impairment), disorientation, impaired cognition, psychosis and frontal lobe release signs have been described. Seizures, either generalized or psychomotor, have occurred in patients receiving high-dose cytarabine. Seizures most commonly occur during or shortly after infusions of cytarabine.

Description
  Text
  NOTE: Cytarabine Liposomal, ARA-C is discussed in a separate monograph.
 
Cytarabine (cytosine arabinoside, Ara-C) is a pyrimidine antimetabolite agent used in the treatment of hematologic malignancies in combination with other chemotherapy agents. It is a congener of the nucleoside cytidine and deoxycytidine that contains an altered sugar, arabinose, instead of ribose and deoxyribose, respectively. The limited cytotoxicity of cytarabine in solid tumors is attributed to the lack of metabolic activation and its selective effects against rapidly dividing cells. Cytarabine is most commonly used as part of induction and consolidation regimens in acute myelogenous leukemia. It is also used as part of combination chemotherapy regimens for acute lymphocytic leukemia, chronic myelogenous leukemia, and Hodgkin's and non-Hodgkin's lymphoma. Intrathecal cytarabine is effective treatment and prophylaxis of central nervous system leukemia and lymphoma. Cytarabine was granted FDA approval in 1969. A liposomal cytarabine product (DepoCyt(TM)) is also available.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Cytarabine is an S-phase specific cytotoxic agent and has no effect on non-dividing cells or cells in any other phase. The cytotoxicity of cytarabine is also dependent upon the rate of DNA synthesis and the duration of exposure of cells to cytarabine. Therefore, in tissue cultures, cytotoxicity is greatest if cells are exposed to cytarabine for extended periods during maximal DNA synthesis to allow cytarabine to be incorporated into a larger number of cells as they pass through the S-phase. Intracellularly, cytarabine is metabolized by deoxycytidine kinases to form 1-beta-D-arabinofuranosylcytosine-5'-triphosphate (Ara-CTP). Ara-CTP competitively inhibits DNA polymerase-alpha and halts DNA strand elongation and repair, which seems to decrease the phosphorylation of cytarabine to the active metabolite. The most important cytotoxic effect of Ara-CTP is incorporation into DNA. Once incorporated into DNA, the tumor cells are unable to remove Ara-CTP and this results in inhibition of template function and chain elongation. In addition, deoxycytidine levels are reduced within the cell due to the production of Ara-CTP. Both of these processes block further polymerization of DNA and lead to shortened DNA strands. Cytarabine also causes unusual multiple duplications of DNA strands that increase the possibility of recombination, crossover, and gene amplification.
 
Other actions of cytarabine include inhibition of ribonucleotide reductase, formation of Ara-CDP-choline and induction of cellular differentiation. Ara-CDP-choline is an analog of cytidine 5'-diphosphocholine (CDP-choline) and inhibits synthesis of membrane glycoproteins and glycolipids altering membrane structure, stability, antigenicity and function. Cytarabine can promote differentiation of leukemic cells and decrease c-myc expression.

  Special Populations
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    High-dose cytarabine requires dose adjustments in patients with renal insufficiency to decrease the incidence of neurotoxicity.
  Text
  Cytarabine is administered intravenously, subcutaneously or intrathecally. Cytarabine is not given orally due to extensive deamination within gut lumen resulting in only 20% absorption. Cytarabine distributes rapidly throughout the body tissues and is transported into cells by active transport. It crosses the blood-brain barrier resulting in cerebrospinal concentrations 20—50% of plasma concentrations. With high doses, cytarabine achieves significant concentrations in tears. The drug undergoes metabolism to its active form via cytidine kinase then both the parent drug and active metabolite undergo deamination by cytidine deaminase to an inactive metabolite, uracil arabinoside (Ara-U). Cytidine deaminase is located primarily in the liver, kidneys, GI mucosa, and granulocytes. The degradation pathway can be saturated at higher doses yielding a higher amount of active metabolites. Ara-U has a half-life of 3—6 hours and enhances the activation of cytarabine through negative feedback inhibition of cytidine deaminase in leukemic cells. Ara-U further potentiates the effects of cytarabine by causing cytostasis in the S-phase with enhanced anabolism and cytotoxicity of subsequent cytarabine doses. Excretion of cytarabine is biphasic. The majority of cytarabine is excreted via the urine in the form of Ara-U. Ninety percent of the cytarabine dose is eliminated in the urine in 24 hours.
 
Once cytarabine enters the CSF, the tissues that have the greatest contact with the CSF phosphorylate the drug. The half-life of conventional cytarabine in the CSF is longer (2—11 hours) than in plasma. The CSF clearance of cytarabine approximates the bulk flow rate suggesting that elimination occurs through this route. During high-dose cytarabine therapy and renal insufficiency, Ara-U accumulates in CSF and inhibits cytidine deaminase resulting in higher concentrations of Ara-CTP, which is neurotoxic.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Dosage should be modified depending on clinical response and degree of hepatic impairment, but no quantitative recommendations are available.
  Renal Impairment
  CrCl 60 mL/minute or greater: No dosage adjustment needed.
CrCl less than 60 mL/minute: Increased risk of cerebellar and cerebral toxicity with high dose regimens. Recommended dosage adjustments are as follows: For a serum creatinine of 1.5 to 1.9 mg/dL or increase of serum creatinine from baseline of 0.5 to 1.2 mg/dL during treatment, reduce the dose of cytarabine from 2 to 3 g/m2 per day to 1 g/m2 per day. For a serum creatinine 2 mg/dL or greater or if the change in baseline serum creatinine was greater than 1.2 mg/dL, reduce the dose of cytarabine from 2 to 3 g/m2 per day to 100 mg/m2 per day.
CrCl 40 to 60 mL/minute: For doses more than 2 g/m2 per dose, decrease the dose to 1 g/m2 per dose. For doses 750 mg to 1 g/m2 per dose, decrease to 500 mg/m2 per dose.
CrCl less than 40 mL/minute: For doses more than 750 mg/m2 per dose, give a dose less than or equal to 200 mg/m2 per day.
