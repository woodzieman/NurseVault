  Nitric Oxide Weaning
Do not abruptly discontinue nitric oxide (NO); however, an optimal weaning regimen has not yet been described. Abrupt discontinuation of nitric oxide could result in worsening pulmonary artery pressure and/or blood oxygenation (PaO2), even in neonates with no apparent response to NO.
Inhalation rates should be decreased slowly in a stepwise fashion, pausing at each step for several hours to monitor for hypoxemia.
Decreases of 5 ppm every 15 minutes to the next lowest tolerable dose, with additional attempts to wean every 6 hours has been described; however, this rate of dosage reduction is faster than is generally recommended. In this study of 10 patients, 2 of the 5 patients that responded to NO and underwent weaning required decreases of 1 to 3 ppm every 12 to 24 hours for complete weaning.
In trials of inhaled NO withdrawal in neonates, concentrations of NO were reduced by 20% once patients were determined to be responders or failures to NO; if ventilatory and hemodynamic status were unchanged after 30 minutes, further 20% reductions in the concentration could occur anytime within the next 4 hours. The investigators in one study found that rebound hypertension was minimized if the oxygenation index was 60 mmHg and a pH < 7.55) with 20 ppm were weaned to 5 ppm as tolerated at the end of 4 hours of treatment.

        Dosage Description
        Many studies have been published describing the use of nitric oxide in ALI or ARDS; short-term improvements in oxygenation and vascular resistance associated with inhaled nitric oxide have not been documented to impact mortality. According to the manufacturer, the use of nitric oxide in ARDS is not recommended based on the lack of beneficial effects in clinical trials on primary outcome parameters (e.g., days alive, days off ventilator support) despite acute improvements in oxygenation. The use of nitric oxide in ARDS has been controversial, and the optimal dose and duration of therapy is unknown. Studies have suggested that in the absence of sepsis, 30%—80% of ARDS patients will respond to nitric oxide at inhaled doses ranging from 1 ppm or less to 40 ppm. Response rates in septic ARDS patients have been lower probably due to the concomitant systemic administration of vasopressors that may antagonize the vasodilatory effects of nitric oxide. In a randomized trial, an acute response (defined as a PaO2 increase of 20% or more) to nitric oxide was seen in 60% of patients, while 24% of placebo patients also had an acute response during the first 4 hours. The initial increase in oxygenation translated into a reduction in FIO2 over the first day and a reduction in the intensity of mechanical ventilation over the first 4 days of treatment. Mortality and other outcome measures did not differ in patients treated with nitric oxide compared to controls.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Inhaled nitric oxide is contraindicated in the treatment of neonates known to be dependent upon right-to-left shunting of blood (i.e., patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)-dependent heart lesions). The risks of nitric oxide therapy should be carefully considered in patients with pulmonary edema, acute respiratory infection, and severe left ventricular function impairment (e.g., heart failure patients or heart transplant candidates). Patients with severe left ventricular dysfunction (NYHA Class III-IV) should receive diagnostic or therapeutic exposure to nitric oxide only in combination with other agents known to maintain or improve left ventricular function. In these patients, nitric oxide inhalation may vasodilate the pulmonary vasculature and increase blood flow to the left ventricle, and acutely increase left atrial pressure and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) and promote pulmonary edema. Cardiac output, left atrial pressure or PCWP should be monitored if nitric oxide is administered to patients with severe left ventricular dysfunction.
 
Abrupt discontinuation of inhaled nitric oxide may lead to decreased oxygenation and increased right-to-left shunting and pulmonary artery pressure (PAP). Deterioration in oxygenation and elevation of pulmonary arterial pressure may also occur in children with no apparent response to nitric oxide inhalation therapy. Weaning of nitric oxide inhalation therapy should proceed slowly to prevent acute oxygen desaturation and rebound pulmonary hypertension. An optimal regimen for withdrawal has not been determined. Close monitoring of oxygenation, pulmonary pressures, and clinical status should occur during step-wise withdrawal. A rebound phenomenon has been described clinically as a slow increase of PAP occurring several minutes after stopping inhaled nitric oxide with stable systolic arterial pressure (SAP) and PaO2. This is different from failure to wean from inhaled nitric oxide where abrupt increases in PAP occur with declines in SAP and PaO2. Dipyridamole and sildenafil have both been reported to prevent rebound pulmonary hypertension due to withdrawal of nitric oxide inhalation in children.
 
Nitric oxide is inactivated by combination with oxyhemoglobin to form methemoglobin and nitrate. Patients with congenital or acquired methemoglobin reductase deficiency should not receive nitric oxide inhalation therapy as they may develop significant methemoglobinemia. Methemoglobin levels should be monitored prior to, 1, and 6 hours after initiating therapy and daily thereafter and with each dosage increase.
 
Patients with thrombocytopenia or coagulopathy should be monitored closely during nitric oxide inhalation therapy. Nitric oxide may inhibit platelet aggregation and increase bleeding time in some patients.
 
The safety of nitric oxide inhalation during pregnancy has not been established (FDA pregnancy risk category C). It is not known whether inhaled nitric oxide will cause fetal harm if given to a pregnant women or can affect reproduction capacity. It is not known whether inhaled nitric oxide is excreted in breast milk. Nitric oxide should not be used in women who are breast-feeding their infants.
 
Accidental exposure should be avoided. The 8-hour weighted average exposure limit set by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) for nitric oxide is 25 ppm and for nitric dioxide (NO2) the limit is 5 ppm. Repeated determinations of nitric oxide by chemiluminescence (especially if low doses (1—5 ppm) are given) or electrochemical analyzers (best for doses over 20 ppm) in inspired gas should occur during nitric oxide therapy. Contact time between nitric oxide and oxygen should be as short as possible to minimize the formation of NO2. Exhaled gas from patients breathing nitric oxide may contain high concentrations of NO2. Although controversial, systems to scavenge exhaled gases may be used to prevent accumulation of NO2 in the patient's room. Scavenging of NO2 from the expiratory limb has been achieved by diverting the exhaled gas to wall suction, to the outdoors, or to canisters containing soda lime or other absorbent materials. The most important requirements for safe administration of inhalation nitric oxide are continuous analysis of nitric oxide and NO2 concentrations, frequent calibration of the monitoring equipment, frequent analysis of blood methemoglobin levels, use of certified tanks, and administration of the lowest nitric oxide concentration required.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  During clinical trials in neonates, the following adverse reactions occurred more often in patients treated with nitric oxide inhalation than placebo: hypotension (13%), withdrawal syndrome (12%), atelectasis (9%), hematuria (8%), hyperglycemia (8%), sepsis (7%), infection (6%), cellulitis (5%), and stridor (5%). Systemic hemodynamic changes are uncommon due to rapid inactivation of nitric oxide by hemoglobin.Methemoglobinemia increases with the dose of inhaled nitric oxide. Elevated levels of methemoglobin reduce the oxygen delivery capacity of the circulation. During clinical trials, maximum levels usually were reached approximately 8 hours after initiating nitric oxide inhalation therapy, although methemoglobin levels have peaked as late as 40 hours following initiation of inhaled nitric oxide therapy. In one study, 13 of 37 (35%) of neonates treated with nitric oxide 80 ppm had methemoglobin levels exceeding 7%. Following discontinuation or reduction of the inhaled nitric oxide dosage, the methemoglobin levels returned to baseline over a period of hours. Nitric oxide forms a toxic metabolite, nitric dioxide (NO2), as it is exposed to oxygen. Elevated NO2 levels may cause acute lung injury, including pulmonary edema. Limiting the contact time of nitric oxide and oxygen and using the lowest concentrations of nitric oxide will help to decrease NO2 formation. Elevated NO2 levels of 2.6 ppm were seen in neonates treated with inhaled nitric oxide 80 ppm. NO2 levels were 3 ppm or methemoglobin levels > 7% were treated by reducing the dose of or discontinuing nitric oxide inhalation.Prolonged bleeding time has been reported in neonates and adults treated with nitric oxide inhalation. Nitric oxide inhibits platelet aggregation via activation of intracellular guanylate cyclase and increased levels of cGMP. However, in multicenter studies in neonates, no difference in the frequency of bleeding events was observed in nitric oxide-treated patients compared with placebo-treated patients. In a placebo-controlled trial of healthy volunteers, inhaled nitric oxide did not influence either bleeding time, platelet activity, or skin perfusion.Overdosage with inhaled nitric oxide (> 500—1000 ppm) will lead to severe methemoglobinemia, rapid increases in nitric dioxide (NO2), pulmonary alveolar edema and hemorrhage, hypoxia, and death within minutes to hours.The following adverse events for nitric oxide have been reported during post-marketing surveillance (without regard to causality): dosage errors associated with the drug delivery system, headache associated with environmental exposure of nitric oxide to hospital staff, hypotension associate with acute withdrawal of the drug, hypoxemia associated with acute withdrawal of the drug, and pulmonary edema in patients with CREST syndrome.

Description
  Text
  Inhaled nitric oxide is a selective pulmonary vasodilator and is supplied as a gaseous blend of nitric oxide and nitrogen. Nitric oxide is a colorless, almost odorless gas. In the environment, nitric oxide gas is considered an air pollutant and is formed from combustion (e.g., fossil fuel combustion or tobacco smoke) and lightening. In 1987, nitric oxide was identified as a key endothelium-derived relaxing factor (EDRF). Nitric oxide has been identified as an important endogenous mediator in the cardiovascular, immune, and nervous systems. Inhaled nitric oxide has been widely studied for its selective pulmonary vasodilation effects. Inhaled nitric oxide has been studied in neonates with persistent pulmonary hypertension (PPHN) and has been shown to decrease the extent to which extracorporeal membrane oxygenation is needed for hypoxemic respiratory failure (oxygenation index more than 25). However, up to 30% to 40% of neonates do not achieve a sustained improvement in oxygenation, and nitric oxide does not reduce mortality or length of hospitalization in newborns affected by PPHN. Although doses up to 80 ppm have been administered in neonates, doses over 20 ppm have not been shown to be superior and increase the risk of methemoglobinemia and other complications. In adults, inhaled nitric oxide has been studied in patients with acute lung injury and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS); however, short-term improvements in oxygenation and vascular resistance associated with inhaled nitric oxide have not been documented to impact mortality. Nitric oxide was FDA-approved in 1999.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: Inhaled nitric oxide produces selective pulmonary vasodilation. Nitric oxide is a very reactive compound due to the presence of an unpaired electron. Nitric oxide can react with thiols, DNA, lipids, superoxide, heme proteins, and metals leading to diverse and contrasting effects. When inhaled, nitric oxide reacts with the iron in the heme portion of soluble guanylate cyclase, activating the enzyme. Activated guanylate cyclase produces cyclic guanosine 3',5'-monophosphate (cGMP), which then leads to vasodilation. Nitroglycerin and nitroprusside also stimulate cGMP formation, which results in smooth muscle relaxation. The common mechanism of action of these drugs is the release of nitric oxide. Inhaled nitric oxide appears to increase the partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO2) by dilating pulmonary vessels in better ventilated areas of the lung and redistributing pulmonary blood flow away from lung regions with low ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) ratios toward regions with normal ratios. In persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN), which occurs as a primary developmental defect or secondary to other conditions, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) is high. During PPHN, hypoxemia results secondary to the right-to-left shunting of blood through the patent ductus arteriosus or foramen ovale. Inhaled nitric oxide improves oxygenation (significantly increases PaO2) in neonates with PPHN.In the body, nitric oxide is produced via synthesis from L-arginine by nitric oxide synthase (NOS) in a variety tissues. Nitric oxide regulates many physiologic activities. Three NOS isoforms have been described: neuronal NOS, inducible NOS, a cytokine-inducible form, and endothelial NOS. It has been shown that all NOS isoforms can be induced or constitutively expressed. In addition, nitric oxide can be formed during reduced and acidotic conditions.•Neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS): In the peripheral nervous system, nitric oxide regulates smooth muscle relaxation in gastrointestinal, urogenital, and respiratory tracts via nonadrenergic nerves that contain nNOS. In the central nervous system, nitric oxide is essential in regulating information storage in the brain and has effects on brain development, memory function, behavior, and pain perception. In skeletal smooth muscle, the expression of nitric oxide via nNOS modulates contractile force, myocyte development, myofiber differentiation, and myotube innervation. Neuronal NOS also may be found in cardiac nerve terminals and in the retina.•Inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS): Many cells, especially macrophages and granulocytes, express iNOS in response to infectious and inflammatory stimuli. These effects are beneficial in certain bacterial and parasitic infections (e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis and toxoplasma gondii) and in response to inflammation. However, iNOS expression has been associated with worsening of other infectious disease (e.g., influenza pneumonitis) and inflammatory conditions (e.g., autoimmune vasculitis). Nitric oxide appear to worsen arterial vasodilation associated with sepsis.•Endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS): Endothelial NOS forms endothelial-derived relaxing factor (EDRF), nitric oxide. Acetlycholine, bradykinin, and other agents that increase intracellular calcium levels stimulate the activity of eNOS. Endothelial NOS is important in the regulation of systemic and pulmonary vascular tone and in lung development and disease. In cardiac endothelial cells, eNOS activity inhibits contractile tone and proliferation of smooth muscle cells, decreases platelet aggregation and monocyte adhesion, increases diastolic relaxation, and decreased the oxygen consumption by cardiac tissues. In addition, nitric oxide in cardiac myocytes opposes the inotropic action of catecholamines after muscarinic cholinergic or beta-adrenergic receptor stimulation.•Activity in sickle cell disease: The exact mechanism of nitric oxide in sickle cell disease has not been established. Nitric oxide is thought to bind to beta-hemoglobin (at cysteine-93) to form S-nitrosohemoglobin. Nitric oxide is then carried to the microvasculature where it is released upon deoxygenation leading to vasodilation. Other studies suggest that the binding of nitric oxide to hemoglobin may increase oxygen capacity, alter hemoglobin protein structure, or interfere with polymerization interactions. Conflicting results have been reported regarding the ability of nitric oxide to improve the oxygen capacity of sickle cell erythrocytes. However, if nitric oxide does cause vasodilation in the periphery, there may be a benefit to patients by preventing or reversing the cycle of hemoglobin S polymerization, sickling, and vasooclusion.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Inhalation Route
    Description
    Approximately 90% of nitric oxide is absorbed during steady state inhalation. Nitric oxide is absorbed into the pulmonary vasculature, but systemic exposure is limited by rapid inactivation in blood cells.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Pediatrics
    Description
    Neonates
In neonates with respiratory failure, methemoglobin levels increased during the first 8 hours of nitric oxide exposure. Mean methemoglobin levels remained below 1% for neonates receiving 5 and 20 ppm nitric oxide, but reached approximately 5% in neonates receiving 80 ppm nitric oxide. Methemoglobin levels more than7% were attained in 35% of patients receiving 80 ppm. In patients with methemoglobin levels more than7%, the time to peak methemoglobin levels was 10 +/- 9 hours (median, 8 hours).
  Text
  Nitric oxide is administered by inhalation. Nitric oxide is very lipophilic and readily diffuses across cell via passive diffusion. The half-life of nitric oxide is a few seconds. Methemoglobin and nitrate are the end products of nitric oxide metabolism present in the systemic circulation. In blood cells, nitric oxide combines with hemoglobin that is 60 to 100% oxygen-saturated. At this level of oxygen saturation, nitric oxide combines predominantly with oxyhemoglobin to produce methemoglobin and nitrate. At low oxygen saturation, nitric oxide can combine with deoxyhemoglobin to transiently form nitrosylhemoglobin, which is converted to nitrogen oxides and methemoglobin upon exposure to oxygen. Within the pulmonary system, nitric oxide can combine with oxygen and water to produce nitric dioxide and nitrite, respectively, which interact with oxyhemoglobin to produce methemoglobin and nitrate. Nitrate is the predominant nitric oxide metabolite excreted in the urine. About 70% of the dose appears in the urine as nitrate within 48 hours. Nitrate is cleared from the plasma by the kidney and the clearance rate approaches the glomerular filtration rate.
