  General Information
  Prior to ozanimod administration
Obtain a recent (within the last 6 months or after discontinuation of prior MS or UC therapy) complete blood count, including lymphocyte count.
Obtain an electrocardiogram (ECG) in all patients prior to ozanimod initiation. Certain patients with preexisting conditions also require a cardiac evaluation by an appropriately trained physician prior to treatment initiation.
Determine whether patients are taking drugs that could slow heart rate or atrioventricular (AV) conduction prior to treatment initiation.
Evaluate the use of concomitant immunosuppressive medications and consider their additive effects.

    Headline
    acute coronary syndrome, acute heart failure, cardiac arrest, cardiac arrhythmias, cerebrovascular disease, heart failure, hypertension
    General Information
    Ozanimod is contraindicated in people who in the past 6 months have experienced acute coronary syndrome (e.g., myocardial infarction, unstable angina), stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA), decompensated acute heart failure requiring hospitalization, New York Heart Association Class III heart failure, or New York Heart Association Class IV heart failure. Ozanimod is also contraindicated in people with a history or presence of Mobitz Type II second or third-degree AV block, sick sinus syndrome, or sino-atrial block, unless the patient has a functioning pacemaker. Ozanimod causes reduced heart rate and may induce bradyarrhythmias or AV block. Ozanimod-induced effects on heart rate are maximal 5 hours after the first dose, returning to baseline 6 hours after the first dose. With continued ozanimod titration, the maximal heart rate effect occurs on Day 8. People with some preexisting conditions (e.g., resting baseline heart rate less than 55 beats/minute, ischemic heart disease, history of cardiac arrest or myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular disease or stroke, congestive heart failure, uncontrolled hypertension, AV block, sinus node dysfunction (e.g., sick sinus syndrome or sino-atrial block), or cardiac arrhythmias requiring treatment with Class Ia or Class III anti-arrhythmic drugs may poorly tolerate the ozanimod-induced bradycardia, or experience serious rhythm disturbances after the first dose. Prior to treatment with ozanimod, patients with pre-existing conditions should have a cardiac evaluation by a physician appropriately trained to conduct such evaluation. Ozanimod may also increase blood pressure and produce hypertension, so all patients should receive periodic blood pressure monitoring during treatment.

    Headline
    bradycardia, cardiomyopathy, congenital long QT syndrome, coronary artery disease, females, geriatric, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, QT prolongation
    General Information
    Avoid use of ozanimod in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemia heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk. Ozanimod was not studied in individuals who had significant QT prolongation (QTcF of 450 msec or more in males or 470 msec or more in females), risk factors for QT prolongation, or other conduction abnormalities or cardiac conditions that in the opinion of the treating investigator could jeopardize the individual's health. If treatment with ozanimod is considered in these conditions, consult a cardiologist for advice before initiating ozanimod.

    Headline
    sleep apnea
    General Information
    Ozanimod is contraindicated for use in individuals with severe untreated sleep apnea. Dose-dependent reductions in absolute forced expiratory volume over 1 second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) were seen as early as 3 months after treatment initiation during clinical trials for both multiple sclerosis and ulcerative colitis. There is insufficient information to determine the reversibility of the decrease in FEV1 or FVC after drug discontinuation or whether changes are progressive with continued use. Spirometric evaluation of respiratory function should be performed during therapy with ozanimod if clinically indicated.
    Headline
    infection
    General Information
    People with an active infection should not start ozanimod treatment until the infection is resolved. Ozanimod may increase the risk of infection. Monitor for signs and symptoms of infection during treatment. Serious infections with opportunistic pathogens including viral infection [e.g., John Cunningham virus (JCV), herpes infection, varicella-zoster], fungal infection (e.g., cryptococcal infection), and bacterial infection have been reported with ozanimod and other sphingosine 1-phoshpate (S1P) receptor modulators. Cases of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) have also occurred in MS patients who received ozanimod and other S1P receptor modulators. PML is an opportunistic viral infection of the brain caused by the JC virus (JCV) that typically only occurs in patients who are immunocompromised, and that usually leads to death or severe disability. If a serious infection occurs, institute appropriate therapy and withhold treatment until the infection resolves. Reassess the benefits and risks of re-initiating therapy. If PML is confirmed, ozaminod should be permanently discontinued. Ozanimod remains in the blood and has pharmacodynamic effects, including decreased lymphocyte counts, for up to 3 months following the last dose, and increased risks for infection may continue during this time. Lymphocyte counts generally return to the normal range within 30 to 90 days of stopping therapy. Consider the risks of additive effects (e.g., immunosuppression) of other medications even during times of ozanimod discontinuation. A recent CBC (i.e., within 6 months or after discontinuation of prior therapy) should be available before initiating or re-initiating ozanimod treatment.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, uveitis
    General Information
    Ozanimod increases the risk of macular edema. People with diabetes mellitus or a history of uveitis are at increased risk of macular edema during ozanimod treatment. Obtain an ophthalmologic evaluation of the fundus, including the macula, near the start of treatment in all individuals, periodically during treatment, and at any time a visual disturbance is reported during ozanimod therapy. Consider discontinuation of ozanimod if macular edema develops. The decision to continue treatment in individuals with macular edema should be based on the benefits and risks to the individual. The risk of recurrence after a rechallenge is unknown. Macular edema may lead to permanent visual loss over an extended period of time (e.g., 6 months).
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class A, Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Ozanimod is not recommended for use in people with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure. Reduce the ozanimod dosage in people with mild hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A) or moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B). Clinically significant hepatic injury, including acute hepatic failure requiring transplant, has occurred in people treated with ozanimod. There are no data to establish that people with preexisting hepatic disease are at increased risk of developing ozanimod-induced liver dysfunction. Ozanimod recipients should be monitored for signs and symptoms of any hepatic injury. Obtain liver function tests (LFTs), including transaminase and bilirubin levels, if not recently available (i.e., within 6 months), before initiation of ozanimod. Obtain these LFTs periodically during treatment and until 2 months after ozanimod discontinuation. Patients who develop signs of liver injury should have hepatic enzymes promptly checked, and ozanimod treatment should be interrupted. Do not resume ozanimod if a plausible alternative etiology for the signs and symptoms cannot be established, because these patients are at risk for severe drug-induced liver injury.
    Headline
    skin cancer
    General Information
    Perform a baseline skin examination before or shortly after ozanimod initiation and periodically during treatment for all patients, particularly for those with risk factors for skin cancer. Monitor for and promptly evaluate any suspicious skin lesions. Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and melanoma were reported during clinical trials for ozanimod. Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), Kaposi's sarcoma, and Merkel cell carcinoma have been reported during treatment with sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor (S1P) modulators.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Ozanimod may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy, based on animal data and its mechanism of action. Advise pregnant people of the potential fetal risk. There are no adequate data on the developmental risk associated with the use of ozanimod during human pregnancy. In animal studies, oral administration of ozanimod resulted in adverse effects on development, increased embryo-fetal mortality, increased fetal malformations and skeletal variations (abnormal/delayed ossification), neurobehavioral changes, and reduced fetal body weight. In rabbits, fetal blood vessel malformations occurred at clinically relevant maternal ozanimod and metabolite exposures. Ozanimod targets the sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor, which is known to be involved in vascular formation and neural development during embryogenesis. Experts generally recommend discontinuing these agents throughout gestation and lactation. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant individuals exposed to ozanimod; information about the registry can be obtained at www.zeposiapregnancyregistry.com or by calling 1-877-301-9314.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with ozanimod. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking ozanimod and for 3 months after the last dose. Discuss family planning early and frequently in patients with chronic conditions, such as multiple sclerosis and ulcerative colitis, using shared decision-making to align pregnancy timing with disease stability, treatment selection and washout needs, contraception, and fertility considerations.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking ozanimod. There are no data on the presence of ozanimod in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Ozanimod has been detected in the milk of lactating rats. Ozanimod and its active metabolites are highly protein bound and therefore are not expected to transfer into human milk in clinically significant amounts. However, the drug's slow clearance and long half-life may pose a risk of toxicity to the breastfed child. For patients with multiple sclerosis who are breastfeeding, consider alternative therapies such as glatiramer, interferon beta-1a, or interferon beta-1b.

Interactions
Acebutolol: (Moderate) Ozanimod may cause bradycardia and AV-conduction delays, which may be enhanced with the concomitant use of beta-blockers. If a calcium channel blocker that slows heart rate/cardiac conduction is also prescribed with ozanimod and a beta-blocker, a cardiologist should be consulted due to the likelyhood of additive effects.
Adagrasib: (Major) Concomitant use of adagrasib and ozanimod increases the risk of QT/QTc prolongation and torsade de pointes (TdP). Avoid concomitant use if possible, especially in patients with additional risk factors for TdP. Consider taking steps to minimize the risk for QT/QTc interval prolongation and TdP, such as electrolyte monitoring and repletion and ECG monitoring, if concomitant use is necessary. Ozanimod has a limited effect on the QT/QTc interval at therapeutic doses but may cause bradycardia and atrioventricular conduction delays which may increase the risk for TdP in patients with a prolonged QT/QTc interval.
Alemtuzumab: (Major) Initiating treatment with ozanimod after alemtuzumab treatment is not recommended due to the characteristics and duration of the immunosuppressive effects of alemtuzumab.
Alfuzosin: (Major) In general, do not initiate ozanimod in patients taking alfuzosin due to the risk of additive bradycardia, QT prolongation, and torsade de pointes (TdP). If treatment initiation is considered, seek advice from a cardiologist. Ozanimod initiation may result in a transient decrease in heart rate and atrioventricular conduction delays. Ozanimod has not been studied in patients taking concurrent QT prolonging drugs; however, QT prolonging drugs have been associated with TdP in patients with bradycardia. Alfuzosin may prolong the QT interval in a dose-dependent manner.

    Headline
    AV block, bradycardia, hypertension, hypertensive crisis, orthostatic hypotension
    General Information
    Initiation of ozanimod results in a transient decrease in heart rate and atrioventricular (AV) conduction delays. An up-titration of the dose is necessary to minimize decreases in heart rate. Bradycardia was reported on the day of treatment initiation in 0.2% to 0.6% of multiple sclerosis (MS) patients who received ozanimod compared to no patients who received interferon beta-1a or placebo. After day 1, the incidence of bradycardia was 0.8% or less. After an initial dose of ozanimod 0.23 mg, the greatest mean decrease from baseline in heart rate (0.7 to 1.2 bpm) occurred at hour 5 on day 1. Near return to baseline occurred at hour 6. With continued dose titration, the maximal heart rate effect of ozanimod occurred on day 8. Heart rates below 40 bpm were not observed. In the UC studies, bradycardia was reported on the day of treatment initiation in 1 ozanimod-treated patient (0.2%) vs. 0% with placebo. After Day 1, bradycardia was reported in 1 UC patient (0.2%) treated with ozanimod. Second or third-degree AV block was not observed in MS or UC patients treated with ozanimod with dose titration during clinical trials; however, first and second degree AV block was observed in healthy volunteers who received ozanimod at exposures higher than the recommended dosage without dose titration. Hypertension was reported in 4% or less of patients who received ozanimod during clinical trials. Patients treated with ozanimod had an average increase of approximately 1 to 3.5 mmHg in systolic blood pressure (SBP) over patients who received interferon beta-1a or placebo, and no effect on diastolic pressure. The increase in SBP was first detected after approximately 3 months of treatment and persisted throughout treatment. In MS trials, 2 patients treated with ozanimod experienced a hypertensive crisis that was not clearly influenced by the use of a concomitant medication. In UC trials, hypertensive crisis was reported in 2 actively-treated patients and 1 placebo-treated patient. Blood pressure should be monitored during treatment managed appropriately. During clinical trials, orthostatic hypotension was reported in 4% of MS patients who received ozanimod compared to 3% of patients who received interferon beta-1a.

    Headline
    encephalopathy, immune reconstitution syndrome, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy
    General Information
    In controlled clinical trials of ozanimod, 1 case of posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) was reported. Symptoms include sudden onset of severe headache, altered mental status, visual disturbance, and seizure. Symptoms of PRES are usually reversible but may progress into ischemic stroke or cerebral hemorrhage. If PRES is suspected, discontinue ozanimod. Delay in diagnosis and treatment may lead to permanent neurologic sequelae. A case of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), an opportunistic viral infection of the brain caused by the JC virus (JCV), has been reported with ozanimod during postmarketing experience. PML has been associated with some risk factors, including immunocompromised patients and the use of multiple immunosuppressants. PML has been reported in patients treated with other sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) receptor modulators. Longer treatment duration with S1P receptor modulators increases the risk of PML. Most cases of PML occurred in patients treated with S1P receptor modulators for at least 18 months. Withhold ozanimod and perform an appropriate diagnostic evaluation at the first sign or symptom of PML. Typical symptoms of PML are diverse, progress over days to weeks, and include progressive weakness on one side or clumsiness of the limbs; vision disturbance; and changes in thinking, memory, and orientation leading to confusion and personality changes. MRI findings may be visible before clinical signs and symptoms. Hence, MRI monitoring for signs that may be consistent with PML may be useful, and any suspicious findings should spark further investigation to allow for an early diagnosis of PML, if present. Cases of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) or immune reconstitution syndrome have been reported in patients treated with other S1P receptor modulators who developed PML and subsequently discontinued treatment. PML-associated IRIS may lead to rapid onset of serious neurological complications or death, and is often associated with characteristic changes on MRI. PML-associated IRIS onset occurred within a few months following S1P receptor modulator discontinuation. Monitor patients and appropriately treat any inflammation associated with IRIS.

    Headline
    multiple sclerosis exacerbation
    General Information
    In patients with multiple sclerosis (MS), monitor for severe multiple sclerosis exacerbation after ozanimod discontinuation. Rare cases of severely increased MS disability with multiple new lesions on MRI have been observed after discontinuation of another sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor modulator, fingolimod. Cases typically within 12 weeks, although cases up to 24 weeks after treatment have been reported. Most MS patients did not return to the functional status they had before fingolimod discontinuation, and permanent disease worsening occurred in some cases. Over 8 years, 35 cases of severe increases in disability with new MRI lesions occurred. Following drug discontinuation, disability began in less than 12 weeks in 29 cases, and between 12 and 24 weeks in 6 cases. Time on fingolimod before discontinuation ranged from 7 to 96 months. The disability was more severe than typical MS relapses, as several patients who were able to walk without assistance while taking fingolimod progressed to needing wheelchairs or became bed-bound. Eight patients experienced permanent disability, 17 patients had a partial recovery, and 6 patients had a full recovery (reported as either a return in Expanded Disability Status Scale score reported while on fingolimod or complete recovery as recognized by clinical judgment). Optimal methods for the treatment of severe disability have not been defined; however, all 35 patients received corticosteroids as the initial treatment. Of those who fully recovered, 3 received only IV methylprednisolone, and the other 3 received plasma exchange, intrathecal triamcinolone, or re-started the drug. Advise patients of the potential risk of an exacerbation of their MS when ozanimod is discontinued, and to seek immediate medical attention if they experience new or worsened symptoms of MS. If an increase in disability occurs, test for new or enhancing lesions on MRI and begin appropriate treatment if clinically indicated.

Description
  Text
  Ozanimod is an oral sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor modulator. It is indicated for the treatment of relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis (MS) in adults, to include clinically isolated syndrome, relapsing-remitting disease, and active secondary progressive disease. Ozanimod is additionally indicated for moderately to severely active ulcerative colitis (UC) in adults. Oral sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor modulators reduce the frequency of clinical exacerbations and delay the accumulation of physical disability in patients with MS. The efficacy of ozanimod for the treatment of relapsing forms of MS was demonstrated in 2, randomized, double-blind, active comparator trials, in which ozanimod was compared to interferon beta-1a. Across both trials, ozanimod significantly reduced annualized relapse rates compared to interferon beta-1a (relative reductions of 38% to 48%) and the drug reduced 3-month confirmed disability progression, defined as a 1-point increase in the Expanded Disability Status Scale (7.6% ozanimod vs. 7.8% interferon beta-1a; not statistically significant). The efficacy of ozanimod for the treatment of adults with moderately to severely active UC was established in 2, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials. Significantly more subjects with UC treated with ozanimod achieved clinical remission (12% treatment difference), clinical response (22% treatment difference), endoscopic improvement (16% treatment difference), and endoscopic-histologic mucosal improvement (9% treatment difference) vs. placebo at week 10. Guidelines recommend ozanimod as an effective advanced therapy for induction and maintenance of UC remission in individuals with moderate-to-severe UC. The American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) recommends ozanimod as a treatment option for moderate-to-severe disease, while the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) categorizes ozanimod as a higher-efficacy advanced therapy in advanced therapy-naive individuals and a lower-efficacy option in those previously exposed to biologic or other advanced therapies. Individuals with certain cardiac conditions or with hepatic impairment should not receive ozanimod. Ozanimod was FDA-approved in 2020.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Ozanimod is a sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) receptor modulator that binds with high affinity to S1P receptors 1 and 5. Ozanimod blocks the capacity of lymphocytes to egress from lymph nodes, reducing the number of lymphocytes in peripheral blood. The mechanism by which ozanimod exerts therapeutic effects in multiple sclerosis and ulcerative colitis is unknown but may involve the reduction of lymphocyte migration into the central nervous system and intestine.

The interconversion between CC112273 and CC1084037 favors CC112273. Approximately 94% of circulating total active drug exposure is represented by ozanimod (6%), CC112273 (73%), and CC1084037 (15%), in humans. Following a single oral dose of radiolabeled ozanimod 0.92 mg, approximately 26% of the radioactivity was recovered in urine and 37% in feces, primarily composed of inactive metabolites. Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2C8, BCRP, CYP3A4
Ozanimod is metabolized by multiple enzymes to form its circulating major active metabolites (e.g., CC112273 and CC1084037) and minor active metabolites (e.g., RP101988, RP101075, and RP112509). The major active metabolite CC112273 is metabolized by CYP2C8. Ozanimod is metabolized by CYP3A4 to form the minor metabolite RP101075.
