Drug Class: Vigabatrin is an oral antiepileptic agent (AED) indicated for the treatment of refractory complex partial seizures as adjunct therapy in patients 2 years of age and older who have not responded to alternative therapy.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 3,000 mg/day PO for refractory complex partial seizures using tablets or oral powder for suspension. Safety and efficacy have not been established for Vigafyde oral solution.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): blurred vision, conjunctivitis, diplopia, ocular pain, optic neuritis, visual impairment, agitation, anxiety, confusion, delirium, depression, hallucinations, irritability, psychosis, suicidal ideation, asthenia, chest pain (unspecified), malaise, malignant hyperthermia, polydipsia, cholestasis, dental pain, gi bleeding, hepatitis, acne vulgaris, alopecia, angioedema, maculopapular rash, pruritus, rash

In an Overdose: Vigabatrin overdoses have been reported during clinical trials and post-marketing experience worldwide. No overdoses resulted in death, and nearly half of the cases involved the ingestion of multiple medications.

Vigabatrin causes permanent bilateral concentric visual field constriction in 30% or more of adult patients; the incidence in pediatric patients is not well defined. Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Use the lowest dose and shortest duration of vigabatrin necessary to achieve clinical goals. Periodically assess individual response and continued need for treatment. Withdraw vigabatrin if:
Clinical failure is evident at any point in time.
For infantile spasms, significant clinical benefit is not achieved within 2 to 4 weeks.
For complex partial seizures, significant clinical benefit is not achieved within 3 months.
As with most antiepileptic drugs, withdraw vigabatrin gradually because of the risk of increased seizure frequency and status epilepticus.
In the event of a serious adverse reaction, rapid discontinuation can be considered.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Vigabatrin causes permanent visual disturbance and vision loss in some pediatric and adult patients. Vigabatrin causes permanent bilateral concentric visual field constriction in 30% or more of adult patients; the incidence in pediatric patients is not well defined. The visual field defect can range in severity from mild to severe, including tunnel vision to within 10 degrees of visual fixation. Vigabatrin can also cause decreased visual acuity due to central retina damage. Vision may worsen after vigabatrin discontinuation. Due to the risk for irreversible vision damage, vigabatrin should be discontinued if a significant clinical response is not achieved within 2 to 4 weeks of initiation in pediatric patients receiving vigabatrin for infantile spasms, within 3 months of initiation in adults, or if clinical failure is obvious at any time point. Vision loss is difficult to detect in pediatric patients and may not be detected until it is severe. In all patients, vision should be assessed at baseline (no later than 4 weeks after vigabatrin initiation), at least every 3 months during therapy, and approximately 3 to 6 months after vigabatrin discontinuation; assessment should include both acuity and visual fields whenever possible. Vision assessment must be performed by an ophthalmic professional with expertise in visual field interpretation and the ability to perform a dilated indirect ophthalmoscopy of the retina. The onset of the vision disturbance can occur at any time during therapy, even after months or years. The risk increases with increasing dose and duration; there is no known risk free exposure. Vigabatrin should be used at the lowest dose and shortest duration necessary to achieve clinical goals. Vigabatrin should not be used in patients with other risk factors for irreversible vision loss unless the benefits outweigh the risks. Vigabatrin also should not be used in patients taking other drugs that may cause serious ophthalmic adverse effects (see Drug Interactions). Due to the risk of permanent vision loss, vigabatrin is only available through a restricted distribution program called SHARE. Only prescribers and pharmacies registered with the SHARE program may prescribe and dispense vigabatrin.
 
In January 2008, the FDA alerted healthcare professionals of an increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior in patients receiving anticonvulsants to treat epilepsy, psychiatric disorders, or other conditions (e.g., migraine, neuropathic pain). This alert followed an initial request by the FDA in March 2005 for manufacturers of marketed anticonvulsants to provide data from existing controlled clinical trials for analysis. Prior to this request, preliminary evidence had suggested a possible link between anticonvulsant use and suicidality. The primary analysis consisted of 199 placebo-controlled clinical studies with a total of 27,863 patients in drug treatment groups and 16,029 patients in placebo groups (>= 5 years of age). There were 4 completed suicides among patients in drug treatment groups versus none in the placebo groups. Patients receiving anticonvulsants had approximately twice the risk of suicidal behavior or ideation (0.43%) as patients receiving placebo (0.24%), corresponding to an estimated 2.1 per 1000 (95% CI: 0.7—4.2) more patients in the drug treatment groups who developed suicidal behavior or ideation. The relative risk for suicidality was higher in patients with epilepsy compared to those with other conditions; however, the absolute risk differences were similar in trials for epilepsy and psychiatric indications. Age was not a determining factor. The increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior was observed between 1 and 24 weeks after therapy initiation. However, a longer duration of therapy should not preclude the possibility of an association to the drug since most studies included in the analysis did not continue beyond 24 weeks. Data were analyzed from drugs with adequately designed clinical trials including carbamazepine, felbamate, gabapentin, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine, pregabalin, tiagabine, topiramate, valproate, and zonisamide. However, this risk is considered to be a class effect. All patients beginning treatment with vigabatrin should be closely monitored for emerging or worsening depression or suicidal thoughts/behavior. Patients and caregivers should be informed of the increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors and should be advised to immediately report the emergence or worsening of depression, the emergence of suicidal thoughts or behavior, thoughts of self-harm, or other unusual changes in mood or behavior. Anticonvulsants should be prescribed in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management in order to reduce the risk of overdose.
 
Abrupt discontinuation of vigabatrin should not be undertaken. If discontinuation becomes necessary, vigabatrin should be withdrawn gradually (see Dosage).
 
Vigabatrin may cause somnolence and fatigue. Patients should be advised to avoid driving or operating machinery, or performing other tasks that require mental alertness until they are aware of whether vigabatrin adversely affects their cognitive and/or motor performance. Patients should also be informed of the possibility for enhanced drowsiness or dizziness with concurrent use of alcohol.
 
The safety and efficacy of vigabatrin in neonates and pediatric patients > 2 years old has not been established. In addition to the general precautions that must be considered before vigabatrin therapy, there are additional precautions that are specific to infants and children. First, assessing vision loss in infants and children is difficult; therefore, vision loss or impairment may go unrecognized until it is severe (see Precautions content for visual disturbance). Vigabatrin-induced vision loss may be more common and/or more severe in infants and children compared to adults; the functional and developmental impact of vigabatrin-induced vision changes in pediatric patients is not clearly defined. Abnormal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) signal changes have been observed in infants receiving vigabatrin for infantile spasms (see Adverse Reactions). In some cases, the infants experienced simultaneous motor abnormalities; however, a causal relationship was not established. Intramyelinic edema (IME), characterized by fluid accumulation and separation of the outer layers of myelin, has been observed in brain white matter tracts of several animal species. In addition, neurological toxicity, including seizures, hypomyelination, retinal dysplasia, and learning deficits, was observed in rats exposed to vigabatrin in utero and during the neonatal and juvenile periods; vacuolar changes in grey matter, which are considered to be different than IME, have also been observed in neonatal and juvenile rats receiving vigabatrin. These effects occurred at plasma vigabatrin concentrations substantially lower than those achieved clinically in human children. It has not been determined whether this neurotoxicity in animals is related to the MRI changes observed in human infants.
 
Vigabatrin is primarily renally eliminated. A dosage adjustment is required for patients with any degree of renal impairment (see Dosage).
 
Clinical studies of vigabatrin did not include a sufficient number of elderly patients to determine whether they respond differently than young patients. Vigabatrin is primarily renally eliminated, and elderly patients are more likely to have decreased renal function. The manufacturer recommends consideration of an adjustment in dose or frequency in elderly patients; however, specific recommendations are not available. In one small study, 4 out of 5 elderly patients (> 65 years) with reduced renal function (CrCl < 50 ml/min) experienced moderate to severe sedation and confusion after receiving a single dose of vigabatrin 1.5 g PO; the adverse effects lasted up to 5 days.
 
Vigabatrin is not classified as a controlled substance; however, the manufacturer advises caution in patients with a history of substance abuse since the drug has CNS active properties and has not been formally evaluated for its abuse, tolerance, or physical dependence potential.
 
Vigabatrin is classified as FDA pregnancy category C. There are no adequate controlled trials of vigabatrin use in pregnant women; however, in post-marketing experience since vigabatrin approval worldwide, numerous birth defects have been reported. Due to the nature of post-marketing reporting, incidence nor a causal relationship to the drug can be determined. The following are some, but not all, of the birth defects that have been reported: congenital cardiac defects, congenital hemangioma, congenital hydronephrosis and vesicoureteric reflux, male genital malformation, dysmorphism, fetal anticonvulsant syndrome, hip dysplasia, limb malformations, and renal aplasia. In addition, developmental toxicity and teratogenic effects have occurred when vigabatrin was administered to pregnant animals. In 2 separate studies, vigabatrin 50 to 200 mg/kg PO was administered to pregnant rabbits during the period of organogenesis and resulted in an increased incidence of cleft palate and embryo-fetal death. The no-effect dose for teratogenicity and embrolethality in rabbits is approximately half the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) of 3 g/day on a body surface area basis. Vigabatrin 50, 100, or 150 mg/kg PO was administered to pregnant rats during the period of organogenesis and resulted in decreased fetal weights and increased fetal anatomic variations (unspecified). The no-effect dose for embryo-fetal toxicity in rats is approximately 1/5 the MRHD in adults on a body surface area basis. When vigabatrin 50, 100, or 150 mg/kg was administered to rats from the latter part of pregnancy through weaning, neurological toxicity (e.g., hippocampal vacuolation and seizures) was noted in the offspring. Vigabatrin 5, 15, or 50 mg/kg PO was administered to newly born rats during the period of development that is thought to correspond to late pregnancy in humans in terms of brain development; the young rats developed significant neurobehavioral and neurohistopathological abnormalities. The no-effect dose for developmental neurotoxicity in juvenile rats was associated with plasma vigabatrin exposures (AUC) less than 1/30 of those measured in human pediatric patients receiving an oral dose of 50 mg/kg. Vigabatrin should only be used during pregnancy if the potential benefit outweighs the significant risk to the fetus. Physicians are advised to recommend that pregnant patients receiving vigabatrin enroll in the North American Antiepileptic Drug (NAAED) Pregnancy Registry to provide information about the effects of in utero exposure to the drug. Patients must call 1-888-233-2334 to enroll in the registry.
 
According to the manufacturer, a decision should be made whether to discontinue breast-feeding or vigabatrin, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother. Vigabatrin is excreted in human milk. When vigabatrin 50, 100, or 150 mg/kg was administered to rats from the latter part of pregnancy through weaning, neurological toxicity (e.g., hippocampal vacuolation and seizures) was noted in the offspring. In addition, special precautions apply to the use vigabatrin in infants (see Precautions content for infants). Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
Vigabatrin decreases alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) plasma activity in up to 90% of patients; ALT and AST become undetectable in some patients. The reductions may invalidate the use of these markers to detect early hepatic dysfunction.

    Headline
    visual impairment
    General Information
    Do not use vigabatrin in individuals with, or at high risk of, irreversible visual impairment or with other drugs associated with serious adverse ophthalmic events unless the benefits of treatment clearly outweigh the risks. Use the lowest dosage and shortest exposure to vigabatrin consistent with clinical objectives. Vigabatrin can cause permanent bilateral concentric visual field constriction, including tunnel vision that can result in disability. In some cases, vigabatrin may also decrease visual acuity. The risk of vision loss increases with increasing vigabatrin dose and cumulative exposure, but there is no dose or exposure to vigabatrin known to be free of risk of vision loss. Risk of new and worsening vision loss continues as long as vigabatrin is used, and possibly after discontinuing vigabatrin. Monitoring of vision by an ophthalmic professional with expertise in visual field interpretation and the ability to perform dilated indirect ophthalmoscopy of the retina is recommended. For individuals receiving vigabatrin, vision assessment is recommended at baseline (no later than 4 weeks after starting therapy), at least every 3 months while on therapy, and about 3 to 6 months after the discontinuation of therapy. Individualize the diagnostic approach for the individual and clinical situation. Because assessing vision may be difficult in infants and children, the frequency and extent of vision loss is poorly characterized in these populations; vision loss may not be detected in infants until it is severe. For this reason, the understanding of the risk is primarily based on adult experience. Because of this risk and because, when it is effective, vigabatrin provides an observable symptomatic benefit; periodically assess individual response and continued need for treatment. Discontinue vigabatrin if a significant clinical response is not achieved within 3 months of initiation for complex partial seizures, within 2 to 4 weeks of initiation for infantile spasms, or if clinical failure is obvious at any time point.
    Headline
    mood disorder, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Monitor for worsening of depression, suicidal thoughts or behavior, or any unusual changes in behavior in individuals with mood disorder or suicidal ideation; antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), including vigabatrin, increase the risk of suicidal thoughts or behavior in individuals taking these medications for any indication. Suicidal behavior and ideation may also occur after vigabatrin discontinuation. When considering AED use, balance the risk of suicidal thoughts or behaviors with the risk of untreated illness. The relative risk for suicidal thoughts or behavior was higher in clinical trials in subjects with epilepsy than in clinical trials in subjects with psychiatric or other conditions, but the absolute risk differences were similar for the epilepsy and psychiatric indications. Epilepsy and many other illnesses for which AEDs are prescribed are themselves associated with morbidity and mortality and an increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behavior. If suicidal thoughts and behavior emerge during treatment, consider whether the emergence of these symptoms may be related to the illness being treated.

    Headline
    adolescents, children, infants, neonates
    General Information
    Monitor pediatric populations, particularly those with pre-existing myelin abnormalities, closely for neurotoxicity during vigabatrin therapy. Abnormal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) signal changes have been observed in infants receiving vigabatrin for infantile spasms. MRI abnormalities tend to peak after 3 to 6 months of vigabatrin exposure and typically resolve, even with continued use of the drug. Simultaneous motor abnormalities have been reported in some infants; however, a causal relationship has not been established. MRI abnormalities affect infants and children 6 years and younger and are associated with high-dose therapy. Some investigators believe MRI changes are associated with developmental changes in myelination or an unknown underlying metabolic disorder. The clinical significance of MRI changes is unknown and the potential for long-term clinical sequelae has not been adequately studied. Animal models have suggested neurotoxicity may be more prevalent in neonates, infants, children, and adolescents compared to adults. Neurotoxicity has been observed in rats with vigabatrin exposure during late gestation and the neonatal and juvenile periods of development, and in dogs exposed to vigabatrin during the juvenile period. Neurohistopathological (brain vacuolation, hypomyelination, retinal dysplasia) and neurobehavioral (seizures, neuromotor impairment, learning deficits) abnormalities occurred in young rats at vigabatrin doses lower than those producing neurotoxicity in adult animals and were associated with drug plasma concentrations substantially lower than those achieved clinically in human infants and children. Although neurobehavioral effects were not assessed, similar neurohistopathological abnormalities were observed in juvenile dogs. White matter vacuolation and intramyelinic edema (IME) have been associated with vigabatrin in animal studies involving several species. IME has been very rarely reported in postmortem examination of vigabatrin-treated infants. A relationship between MRI abnormalities in infants and neurotoxicity has not been established.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Oral administration of a single dose of 1.5 g of vigabatrin to geriatric adults with reduced creatinine clearance (less than 50 mL/minute) was associated with moderate to severe sedation and confusion in 4 of 5 subjects, lasting up to 5 days. The renal clearance of vigabatrin was 36% lower in healthy elderly subjects than in young healthy males. Consider vigabatrin dosage adjustment; such individuals may respond to a lower maintenance dose. According to the Beers Criteria, anticonvulsants are considered potentially inappropriate medications in geriatric adults with a history of falls or fractures; avoid in at-risk individuals, except for treating seizure and mood disorders, since anticonvulsants can produce ataxia, impaired psychomotor function, syncope, and additional falls. If vigabatrin must be used, consider reducing the use of other CNS-active medications that increase the risk of falls and fractures and implement strategies to reduce fall risk.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Limited data from case reports and cohort studies on the use of vigabatrin during pregnancy have not established a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. However, based on animal data, vigabatrin may cause fetal harm. Administration of vigabatrin at clinically relevant doses during organogenesis was associated with an increase in fetal malformations (cleft palate) and embryo-fetal death in rabbits and decreased fetal body weight and increased fetal anatomic variations in rats. In rats, exposure during the latter part of pregnancy through weaning produced long-term neurohistopathological (hippocampal vacuolation) and neurobehavioral (convulsions) abnormalities in offspring. Neonatal and juvenile rat studies showed neurobehavioral (convulsions, neuromotor impairment, learning deficits) and neurohistopathological (brain vacuolation, decreased myelination, and retinal dysplasia) abnormalities. In terms of brain development, the early postnatal period in rats is generally thought to correspond to late pregnancy in humans. It is unclear whether prenatal vigabatrin exposure carries a risk for visual defects. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant patients exposed to vigabatrin; information about the registry can be obtained at www.aedpregnancyregistry.org or by calling 1-888-233-2334.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use vigabatrin with caution during breast-feeding. Limited data suggest that vigabatrin doses of up to 2,000 mg daily produce low levels in human milk, with a relative infant dose between 1.5% and 2.7%. Although these amounts are much less than the approved infant dosage, caution remains due to limited data on vigabatrin's use during lactation and the potential for both short-term and long-term neurotoxic effects, such as sedation and developmental delay. If used during lactation, monitor the child for sedation and respiratory difficulties. There are no data on the effects of vigabatrin on milk production or its effects on the breastfed child. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition.

  General Information
  Vigabatrin causes permanent visual impairment and vision loss in some pediatric and adult patients. Vigabatrin causes permanent bilateral concentric visual field constriction in 30% or more of adult patients; the incidence in pediatric patients is not well defined. The visual field defect can range in severity from mild to severe, including tunnel vision to within 10 degrees of visual fixation. Vigabatrin can also cause decreased visual acuity due to central retina damage. The onset of the vision disturbance can occur at any time during therapy, even after months or years, and may worsen quickly. The risk increases with increasing dose and duration; however, there is no known risk free exposure. Vision may worsen after vigabatrin discontinuation. Due to the risk for irreversible vision damage, vigabatrin should be discontinued if a significant clinical response is not achieved within 2 to 4 weeks of initiation in pediatric patients receiving vigabatrin for infantile spasms, within 3 months of initiation in adults, or if clinical failure is obvious at any time point. Vision loss is difficult to detect in pediatric patients and may not be detected until it is severe. In all patients, vision should be assessed at baseline (no later than 4 weeks after vigabatrin initiation), at least every 3 months during therapy, and approximately 3 to 6 months after vigabatrin discontinuation; assessment should include both acuity and visual fields whenever possible. Vision assessment must be performed by an ophthalmic professional with expertise in visual field interpretation and the ability to perform a dilated indirect ophthalmoscopy of the retina. Vigabatrin should be used at the lowest dose and shortest duration necessary to achieve clinical goals.
 
Other eye disorders, in addition to visual impairment, have been reported in association with vigabatrin use. In a dose response study in pediatric patients with infantile spasms, strabismus occurred in 5% of patients in each the low dose group (18 to 36 mg/kg/day, n = 114) and the high dose group (100 – 148 mg/kg/day, n = 108). Conjunctivitis occurred in 5% of the low dose group and 2% of the high dose group. Pooled data from 2 vigabatrin controlled trials in adults showed that diplopia occurred in 3% and 0% of patients who received vigabatrin (n = 222) and placebo (n = 135), respectively. Blurred vision occurred in 11% and 5% of vigabatrin and placebo groups, respectively, and asthenopia occurred in 2% of vigabatrin patients and 0% of placebo patients.
 
In clinical studies of vigabatrin in combination with other antiepileptic drugs that included 3139 adult and 999 pediatric patients, headache was reported in 18% of patients who received vigabatrin. Other nervous system adverse events included dizziness (15%), seizures (11%), tremor (7%), nystagmus (7%), memory impairment (7%), insomnia (7%), irritability (7%), and abnormal coordination (7%). Pooled data from 2 vigabatrin controlled trials in adults also reported each of the following adverse reactions in 5% of patients that received vigabatrin (n = 222): disturbance in attention, sensory disturbance, and hyporeflexia. In a dose response study in pediatric patients with infantile spasms, status epilepticus occurred in 6% of patients in the low dose group (18 to 36 mg/kg/day, n = 114) and 4% of patients in the high dose group (100 to 148 mg/kg/day, n = 108). In patients in the low and high dose groups, hypotonia occurred in 4% and 6%, respectively, and irritability and insomnia occurred in 16% and 23% and 10% and 12%, respectively.
 
Pooled data from controlled trials showed that edema occurred in 1% of vigabatrin patients compared to 0% of placebo patients, and peripheral edema occurred in 2% of vigabatrin patients compared to 1% of placebo patients. The development of edema did not appear to be associated with cardiovascular adverse events, such as heart failure or hypertension, and it was not associated with laboratory findings suggestive of renal or hepatic dysfunction. Facial edema was reported during world-wide post-marketing experience; however, incidence and causality have not been established. Clinical trials were not designed to determine whether vigabatrin causes edema in pediatric patients.
 
Abnormal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) signal changes have been observed in infants receiving vigabatrin for infantile spasms. These changes are characterized by increased T2 signal and restricted diffusion in a symmetric pattern involving the thalamus, basal ganglia, brain stem, and cerebellum. In most cases, the abnormalities resolved after vigabatrin discontinuation. In some cases, the infants experienced simultaneous motor abnormalities; however, a causal relationship was not established. In a blinded study of MRI images from 656 patients >= 3 years of age taking either vigabatrin or placebo for complex partial seizures, the MRI changes were not observed in older children or adults. Intramyelinic edema (IME), characterized by fluid accumulation and separation of the outer layers of myelin, has been observed in brain white matter tracts of several animal species. In addition, neurological toxicity, including seizures, hypomyelination, retinal dysplasia, and learning deficits, was observed in rats exposed to vigabatrin in utero and during the neonatal and juvenile periods; vacuolar changes in grey matter, which are considered to be different than IME, have also been observed in neonatal and juvenile rats receiving vigabatrin. These effects occurred at plasma vigabatrin concentrations substantially lower than those achieved clinically in human children. It has not been determined whether this neurotoxicity in animals is related to the MRI changes observed in human infants.
 
In controlled trials in adults, anemia or a potentially clinically important hematological effect involving hemoglobin, hematocrit, and/or red blood cell indices occurred in 5.7% (16/280) of patients receiving vigabatrin compared to 1.6% (3/188) of those receiving placebo. In pre-marketing controlled trials in the U.S., there were mean decreases in hemoglobin of approximately 3% and 0% in vigabatrin and placebo groups respectively.
 
In clinical studies of vigabatrin in combination with other antiepileptic drugs that included 3139 adult and 999 pediatric patients, somnolence and fatigue were reported in 17% and 16% of patients, respectively. Pooled data from 2 vigabatrin controlled trials in adults showed that somnolence occurred in 24% (54/222) of patients receiving vigabatrin and fatigue occurred in 28%. In pediatric trials of patients with infantile spasms, somnolence has been reported in 17% to 45% of patients. In a dose response study in patients with infantile spasms, sedation occurred in 19% and 17% of patients who received the low dose (18 to 36 mg/kg/day, n = 114) and high dose (100 to 148 mg/kg/day, n = 108), respectively, and lethargy occurred in 5% and 7% of those who received the low and high dose, respectively.

In a pooled analysis of North American controlled and uncontrolled trials, 4.2% (19/457) developed signs and/or symptoms of peripheral neuropathy. These clinical studies were not designed to investigate peripheral neuropathy and did not include nerve conduction studies. There is insufficient data to determine whether there is a relationship between the development of peripheral neuropathy and vigabatrin dose or duration, and it is not known if the neuropathy is completely reversible after vigabatrin discontinuation. Clinical trials were not designed to determine whether vigabatrin causes peripheral neuropathy in pediatric patients.

The mean weight gain was 3.5 kg in the vigabatrin group compared to 1.6 kg in the placebo group. In epilepsy trials, 0.6% (31/4855) of patients discontinued vigabatrin due to weight gain. Weight gain was not related to the presence or edema. Clinical trials were not designed to determine whether vigabatrin causes weight gain in pediatric patients. In a dose response study in pediatric patients with infantile spasms, decreased appetite occurred in 9% of patients in the low dose group (18 to 36 mg/kg/day, n = 114) and 7% of patients in the high dose group (100 to 148 mg/kg/day, n = 108).

In clinical trials of pediatric patients with infantile spasms, infections, particularly those that are common in all infants and children, occurred at a higher rate. In post-marketing experience worldwide, acute psychosis has been reported; however, incidence and causal relationship is not established.

In this same trial, constipation was reported in 14% and 12% of the low and high dose groups, respectively, and diarrhea was reported in 13% and 12%. In post-marketing experience worldwide, GI bleeding, esophagitis, and cholestasis have been reported; however, incidence and causal relationship is not established.

In a dose response study in pediatric patients with infantile spasms, fever occurred in 29% of patients in the low dose group (18 to 36 mg/kg/day, n = 114) and 19% of patients in the high dose group (100 to 148 mg/kg/day, n = 108); rash (unspecified) occurred in 8% of the low dose group and 11% of the high dose group. In post-marketing experience worldwide, maculopapular rash, pruritus, angioedema, and malignant hyperthermia have been reported; however, incidence and causal relationship is not established.
 
Pooled data from 2 vigabatrin controlled trials in adults reported arthralgia as the most commonly occurring musculoskeletal adverse reaction, occurring in 8% and 3% of patients receiving vigabatrin (n = 222) and placebo (n = 135), respectively. Other related adverse reactions reported included back pain (6% vs. 2%), extremity pain (5% vs. 4%), myalgia (3% vs. 2%), joint swelling (2% vs. 0%), muscle spasms (2% vs. 1%), and shoulder pain (2% vs. 1%).
 
In post-marketing experience worldwide, laryngeal edema, pulmonary embolism, respiratory depression (respiratory failure), and stridor have been reported; however, incidence and causal relationship is not established.
 
In post-marketing experience worldwide, multi-organ failure has been reported; however, incidence and causal relationship is not established.
 
Vigabatrin overdoses have been reported during clinical trials and post-marketing experience worldwide. The reported ingested doses range from 3 g to 90 g, but the majority were between 7.5 g and 30 g. No overdoses resulted in death, and nearly half of the cases involved the ingestion of multiple medications. Coma, unconsciousness, and/or drowsiness were described in most cases. Other less commonly reported symptoms include vertigo, psychosis, respiratory depression, bradycardia, agitation, confusion, headache, hypotension, abnormal behavior, increased seizure activity, status epilepticus, and speech disorder. Symptoms resolved with supportive care. There is no antidote for vigabatrin; supportive measures and standard procedures to remove unabsorbed drug (e.g., emesis or gastric lavage) should be used. In an in vitro trial, activated charcoal did not significantly adsorb vigabatrin.

    Headline
    laboratory test interference
    General Information
    Vigabatrin may cause laboratory test interference. Vigabatrin decreases alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) plasma activity in up to 90% of individuals; these enzymes become undetectable in some individuals. ALT and AST reductions may preclude the use of these markers, especially ALT, to detect early hepatic injury. Vigabatrin may also increase the amount of amino acids in the urine, possibly leading to a false positive test for certain rare genetic metabolic diseases (e.g., alpha aminoadipic aciduria).

Description
  Text
  Vigabatrin is an oral antiepileptic agent (AED) indicated for the treatment of refractory complex partial seizures as adjunct therapy in patients 2 years of age and older who have not responded to alternative therapy. Vigabatrin is also indicated for infantile spasms in pediatric patients 1 month to 2 years. Vigabatrin ready to use oral solution (Vigafyde) is only approved for use in infantile spams. Vigabatrin increases concentrations of gamma aminobutyric acid (GABA) in the brain by irreversibly inhibiting the enzyme responsible for GABA metabolism (GABA transaminase). It has been investigated for the treatment of many seizure types but appears to be most effective in treating complex partial seizures and infantile spasms, particularly those associated with tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC). Despite proven efficacy, vigabatrin use has been limited by the associated risk of retinopathy, characterized by irreversible and progressive bilateral concentric peripheral visual field constriction. Vigabatrin is only available through a restricted distribution program; only prescribers and pharmacies registered with the program may prescribe and dispense vigabatrin. With the exception of visual field defects, vigabatrin is generally well-tolerated with an adverse reaction profile comparable to that of other AEDs. Vigabatrin was FDA-approved in 2009.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Vigabatrin is a structural analog of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) that irreversibly inhibits and temporarily depletes GABA transaminase (GABA-T), the enzyme responsible for GABA degradation. GABA-T inhibition results in increased GABA concentrations in the brain and, therefore, increased inhibition of excitatory processes that initiate seizure activity. Restoration of GABA-T takes several days. Plasma concentrations of vigabatrin do not correlate to clinical effectiveness; decreases in seizure activity are directly related to increased GABA concentrations in the brain. GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system (CNS); approximately 30% of all synapses utilize this neurotransmitter. There are 3 known GABA receptors: GABAA and GABAB, which are widely present in the CNS, and GABAC, which is primarily found in the retina. GABAA receptors are ligand-gated ion channels located on the postsynaptic membrane; activation permits the influx of chloride into the cell, resulting in neuronal hyperpolarization and preventing propagation of neural impulses. GABAB receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors located on postsynaptic membranes and presynaptic terminals that, upon activation, initiate an intracellular signaling cascade. This signaling ultimately results in either neuronal hyperpolarization through the opening of potassium channels or a reduction in the activity of voltage-dependent calcium channels that mediate glutamate release at axon terminals. There is some evidence suggesting vigabatrin may also stimulate GABA release and inhibit glial uptake of the neurotransmitter.
 
The mechanism for vigabatrin-associated peripheral visual field defects is not fully understood. In animal studies, vigabatrin has been associated with a dosage-related decrease in GABA-T activity and increase in GABA concentrations that are more pronounced in the retina relative to other tissues. Although GABA is an inhibitory neurotransmitter, some evidence suggests it becomes excitatory and potentially excitotoxic when present in excessive concentrations in damaged neurons. Several human studies have demonstrated a correlation between thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer and vigabatrin-associated defects. Atrophy appears to emerge as a result of injury to both retinal photoreceptors in the outer retina and retinal ganglion cells and their axons in the inner retina. The amacrine cells of the inner retina, which support retinal cone function, and the Muller cells of the peripheral retina have both been implicated as potential sites of retinotoxicity in animal studies. Additionally, some evidence suggests that a vigabatrin-induced taurine deficiency may be associated with retinotoxicity. It is believed that the organic acid taurine is critical for normal retinal development and function. Although the mechanism is unclear, it has been proposed that vigabatrin-induced increases in GABA may elicit competitive inhibition of the blood-retina taurine transporter or that vigabatrin may have a direct effect on taurine uptake and release.

  Text
  Vigabatrin is administered orally as a racemic mixture of 2 enantiomers, S(+) and R(-); only the S(+) enantiomer is pharmacologically active. Vigabatrin does not bind to plasma proteins and is widely distributed throughout the body. The mean steady state volume of distribution is 1.1 L/kg in adult patients. Drug clearance is 7 L/hour in adult patients. The half-life in adults is approximately 10.5 hours. Of note, a direct correlation between plasma vigabatrin concentrations and efficacy has not been established; the duration of action is thought to be dependent on the rate of GABA synthesis rather than vigabatrin elimination.

Vigabatrin induces CYP2C9, but does not appear to induce other hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes. Vigabatrin is not metabolized by hepatic enzymes.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  NOTE: Although dosage adjustment is recommended for pediatric patients with renal impairment, specific guidelines are not available for infants and children younger than 2 years.
 
Based on estimated CrCl, the following dosage adjustments are recommended for patients 2 years and older:
CrCl more than 80 mL/minute: No dosage adjustment necessary.
CrCl 51 to 80 mL/minute: Reduce dose by 25%.
CrCl 31 to 50 mL/minute: Reduce dose by 50%.
CrCl 11 to 30 mL/minute: Reduce dose by 75%.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
The effect of dialysis on vigabatrin elimination has not been determined. In case reports of patients with renal failure receiving therapeutic doses of vigabatrin, hemodialysis reduced vigabatrin plasma concentrations by 40% to 60%. Dosing recommendations are not available.
