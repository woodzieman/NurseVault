          Dosage Description
          Cycle 1: 1 mg IV infusion on day 1; give 2 mg IV infusion on day 8, and 60 mg IV infusion on day 15. Cycle 2: 60 mg IV infusion on day 1. Cycle 3 and beyond: 30 mg IV infusion on day 1, then repeat every 21 days. In those achieving a complete response, stop treatment after 8 cycles. In those who have a partial response or stable disease, treatment may extend beyond 8 cycles until disease progression for up to a maximum of 17 cycles. Therapy interruption or discontinuation may be necessary in those who develop toxicity. Ensure adequate hydration prior to IV infusion. Premedicate before each dose in Cycles 1 and 2 as follows: dexamethasone 20 mg IV (preferred) or methylprednisolone 80 mg IV (complete at least 1 hour prior to the infusion) and diphenhydramine 50 to 100 mg (or equivalent) PO/IV and acetaminophen 500 to 1,000 mg PO at least 30 minutes before infusion. Continue premedication with subsequent IV infusion doses in those who experience cytokine release syndrome with a prior dose. At a median follow-up of 18.3 months, the independent review committee-assessed complete response rate was 60% in patients who had relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma who received mosunetuzumab in a multicenter, single-arm, phase 2 trial (n = 90). Additionally, 80% of patients achieved an objective response. The median time to and duration of response was 1.4 (range, 1.1 to 8.9) months and 22.8 (95% CI, 9.7 to not reached) months, respectively; the median progression-free survival time was 17.9 months. In this study, patients (median age, 60 [range, 29 to 90] years; bulky disease, 34%) had received a median of 3 (range, 2 to 10) prior therapies and all had received prior therapy with an alkylator and an anti-CD20 agent; 21% of patients had previously received an autologous stem-cell transplantation.

    Headline
    cytokine release syndrome
    General Information
    Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is a potentially fatal reaction that has been reported with mosunetuzumab use. To reduce the risk of CRS, ensure adequate hydration, administer mosunetuzumab according to a step-up dosing schedule, and give pretreatment medications. Interruption or discontinuation of therapy may be necessary in patients who develop CRS; evaluate the need for hospitalization, supportive care, and further management (using current practice guidelines) in patients with severe CRS. Advise patients who experience CRS or other adverse reactions that impair consciousness during mosunetuzumab therapy to refrain from driving or operating machinery or performing other dangerous duties or activities requiring coordination and concentration until the toxicity resolves.

    Headline
    infection
    General Information
    Do not administer mosunetuzumab to patients who have an active infection. Severe and fatal infections have been reported with mosunetuzumab therapy. Use mosunetuzumab with caution in patients with a history of recurring or chronic infections (e.g., Epstein-Barr virus), with underlying conditions that may predispose them to getting infections, or who have had significant prior immunosuppression treatment. Administer prophylactic antibiotics as recommended according to clinical guidelines. Therapy interruption or permanent discontinuation may be necessary in patients who develop an infection.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Based on its mechanism of action, mosunetuzumab may cause fetal harm. There are no available data on the use of mosunetuzumab during human pregnancy to evaluate for a drug-associated risk. In addition, animal reproductive and developmental toxicity studies have not been conducted. Mosunetuzumab causes T-cell activation and cytokine release; immune activation may compromise pregnancy maintenance. Human immunoglobulin (IgG) crosses the placenta; therefore, mosunetuzumab may be transmitted from the pregnant person to the developing fetus. In addition, based on findings in non-pregnant animals, mosunetuzumab can cause B-cell lymphocytopenia in infants exposed to mosunetuzumab in utero.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with mosunetuzumab. Mosunetuzumab may cause fetal harm. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking mosunetuzumab and for 3 months after the last dose.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Counsel patients to avoid breast-feeding during mosunetuzumab treatment and for 3 months after the last dose. There is no information regarding the presence of mosunetuzumab in human milk, its effect on the breastfed child, or its effect on milk production. Because IgG is present in human milk, there is the potential for mosunetuzumab absorption and subsequent B-cell depletion in the breast-fed child.

Interactions
Monitor for toxicity and adjust doses as needed for CYP substrates, especially during the first 2 weeks of each mosunetuzumab cycle and during and after cytokine release syndrome. Mosunetuzumab causes cytokine release that may alter CYP enzyme activity.

    Headline
    infection, pharyngitis, sepsis, sinusitis
    General Information
    Infection has been reported with mosunetuzumab therapy. In a pooled safety analysis (n = 218), infections including opportunistic infections were reported in 17% (grade 3 or 4, 14%) of patients with hematologic malignancies who received IV mosunetuzumab; fatal infection occurred in 0.9% of patients. The most common grade 3 or higher infections were pneumonia, sepsis, and upper respiratory tract infection. In a single cohort of an open-label trial, infections (including opportunistic infections) occurred in 17% (grade 3 or 4, 16%; grade 5, 3.2%) of patients with follicular lymphoma treated with subcutaneous mosunetuzumab. The most common grade 3 or higher infections in patients treated with subcutaneous mosunetuzumab were pneumonia, sepsis, and COVID-19; fatal cases of COVID-19 occurred in 3.2% of the same patients. Additional infections including upper respiratory tract infection (14% to 15%; grade 3, 2.1% to 2.2%), urinary tract infection including acute pyelonephritis (10%; grade 3, 1.1%), pneumonia (13% or less; grade 3 or 4, 4.3% or less), sepsis (less than 10%), COVID-19 infection (27% or less; grade 3 or 4, 4.3% or less), herpes zoster infection (less than 10%), cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection (less than 10%), Epstein-Barr viremia (less than 10%), and Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (less than 10%) occurred in patients with relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma who received IV or subcutaneous mosunetuzumab in separate cohorts of the same open-label trial; serious cases of pneumonia, sepsis, COVID-19 infection, and Epstein-Barr viremia were reported in 2% or more of patients treated with IV mosunetuzumab in this trial. The term upper respiratory tract infection included naso-pharyngitis, sinusitis, and rhinovirus infection. Therapy interruption or permanent discontinuation may be necessary in patients who develop an infection.

    anemia, lymphopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia
    General Information
    Myelosuppression including lymphopenia, neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia has been reported with mosunetuzumab therapy. Grade 3 or 4 decreases from baseline in lymphocytes (69% to 92%), neutrophils (26% to 38%), hemoglobin (10% to 19%), and platelets (6% to 12%) occurred in patients with hematologic malignancies treated with IV or subcutaneous mosunetuzumab across clinical trials; febrile neutropenia occurred in 2% to 2.1% of patients in the same population. An interruption or discontinuation of therapy may be necessary in patients who develop severe myelosuppression. Consider the prophylactic use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factors.

    pleural effusion, tumor flare
    General Information
    Tumor flare occurred in 1.1% to less than 10% of patients treated with IV or subcutaneous mosunetuzumab in separate cohorts of the same open-label clinical trial; serious cases of tumor flare occurred in 2% of more of the patients treated with IV mosunetuzumab in the same population.     cytokine release syndrome, hypotension, hypoxia, sinus tachycardia
    General Information
    Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) has been reported with mosunetuzumab therapy. Common symptoms include fever, chills, hypotension, sinus tachycardia, hypoxia, and headache. In a pooled safety analysis (n = 218), CRS was reported in 39% (grade 3, 2%; grade 4, 0.5%) of patients with hematologic malignancies who received IV mosunetuzumab; recurrent CRS occurred in 28% of patients. CRS developed most frequently following doses in cycle 1; the median duration of CRS in all cycles was 3 days. CRS occurred in 30% (grade 3, 2.1%) of patients (n = 94) with relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma treated with subcutaneous mosunetuzumab in a 1 cohort of a clinical trial; recurrent CRS occurred in 14% of patients. CRS occurred most frequently following the first two subcutaneous mosunetuzumab doses with 19% of patients experiencing CRS after the cycle 1 day 1 dose, 13% after the cycle 1 day 8 dose, and 2.1% after the dose on cycle 1 day 15. CRS resolved in all patients treated with subcutaneous mosunetuzumab with a median duration of 2 days. Continue premedication with subsequent mosunetuzumab doses in patients who experienced CRS after a prior dose. Interruption or discontinuation of therapy may be necessary in patients who develop CRS; evaluate the need for hospitalization, supportive care, and further management (using current practice guidelines) in patients with severe CRS. Advise patients who experience CRS or other adverse reactions that impair consciousness during epcoritamab therapy to refrain from driving or operating machinery until the toxicity resolves.

    anxiety, ataxia, confusion, delirium, dizziness, drowsiness, encephalopathy, gait disturbance, headache, hypoesthesia, immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, impaired cognition, insomnia, migraine, neurotoxicity, paresthesias, peripheral neuropathy, tremor, vertigo
    General Information
    Neurotoxicity including immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) has been reported with mosunetuzumab therapy; some cases were serious or life-threatening. Neurotoxicity may occur concurrently with cytokine release syndrome. Concomitant use of mosunetuzumab with other medications or substances that cause dizziness or mental status changes may increase the risk of neurotoxicity. In a pooled safety analysis (n = 218), neurotoxicity (39%; grade 3, 3%) including headache (21%), peripheral neuropathy (13%), dizziness (11%), mental status changes (6%), and ICANS (1%) were reported in patients with hematologic malignancies who received IV mosunetuzumab. Neurotoxicity (52%; grade 3, 1.1%) including headache (17%), insomnia (15%), dizziness including vertigo (10%), mental status changes (7%), tremor (less than 10%), and ICANS or suspected ICANS (3.1%, all grade 1) also occurred in patients with relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma treated with subcutaneous mosunetuzumab in a single cohort of an open label trial. Across a broader clinical trial population (n = 949), ICANS or suspected ICANS occurred in 2.2% of patients treated with IV or subcutaneous mosunetuzumab with grade 1 to 2 events occurring in 20 patients and grade 3 events occurring in 1 patient. ICANS occurred during the first cycle of treatment in 75% of patients with a median time to onset of 17 days; 88% of cases resolved after a median duration of 3 days. The term mental status changes included confusion, disturbance in attention, cognitive disorder/impaired cognition, delirium, encephalopathy, lethargy, and somnolence/drowsiness. Headache including migraine (32%; grade 3, 1.1%), dizziness including vertigo (12%), mental status changes (less than 10%), and motor dysfunction including ataxia, gait disturbance, and tremor (less than 10%) occurred in patients with relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma who received IV mosunetuzumab (n = 90) in an open-label, multicohort trial. Psychiatric adverse events including insomnia (12%) and anxiety (less than 10%) also occurred in patients in the same population. Peripheral neuropathy occurred in 11% of patients treated with subcutaneous mosunetuzumab and 20% of patients treated with IV mosunetuzumab in separate cohorts of the same clinical trial. The term peripheral neuropathy included paresthesias, dysesthesia, hypoesthesia, burning sensation, and neuralgia. Consider a neurology consultation for further evaluation and management in any patient with tremors, dizziness, insomnia, severe neurotoxicity, or any other adverse reactions that impair consciousness. Interruption or discontinuation of therapy may be necessary in patients who develop neurotoxicity; evaluate the need for supportive care and further management (using current practice guidelines) in patients with severe neurotoxicity or ICANS. Advise patients who experience neurotoxicity during mosunetuzumab therapy to refrain from driving or operating machinery until the toxicity resolves.

    General Information
    Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), including fatal cases, has occurred with mosunetuzumab therapy. HLH occurred in 0.5% of patients (n = 1,536) treated with IV or subcutaneous mosunetuzumab across a broad clinical trial population; most cases (5/7) occurred within the first 28 days following treatment initiation with 3 cases preceded by confirmed or suspected cytokine release syndrome (CRS). If HLH is suspected, interrupt mosunetuzumab therapy, evaluate and treat promptly per current practice guidelines.

Description
  Text
  Mosunetuzumab-axgb is a bispecific monoclonal antibody that is a CD20-directed CD3 T-cell engager. It may be given as an intravenous infusion or as a subcutaneous injection and is indicated for the treatment of adults with relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma after 2 or more lines of systemic therapy. Mosunetuzumab labeling has a boxed warning for cytokine release syndrome; severe neurologic toxicity has also been reported.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mosunetuzumab is a bispecific T-cell engaging (BiTE) monoclonal antibody that binds to CD20 expressed on lymphoma cells and CD3 expressed on the surface of T-cells. It is a humanized immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) antibody produced from Chinese Hamster Ovary cells using recombinant DNA technology. The binding of T-cells to lymphoma cells triggers a signaling cascade leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and proliferation of T-cells ultimately resulting in the lysis of CD20-positive B-cells.

Pharmacodynamics
Following the recommended dosage of IV mosunetuzumab, peripheral B-cell counts decreased to undetectable levels in 95% of patients by day 1 of cycle 2; this depletion was sustained at cycles 4 and 8. A transient elevation of cytokines (IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-alpha, and IFN-gamma) occurred at doses of 0.4 mg or more. Peak cytokine levels were observed in the first cycle within 24 hours after the day 1 (1 mg) and day 15 (60 mg) IV mosunetuzumab doses; cytokine levels returned to baseline prior to the next infusion. Following the recommended dosage of subcutaneous mosunetuzumab, peripheral B-cell counts decreased to undetectable levels in 94% of patients by day 1 of cycle 2; this depletion was sustained at Cycles 4 and 8. Following subcutaneous administration of mosunetuzumab, transient elevation of cytokines (IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-alpha, IFN-gamma) were observed at doses of 1.6 mg and higher with peak cytokine levels generally observed within 48 hours following the first full dose (45 mg) on day 8 of Cycle 1 and returning to baseline prior to the third full dose (45 mg) on day 1 of Cycle 2. The observed pattern of cytokine release following subcutaneous administration appeared slower and reduced relative to IV administration.
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP substrates
Mosunetuzumab may cause a cytokine release that suppresses CYP enzyme activity and which may increase the exposure of CYP substrates. No clinical studies evaluating the drug interaction potential of mosunetuzumab have been conducted. Following IV administration, increased exposure of CYP450 substrates is more likely to occur after the first dose of Cycle 1 and for up to 14 days after the second 60 mg IV dose of Cycle 2. Following subcutaneous administration, increased exposure of CYP450 substrates is more likely to occur after the first dose on day 1 of Cycle 1 and for up to 14 days after the 45 mg subcutaneous dose on day 8 of Cycle 1. Increased exposure of CYP450 substrates is also more likely if cytokine release syndrome (CRS) occurs during treatment with mosunetuzumab. Monitor and adjust concomitantly administered narrow therapeutic index drugs when indicated or suggested by the prescribing label for such medications.

Mosunetuzumab is available as single-dose 1-mg/mL or 30-mg/30 mL solution vials; further dilution is necessary before administration.

Withdraw an equal volume (as calculated above for the patient dose) from an infusion bag containing 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection or 0.45% Sodium Chloride Injection (and discard) prior to injecting the required dose to make the IV infusion.

30-mg dose: Inject 30 mL of drug into 50 mL, 100 mL, or 250 mL of diluent.
