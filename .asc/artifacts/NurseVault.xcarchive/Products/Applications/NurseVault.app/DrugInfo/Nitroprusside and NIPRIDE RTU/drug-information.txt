Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General dosing information:
Monitor blood pressure continuously, preferably using an intraarterial pressure sensor; nitroprusside can induce unlimited blood pressure reduction.
Confirm the drug effect at any infusion rate after an additional 5 minutes before titrating to a higher dose; small variations in infusion rate can lead to wide, undesirable variations in blood pressure.
Monitor urine output in addition to hemodynamics when nitroprusside is used for the treatment of acute cardiac failure.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Nitroprusside-induced hypotension can be severe, and this drug should be administered only in a setting where adequate equipment and personnel are available to monitor blood pressure closely. Nitroprusside should not be used in the treatment of compensatory hypertension, where the primary hemodynamic lesion is aortic coarctation or an arteriovenous shunt. The infusion should be titrated to maintain the desired blood pressure endpoints and clinical response (see Dosage and Administration). Sodium nitroprusside should not be used to produce hypotension during surgery in patients with known inadequate cerebral circulation, or in moribund patients coming to emergency surgery.
 
Sodium nitroprusside should not be used for the treatment of acute congestive heart failure associated with reduced peripheral vascular resistance such as high-output heart failure that may be seen in endotoxic sepsis.
 
Cyanide ions, a byproduct of nitroprusside metabolism, can build up to toxic levels during nitroprusside therapy. Nitroprusside infusions of 10 mcg/kg are considered maximal, and this rate should not be continued for more than 10 minutes to avoid cyanide toxicity (see Dosage).
 
Nitroprusside should not be used in patients with severe renal disease, renal failure, anuria, or renal impairment because excretion of thiocyanate, a nitroprusside metabolite, might be decreased, leading to toxicity. Prolonged infusion (e.g., more than several days) of nitroprusside should be avoided in patients with renal impairment; thiocyanate serum concentrations may accumulate in patients with renal impairment (see Dosage). Elderly patients are more likely to have an age-related decline in renal function. Although sufficient data are lacking on the effect of advanced aging on the response to nitroprusside, the elderly may be more sensitive to the hypotensive effects of the drug. Elderly patients may have reduced baroceptor function, and orthostatic hypotension may occur when vasodilators are administered.
 
Nitroprusside should not be used in patients with hepatic disease. Thiocyanate is the metabolite of nitroprusside. Since cyanide is metabolized by hepatic enzymes, it may accumulate in patients with severe liver impairment.
 
Thiocyanate, the metabolite of nitroprusside, inhibits the uptake and binding of iodine by the thyroid. Thus, nitroprusside should not be used in patients with hypothyroidism.
 
Nitroprusside should not be used in patients with hyponatremia because the drug causes an increase in sodium excretion that might be hazardous to such patients.
 
Nitroprusside should not be used in patients with vitamin B12 deficiency or low plasma vitamin B12 concentrations because the drug can alter vitamin B12 distribution and metabolism.
 
Patients with cerebrovascular disease or insufficiency, or coronary artery disease may be less tolerant of drug-induced hypotension, and nitroprusside should be used cautiously in these patients.
 
Patients with hereditary optic nerve atrophy (Leber's disease) or toxic amblyopia are deficient in the enzyme rhodanase, which is crucial for the metabolism of nitroprusside. These patients may be at an increased risk of developing cyanide toxicity while receiving nitroprusside therapy.
 
Nitroprusside can cause increases in intracranial pressure and therefore should not be used in patients with preexisting increased intracranial pressure including encephalopathy.
 
Nitroprusside should be used with caution in patients with pulmonary disease. Nitroprusside may aggravate pre-existing hypoxemia.
 
Nitroprusside should be used with caution in when used in patients for controlled hypotension during anesthesia have anemia or hypovolemia. The patient's ability to compensate may be diminished. These conditions should be corrected prior to use of nitroprusside.
 
Nitroprusside is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. Although no adequate human studies have examined the effects of this drug on the fetus, animal reproduction studies have shown adverse fetal effects. Therefore, in making the decision to administer this drug during pregnancy, the potential risks to the fetus must be weighed against the potential benefits to the mother.
 
It is not known whether nitroprusside and its metabolites are excreted in breast milk. Because of the potential for adverse reactions in nursing infants, decide whether to discontinue breast-feeding or to discontinue the drug, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother.

    increased intracranial pressure
    General Information
    Use nitroprusside with extreme caution in individuals with increased intracranial pressure as nitroprusside can cause a further increase in pressure.
    Headline
    hereditary optic nerve atrophy (Leber's disease), toxic optic neuropathy
    General Information
    Nitroprusside is contraindicated in individuals with hereditary optic nerve atrophy (Leber's disease) or toxic optic neuropathy. These individuals have unusually high cyanide/thiocyanate ratios, and these conditions are probably associated with defective or absent rhodanase, which is an enzyme that is crucial for the metabolism of nitroprusside.
    Headline
    aortic coarctation, arteriovenous shunt, ASA physical status class 5, cerebrovascular insufficiency, hypotension
    General Information
    Nitroprusside is contraindicated for the treatment of compensatory hypertension, where the primary hemodynamic lesion is aortic coarctation or an arteriovenous shunt. Nitroprusside is also contraindicated for use to produce hypotension during surgery in individuals with known inadequate cerebral circulation (cerebrovascular insufficiency) or moribund state (ASA physical status class 5E) coming to emergency surgery. Nitroprusside can cause excessive hypotension leading to hypoperfusion of vital organs, which can lead to irreversible ischemic injuries or death in individuals who are not properly monitored. Use nitroprusside only when available equipment and personnel allow blood pressure to be continuously monitored.
    Headline
    high output acute heart failure
    General Information
    Nitroprusside is contraindicated for the treatment of acute heart failure associated with reduced peripheral vascular resistance such as high output acute heart failure that may be seen in endotoxic sepsis. Administration of nitroprusside to individuals with acute heart failure and increased peripheral vascular resistance causes reductions in peripheral resistance, increases in cardiac output, and reductions in left ventricular filling pressure.
    Headline
    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use nitroprusside with caution in individuals with hepatic insufficiency or hepatic failure; individuals with hepatic dysfunction are more susceptible to cyanide toxicity.
    Headline
    anuria, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Individuals with renal impairment will predictably develop thiocyanate toxicity after prolonged, rapid infusions. Use limited, lower mean infusion rates for individuals with renal impairment or renal failure (eGFR less than 30 mL/minute/1.73 m2) or anuria. Most of the cyanide produced during metabolism of nitroprusside is eliminated renally in the form of thiocyanate. In renal failure, thiocyanate half-life can be doubled or tripled.
    Headline
    anemia, ASA physical status class 4, hypovolemia
    General Information
    Correct pre-existing anemia and hypovolemia before using nitroprusside for controlled hypotension during surgery. The individual's ability to compensate for these conditions may be diminished during anesthesia. Hypotensive anesthetic techniques may also cause abnormalities of the pulmonary ventilation/perfusion ratio; individuals intolerant of these abnormalities may require a higher fraction of inspired oxygen. Use nitroprusside with extreme caution in individuals who are especially poor surgical risks (ASA physical status class 4 and 4E).
    Headline
    cyanide toxicity
    General Information
    Nitroprusside metabolism produces dose-related cyanide. Nitroprusside infusion rates more than 2 mcg/kg/minute generate cyanide ion faster than the body can normally dispose of it. An individual's ability to buffer cyanide will be exceeded in less than 1 hour at the maximum infusion rate (10 mcg/kg/minute); limit infusions at the maximum rate to as short a duration as possible to avoid cyanide toxicity. In pediatric individuals, risk of cyanide toxicity has been associated with infusion rates of about 2 mcg/kg/minute or more for longer than 24 hours. Most of the cyanide produced during the metabolism of nitroprusside is eliminated in the form of thiocyanate. Thiocyanate is life-threatening when concentrations reach approximately 200 mg/L; therefore, routine monitoring of plasma thiocyanate concentrations is recommended in individuals with normal renal function when cumulative nitroprusside doses exceed 7 mg/kg/day.
    Headline
    improper use
    General Information
    Nitroprusside injection concentrate is not suitable for direct injection; the solution must be further diluted in sterile 5% Dextrose Injection before infusion to avoid improper use. Nitroprusside is for intravenous infusion only.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Use nitroprusside with caution in geriatric adults as they may be more sensitive to the hypotensive effects of nitroprusside.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Nitroprusside, especially in large doses or with prolonged use, may cause fetal cyanide toxicity if used during pregnancy, which may be fatal to the fetus. In the unusual case that there is no appropriate alternative to nitroprusside, advise pregnant individuals of the potential fetal risk. Human data are limited and insufficient to establish a definitive risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. Limited case reports observed adverse events, including stillbirth, in patients with severe pregnancy-induced hypertension who were treated with nitroprusside. Studies in sheep demonstrate that nitroprusside crosses the placenta; fetal cyanide concentrations were dose-related to maternal concentrations of nitroprusside. An infusion of 25 mcg/kg/minute for one hour resulted in death of all fetuses. Pregnant ewes infused with 1 mcg/kg/minute of nitroprusside for one hour delivered normal lambs.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Avoid the use of nitroprusside during breast-feeding due to the potential for serious side effects in the breastfed child. There are no data on the presence of nitroprusside in human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effects on milk production. Due to its short half-life (about 2 minutes), it is unlikely to be present in human milk. However, thiocyanate, a toxic metabolite of nitroprusside, is present in human milk and can be directly toxic to the breast-fed child. The half-life of thiocyanate is approximately 3 days. Cyanide, another metabolite, may also enter breast milk. If nitroprusside use is unavoidable, the patient should refrain from breast-feeding.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Nitroprusside is metabolized to cyanide and thiocyanate, which, although they do not contribute to the antihypertensive action, have the potential to cause severe toxic reactions. Cyanide accumulation can occur at infusion rates that exceed the capacity of hepatic rhodanase. Excess cyanide binds to mitochondrial cytochromes, inhibiting cellular respiration and possibly causing cell death. Although cyanide concentrations can be measured, this is usually impractical because toxicity can proceed more quickly than the specimen can be assayed. Early signs of cyanide toxicity are metabolic acidosis and an increase in blood lactate or lactate-pyruvate ratio. Other symptoms of cyanogen toxicity include coma, mydriasis, absent reflexes, dyspnea or shallow breathing, imperceptible pulse, or distant heart sounds. In general, cyanide toxicity is rare unless large doses of nitroprusside are administered to patients with renal insufficiency. Thiocyanate toxicity can also occur in patients with renal insufficiency who receive nitroprusside, but the onset is slower than cyanide toxicity. Thiocyanate toxicity occurs at plasma levels of 50—100 mcg/mL. Symptoms of thiocyanate toxicity include confusion or psychosis, hyperreflexia, muscle cramps, seizures, tinnitus, and/or coma.The most common adverse effect of nitroprusside therapy is hypotension. Nitroprusside is extremely potent and has a rapid onset of action. Sudden reductions in blood pressure can cause nausea/vomiting or syncope. Nitroprusside-induced hypotension generally subsides within 10 minutes following discontinuation of infusion. Patients can be placed in the Trendelenburg position during this time to increase venous return.Abdominal pain, apprehension, diaphoresis, dizziness, headache, muscle twitching, palpitations, restlessness, retrosternal discomfort commonly occurs with nitroprusside therapy when the blood pressure is reduced too rapidly. Although nitrate-induced headaches usually diminish quickly, aspirin or other analgesic agents may be given to alleviate the pain. Other reported adverse effects during nitroprusside administration include rash, decreased platelet aggregation, methemoglobinemia, ataxia, adynamic ileus, sinus bradycardia, sinus tachycardia, increased intracranial pressure, and flushing.

    Headline
    cyanide toxicity, metabolic acidosis, thiocyanate toxicity
    General Information
    Nitroprusside is metabolized to cyanide and thiocyanate, both of which can cause toxicity. Cyanide toxicity causes venous hyperoxemia. An early manifestation of cyanide toxicity is increasing dosage requirements to maintain blood pressure control. Metabolic acidosis may occur, but its emergence may not be evident for more than an hour after toxic cyanide concentrations accumulate. Discontinue nitroprusside and consider specific treatment (e.g., sodium nitrite, sodium thiosulfate) if cyanide toxicity develops. Suspicion of cyanide toxicity is adequate grounds for initiation of treatment. Most patients reported to have experienced cyanide toxicity have received relatively prolonged infusions, and the only patients whose deaths have been unequivocally attributed to nitroprusside-induced cyanide toxicity have been patients who had received nitroprusside infusions at rates (30 to 120 mcg/kg/minute) much greater than those now recommended. Elevated cyanide concentrations, metabolic acidosis, and marked clinical deterioration, however, have occasionally been reported in patients who received infusions at recommended rates for only a few hours, and in a single case, for only 35 minutes. In some of these cases, infusion of sodium thiosulfate caused dramatic clinical improvement, supporting the diagnosis of cyanide toxicity. Most of the cyanide produced during the metabolism of nitroprusside is eliminated in the form of thiocyanate. Thiocyanate is mildly neurotoxic (tinnitus, miosis, hyperreflexia) at serum concentrations of 60 mg/L. Thiocyanate is life-threatening when concentrations reach approximately 200 mg/L; therefore, routine monitoring of plasma thiocyanate concentrations is recommended in patients with normal renal function when cumulative nitroprusside doses exceed 7 mg/kg/day. Renal hemodialysis may be used to eliminate thiocyanate in cases of severe thiocyanate toxicity.

    General Information
    Nitroprusside can cause sequestration of hemoglobin as methemoglobin. The back-conversion process is normally rapid, and clinically significant methemoglobinemia (more than 10%) is seen only rarely in patients receiving nitroprusside. Even patients congenitally incapable of back-converting methemoglobin should demonstrate 10% methemoglobinemia only after they have received about 10 mg/kg of nitroprusside, and a patient receiving nitroprusside at the maximum recommended rate of 10 mcg/kg/minute would take over 16 hours to reach this total accumulated dose. Suspect methemoglobinemia in patients who have received more than 10 mg/kg of nitroprusside and who exhibit signs of impaired oxygen delivery despite adequate cardiac output and adequate arterial pO2. Methemoglobinemic blood is chocolate brown, without the expected color change on exposure to air. When methemoglobinemia is diagnosed, the treatment of choice is methylene blue 1 to 2 mg/kg IV over several minutes. Use methylene blue with extreme caution in patients likely to have substantial amounts of cyanide bound to methemoglobin as cyanmethemoglobin.

Description
  Text
  Nitroprusside is an intravenous direct-acting vasodilator indicated for the immediate reduction of blood pressure in patients in hypertensive crises, for the induction and maintenance of controlled hypotension to reduce bleeding in patients undergoing surgery, and for the treatment of acute heart failure to reduce left ventricular end-diastolic pressure, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, peripheral vascular resistance, and mean arterial blood pressure. It is a potent vasodilator with a rapid onset and short duration of action. Nitroprusside is primarily used to manage hypertensive emergencies but is also used when immediate reduction of preload or afterload is needed. Guidelines suggest nitroprusside as an adjuvant to diuretics for relief of dyspnea in patients with acutely decompensated heart failure if symptomatic hypotension is absent. Use of nitroprusside is limited by the potential for cyanide and/or thiocyanate toxicity. Cyanide can accumulate during prolonged or high-dose nitroprusside infusions or in the setting of renal or hepatic dysfunction.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The peripheral vasodilatory effects of nitroprusside are due to a direct action of the drug on arterial and venous smooth muscle. Other smooth muscle tissue in the body is not affected, and myocardial contractility is unaffected. Nitroprusside-induced peripheral vasodilation results in a reduced left ventricular afterload, and this, along with a reduced venous return to the heart (due to venous pooling of the blood and decreased arteriolar resistance), results in a slight increase in heart rate and decrease in cardiac output in hypertensive patients. In patients with congestive heart failure, nitroprusside improves left ventricular heart performance, with increases in cardiac index, cardiac output, and stroke volume. Heart rate also slows in these patients, and arrhythmias can be reduced or abolished. Nitroprusside also can decrease myocardial oxygen demand, which is beneficial to patients with ischemia.

  Text
  Nitroprusside is administered intravenously. It is rapidly distributed to a volume that is approximately coextensive with the extracellular space. The circulatory half-life of nitroprusside is about 2 minutes. Nitroprusside is metabolized by combination with hemoglobin to produce cyanide and cyanmethemoglobin. Methemoglobin, obtained from hemoglobin, can sequester cyanide as cyanmethemoglobin. Nitroprusside metabolism can lead to methemoglobin formation through dissociation of cyanmethemoglobin formed in the original reaction of nitroprusside with hemoglobin and by direct oxidation of hemoglobin by the released nitroso group. Relatively large quantities of nitroprusside are required to produce significant methemoglobinemia. Some cyanide is eliminated from the body as expired hydrogen cyanide, but most is converted to thiocyanate by thiosulfate-cyanide sulfur transferase (rhodanase), a mitochondrial enzyme. This reaction is rate-limited by the availability of sulfur donors (i.e., thiosulfate, cystine, and cysteine). Thiocyanate is eliminated in the urine, with a half-life of about 3 days. A patient with a normal red cell mass and methemoglobin concentrations can buffer approximately 175 mcg/kg of cyanide, which corresponds to a little less than 500 mcg/kg of infused nitroprusside.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed. Use caution when administering nitroprusside to patients with hepatic disease; patients with hepatic dysfunction are more susceptible to cyanide toxicity.
  Renal Impairment
  eGFR 30 mL/minute/1.73 m2 or more: No dosage adjustment needed.
eGFR less than 30 mL/minute/1.73 m2: Limit mean infusion rate to less than 3 mcg/kg/minute.
Anuria: Limit the mean infusion rate to 1 mcg/kg/minute.
