Drug Class: Chromium, in its trivalent state (i.e., Cr3+), is an essential trace element that is required for proper carbohydrate, lipid, and nucleic acid metabolism in the human body.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 15 mcg/day IV. Upper tolerable limits for PO dietary intake not established.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): interstitial nephritis, renal failure (unspecified), renal tubular necrosis

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Some chromium products are not for human use or ingestion. Chromium products labeled for industrial or craft use can be very toxic. A case of mistaken ingestion of a chromium-containing leather tanning solution by a patient has been reported. Patients should not take chromium products that are not labeled for use as nutritional supplements. If accidental exposure to such a product occurs, the patient should be instructed to contact a local poison control center and health care professional immediately.Parenteral chromium products must be diluted prior to intravenous administration. Direct intravenous injection or intramuscular administration is contraindicated, as the acidic pH of the parenteral chromium solution may cause considerable tissue necrosis.Some parenteral chromium and trace element products contain benzyl alcohol and should be avoided in patients with a history of benzyl alcohol hypersensitivity. Because benzyl alcohol has been associated with a 'gasping syndrome' in neonates, excessive exposure to benzyl alcohol containing products should be avoided.Patients with diabetes mellitus should pursue chromium supplementation with products such as chromium picolinate only under the supervision and advice of a qualified health care professional. Chromium supplementation may influence serum blood glucose concentrations and hemoglobin A(1c) levels; medications used for the treatment of diabetes may need dosages adjusted during chromium use. Frequent monitoring of blood sugar and other clinical parameters is recommended. Patients with diabetes should be well-versed in the detection and management of hypoglycemia.Chromium elimination may be decreased in patients with renal disease or renal impairment. Dosage reductions in supplemental parenteral or oral doses may be needed. Since chromium is primarily excreted via the renal route, supplementation should be approached with caution, particularly in patients maintained on dialysis or with renal failure.There is insufficient clinical data to promote chromium's role in obesity treatment at this time. In limited but controlled clinical trials, chromium does not appear to induce significant effects on body-fat composition or weight loss.The use of oral chromium supplements in children, like chromium picolinate, is not recommended. If dietary intakes of chromium are judged to be insufficient, the use of a general multivitamin and mineral supplement formulated for children should provide the needed nutritional supplementation (i.e., the estimated adequate intake or AI). Chromium supplementation has only been established for children receiving maintenance nutrition with parenteral nutritional formulas (TPN) to prevent or treat chromium deficiency.Chromium supplements should be used with caution in the lactating mother who is breast-feeding her infant. Chromium is normally a component of human breast milk. Current studies of chromium distribution indicate that the level of chromium excreted in human breast milk is independent of the mother's daily dietary intake. However, it is unknown if supplementation of chromium in levels above the current dietary adequate intake (AI) will influence breast-milk concentrations. According to the American Academy of Pediatrics, supplementation of chromium through the use of a general multivitamin and mineral supplement is not expected to be harmful.Parenteral chromium injections are classified in FDA pregnancy category C. The use of chromium in pregnancy is only established for the prevention of chromium deficiency in patients maintained on parenteral nutritional formulas (TPN). Other claimed uses for chromium, such as the use of chromium picolinate for the adjunctive treatment of gestational diabetes, are not recommended; safety and efficacy have not been established in these patients at this time. Currently available studies have not been well controlled and have not established the safety or efficacy of chromium intake in excess of the estimated adequate intake (AI) during pregnancy. Supplementation of chromium through the use of a general multivitamin and mineral supplement is not expected to be harmful.

    Headline
    premature neonates, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Chromium is primarily excreted via the kidneys, and its elimination may be reduced in patients with renal impairment or renal failure. Use caution with supplementation in this population, and consider dosage reduction for parenteral or oral formulations as appropriate. Parenteral chromium also contains aluminum, and aluminum toxicity may occur with prolonged administration in high-risk patients, including those with renal impairment and premature neonates. Premature neonates are at particular risk for aluminum toxicity because of immature renal function, and they require large amounts of calcium and phosphate solutions, which contain aluminum. Research indicates that patients with renal impairment, who receive parenteral aluminum at more than 4 to 5 mcg/kg/day, may develop aluminum-related CNS and bone toxicities. Tissue loading may occur at lower administration rates.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Animal reproduction studies have not been conducted with chromium injection, and it is unknown whether it can cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy. However, adverse effects have not been reported with the normal daily intake of chromium within the recommended dietary daily intake for a pregnant female. The use of chromium in pregnancy is only established for the prevention of chromium deficiency in patients maintained on parenteral nutrition (PN). Other claimed uses for chromium, such as the use of chromium picolinate for the adjunctive treatment of gestational diabetes, are not recommended; safety and efficacy have not been established in these patients at this time. Available studies have not been well controlled and have not established the safety or efficacy of chromium intake over the estimated adequate intake (AI) during pregnancy. The use of chromium over recommended dietary intake during normal pregnancy should be avoided unless, in the judgment of the physician, potential benefits in a specific, unique case outweigh the significant hazards involved.
    Headline
    planned procedure
    General Information
    Advise patients to discontinue chromium supplements at least 2 weeks prior to a planned procedure (e.g., surgery) due to the potential for altered glucose metabolism and possible interference with perioperative glycemic control.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Chromium injection, when supplemented in parenteral nutrition formulas at recommended daily doses, rarely causes adverse effects. Improved glucose tolerance, as noted by reductions in blood sugar and insulin requirements, may result from chromium supplementation in patients maintained on parenteral nutrition (TPN). Adverse reactions due to excessive parenteral supplementation of chromium have included gastritis or GI ulceration, hypoglycemia, nausea/vomiting, and seizures. CNS depression and drowsiness may be possible. If administered inadvertently as an undiluted intravenous injection or if given via direct intramuscular injection, chromium may cause phlebitis and local tissue irritation. Parenteral chromium injections should always be diluted in large volume parenteral fluids, such as TPN, prior to intravenous administration.Although chromium picolinate is thought to be safe, case reports of nephrotoxicity from oral supplementation have appeared in the medical literature. All subjects consumed doses well above the recommended adequate oral intake (AI) of 200 mcg per day. Rhabdomyolysis has been associated with ingestion of 1200 mcg of chromium for 2 days by an otherwise healthy body-builder. Reported symptoms included dehydration, musculoskeletal pain, muscular weakness, and bilateral leg muscle cramps. A dose of 600 mcg PO per day for 6 weeks reportedly resulted in chronic renal insufficiency in one patient according to the case report. Description
  Text
  Chromium, in its trivalent state (i.e., Cr3+), is an essential trace element that is required for proper carbohydrate, lipid, and nucleic acid metabolism in the human body. Dietary sources of chromium (Cr3+) include whole grains, egg yolks, brewer's yeast, liver, meats, nuts, potatoes with skin, and beer. Chromium supplementation is only be expected to improve disorders that are due to chromium deficiency; true deficiency is rare as most individuals get adequate dietary intake. Overt signs and symptoms of chromium deficiency are usually only observed in people eating diets high in refined foods or who are receiving long-term total parenteral nutrition without chromium supplementation. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) states that evidence is insufficient to support the routine use of supplements containing chromium and other micronutrients for glycemic control in people with diabetes mellitus; supplementation of chromium to improve glycemic control is not recommended. Controversy exists concerning other claimed benefits of chromium supplementation. Multiple studies have concluded that there is no evidence that chromium supplementation increases muscle mass to a level greater than that which is produced with a healthy diet and exercise alone. There also appears to be no basis for claims that chromium reduces body-fat or weight. Clinical studies of chromium intake have revealed no additional changes in body-fat percentages or weight loss in either obese females or young athletes following controlled diet and exercise regimens.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Chromium's role in the maintenance of proper metabolism and body functions is not completely understood. After absorption, chromium is transported in the plasma by transferrin and other low-molecular-weight organic transporters. While chromium competes with iron for transferrin binding sites, chromium supplementation does not appear to alter iron metabolism or hematological indices. Chromium's relationship to glucose metabolism is the best-understood function. The glucose tolerance factor (GTF) is a complex of 2 molecules of nicotinic acid with a small oligopeptide complexed to trivalent chromium. GTF is required for insulin action at the cell surface. It is currently thought that GTF facilitates the binding of insulin to insulin receptors in tissues or aides in glucose transport across cell membranes. Chromium may influence cholesterol and triglyceride metabolism via alterations in the enzymes that regulate cholesterol synthesis. Increased HDL-cholesterol has been reported following chromium supplementation in humans; total cholesterol measurements appear unchanged. Chromium also appears to be important for proper peripheral nerve function. The mechanism of these actions is unclear.

  Text
  Chromium is administered intravenously as chromic chloride after dilution with compatible intravenous fluids and orally as trivalent chromium (Cr3+) formulated as the picolinate and nicotinate salts. Intestinal absorption of trivalent chromium from the diet is normally low (i.e., 0.5—10% of food chromium content). Atomic absorption spectrometry can accurately assess chromium levels in blood, urine, or hair, but results cannot be related to the amount of total chromium in tissue stores. Chromium appears to be distributed throughout body tissues. The metabolism of chromium in the body is still poorly understood. Diurnal variations in serum chromium occur secondary to the ingestion of meals and the subsequent increase in insulin secretion. Most chromium ingested in the diet or as supplements is excreted renally via glomerular filtration or via a low-molecular-weight organic transport system; the exact route of renal excretion is not clear but appears to approximate a two-compartment model. Normal renal excretion of chromium is < 1 mcg/24 hours. However, urinary excretion may be increased to 3—50 mcg/24 hour with chromium supplementation above dietary intake. Small amounts of chromium are excreted in the bile and small intestine.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  Quantitative guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available. Chromium is primarily excreted via the renal route; severe kidney disease may make it necessary to reduce or omit chromium doses.
