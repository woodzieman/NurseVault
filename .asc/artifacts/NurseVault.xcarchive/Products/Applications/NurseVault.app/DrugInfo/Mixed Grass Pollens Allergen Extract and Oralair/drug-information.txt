Drug Class: Mixed grass pollens allergen extract is an oral immunotherapy approved for treatment of allergic rhinitis (hay fever) with or without conjunctivitis that is induced by certain grass pollens in people ages 5 to 65 years.

Routes: sublingual; Common adult doses: 300 IR/day SL.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): dysphonia, infection, throat irritation, gastroesophageal reflux, hypersalivation, weight loss, anxiety, dizziness, drowsiness, headache, hypoesthesia, paresthesias, tinnitus, asthenia, hyperemia, malaise, pallor, xerophthalmia, eosinophilia, lymphadenopathy, myocarditis, chest pain (unspecified), hypotension, palpitations, sinus tachycardia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Mixed grass pollens allergen extract can cause life-threatening allergic reactions, including a risk of serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis.

Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  Hypersensitivity to at least 1 of the 5 grass pollens contained in the product (Sweet Vernal, Orchard, Perennial Rye, Timothy, or Kentucky Bluegrass) should be confirmed by positive skin test or in vitro testing for pollen-specific IgE antibodies prior to administration.
Initiate treatment 4 months prior to the expected onset of each grass pollen season and continue throughout the season.
Safety data regarding starting treatment during the grass pollen season or restarting treatment after missing a dose are not available.

    Headline
    cardiac arrhythmias, hypertension, myocardial infarction, serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis, unstable angina
    General Information
    Mixed grass pollens allergen extract can cause life-threatening allergic reactions, including a risk of serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis. Use with great caution in individuals with a history of atopy or sublingual allergen immunotherapy hypersensitivity; these individuals may be predisposed to severe allergic reactions. Mixed grass pollens allergen extract is contraindicated in individuals with a history of severe local reaction to sublingual allergen immunotherapy and/or severe systemic allergic reactions of any type. Systemic allergic reactions including life-threatening anaphylaxis and severe local reactions (e.g., laryngopharyngeal swelling) that may compromise breathing may occur; treatment with epinephrine may be required. Individuals who experience any systemic reaction to mixed grass pollens allergen extract should stop taking the extract. Individuals who have persistent or escalating local reactions should be reevaluated, and discontinuation of mixed grass pollens allergen extract should be considered. Administration of the first dose requires a specialized care setting due to the risk of severe allergic reactions; give the first dose in a healthcare setting where acute allergic reactions can be recognized and treated by an experienced clinician. Observe the patient for at least 30 minutes post-dose for signs or symptoms of a severe systemic or local reaction. If the patient tolerates the first dose, subsequent doses may be administered outside of the healthcare setting. It is recommended that auto-injectable epinephrine be made available to individuals; instruct individuals to recognize symptoms of a severe allergic reaction, about the proper use of epinephrine, and to seek immediate medical care upon use. Mixed grass pollens allergen extract may not be suitable for individuals with medical conditions that may decrease the patient's ability to survive a serious allergic reaction or increase the risk of adverse reactions after epinephrine administration. These conditions may include, but are not limited to, cardiovascular disease, such as unstable angina, recent myocardial infarction, significant cardiac arrhythmias, and uncontrolled hypertension. Further, mixed grass pollens allergen extract may not be suitable for individuals taking medications that can potentiate or inhibit the effects of epinephrine or decrease the effectiveness of inhaled bronchodilators; these medications include beta-adrenergic blockers, alpha-adrenergic blockers, ergot alkaloids, tricyclic antidepressants, levothyroxine, monoamine oxidase inhibitors, chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, cardiac glycosides, and diuretics. Concomitant use of mixed grass pollens allergen extract with other allergen immunotherapy has not been studied; concurrent use may increase the risk of local or systemic reactions to either subcutaneous or sublingual immunotherapy. The risks of mixed grass pollens allergen extract may be increased when treatment is initiated during the grass pollen season.
    Headline
    pulmonary disease, severe asthma, uncontrolled asthma
    General Information
    Mixed grass pollens allergen extract is contraindicated in individuals with severe asthma, unstable asthma, or uncontrolled asthma (e.g., status asthmaticus or acute bronchospasm). Individuals with recurrent asthma exacerbations should be reevaluated for appropriateness of therapy with mixed grass pollens allergen extract and discontinuation considered. Mixed grass pollens allergen extract may not be suitable for individuals who have pulmonary disease or acute or chronic compromised lung function that may reduce the patient's ability to survive a serious allergic reaction or increase the risk of adverse reactions after epinephrine administration (e.g., asthma, chronic lung disease (CLD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), or emphysema). The extract has not been studied in individuals with moderate or severe asthma or in those who require daily medications for asthma treatment.
    Headline
    eosinophilic esophagitis
    General Information
    Mixed grass pollens allergen extract is contraindicated in individuals with a history of eosinophilic esophagitis. Discontinue the extract in individuals who experience severe or persistent gastro-esophageal symptoms (e.g., dysphagia, chest pain) and consider a diagnosis of eosinophilic esophagitis.

    Available human data do not establish the presence or absence of pregnancy-related risk associated with the use of mixed grass pollens allergen extract. Reproductive and developmental toxicity studies in animals revealed no evidence of fetal harm.
    Headline
    oral wound, stomatitis
    General Information
    Withhold therapy with mixed grass pollens allergen extract in individuals with any type of oral inflammation (e.g., oral lichen planus, mouth ulcers, stomatitis, or thrush) or an oral wound, such as those after dental work including dental extraction or oral surgery, until complete healing of the oral cavity occurs.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  Adverse Reactions
    Headline
    anaphylactic shock, anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema, atopic dermatitis, cough, dyspnea, edema, flushing, laryngeal edema, pruritus, rash, serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis, urticaria, wheezing
    General Information
    Mixed grass pollens allergen extract can cause life-threatening serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis, including anaphylactoid reactions, severe angioedema (e.g., laryngopharyngeal edema), and anaphylactic shock. Serious adverse reactions considered related to the medication include 1 severe hypersensitivity reaction manifested as violent coughing and dyspnea that began 5 minutes after drug administration; the reaction resolved after treatment with antihistamines, albuterol, and prednisolone. A second patient experienced severe laryngeal edema that resolved upon treatment with prednisolone. Across 6 clinical trials in adults (n = 1,038), edema of the mouth, lip, throat, or tongue occurred in 8.2%, 4.4%, 3.8%, and 2.7% of patients, respectively. Overall, cough was observed in 7.3% of patients. Dermatologic or mucosal tissue reactions included ear pruritus (8.4%), tongue pruritus (7.9%), oral pruritus (25.1%), and urticaria (2.3%). Dermatologic reactions reported in clinical trials in children and adolescents included oral pruritus (11.7%), tongue pruritus (8.1%), mouth edema (6.2%), cough (6.2%), ear pruritus (5.2%), eye pruritus (4.6%), lip edema (3.3%), lip pruritus (3.2%), atopic dermatitis (3.2%), and tongue edema (2.3%). Two pediatric patients experienced serious adverse reactions related to mixed grass pollens allergen extract therapy. On day 5, an 8-year-old patient with a history of allergic bronchial asthma developed oral pruritus, conjunctivitis, urticaria, and asthma exacerbation 15 minutes after administration of mixed grass pollens allergen extract therapy. The reactions fully resolved 30 minutes after treatment with antihistamines and inhaled salbutamol and the patient continued therapy. On day 26, a 6-year-old patient developed severe lip, eye, and eyelid swelling after administration of mixed grass pollens allergen extract therapy. The patient was treated with intravenous antihistamines and prednisolone and the reactions fully resolved within 6 hours, but therapy was discontinued. During postmarketing safety studies, anaphylactoid reactions, oral allergy syndrome, flushing, dyspnea, and laryngeal edema occurred. Reactions reported during postmarketing experience include angioedema, stridor, face edema, wheezing, rash, and pruritus. Due to the potential for severe allergic reactions, give the first dose in a healthcare setting where acute allergic reactions can be treated by a physician experienced in the diagnosis and treatment of severe allergic reactions. Observe the patient for at least 30 minutes post-dose for signs or symptoms of a severe systemic or local reaction. If the patient tolerates the first dose, subsequent doses may be administered outside of the healthcare setting. Auto-injectable epinephrine should be made available to patients; instruct patients to recognize symptoms of a severe allergic reaction, about the proper use of epinephrine, and to seek immediate medical care upon use.

Description
  Text
  Mixed grass pollens allergen extract is an oral immunotherapy approved for treatment of allergic rhinitis (hay fever) with or without conjunctivitis that is induced by certain grass pollens in people ages 5 to 65 years. Mixed grass pollens allergen extract contains a mixture of freeze-dried extracts from the pollens of 5 grass species, including Kentucky Blue Grass, Orchard, Perennial Rye, Sweet Vernal, and Timothy. Severe allergic reactions, such as anaphylaxis, which can be life-threatening, can occur. After administration of the first dose at the health care provider's office, where the patient can be observed for potential adverse reactions, mixed grass pollens allergen extract can be taken at home.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  When mixed grass pollens allergen extract is allowed to dissolve sublingually, allergens bind to epithelial cells and cross the oral mucosa, where they are taken up by tolerogenic antigen-presenting cells (i.e., Langerhans cells and myeloid dendritic cells). The allergens are then processed into small immunogenic peptides, and the antigen-presenting cells migrate into local regional lymph nodes (submaxillary, cervical, internal jugular). There, allergen peptide fragments are presented to naive CD4+ T cells. This interaction stimulates suppressive T helper (Th) 1 and regulatory T cells and inhibits the activation and proliferation of Th2 cells. Subsequently, T cells encourage B cells to produce protective antibody responses, including secretion of allergen-specific IgG4 and IgA and, later, inhibition of IgE. Regulatory T cells may also suppress other inflammatory cells (e.g., eosinophils, mast cells, basophils) either by cytokine secretion or direct cell-to-cell contact. CD4+ T cells eventually migrate into the blood and tissues, resulting in allergen tolerance.

Pharmacokinetics
  Text
  Mixed grass pollens allergen extract is administered sublingually. The pharmacokinetics of the extract are not well defined and in vivo human research is lacking. Limited pharmacokinetic data are available for sublingual immunotherapy in general. However, direct contact with the oral mucosa has been determined to be the critical step in ensuring adequate exposure.
 
Parietaria judaica is a perennial plant with highly allergenic pollen. Human pharmacodynamic studies of radiolabeled Parietaria judaica allergen have shown little systemic absorption into the bloodstream through the sublingual mucosa, despite its highly vascular nature. In one study, radioactivity was not detectable in the plasma until swallowing occurred, at which point the plasma radioactivity slowly rose and peaked at approximately 2 hours. In another study using Parietaria judaica, a small amount of the allergen (about 2% of the administered dose) was detected within the oral mucosa 20 hours after dosing. It has been suggested allergens bind to epithelial cells within a few minutes. In a biodistribution study of sublingual radiolabeled ovalbumin in mice, allergen crossed the oral mucosa within 15—30 minutes and was captured by antigen-presenting cells within 30—60 minutes. At 60 minutes, allergen began to disappear from the submucosa, perhaps coinciding with uptake and processing by the antigen-presenting cells. Within 12—24 hours after administration, the antigen-presenting cells migrate to the lymph nodes where they interact with CD4+ cells and further promote the desensitization process.
