Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General dosing information:
Perform hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection screening in all individuals before ocrelizumab initiation. Ocrelizumab is contraindicated in individuals with active HBV infection, confirmed by positive results for surface antigen [HBsAg] and anti-HBV tests. Consult liver disease experts before starting and during treatment with ocrelizumab for individuals who are negative for HBsAg and positive for HB core antibody [HBcAb+], or who are carriers of HBV [HBsAg+].
Obtain serum aminotransferases (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and aspartate aminotransferase [AST]), alkaline phosphatase, and bilirubin concentrations before ocrelizumab initiation.
Perform testing for quantitative serum immunoglobulins before starting ocrelizumab treatment. Consult immunology experts before starting treatment with ocrelizumab for individuals with low serum immunoglobulins.
Administer all live or live-attenuated vaccinations according to vaccination guidelines at least 4 weeks before initiation of ocrelizumab, and administer non-live vaccines at least 2 weeks before initiation, whenever possible. Vaccination with live-attenuated or live vaccines is not recommended during treatment and until B-cell repletion.
Delay ocrelizumab administration in individuals with an active infection until the infection has resolved.

  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  Contraindications And Precautions
    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use
    General Information
    Ocrelizumab is contraindicated in individuals with a history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use, specifically infusion-related reactions, with severities ranging from mild to life-threatening. Administration of ocrelizumab requires an experienced clinician with access to appropriate medical support to manage severe reactions. Administer methylprednisolone (or an equivalent corticosteroid) and an antihistamine prior to ocrelizumab administration to reduce the frequency and severity of infusion reactions. Consider the addition of an antipyretic. Observe individuals for infusion reactions during the infusion and for 1 or more hours following infusion completion. Infusion reactions may occur up to 24 hours after the infusion. Dose adjustments are necessary for individuals who experience infusion reactions, and adjustments are dependent on the severity of the reaction. For life-threatening reactions, immediately and permanently stop treatment. For less severe reactions, management may include temporarily stopping the infusion and/or reducing the infusion rate. In all cases, administer appropriate supportive treatment.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    infection
    General Information
    Delay ocrelizumab initiation or continued treatment in individuals with an active infection until the infection has resolved. Before each dose, determine if the patient has an active infection. An increased risk of infections, including serious infections, has been observed with other B-cell-depleting therapies. During trials, ocrelizumab increased the risk for upper respiratory tract infections, lower respiratory tract infections, skin infections, and herpes-related infections. Ocrelizumab was not associated with an increased risk of serious infections in MS patients in controlled trials.
    Headline
    hepatitis B infection
    General Information
    Ocrelizumab is contraindicated in individuals with active hepatitis B infection, confirmed by positive results for surface antigen [HBsAg] and anti-hepatitis B virus (HBV) tests. Perform HBV screening in all individuals before initiation of ocrelizumab treatment. Consult liver disease experts before starting and during treatment with ocrelizumab for individuals who are negative for HBsAg and positive for HB core antibody [HBcAb+], or who are carriers of HBV [HBsAg+]. Fulminant hepatitis, hepatic failure, and death caused by HBV reactivation have occurred in individuals treated with other anti-CD20 antibodies.
    Headline
    General Information
    General Information
    Vaccination with live-attenuated or live vaccines is not recommended during ocrelizumab treatment and until B-cell repletion. Administer all age-appropriate live or live-attenuated vaccines according to immunization guidelines at least 4 weeks prior to initiation of ocrelizumab and, whenever possible, administer any age-appropriate non-live vaccines at least 2 weeks prior to initiation of ocrelizumab.
    Headline
    hypogammaglobulinemia
    General Information
    Perform testing for quantitative serum immunoglobulins before initiating ocrelizumab treatment. Consult immunology experts before initiating treatment with ocrelizumab in individuals with low serum immunoglobulins (hypogammaglobulinemia). As expected with any B-cell depleting therapy, decreased immunoglobulin levels are observed with ocrelizumab treatment. There is an association between decreased levels of immunoglobulin G and increased rates of serious infections. Monitor quantitative serum immunoglobulin concentrations during treatment and after discontinuation of treatment, until B-cell repletion, and in the setting of recurrent serious infections. Consider discontinuation of ocrelizumab in patients with serious opportunistic or recurrent serious infections, and if prolonged hypogammaglobulinemia requires treatment with intravenous immunoglobulins.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Ocrelizumab may pose a reproductive risk and may cause fetal harm. Instruct people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while receiving ocrelizumab therapy and for 6 months after the last infusion.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Do not administer live or live-attenuated vaccines before confirming B-cell count recovery, as measured by CD19+ B-cells, in neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero. Depletion of B-cells from ocrelizumab in the exposed neonate or infant may increase the risks from live or live-attenuated vaccines. Non-live vaccines, as indicated, may be administered before recovery from B-cell depletion; however, consider consultation with a qualified specialist to assess whether a protective immune response was mounted.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use ocrelizumab with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of ocrelizumab in human milk, the effects on the breastfed infant, or the effects of the drug on milk production. Ocrelizumab was excreted in the milk of ocrelizumab-treated monkeys. Human IgG is excreted in human milk, and the potential for absorption of ocrelizumab to lead to B-cell depletion in the infant is unknown. Weigh the benefits of breast-feeding along with the clinical need for ocrelizumab and any potential adverse effects of the medication on the breastfed infant or from the underlying maternal condition.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Ocrelizumab may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy, based on animal data and its mechanism of action. Advise pregnant people of the potential fetal risk. Ocrelizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody of an immunoglobulin G1 subtype, and immunoglobulins are known to cross the placental barrier. However, transient peripheral B-cell depletion and lymphocytopenia have been reported in infants born to people exposed to other anti-CD20 antibodies during pregnancy. During animal studies, depletion of B-lymphocytes in lymphoid tissues (spleen and lymph nodes) was observed in the fetuses of pregnant monkeys given ocrelizumab at doses of 2 times to 20 times the recommended human dose of 600 mg (on a mg/kg basis). Increased perinatal mortality was observed in the offspring of pregnant monkeys; the exact cause of death is unknown; however, the 2 offspring affected both had bacterial infections. Other adverse effects observed in the neonate monkeys include renal toxicity (glomerulopathy and inflammation), lymphoid follicle formation in the bone marrow, severe decreases in circulating B-lymphocytes, and reduced testicular weight.

Interactions
Alemtuzumab: (Major) Ocrelizumab has not been studied in combination with other immunosuppressive or immune modulating therapies used for the treatment of multiple sclerosis, such as alemtuzumab. Concomitant use of ocrelizumab with alemtuzumab may increase the risk of immunosuppression. Avoid the use of these drugs together.
Anifrolumab: (Major) Coadministration is not recommended. Anifrolumab has not been studied in combination with other biologic therapies including B-cell targeted therapies such as ocrelizumab. Potential concerns with use of these drugs together include an increased susceptibility to immunosuppression and serious infections, some of which might be fatal.
Anthrax Vaccine: (Moderate) Administer all non-live vaccines at least 2 weeks before ocrelizumab initiation, whenever possible. Ocrelizumab may interfere with the effectiveness of non-live virus vaccines. Attenuated antibody responses to tetanus toxoid-containing vaccine, pneumococcal polysaccharide and pneumococcal conjugate vaccines, and seasonal influenza vaccine were observed in patients exposed to ocrelizumab at the time of vaccination during an open-label study. Infants born to mothers exposed to ocrelizumab during pregnancy may receive non-live vaccines as indicated before B-cell recovery; however, consider assessing the immune response to the vaccine. ACIP recommends that patients receiving any vaccination during immunosuppressive therapy or in the 2 weeks prior to starting therapy should be considered unimmunized and should be revaccinated a minimum of 3 months after discontinuation of therapy. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination.
Bacillus Calmette-Guerin Vaccine, BCG: (Major) Due to the lack of clinical information related to the safety and efficacy of vaccine administration during ocrelizumab use, vaccination with live vaccines or live-attenuated vaccines is not recommended in patients taking ocrelizumab.     Headline
    anaphylactoid reactions, bronchospasm, dizziness, dyspnea, erythema, fatigue, fever, flushing, headache, hypotension, infusion-related reactions, laryngeal edema, nausea, pruritus, rash, sinus tachycardia, throat irritation, urticaria
    General Information
    Infusion-related reactions are common with ocrelizumab, particularly with the first infusion. Across clinical trials, individuals were premedicated with methylprednisolone or an equivalent steroid and may also have received an antihistamine (all pediatric patients) and/or an analgesic/antipyretic before each infusion. In adults, infusion reactions occurred in 34% to 40% of individuals; none were fatal, but 0.3% were serious, some requiring hospitalization. In pediatric patients, 48% had an infusion reaction; 31% of reactions occurred with the first infusion of the initial dose on Day 1, 9% occurred with Dose 2 on Day 15, and 16% occurred with the first subsequent dose at 6 months. The incidence and severity decreased with additional infusions. No fatal reactions occurred in pediatric patients, although 1 patient had a serious reaction requiring hospitalization. Infusion-related reactions may include pruritus, rash, anaphylaxis or anaphylactoid reactions, urticaria, erythema, bronchospasm, throat irritation, oropharyngeal pain, dyspnea, pharyngeal or laryngeal edema, flushing, hypotension, fever, fatigue, headache, dizziness, nausea, and sinus tachycardia. Adults without a prior serious infusion reaction may tolerate a shortened 2-hour infusion; in 1 safety study, reaction rates were similar with 2-hour vs. 3.5-hour infusions. Premedicate before all infusions with methylprednisolone or an equivalent steroid and an antihistamine, and consider acetaminophen. If reactions occur, modify treatment based on severity: permanently discontinue for life-threatening or disabling reactions; immediately interrupt the infusion for severe reactions and restart only after all symptoms have resolved; and reduce the infusion rate for mild to moderate reactions. Provide supportive treatment as needed and observe patients during the infusion and for at least 1 hour after each infusion, as reactions may occur up to 24 hours later.

    cough, hepatic failure, hepatitis, hepatitis B exacerbation, infection, leukoencephalopathy, meningitis, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy
    General Information
    Infections are common among patients treated with ocrelizumab. Serious, including life-threatening or fatal bacterial, viral, parasitic, and fungal, infections have been reported during and after treatment with anti-CD20 B-cell depleting therapies, including ocrelizumab. In adult clinical trials, infections occurred in 58% of individuals with relapsing forms of MS and 70% of individuals with primary progressive MS treated with ocrelizumab. Upper respiratory tract infections occurred in 40% to 49% and lower respiratory tract infections in 8% to 10% of adult patients; infections were mostly mild to moderate. Cough was reported in 7% of adult patients with primary progressive MS. In pediatric patients, 73% of individuals receiving ocrelizumab reported an infection (1.5 infections per patient-year). Serious infections occurred in 3% of pediatric patients (0.03 serious infections per patient-year). Upper respiratory tract infections, nasopharyngitis, and influenza were more frequently reported in ocrelizumab-treated patients compared with fingolimod-treated patients and were mild to moderate. Serious cases of infections caused by herpes simplex virus and varicella zoster virus, including central nervous system infections (encephalitis and meningitis), intraocular infections, and disseminated skin and soft tissue infections, have been reported with ocrelizumab during postmarketing experience. Some infections were life-threatening. Serious herpes infections may occur at any time during treatment. During adult active-controlled trials, herpes-related infections were reported more frequently with ocrelizumab than interferon beta-1a (6% vs. 4%); events included herpes zoster, herpes simplex, oral herpes, genital herpes, and herpes virus infection. These infections were mostly mild to moderate. Herpes-related infections were reported more frequently in ocrelizumab-treated patients compared to placebo (5% vs. 4%) during trials of primary progressive MS. Oral herpes was reported more frequently in ocrelizumab-treated patients compared to placebo-treated patients (2.7% vs. 0.8%). Skin infections occurred more frequently in ocrelizumab-treated patients compared placebo-treated patients (14% vs. 11%). Additionally, reports of babesiosis and pyoderma gangrenosum have been reported with postmarketing use. Delay treatment with ocrelizumab until an active infection resolves. Hepatitis B exacerbation or reactivation has been reported in patients treated with ocrelizumab during postmarketing experience. Fulminant hepatitis, hepatic failure, and death caused by HBV reactivation have occurred in patients treated with other anti-CD20 antibodies. Ocrelizumab is contraindicated for use in patients with active hepatitis B infection. Perform HBV screening for all patients prior to treatment, and do not administer ocrelizumab to patients with active HBV confirmed by positive results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and hepatitis B surface antibody (anti-HB) tests. If patients are negative for HBsAg and positive for HB core antibody (HBcAb+) or are carriers of HBV (HBsAg+), consult liver disease experts before initiation and during treatment. Cases of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), an opportunistic viral infection of the brain caused by the JC virus (JCV), have been reported in patients treated with ocrelizumab in the postmarketing setting. PML cases occurred in patients with no known immune compromising conditions and patients who had not previously received other immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory medications that are associated with PML, including natalizumab. PML has been observed in patients treated with other anti-CD20 antibodies and other MS therapies, and it has been associated with some risk factors (e.g., concomitant immunosuppression, immunocompromised patients). As PML usually leads to death or severe disability, it is of vital importance to withhold ocrelizumab and perform an appropriate diagnostic evaluation at the first sign or symptom of PML. Symptoms of PML are diverse, including progressive weakness on 1 side of the body, clumsiness of limbs, vision disturbances, or confusion or personality alterations from changes in thinking, memory, or orientation. MRI findings of PML may be apparent before clinical signs or symptoms. MRI monitoring for signs that may be consistent with PML may be useful, and any suspicious findings should spark further investigation to allow for an early diagnosis of PML, if present. Discontinue ocrelizumab treatment if PML is confirmed.

    General Information
    Immunosuppression, such as neutropenia, may occur with ocrelizumab therapy. During clinical trials, decreased neutrophil counts occurred in 13% of ocrelizumab-treated patients compared to 10% of placebo-treated patients. Most of the decreased neutrophil counts were between 1,000 to 1,500 cells/microliter. After treatment at 96 weeks, proportions of ocrelizumab-treated patients reporting IgG, IgA, and IgM below the LLN were 1.5%, 2.4%, and 16.5%, respectively. In placebo-controlled studies, proportions of ocrelizumab-treated patients reporting baseline IgG, IgA, and IgM below the LLN were 0%, 0.2%, and 0.2%, respectively. Consider ocrelizumab discontinuation in patients with serious opportunistic or recurrent serious infections, and if prolonged hypogammaglobulinemia requires treatment with intravenous immunoglobulins. When initiating ocrelizumab after an immunosuppressive therapy or initiating an immunosuppressive therapy after ocrelizumab treatment, consider the potential for increased immunosuppression. Ocrelizumab has not been studied in combination with other multiple sclerosis therapies.

    Headline

    General Information
    Clinically significant liver injury, without findings of viral hepatitis, has been reported in the postmarketing setting in people treated with anti-CD20 B-cell depleting therapies approved for the treatment of multiple sclerosis (MS), including ocrelizumab. Signs of liver injury, including markedly elevated hepatic enzymes with elevated total bilirubin (hyperbilirubinemia), have occurred from weeks to months after administration. Description
  Text
  Ocrelizumab is a CD20-directed cytolytic monoclonal antibody. The precise mechanism by which ocrelizumab exerts its therapeutic effects in multiple sclerosis (MS) is unknown, but it is presumed to involve binding to CD20 on pre-B and mature B lymphocytes, resulting in antibody-dependent cellular cytolysis and complement-mediated lysis. B cell activation contributes to CNS damage in individuals with MS. Ocrelizumab is indicated for the treatment of primary progressive MS and relapsing forms of MS, to include clinically isolated syndrome, relapsing-remitting disease, and active secondary progressive disease. The medication has been shown to reduce risk for relapse and to delay progression of disability. The efficacy of ocrelizumab for the treatment of primary progressive MS was demonstrated during a placebo-controlled clinical trial, where a 24% risk reduction in the proportion of individuals with 12-week confirmed disability progression was observed for patients treated with ocrelizumab (32.9% active drug vs. 39.3% placebo). The efficacy of ocrelizumab for the treatment of relapsing forms of MS was demonstrated in 2, randomized, double-blind, active comparator trials, in which ocrelizumab was compared to interferon beta-1a. Across both trials, ocrelizumab significantly reduced annualized relapse rates compared to interferon beta-1a (relative reductions of 46% to 47%) and the proportion of patients with 12-week confirmed disability progression (risk reduction of 40%; 9.8% ocrelizumab vs. 15.2% interferon beta-1a). In pediatric patients with relapsing-remitting MS, ocrelizumab demonstrated non-inferiority to fingolimod in reducing annualized relapse rate and superiority in reducing MRI lesion outcomes. Infusion reactions are common. Administer methylprednisolone, or an equivalent corticosteroid, and an antihistamine prior to each infusion to reduce the frequency and severity of infusion reactions; an antipyretic may also be considered. Ocrelizumab was FDA-approved in 2017.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The exact mechanism by which ocrelizumab exerts its therapeutic effect in multiple sclerosis is unknown. Ocrelizumab likely binds to CD20, a cell surface antigen present on pre-B and mature B lymphocytes. After binding, antibody-dependent cellular cytolysis and complement-mediated lysis occur.
 
Pharmacodynamics
Treatment with ocrelizumab reduces CD19+ B-cell counts in blood by 14 days after infusion in adult and pediatric patients. CD19 B-cell counts were used for evaluation because the presence of ocrelizumab interferes with the CD20 assay. During clinical studies, B-cell counts increased to above the lower limit of normal (LLN) or above baseline counts between infusions at least 1 time in 0.3% to 4.1% of adult patients. The median time for B-cell counts to return to LLN or baseline was 72 weeks (range 27 to 175 weeks) after the last ocrelizumab infusion in 1 adult clinical study (n = 51). B-cell counts increased to either baseline or LLN in 90% of adult patients within 2.5 years after the last infusion.

Rate of infusion modifications in response to infusion reactions based on reaction severity:
Life-threatening or disabling infusion reactions:
Immediately discontinue the infusion and provide appropriate supportive treatment. Permanently discontinue ocrelizumab.
Severe infusion reactions:
Immediately interrupt the infusion and provide appropriate supportive treatment. Wait until all symptoms have resolved before resuming infusion. The rate modifications do not change the dose administered but do increase the duration of infusion time.
