Drug Class: Melatonin (5-methoxy-N-acetyltryptamine) is a neurohormone used to regulate circadian rhythm disruption which occurs in jet lag and sleep disorders such as delayed sleep phase syndrome, irregular sleep-wake disorder, shift-work sleep disorder, and non-24-hour sleep wake disorder in adults.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: Some products are labeled for up to 12 mg/day PO or SL for insomnia.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): pyrosis (heartburn), anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema, hot flashes, hyperhidrosis, pruritus, rash, arthralgia, fluid retention, pharyngitis

In an Overdose: Rechallenge may confirm the diagnosis, but is not always recommended.Two cases of apparent melatonin overdose have been reported.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Consumers of melatonin should be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for melatonin and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products. Unidentifiable impurities have been found in many products.

        Adults
        Dosage Description
        Circadin delayed-release melatonin 2 mg PO once nightly at bedtime. Circadin is an approved drug in Europe for the treatment of insomnia in older adults ; the product has been designated an orphan drug by the FDA for Non-24. In a small pilot study, 13 totally blind subjects living in normal social environments were randomized to receive either Circadin delayed-release melatonin 2 mg PO once nightly at bedtime or placebo for 6 weeks. Active treatment followed 2-weeks of placebo run-in, and active treatment was followed by 2 weeks of placebo for discontinuation. The primary endpoint was demonstration of clinically meaningful effects on sleep duration (upper confidence interval [CI] limit more than 20 minutes). Outcome measures included daily voice recorded sleep diary, quality of life measures, and safety. The mean nightly sleep duration improved by 43 minutes in the melatonin delayed-release group and 16 minutes in the placebo group (mean difference: 27 minutes, 95% CI: -14.4 to 69 minutes; p = 0.18; effect size: 0.82) and met the primary endpoint. Mean sleep latency decreased by 29 minutes with melatonin over placebo (p = 0.13; effect size: 0.92) and nap duration decreased in the melatonin but not in the placebo group. The effects of melatonin persisted during the 2 week discontinuation period. Adverse events were mild or moderate and similar between melatonin and placebo. A larger study powered to demonstrate a significant effect is warranted.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Consumers of melatonin should be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for melatonin and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products. Unidentifiable impurities have been found in many products. Impurities may cause allergic reactions or side effects. Some melatonin products are derived from the pineal glands of beef cattle, and should be used with caution by those with bovine protein hypersensitivity. The use of animal source melatonin products is not recommended due to the risk of viral contamination or infection (e.g., concerns over the transmission of bovine spongiform encephalopathy or "mad-cow disease"). If melatonin is going to be used, a synthetic-source product should be purchased.The American Sleep Disorder Association considers melatonin an experimental drug for insomnia and does not recommend its use without medical supervision. Due to a lack of scientific data and an unknown potential for side effects, melatonin is not recommended for use in neonates, infants, or children without the approval and observation of a health care professional. Caution is also warranted in the elderly due to limited study of melatonin in this patient population at this time. If an adult patient decides to self-treat insomnia with melatonin, it is recommended that use not exceed 2 weeks without consultation of a health care professional.Melatonin may cause drowsiness. Driving or operating machinery, or performing other tasks that require mental alertness requires caution when taking melatonin. Patients should determine how melatonin affects them before participating in these activities. Excessive daytime sedation occurring after melatonin use may indicate excessive dosage.Melatonin should be used with caution in patients who have pre-existence of any neurological disease. Melatonin acts on the central nervous system and has sedative effects. It is unknown if melatonin could potentially worsen depressive conditions. Caution should be used when patients are being treated for other psychiatric diseases (e.g., depression, dementia) by a health care professional, particularly if they are on prescription medication for such problems. Melatonin is not recommended for people who are on prescribed neurologic, psychotropic, or hypnotic medications without the supervision of a qualified health care professional. Little is known about whether melatonin could interfere with prescribed therapies (see Drug Interactions). The decision to self-treat insomnia with melatonin should be weighed against the possibility that the problem could coexist with another medical condition (e.g., depression, neurologic disorders). Since melatonin may affect epileptic activity, patients with a seizure disorder should not use this product except on the advice of a health care professional.There is no experience with melatonin use in the presence of renal disease or hepatic disease. Melatonin should be used with caution in these populations. Patients who are undergoing treatment for neoplastic disease or endocrine disease, have immunosuppression, or who are taking corticosteroid therapy should not use melatonin without a health professional's supervision due to the potential role of melatonin in hormonal and immunomodulatory functions. Preliminary studies showing melatonin may have oncostatic effects have been conducted in animals or in vitro settings and may not apply to humans or to all tumor types.At this time, melatonin should be considered contraindicated in females who are trying to conceive. As a hormone, melatonin modulates steroid hormone actions, including those in reproductive and mammary tissues. Melatonin has been shown to inhibit ovulation in higher doses and may affect reproductive capacity, thus it may impact infertility. In men, there is some evidence that melatonin is important in the regulation of sperm counts and use of melatonin may affect the proper production of sperm.Melatonin should be considered to be contraindicated in pregnancy at this time. In pregnant women, endogenous melatonin crosses the placenta and enters the fetal circulation, and appears to be responsible for setting circadian rhythm influences in utero. Melatonin receptors in the fetus are widespread in both central and peripheral tissues from the third week of fetal development. The administration of exogenous melatonin could potentially disrupt circadian entrainment and other pineal gland influences. Melatonin is also not recommended for use in women who are breast-feeding their infants.

    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. While melatonin supplements and pharmaceuticals are now almost exclusively produced synthetically, there may be available melatonin supplements derived from the pineal glands of beef cattle. These should be avoided by people with bovine protein hypersensitivity.
    Headline
    General Information
    General Information
    Melatonin products have not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration for safety and efficacy and is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Melatonin should only be administered within 30 to 60 minutes of going to bed. People should avoid driving or operating machinery or performing other activities requiring coordination and concentration after use of melatonin. Advise the patient to limit their activity to that necessary for going to bed. Sedation occurring after melatonin use during waking hours may indicate excessive dosage. Complex sleep-related behaviors such as sleep-driving (i.e., driving while not fully awake after ingestion of a hypnotic) and other complex behaviors (e.g., preparing and eating food, making phone calls, or having sex), with amnesia for the event, have been reported in the use of melatonin analogs. Discontinuation of melatonin should be strongly considered in any patient reporting complex sleep behaviors. The use of alcohol and other CNS depressants may increase the risk of these behaviors and cause additive sedation. Advise patients to avoid alcoholic beverages while taking melatonin.
    Headline
    hepatic disease
    General Information
    Individuals with hepatic disease should consult their health care provider prior to the use of melatonin. Published data demonstrates markedly elevated endogenous melatonin levels during daytime hours due to decreased clearance in people with hepatic impairment.
    Headline
    depression, seizures, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Melatonin acts on the central nervous system and has sedative effects. Exacerbation or emergence of cognitive and behavioral abnormalities and worsening of depression (including suicidal ideation and completed suicides) have been seen with melatonin, melatonin analogs and other hypnotics in clinical use. The melatonin analogs contain precautions to this effect in their prescribing labels. As with these melatonin analogs, the emergence of any new changes in mood, cognition, or behavior in a patient taking melatonin requires further evaluation. Additionally, caution is advised in people with a history of seizures, a seizure disorder, or conditions that may lower the seizure threshold. Rare reports of seizures have occurred with melatonin use.
    Headline
    asthma
    General Information
    Melatonin should be used cautiously in patients with asthma, as melatonin may play a role in the expression of asthma symptoms.
    Headline
    autoimmune disease, diabetes mellitus
    General Information
    Melatonin is not recommended for use in people with autoimmune disease. Caution is advised in individuals with diabetes mellitus; melatonin may have effects on glucose levels and insulin, and patients with diabetes mellitus should monitor blood sugar while taking melatonin.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, infants, neonates
    General Information
    Caregivers are encouraged to seek the advice of a pediatric health care professional before starting melatonin in children or adolescents. Education regarding proper sleep hygiene and establishing developmentally appropriate and consistent bedtime schedules are first-line interventions. If melatonin is used, caregivers should select a product with the USP Verified Mark to allow for safer use. A pediatric health care provider should monitor the use of melatonin in any pediatric patient. Due to a lack of scientific data and an unknown potential for side effects, investigational protocols should govern appropriate melatonin use in neonates or infants. Several small, randomized controlled trials suggest the efficacy and relative safety of short-term supplemental melatonin in treating insomnia and sleep disturbances in pediatric patients with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) or other neurodevelopmental disorders; experts generally agree that melatonin may be useful in children and adolescents 2 years and older when behavioral strategies have failed. Melatonin and melatonin analogs have been associated with an effect on reproductive hormones in adults (e.g., decreased testosterone and increased prolactin concentrations). It is not known what effect melatonin would have on the reproductive and gonadal function of pre-pubescent or pubescent pediatric patients and further study is needed regarding the overall safety of chronic use in pediatric patients. Clinicians should counsel parents/caregivers regarding potential adverse effects of melatonin use and the lack of long-term safety data, including an unknown effect on pubertal development.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Due to a lack of clinical data, use of melatonin during pregnancy is not generally recommended. There are no available human clinical data on melatonin-exposed pregnancies. Animal studies do not indicate direct or indirect harmful effects with respect to pregnancy, embryo-fetal development, parturition, or postnatal development. Endogenous melatonin crosses the placenta and enters the fetal circulation, and appears to be responsible for setting circadian rhythm influences in utero. Melatonin receptors in the fetus are widespread in both central and peripheral tissues from the third week of fetal development. The administration of exogenous melatonin could potentially disrupt circadian entrainment and other pineal gland influences. In animal studies, ramelteon, a melatonin analog, produced evidence of developmental toxicity, including teratogenic effects, in rats at doses much greater than the recommended human dose.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use melatonin with caution during breast-feeding. Endogenous melatonin passes into human milk, with the usual daily circadian rhythm of melatonin creating undetectable levels during the day and high levels in the milk at night. Thus, it is likely that exogenous melatonin passes into human milk. However, doses higher than those expected in breast milk after maternal supplementation have been used safely in infants. It is not thought that short-term use during lactation would adversely affect a breastfed child, although some experts recommend against its use in breast-feeding due to the lack of data and a relatively long half-life in preterm neonates.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: Some melatonin preparations, including Melatonex(R), are a combination of several nutraceuticals. Only adverse reactions pertaining to melatonin are discussed in this monograph.Adverse effects of melatonin appear to be infrequent with a few days of use. Much less is known regarding the long term administration of this hormone. Most clinical trials have involved <= 6 months of daily melatonin administration. Adverse reactions reported in clinical trials include headache, depression, sinus tachycardia, and pruritus. Subjective reports of nightmares, dyspepsia, and abdominal pain have also occurred with the clinical use of melatonin. Prolonged sedation and drowsiness have been reported in some patients in clinical studies. Patients experiencing excessive daytime sedation from melatonin may need to consume a lower bedtime dosage. Daytime sleepiness may interfere with driving or operating machinery, or performing other tasks that require mental alertness. One study reported that subjective drowsiness from melatonin may affect attention and concentration while driving. Patients should determine how melatonin affects them before participating in activities requiring alertness.Unidentifiable impurities have been found in many melatonin products. Impurities may cause allergic reactions or side effects. One case report exists in the literature describing a temporal association of melatonin use for insomnia with the development of autoimmune hepatitis confirmed by liver biopsy. The authors have raised the question of melatonin's immunomodulatory effects on the development of the event, but is important to note that product impurities, as with many reported hepatitis cases with other nutraceuticals, could have contributed to this reaction. Discontinuation of the melatonin and treatment with corticosteroid therapy resulted in symptomatic and clinical improvements.Rash, in the form of fixed drug eruption, has been reported after melatonin administration. Fixed eruptions are a unique form of drug allergy, resulting in sharply demarcated plaques. They tend to recur at the same site each time the drug is ingested. The eruption may be accompanied by pruritus or burning, and repeated doses may ellicit pruritus alone in the affected area. The areas resolve with discontinuation of the medication. Rechallenge may confirm the diagnosis, but is not always recommended.Two cases of apparent melatonin overdose have been reported. One 66 year-old gentleman, who was on concommitant treatment with chlordiazepoxide, amitriptyline, lisinopril and diltiazem, had been administering melatonin in a dose of 6 mg every evening for sleep. The night before a surgical procedure, he ingested 24 mg of melatonin to aid relaxation. Within 20 minutes of taking the dose, he exhibited lethargy and disorientation, and called the poison control center. After 5 hours of sleep, the man exhibited short-term amnesia of the events the night before. He informed his surgeon of the event, and proceeded with the operation. No other untoward effects were noted in the 48 hours after the ingestion. The second case involved a 73 year-old woman who was admitted to the hospital with acute psychosis and confusion. Medications taken prior to admission included fluoxetine for depression, conjugated estrogens, beclomethasone nasal spray, multivitamins, and melatonin 3 mg taken once or twice daily. The patient reported that she had taken 30 mg of melatonin earlier in the day of admission. The next morning her abnormal behaviors had resolved; previous laboratory analysis and CT scans revealed no pathology. In both cases, the temporal association of melatonin ingestion to the clinical course of the patients supported melatonin as the cause of the problems. However, the time of day of melatonin administration, the age of the patients, and the presence of other medications as confounding factors cannot be excluded.As a hormone, melatonin modulates steroid hormone actions, including those in reproductive and mammary tissues. There is some evidence that melatonin is important in the regulation of sperm counts, and that increased melatonin results in spermatogenesis inhibition. Melatonin has been shown to inhibit ovulation in combination with norethindrone at doses of 75 mg/day and may cause reversible infertility. It is unknown if melatonin would have any chronic toxicity on steroid-responsive tissues with long term use. Male and female patients who are trying to conceive should avoid melatonin.

    Most central nervous system (CNS) adverse effects of melatonin appear to be infrequent and mild in most patients with a few days of use. The most commonly reported adverse reactions are headache and somnolence. Reported side effects of melatonin in pediatric patients have included morning drowsiness, headache, dizziness, and hypothermia. Prolonged sedation and drowsiness during waking hours have been noted; patients experiencing excessive drowsiness during waking hours following melatonin use at bedtime may need to consume a lower bedtime dosage. One study reported that subjective drowsiness from melatonin may affect attention and concentration while driving; patients should determine how melatonin affects them before participating in activities requiring alertness. Other CNS and psychiatric adverse reactions include dizziness, abnormal dreams, unspecified sleep disturbances, nightmares, and seizures in the published literature. In primarily depressed patients, worsening of depression (including suicidal ideation) have been reported. Hallucinations, as well as behavioral changes such as bizarre behavior, anxiety, agitation, and mania have been reported with the use of melatonin analogs. Neuro-psychiatric symptoms may occur unpredictably. Complex sleep-related behaviors such as sleep-driving (i.e., driving while not fully awake after ingestion of a hypnotic) and other complex behaviors (e.g., preparing and eating food, making phone calls, or having sex), with amnesia for the event, have been reported in association with hypnotic use, including melatonin analogs. The use of alcohol and other hypnotics should be avoided when possible since these may increase the risk of such symptoms. Somnambulism (sleep walking) has been reported when melatonin was used in conjunction with zolpidem. As with other melatonin analogs, the emergence of any new changes in mood, cognition, or behavior requires further evaluation of the patient. Discontinuation of melatonin should be considered for patients who report any complex sleep behavior, worsening depression, or any other unusual changes in moods or behaviors. During excessive melatonin dosage (e.g., 24 to 30 mg of ingestion), impaired cognition, lethargy, disorientation, short-term amnesia, acute psychosis and confusion have been reported.

Description
  Text
  Melatonin (5-methoxy-N-acetyltryptamine) is a neurohormone used to regulate circadian rhythm disruption which occurs in jet lag and sleep disorders such as delayed sleep phase syndrome, irregular sleep-wake disorder, shift-work sleep disorder, and non-24-hour sleep wake disorder in adults. Melatonin appears to provide some benefit and few risks for the short-term treatment of sleep irregularities in pediatric patients with neurodevelopmental disorders. There may be a role for the use of melatonin in children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) who have not responded to behavioral strategies to improve sleep. However, it is recommended that melatonin use in children and adolescents be discussed with and monitored by a pediatric health care professional. Melatonin is considered a dietary supplement in the U.S. and the content in supplements can vary widely. To allow for safer use, a product with the USP Verified Mark should be selected. Melatonin is available by prescription in Europe under the brand name Circadin as a short-term monotherapy treatment for primary insomnia characterized by poor quality of sleep in patients 55 years of age or more. According to the British Association for Psychopharmacology guidelines, extended-release melatonin is the first-choice treatment when a hypnotic is indicated in patients over 55 years of age. Although the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) and the Veterans Affairs/Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guidelines recommend against melatonin as a treatment for primary insomnia in adults due to a lack of reliable efficacy data, research is ongoing. The effects of melatonin as a hormone were first noted in 1917; melatonin was subsequently isolated from the pineal gland of animals and used to produce commercial melatonin products. Currently, available melatonin products are primarily synthesized because of the potential for contamination from animal-based infectious prions and viruses, which may cause serious illness. Products classified as dietary supplements should be used cautiously because rigid quality control standards are not required, and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and purity of marketed products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Melatonin is an endogenous hormone secreted by the pineal gland. The suprachiasmatic nuclei of the hypothalamus controls the numerous physiologic and endocrine circadian rhythms of the body, including that of rest and activity. The circadian clock is set via a process called entrainment, which is a response of the suprachiasmatic nuclei to photic input. Synthesis and secretion of endogenous melatonin is controlled by enzymes secreted by the hypothalamus which are activated by darkness and depressed by environmental light. Exactly how melatonin induces sleep is not clear, but it is probably not through a direct hypnotic effect. In patients with jet lag or circadian rhythm disorders, endogenous melatonin secretion does not correspond to the social or solar sleep-wake cycles imposed by their surroundings, and they experience sleep disruption. Administration of exogenous melatonin appears to re-set the body to the environmental clock and allow patients to normalize physiologic and behavioral sleep patterns. Exogenous melatonin maximally advances delayed rhythms when administered before endogenous melatonin levels begin to increase in the evening hours. In addition to circadian phase-shifting effects, melatonin has been shown to decrease nocturnal core body temperature, which helps to facilitate sleep. To date, pharmacological tolerance to melatonin has not been described.
 
Melatonin is involved in other physiologic processes besides the sleep-wake cycle. Secretion of melatonin from the pineal gland is highest during the pediatric years and tends to decrease with age. This age-related secretion performs important endocrine functions. It is thought that higher pre-pubertal melatonin levels are responsible for keeping the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis in quiescence, and that decreasing melatonin levels with age play a role in the onset of adolescence and sexual maturation. Melatonin receptors have been found in all male and female sexually responsive tissues, indicating that melatonin has a significant role in normal reproductive capacity. Exogenous melatonin can suppress the release of gonadotropin releasing hormone and lutenizing hormone, leading to anovulation and changes in steroid responsive tissues, especially in higher doses. Contraceptive activity has been noted when women are given melatonin in combination with norethindrone.
 
Melatonin also exhibits immunostimulatory and antioxidant actions. In neurodegenerative disease models, melatonin appears to neutralize oxidizing free radicals, specifically by preventing the reduction of antioxidant enzyme activity, and reducing beta-amyloid mediated lipid peroxidation of cell membranes. These actions appear to decrease apoptosis of neuronal cells. Further research is needed to determine if melatonin may preserve function in neurologic diseases where free radicals have been implicated as partially causative of the conditions. In epilepsy, the rise and fall of endogenous melatonin levels may influence seizure activity. Melatonin may play a role in certain cancers, and in some cases, may have antiproliferative effects on some tumors. The actions and role of melatonin in other body processes, such as regulation of the gastrointestinal system, continues to be investigated. Melatonin may also stimulate the activity of natural killer (NK) cells, lymphocytes, and various cytokines. Further study in well-controlled trials should answer further questions regarding melatonin's neurologic, immunologic, and oncostatic activities.

    Description
    After oral administration, melatonin undergoes significant first-pass hepatic metabolism to 6-sulfaoxymelatonin, producing a melatonin bioavailability averages 15% (range: 9—33%); the time to maximum concentrations (Tmax) averages 50 minutes (range: 15 minutes to 210 minutes).   Text
  Melatonin has been administered orally and intravenously. Commercially available dietary supplement formulations of melatonin include oral and sublingual tablets, orally dissolving tablets, soft chews, capsules, teas, lozenges, and oral spray delivery systems. There have been reports of substantial variability in product purity and melatonin content of available products.
 
Melatonin administration follows a different pharmacokinetic profile than that of the endogenous hormone. Melatonin crosses the blood-brain barrier, and also traverses the placenta in pregnancy. Some accumulation of melatonin in fat tissue may occur with prolonged daily administration. The primary metabolic pathway occurs via the liver via oxidative metabolism via CYP1A (isoenzymes CYP1A2 and CYP1A1), with minor roles by CYP2C19 and possibly CYP2C9. The principal metabolite is 6-sulphatoxy-melatonin (6-S-MT), which is inactive. Elimination of melatonin is by renal excretion of metabolites, 89% as sulphated and glucoronide conjugates of 6-hydroxymelatonin and 2% is excreted as unchanged, active melatonin. The mean elimination half-life (T1/2) after oral administration of immediate-release melatonin is roughly 45 minutes; with intravenous administration, the half-life is approximately 28 minutes. The terminal half-life is 3.5 to 4 hours and the excretion of the primary metabolite is completed within 12 hours following a single oral dose of an extended-release product.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP1A2, CYP1A1
Melatonin is primarily and predominantly metabolized by CYP1A2, with some metabolism by CYP1A1, CYP1B1, and minor contributions by CYP2C9 and CYP2C19. Melatonin may exhibit significant interactions with potent CYP1A2 inhibitors, such as fluvoxamine. Melatonin has been observed to induce CYP3A in vitro at supra-therapeutic concentrations only; the clinical relevance of the finding is unknown.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Patients with hepatic impairment are recommended to consult their health care provider prior to melatonin use. Melatonin is primarily metabolized by oxidative hepatic metabolism. Published data demonstrates markedly elevated endogenous melatonin levels during daytime hours due to decreased clearance in patients with hepatic impairment. Therefore, exogenous use of melatonin is not recommended in patients with hepatic impairment.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available at this time; use with caution due to lack of sufficient pharmacokinetic and clinical data. In one study, the use of mutliple days of bedtime doses (2 mg) did not result in accumulation in patients with renal impairment.
