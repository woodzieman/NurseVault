Drug Class: Rh0 [D] immune globulin is a parenteral immune globulin used to prevent isoimmunization in Rh0 [D]-negative women exposed to Rh0 [D]-positive blood and thus, prevent the risk of erythroblastosis fetalis in current or subsequent pregnancies.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Rh0 [D] immune globulin is not indicated for use as immunoglobulin replacement therapy for patients with immune globulin deficiency syndromes such as agammaglobulinemia or hypogammaglobulinemia. Also, intravenous use of WinRho SDF is only for patients with ITP who are Rh0 [D]-positive and have a spleen (see Mechanism of Action). The efficacy of the product in patients with ITP who have asplenia or who are Rh0 [D]-negative has not been established. Also, Rh0 [D]-negative patients who have anti-Rh0 [D] antibodies should not get Rh0 [D] immune globulin (see Mechanism of Action). The presence of anti-Rh0 [D] antibodies is detected by an indirect antiglobulin (Coombs') test.
 
Each vial of WinRho SDF liquid formulation contains 10% maltose; use caution in patients with maltose hypersensitivity. Patients with IgA deficiency often develop antibodies against IgA and are more likely to have anaphylactic or immune-mediated adverse reactions to pooled immunoglobulin products such as Rh0 [D] immune globulin. WinRho SDF contains approximately 5 mcg/ml of IgA, Rhophylac has less than 5 mcg/ml of IgA, and RhoGAM and MICRhoGAM typically contain less than 15 mcg per dose. Patients with albumin hypersensitivity may not be appropriate candidates for Rhophylac receipt, as the product contains 10 mg/ml of human albumin. The benefits of Rh0 [D] immune globulin must be carefully weighed against the potential risk of severe hypersensitivity reactions. The health care professional should have immediate availability of epinephrine (1:1000) injection and other agents used in the treatment of severe anaphylaxis in the event of a serious allergic reaction to Rh0 [D] immune globulin. Immediately stop the administration of Rh0 [D] immune globulin if allergic or anaphylactic-type symptoms occur.
 
BayRho-D (HyperRHO S/D) Full dose or Mini dose, RhoGAM, and MICRhoGAM are for intramuscular administration only. Any condition such as thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, or other bleeding disorder that contraindicates the use of intramuscular (IM) injections contraindicates the use of Rh0 [D] immune globulin that is given intramuscularly. Patients with thrombocytopenia, vitamin K deficiency, a coagulopathy (e.g., hemophilia) or receiving anticoagulant therapy should be monitored closely when given Rh0 [D] immune globulin by IM injection because bleeding can occur at the IM injection site. WinRho SDF and Rhophylac are the only Rh0 [D] immune globulin products that may be used via intravenous administration. WinRho SDF must be given intravenously for the treatment of idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) in patients with a spleen who are Rh0[D]-positive. The efficacy and safety of WinRho SDF for ITP has only been established when given by the intravenous route.
 
Increased destruction of Rh0 [D]-positive red cells occurs after Rh0 [D] immune globulin administration, and this will produce decreased serum hemoglobin levels and associated clinical symptoms. Patients with ITP who have a hemoglobin level < 10 g/dl should receive a reduced dose of WinRho SDF in order to decrease the risk of worsening the severity of the anemia (see Dosage). Rh0 [D] immune globulin should be used with great caution in patients with ITP and a hemoglobin level < 8 g/dl. The safety of Rhophylac in the treatment of ITP has not been established in patients with pre-existing anemia; weigh the benefits of Rhophylac versus the potential risk of increasing the anemia severity. While most of the red cell destruction will occur in the spleen, there have been rare reports of acute hemoglobinuria consistent with intravascular hemolysis that have occurred during Rh0 [D] immune globulin administration to patients with (see Adverse Reactions). Rh0[D]-positive patients with ITP should be monitored for signs and/or symptoms of intravascular hemolysis (IVH), clinically compromising hemolytic anemia, and renal impairment during treatment. Infusion of intravenous immune globulin at a minimal practical concentration and infusion rate is recommended for patients with renal insufficiency or for patients who are predisposed to acute renal failure. Receipt of intravenous immune globulin has been reported to produce renal dysfunction in these patients. Most reports of renal dysfunction have involved products that contain sucrose as a stabilizer. Although WinRho SDF does not contains sucrose, assessment of renal function is recommended before and at appropriate intervals after WinRho SDF administration. If renal dysfunction occurs, use clinical judgment to either decrease the infusion rate or stop the infusion. Rhophylac does not contain sucrose.
 
Rh0 [D] immune globulin is classified as pregnancy category C. Although this agent has not been well studied in pregnant women, some of the products are routinely given during pregnancy. The HyperRHO S/D Mini-Dose product is not indicated for use during pregnancy (see Dosage). Under ideal circumstances, when used to prevent Rh0 [D] isoimmunization from exposure to Rh0[D]-positive blood, Rh0 [D] immune globulin should only be administered to a non-sensitized Rh0 [D]-negative obstetric patient. If there is question about the mother's Rh type or if there is insufficient time to determine fetal Rh typing (as might occur in trauma during pregnancy), then Rh0 [D] immune globulin should be administered as indicated by the clinical situation. Rh0 [D] immune globulin would not be effective in preventing erythroblastosis fetalis in the Rh0 [D]-negative mother who has already formed anti-D IgG antibodies. However, Rh0 [D] negative women who have already been sensitized to the Rh0 [D] erythrocyte factor do not appear to have an increase in adverse reactions if administered the product as a result of unknown Rh status. There is no known obstetric indication for the use of Rh0 [D] immune globulin in women who are Rh0 [D]-positive, as these women are not at risk of isoimmunization from an Rh0 [D]-positive fetus.
 
The Rh0 [D] immune globulin is never to be administered to neonates, as the anti-D activity could precipitate a hemolytic episode if the infant is Rh0 [D]-positive. The safety and efficacy of HyperRHO S/D Mini Dose or Full Dose in children or adolescents less than 16 years of age have not been established.
 
Rh0 [D] immune globulin is not secreted in breast milk, and no hazards are expected during breast-feeding.
 
Only glucose-specific testing methods such as glucose dehydrogenase nicotine-adenine dinucleotide, glucose oxidase- or glucose hexokinase-based tests are appropriate to determine serum glucose concentrations in patients such as those with diabetes mellitus who have received the liquid formulation of WinRho SDF. The liquid formulation of the immune globulin contains maltose, which can cause falsely elevated glucose readings on some testing systems such as glucose dehydrogenase pyrroloquinolinequinone (GDH-PQQ)-glucose monitoring systems. The interaction with glucose testing does not occur with the lyophilized powder formulation of WinRho SDF. Tell patients who get the liquid formulation to only use glucose-specific testing methods. Carefully review the product information of the blood glucose testing system including the information about the test strips. Contact the manufacturer of the testing system if any uncertainty exists about the appropriateness of the system in regard to maltose-containing parenteral products.

    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. WinRho SDF contains maltose, which is derived from corn; therefore, avoid use in individuals with a known corn allergy due to the potential risk of hypersensitivity.
    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    Rh(D) immune globulin is not indicated for use as immunoglobulin replacement therapy for patients with immune globulin deficiency syndromes.
Rh(D)-negative patients who have anti-D antibodies should not get Rh(D) immune globulin. The presence of anti-D antibodies can be identified using an indirect antiglobulin (Coombs') test. Additionally, some products are not approved for use in Rh(D)-positive patients and may be contraindicated in this population according to the manufacturer labeling.
Use intramuscular Rh(D) immune globulin with caution in patients with a bleeding disorder or severe thrombocytopenia due to the risk of bleeding complications. In such cases, use only if the expected benefits outweigh the potential risks.
    Headline
    anemia, hemolysis
    General Information
    Intravascular hemolysis (IVH), including fatal cases, has been reported in Rh(D)-positive patients treated with Rh(D) immune globulin products (e.g., WinRho SDF and Rhophylac) for immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP). IVH may result in severe anemia and multi-organ failure, including acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), acute renal insufficiency or failure, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Some Rh(D) immune globulin products are specifically contraindicated in patients with pre-existing hemolysis, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, or those at high risk for hemolysis. Use caution in patients with pre-existing anemia, as treatment may worsen the severity of anemia. Dose reduction may be necessary in patients with hemoglobin less than 10 g/dL, depending on the specific product. Additionally, some products recommend alternative therapies for patients with hemoglobin less than 8 g/dL due to the increased risk of severe anemia. Monitor patients for signs and symptoms of hemolysis in a health care setting for at least 8 hours after administration. Perform a dipstick urinalysis at baseline, 2 and 4 hours after administration, and prior to the end of the monitoring period. Alert patients to signs of IVH, including back pain, shaking chills, fever, and discolored urine or hematuria. Absence of symptoms within 8 hours does not exclude the possibility of delayed IVH. If signs or symptoms of IVH are present or suspected, perform post-treatment laboratory tests, including plasma hemoglobin, haptoglobin, LDH, and direct and indirect plasma bilirubin. If patients with ITP require transfusion after receiving Rh(D) immune globulin, use Rh(D)-negative packed red blood cells (PRBCs) to avoid exacerbating ongoing hemolysis.
    Headline
    IgA deficiency with antibodies against IgA
    General Information
    Rh(D) immune globulin products indicated for the treatment of immune thrombocytopenic purpura (e.g., WinRho SDF, Rhophylac) are contraindicated in individuals with IgA deficiency with antibodies against IgA and a history of hypersensitivity.     General Information
    Administer Rh(D) immune globulin at the lowest practical infusion rate in patients at increased risk of renal injury, such as those with renal impairment or renal failure, diabetes mellitus, age older than 65 years, hypovolemia, sepsis, paraproteinemia, or those receiving nephrotoxic medications, and ensure adequate hydration prior to administration.     geriatric
    General Information
    In geriatric adults, initiate Rh(D) immune globulin for the treatment of immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) at the lower end of the dosing range.     Thrombosis may occur after treatment with immune globulin products. Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Adverse reactions from Rh0 [D] immune globulin administration by either the IV or IM route are infrequent and usually inconsequential. Adverse reactions with intravenous Rh0 [D] immune globulin administration are similar to those seen with other intravenous blood products or immune globulins. The most common adverse reactions are headache, chills, fever, asthenia, pallor, diarrhea, nausea/vomiting, arthralgia, myalgia, dizziness, hyperkinesis, abdominal pain, back pain, hypotension, hypertension, increased LDH, drowsiness, vasodilation, pruritus, and rash (unspecified). Low-grade fever (1%), headache (2%), and chills (2%) are the most common reactions during intravenous administration. An injection site reaction occurs in a small proportion of patients after intramuscular administration and is characterized by mild erythema, swelling, pain, and induration. In some cases, low-grade fever may also be present after IM administration. Receipt of antihistamines or corticosteroids may be helpful. Also, dividing large doses into several doses for administration at several sites may help reduce local adverse effects (see Administration). Repeated doses of Rh0 [D] immune globulin have rarely caused mild hyperbilirubinemia or splenomegaly and may have been due to the increased rate of destruction of transfused red blood cells in certain clinical situations.
 
Anaphylactic shock is rare but has occurred after Rh0 [D] immune globulin administration. If hypotension, anaphylaxis, or a severe allergic reaction occurs, the administration of Rh0 [D] immune globulin should be discontinued. Epinephrine and supportive care should be administered as necessary. Tell patients to immediately report any early signs of a hypersensitivity reaction such as urticaria, chest tightness or chest pain (unspecified), wheezing, facial or throat swelling, and orthostatic hypotension.
 
A hemolytic reaction may occur in patients who got Rh positive red blood cells and Rh0 [D] immune globulin. Associated signs and symptoms may include fever, back pain, nausea/vomiting, hypotension, hypertension, hemoglobinuria, hyperbilirubinemia, increased serum creatinine concentrations, and decreased haptoglobin. In addition to the incompatible blood transfusion receipt, Rh0 [D] immune globulin (i.e., WinRho® SDF) is given intravenously to Rh0 [D]-positive patients with idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP); Rhophylac® is also indicated for the treatment of Rh0 [D]-positive patients with chronic ITP. Increased destruction of Rh0 [D]-positive red cells with attendant decreased serum hemoglobin concentrations and associated clinical symptoms occurs after Rh0 [D] immune globulin administration. As expected, mild extravascular hemolysis, manifested as an increase in bilirubin, a decrease in hemoglobin, or a decrease in haptoglobin, was observed after Rhophylac® administration for ITP. An increase in blood bilirubin was seen in 21% of 98 patients, and the median decrease in hemoglobin of 0.8 g/dl was greatest at day 6 and day 8. The mean maximum decrease in hemoglobin was 1.7 g/dl (range, +0.4 to -6.1 g/dl) among patients who got the recommended initial intravenous dose of WinRho® SDF. Five of 137 patients had a hemoglobin decrease of greater than 4 g/dl. To reduce the risk of increasing the anemia severity, a reduced WinRho® SDF dose is recommended for patients with a hemoglobin concentration of less than 10 g/dl (see Dosage). Extreme caution is recommended if the product is used in patients with a hemoglobin concentration of less than 8 g/dl. The safety of Rhophylac® in the treatment of ITP has not been established in patients with pre-existing anemia; weigh the benefits of Rhophylac® versus the potential risk of increasing the anemia severity. While most of the red cell destruction will occur in the spleen, there have been rare reports of acute hemoglobinuria consistent with intravascular hemolysis that have occurred during Rh0 [D] immune globulin administration. Hemolysis and hematuria may be accompanied by reversible acute renal impairment. Acute renal insufficiency was noted within 2—48 hours of the onset of intravascular hemolysis. Between April 1996 and April 1999, 15 cases of intravascular hemolysis following treatment of Rh0 [D]-positive ITP patients with WinRho® SDF were reported; 4 of the 15 died, and 2 patients underwent dialysis due to hemolytic-uremic syndrome (HUS). An additional 26 cases of possible IVH in ITP patients treated with WinRho® SDF were reported to the FDA between May and October 1999 and are being further evaluated. Signs and symptoms most commonly occurred within 4 hours of administration and included back pain, shaking chills, fever, and discolored urine. Intravascular hemolysis occurred in 4 of 98 patients who got Rhophylac®. Two patients had hypotension, nausea, chills, headache, and a decrease in haptoglobin and hemoglobin; one patient had headache, dizziness, nausea, pallor, shivering, and weakness requiring hospitalization; and one patient had an increase in blood pressure and severe headache. All patients recovered completely. Potentially serious complications of the condition such as clinically compromising anemia, acute renal insufficiency, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) have occurred. The etiology of these events appears to be complex and the effects of age, sex, pretreatment renal function, pretreatment hemoglobin levels, other concomitantly administered blood/blood products, or significant comorbid conditions at DIC onset remain unknown. Furthermore, previous uneventful administration of WinRho® SDF does not preclude the possibility of an occurrence of intravascular hemolysis and its complications upon subsequent administration of the product. An estimated incidence rate of 0.7 % for intravascular hemolysis following administration of Rh0 [D] immune globulin for treatment of ITP was obtained from unpublished data from clinical trials. According to the FDA, this rate may underestimate the frequency of intravascular hemolysis because not all patients were monitored for hemolysis, which was observed only coincidentally. Monitor patients with ITP who receive WinRho® SDF or Rhophylac® for signs and symptoms of intravascular hemolysis and its complications including hemoglobinuria, pallor, hypotension, sinus tachycardia, oliguria, anuria, edema, dyspnea, ecchymosis, and prolonged bleeding time. Discuss the risks and benefits of WinRho® SDF or Rhophylac® with each patient, and educate patients about the signs and symptoms of intravascular hemolysis such as back pain, shaking chills, fever, discolored urine, decreased urine output, sudden weight gain, fluid retention/edema, and shortness of breath. Tell patients to immediately report any symptoms to their physicians. Perform confirmatory laboratory testing in a patient who has signs or symptoms of intravascular hemolysis. Testing may include a complete blood count, haptoglobin, plasma hemoglobin, urine dipstick, renal and liver function assessment, and DIC specific tests such as D-dimer, fibrin degradation products, or fibrin split products. If patients who develop hemolysis with clinically compromising anemia after receiving Rh0 [D] immune globulin are to be transfused, use Rh0 [D]-negative packed red blood cells to avoid exacerbating ongoing hemolysis. Healthcare professionals should report serious adverse events possibly associated with the use of WinRho® SDF by calling 1—800—423—2090. Alternatively, this information may be reported to FDA's MedWatch reporting system at 1—800—FDA—1088.
 
Rh0 [D] immune globulin is derived from human plasma donors and carries the possibility of causing iatrogenic infection via bloodborne pathogens. The risk is considered to be low due to the careful screening of plasma donors. In some Rh0 [D] immune globulin preparations, the use of a solvent detergent viral inactivation process is effective at reducing the transmission of active lipid enveloped viruses such as HIV-1, HIV-2, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C. Cold-alcohol filtration is used in the processing of other Rh0 [D] immune globulin products; this process reduces the levels of both lipid-enveloped and non-lipid enveloped viruses in those solutions. Special virus filters available for use with some Rh0 [D] immune globulin products may further reduce the risk of transmission of non-lipid enveloped viruses such as hepatitis A. New bloodborne pathogens not controlled by present measures, however, can theoretically emerge at any time. Any infections thought by the prescriber or health care professional of being possibly transmitted by an immune globulin product should be reported to the product's manufacturer and the FDA MedWatch program.

    General Information
    Administration of Rh(D) immune globulin products may result in laboratory test interference. These products can affect blood typing, antibody screening, and the direct antiglobulin (Coombs') test in both the mother and newborn. In addition, Rh(D) immune globulin products may contain antibodies to other Rh antigens (e.g., A, B, C and E) or other blood group antibodies, which may be detectable by sensitive serologic methods after administration. Some formulations (e.g., WinRho SDF) contains maltose, which can cause falsely elevated blood glucose readings when using testing systems based on glucose dehydrogenase pyrroloquinolinequinone (GDH-PQQ) or glucose-dye-oxidoreductase methods. Use only glucose-specific testing systems to monitor blood glucose in individuals receiving WinRho SDF. Carefully review the product information of the blood glucose testing system, including the information about the test strips. Contact the manufacturer of the testing system if any uncertainty exists regarding suitability.

Description
  Text
  Rh0 [D] immune globulin is a parenteral immune globulin used to prevent isoimmunization in Rh0 [D]-negative women exposed to Rh0 [D]-positive blood and thus, prevent the risk of erythroblastosis fetalis in current or subsequent pregnancies. Rh0 [D] immune globulin is a solution containing IgG antibodies against erythrocyte antigen Rh0 [D]. The antibodies are collected from the plasma of donors; trace amounts of IgA are also present in the pooled solutions. Depending on the formulation, Rh0 immune globulin can be administered intramuscularly or intravenously. Rh0 [D] immune globulin products undergo alcohol filtration and/or solvent-detergent treatment to reduce the risk of transmission of some pathogenic viruses. Rh0 [D] immune globulin is indicated following Rh-D-incompatible delivery, abortion, miscarriage, or following circumstances that might result in maternal exposure to fetal blood of unknown type such as amniocentesis, ectopic pregnancy, and abdominal trauma during pregnancy. Rh0 [D] immune globulin may also be administered to prevent isoimmunization resulting from Rh0 [D]-incompatible erythrocyte or whole blood transfusions in Rh0 [D]-negative patients. One Rh0 [D] immune globulin product, WinRho SDF, given intravenously, is also indicated for acute and chronic immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) in certain patients; patients and clinicians need to be alert for the potential development of intravascular hemolysis. Also, administration of the liquid WinRho SDF product may cause falsely elevated serum glucose concentrations. Determination of serum glucose concentrations must be done with a glucose-specific testing system. Rh0 [D] immune globulin received FDA licensing approval in 1968; WinRho SDF was approved for intravenous or intramuscular use in March 1995.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Although the mechanism is not well understood, it is believed that the anti-Rh0[D] antibodies contained in Rh0 [D] immune globulin interact directly with the Rh0[D] antigens, thereby preventing the interaction between the antigens and the maternal immune system. Sensitization to Rh0[D]-positive blood is less likely to occur. If Rh immunization has occurred, Rh0 [D] immune globulin is ineffective. Clinical studies indicate that administration of 300 mcg (1,500 International Units) Rh0 [D] immune globulin within 72 hours after delivery of an Rh0[D]-positive infant by an Rh0[D]-negative mother decreases the incidence of active anti-Rh0 [D] production from 12 to 13% to 1% to 2%. When treatment is administered both antenatally at 28 weeks of gestation and postpartum, the Rh isoimmunization rate drops to around 0.1%. The appropriate use of Rh0 [D] immune globulin has reduced the prevalence of Rh isoimmunization in the U.S. and Canada by 96% since the 1940s.
 
The actions of Rh0 [D] immune globulin in immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) are not well understood. Intravenous infusion of Rh0 [D] immune globulin into an Rh0 [D]-positive patient leads to antibody coating of circulating erythrocytes. These coated red cells are cleared primarily by the spleen. The immune-mediated clearance of these sensitized erythrocytes occupies the reticuloendothelial system (RES) and allows for the survival of antibody-coated platelets. The primary mechanism of action of Rh0 [D] immune globulin appears to occur via immunologic blockade of Fc-receptors within the RES. Other immunomodulatory mechanisms may also play a role in Rh0 [D] immune globulin efficacy in ITP. After administration, Rh0 [D] immune globulin produces a 2 to 3 day delay in increasing the platelet count. The mean duration of response is about 30 days. Children with ITP tend to respond better than adults to Rh0 [D] immune globulin therapy. Repeated Rh0 [D] immune globulin infusions do not cure the disease but are used to maintain platelet counts at levels sufficient enough to provide adequate hemostasis (more than 30,000/microL). Rh0 [D] immune globulin is not effective in splenectomized or Rh0 [D]-negative patients with ITP.

  Rho [D] is administered via intravenous or intramuscular injection. The pharmacokinetics of Rh0 [D] immune globulin are not well described, but passively acquired anti-Rh0 [D] antibodies are not detectable 6 months after administration. If a Rh negative mother is exposed to Rh positive cells early in pregnancy, repeat doses of Rh0 [D] immune globulin may be needed (see Dosage). In order to maintain protection throughout pregnancy, the concentration of passively acquired anti-Rh0 [D] must not fall below the concentration necessary to prevent an immune response to Rh positive red cells.
