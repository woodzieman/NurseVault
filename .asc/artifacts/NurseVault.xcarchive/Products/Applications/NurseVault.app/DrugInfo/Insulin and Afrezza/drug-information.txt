Drug Class: Insulin, inhaled is a dry powder formulation of rapid-acting recombinant human insulin indicated to improve glycemic control in individuals with diabetes mellitus.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC), inhalation; Common adult doses: Specific maximum dosage information is not available. Individualize dosage based on careful monitoring of blood glucose and other clinical parameters.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  NOTE: In October 2007, Pfizer announced that Exubera® will be discontinued secondary to its limited use; a transition period where Exubera® will continue to be available is occurring so that patients can be switched to other therapies. Technosphere, another inhaled insulin that is in phase III trials, will be made available to Exubera® patients who continue to need inhaled insulin for medical reasons.Inhaled insulin differs from SC regular insulin by a more rapid onset; the onset of the Exubera® brand of inhaled insulin is 10—20 minutes. Duration of activity of inhaled insulin and SC regular insulin is similar (i.e., approximately 6 hours). Inhaled insulin is to be administered within 10 minutes before a meal. When inhaled insulin is administered to patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus, a longer-acting insulin should be part of the therapeutic regimen to maintain adequate glucose control. In patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, if insulin is used as monotherapy, both inhaled insulin and a longer-acting insulin should be part of the therapeutic regimen. Regular self-monitoring of blood glucose is recommended in all patients with diabetes mellitus, especially those on insulin therapy.Changes in insulin products should be made by experienced medical personnel. Changes in insulin species source (i.e., animal versus human, etc.), purity, brand, or route of administration can necessitate dosage adjustments. Treatment must be individualized. Diabetic patients must follow a regular, prescribed diet and exercise schedule to avoid either hypo- or hyperglycemia. The timing of meals and exercise with insulin administration is extremely important, and should remain consistent, unless prescribed otherwise. Fever, thyroid disease, infection, recent trauma or surgery, diarrhea secondary to malabsorption, vomiting, and certain medications can also affect insulin requirements, requiring dosage adjustments. Diabetic patients should be given a 'sick-day' plan to take appropriate action with blood glucose monitoring and insulin therapy when acute illness is present.Six new cases of lung cancer have been reported in patients taking the Exubera® brand of inhaled insulin during clinical trials (0.13 cases/100 patient-years); only 1 patient in the control group was diagnosed with lung cancer (0.02 cases/100 patient-years). Additionally, a case of lung cancer has been reported in 1 patient receiving Exubera® during post-marketing surveillance. Because of the small numbers, a cause and effect relationship cannot be established. It should be noted that all patients diagnosed with lung cancer had a prior history of smoking cigarettes.The Exubera® brand of inhaled insulin is contraindicated in patients with unstable or poorly controlled pulmonary disease because of wide variations in lung function that could affect the absorption of Exubera® and increase the risk of hypo- or hyperglycemia. In addition, the use of Exubera® in patients with any underlying pulmonary disease, including chronic obstructive lung disease (COPD), asthma, and cystic fibrosis, is not recommended; the safety and efficacy in these populations have not been established. Limited data on the use of Exubera® in patients with pulmonary disease are available; in patients with mild asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), inhaled insulin was found to be efficacious and well tolerated, although based on pharmacokinetic data, patients with asthma may require higher doses of inhaled insulin and patients with COPD may require lower doses of inhaled insulin. Furthermore, in patients with a concurrent respiratory infection (bronchitis, upper respiratory tract infection, rhinitis), drug discontinuation or interruption was rare (3—4%); worsening of glycemic control or adverse effects were not more common. Exubera® can be used in patients with an intercurrent respiratory illness; however, close monitoring of glycemic control is warranted.Pulmonary function tests have been performed during clinical investigations of Exubera® brand inhaled insulin; the results indicate that treatment with inhaled insulin is associated with small decreases in FEV1 and carbon monoxide diffusion capacity (DLco); however, these declines were small, non-progressive beyond 2 weeks of therapy, and reversible with drug discontinuation. Furthermore, the reversibility of the declines in lung function have been demonstrated after 3—4 years of exposure to inhaled insulin. As a result of these findings, the manufacturer recommends that all patients have pulmonary function assessed prior to beginning therapy with Exubera®; assessment of DLco should also be considered. Exubera® is not recommended for use in patients with a baseline FEV1 or DLco < 70% predicted. In addition, pulmonary function tests should be performed after the first 6 months of therapy, and annually thereafter, even in the absence of pulmonary symptoms. In patients who have a decline of 20% of FEV1 from baseline, pulmonary function tests should be repeated. If the 20% decline is confirmed, Exubera® should be discontinued. The presence of pulmonary symptoms and lesser declines in pulmonary function may require more frequent monitoring and consideration of discontinuation of Exubera®.Hepatic disease, renal impairment, or renal failure may affect insulin dosage requirements. Some pharmacokinetic studies have shown increased circulating levels of insulin in patients with hepatic or renal failure. Insulin dosage adjustments may be needed in some patients.The American Diabetes Association recommends that regular insulin by continuous intravenous infusion be used to treat hyperglycemic crisis including diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS) (and diabetic coma) unless it is considered mild. Injectable regular insulin is also preferred for those patients with poor tissue perfusion, shock, or cardiovascular collapse, or in patients requiring insulin for the treatment of hyperkalemia.Hypoglycemia is the most common adverse effect of inhaled insulin therapy; hypoglycemia is the major barrier to achieving optimal glycemic control long term. Clinical investigations of inhaled insulin indicate that the overall incidence of hypoglycemia is similar to or slightly less than regular SC insulin. Insulin should not be administered to patients during episodes of hypoglycemia. Patients at risk for hypoglycemia are those who have brittle diabetes, have received an overdose of insulin, have a delayed or decreased food intake, or undergo an excessive amount of exercise relative to their usual insulin dose. Patients at risk for severe, iatrogenic hypoglycemia include those with insulin deficiency (i.e., type 1 diabetes mellitus and advanced type 2 diabetes mellitus), those with a history of severe hypoglycemia or hypoglycemia unawareness, and those undergoing intensive insulin therapy. Patient and family education regarding hypoglycemia management is crucial; the patient and patient's family should be instructed on how to recognize and manage the symptoms of hypoglycemia. Early warning signs of hypoglycemia may be less obvious in patients with hypoglycemia unawareness, which can be due to a long history of diabetes (where deficiencies in the release or response to counterregulatory hormones exist), with autonomic neuropathy, or taking beta-blockers. Patients should be aware of the need to have a readily available source of glucose (dextrose, d-glucose) or other carbohydrate to treat hypoglycemic episodes. In patients who are currently taking an alpha-glucosidase inhibitor (i.e., acarbose or miglitol) along with their insulin, oral glucose (dextrose) should be used to treat hypoglycemia; sucrose (table sugar) is unsuitable. In severe hypoglycemia, intravenous dextrose or glucagon injections may be needed. Insulin should not be used by the family to treat those patients who are unconscious. The number of elderly subjects studied during clinical investigations of the Exubera® brand of inhaled insulin is limited; however, the data available do not suggest that special dosing considerations are needed. Regardless, elderly patients are especially at risk for hypoglycemic episodes when using insulin. The specific reasons identified include intensive insulin therapy, use of an excessive insulin dose, improper timing of insulin administration with regards to meals, injection of the wrong type of insulin, renal failure, severe liver disease, alcohol ingestion, defective counterregulatory hormone release, missing meals/fasting, and gastroparesis. Because hypoglycemic events may be difficult to recognize in some elderly patients, the initial dosing and dosing increments of any insulin product should be conservative. Severe or frequent hypoglycemia in any patient is an indication for the modification of treatment regimens, including setting higher glycemic goals.In addition to hypoglycemia, hypokalemia may also occur as insulin facilitates the intracellular uptake of potassium. Patients at risk for hypokalemia (e.g., patients who are using potassium-lowering drugs or taking potassium concentration sensitive drugs) should be monitored closely for these effects.The Exubera® brand of inhaled insulin is contraindicated for use in patients hypersensitivity to insulin or the excipients in the formulations including glycine, sodium citrate, and mannitol hypersensitivity. Rare, but potentially serious, is generalized allergy to insulin, which may cause rash, pruritus, shortness of breath, wheezing, hypotension, tachycardia, and diaphoresis. Severe cases, including anaphylactoid reactions, may be life threatening.The Exubera® brand of inhaled insulin is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. Long term studies on the use of Exubera® have not been conducted. A phase 1 clinical trial enrolled pregnant patients; no safety concerns were noted during this study. Furthermore, 10 women treated with inhaled insulin became pregnant while participating in phase 2/3 clinical trials. The outcomes of the 10 pregnancies do not suggest a safety risk. Fetal abnormalities in animal studies of insulin have not been reported when insulin is used at doses similar to those that would be used in humans; however, high doses of insulin inducing maternal hypoglycemia have been associated with fetal toxicity such as pre- and post-implantation losses and visceral/skeletal abnormalities. Most experts recommend human insulin as the therapy of choice to maintain blood glucose as close to normal as possible during pregnancy in patients with Type 1 or 2 diabetes mellitus, and, if diet therapy alone is not successful, for those patients with gestational diabetes. Until more data on the use of inhaled insulin in pregnancy are available, inhaled insulin should only be given to pregnant women if clearly needed. In most cases, injectable human insulin should be used. In general, insulin requirements decline during the first trimester and increase during the second and third trimesters. Careful monitoring of the patient on insulin is required throughout pregnancy. Optimizing glycemic control before conception and during pregnancy appears to improve fetal outcome; this should include the avoidance of episodes of hypoglycemia as the toxic effects of maternal hypoglycemia on the fetus have been well-documented. During the perinatal period, careful monitoring of neonates born to mothers with diabetes is recommended. Post-partum, maternal insulin requirements may need adjustment.It is unknown whether inhaled insulin is excreted in human milk; however, many drugs, including human insulin, are excreted in human milk. Insulin is degraded in the gastrointestinal tract; therefore, any insulin secreted into breast milk would not be absorbed by a breast-feeding infant. The American Diabetes Association encourages breast-feeding in women with pre-existing diabetes mellitus or gestational diabetes ; accordingly, women on insulin therapy should be encouraged to breast-feed if no contraindications exist. Breast-feeding may decrease insulin requirements, despite the need for increased caloric intake. Careful observation of increased maternal caloric needs and maternal blood glucose concentrations are needed.The safety and efficacy of the Exubera® brand of inhaled insulin in children has not been established. Phase II/III clinical trials included the study of 180 adolescents (92 of which received inhaled insulin) aged 12—17 years and 119 children (60 of which received inhaled insulin) aged 6—11 years. Differences in fasting plasma glucose, postprandial plasma glucose, and HbA1c were not different between children or adolescents treated with inhaled versus subcutaneous insulin. However, because data on the use of inhaled insulin in children and infants are limited; inhaled insulin is not recommended for use in children until more data are available. Treatment of children and infants on insulin therapy requires special care. In general, special attention must be given to caloric intake, insulin dosage adjustments, and avoidance of low blood glucose. Because children < 5 years of age may not be able to identify symptoms of hypoglycemia, several pediatric textbooks recommend less stringent goals for fasting or preprandial blood glucose concentrations (100—200 mg/dL) and HbA1C (7.5—9).Tobacco smoking is contraindicated during treatment with the Exubera® brand of inhaled insulin. Patients who smoke tobacco or have quit smoking within the past 6 months should not use Exubera®. If a patients starts or resumes smoking, Exubera® should be discontinued immediately due to the increased risk of hypoglycemia; alternative antidiabetic therapy should be instituted. Tobacco smoking can alter the effect of inhaled insulin in several ways. First, nicotine activates neuroendocrine pathways (e.g., increases in circulating cortisol and catecholamine levels) and may increase plasma glucose. Second, tobacco smoking is known to aggravate insulin resistance. Finally, compared with non-smokers, insulin exposure after inhalation is greater in patients who smoke (see Pharmacokinetics). In a study of healthy individuals using the AERx insulin Diabetes Management system (iDMS), patients who smoked had a significantly greater AUC and Cmax with a shorter Tmax when compared to non-smokers; however, other safety issues were not noted.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    acute bronchospasm, chronic lung disease
    General Information
    Inhaled insulin is contraindicated in individuals with chronic lung disease (CLD), such as asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, because of the risk of acute bronchospasm. Acute bronchospasm has been observed in patients with chronic lung disease treated with inhaled insulin. Prior to initiating therapy with inhaled insulin in all patients, perform a detailed medical history, physical examination, and spirometry (FEV1) to identify any potential underlying lung disease. Assess pulmonary function (e.g., spirometry) after the first 6 months of therapy, and annually thereafter, even in the absence of pulmonary symptoms. In inhaled insulin recipients who have a decline of more than 20% in FEV1 from baseline, consider discontinuing inhaled insulin. Consider more frequent monitoring of pulmonary function in treated patients with pulmonary symptoms such as wheezing, bronchospasm, breathing difficulties, or persistent or recurring cough. If symptoms persist, discontinue inhaled insulin.
    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    People with type 1 diabetes mellitus should always use inhaled insulin in combination with basal insulin. Inhaled insulin is not recommended for the treatment of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA); injectable insulin therapy is recommended for patients with DKA.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Use inhaled insulin with caution in people with renal impairment or renal failure since they may be at an increased risk of hypoglycemia and may require more frequent dosage adjustment and more frequent blood glucose monitoring.
    Headline
    increased risk for lung cancer, lung cancer
    General Information
    For patients with active lung cancer, at increased risk for lung cancer, or with a prior history of lung cancer, consider whether the benefits of inhaled insulin use outweigh the potential risk for new lung tumors. In clinical trials, 2 cases of lung cancer were observed in patients exposed to inhaled insulin and no cases occurred with comparator treatment. In both cases, a prior history of heavy tobacco use was identified as a risk factor for lung cancer. Two additional cases of lung cancer (squamous cell and lung blastoma) occurred in non-smokers exposed to inhaled insulin and these cases were reported after clinical trial completion. The data are insufficient to determine whether inhaled insulin has an effect on lung or respiratory tract tumors.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class A, Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use inhaled insulin with caution in people with hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A, Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure since they may be at an increased risk of hypoglycemia and may require more frequent dosage adjustment and more frequent blood glucose monitoring.
    Headline
    tobacco smoking
    General Information
    Do not use inhaled insulin in people who smoke or who have recently (within the past 6 months) discontinued tobacco smoking. The safety and efficacy of inhaled insulin in people who smoke has not been established.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    The limited available data with inhaled insulin use during pregnancy are not sufficient to inform of drug-associated risk for adverse developmental outcomes. In animal reproduction studies, there were no adverse developmental outcomes with subcutaneous administration of carrier particles (vehicle without insulin) to pregnant rats during organogenesis at doses 21-times the human daily dose of 99 mg inhaled insulin, based on AUC. Injectable Insulin is the drug of choice in pregnant individuals with diabetes. Available data from published studies over decades have not established an association with human insulin use during pregnancy and major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. Optimizing glycemic control before conception and during pregnancy appears to improve maternal and fetal outcomes; the adverse effects of maternal hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia on the fetus have been well-documented. Careful glucose monitoring and management of patients with diabetes during labor and obstetric delivery are also required. Postpartum, maternal insulin requirements may need adjustment. Most experts, including the American College of Obstetrics and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the American Diabetes Association (ADA), recommend human insulin as the therapy of choice to maintain blood glucose as close to normal as possible in pregnant individuals with Type 1 or 2 diabetes mellitus, and, if diet therapy alone is not successful, for those patients with gestational diabetes; insulin does not cross the placenta. During pregnancy, the patient should receive an insulin regimen consisting of multiple injections using long-acting or intermediate-acting insulin in combination with short-acting insulin analogues, including insulin lispro or insulin aspart; these agents are preferred over regular insulin because of their more rapid onset of action.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    The Beers Criteria state that using a short- or rapid-action insulin alone to manage hyperglycemia in geriatric patients is potentially inappropriate. This practice should be avoided due to an increased risk of hypoglycemia and no improvement in managing hyperglycemia, regardless of the care setting. However, this recommendation does not apply when a short- or rapid-acting insulin is used alongside scheduled insulin such as basal or long-acting insulin. Geriatric adults are especially at risk for hypoglycemic episodes when using insulin. Initial dosing and titration of any insulin product should be conservative in the older adult. Risk factors for hypoglycemia in the older adult include intensive insulin therapy, use of an excessive insulin dose, improper timing of insulin administration with regard to meals, injection of the wrong type of insulin, renal failure, severe liver disease, alcohol ingestion, defective counter-regulatory hormone release, missing meals/fasting, and gastroparesis.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Inhaled insulin is compatible with breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of inhaled insulin in human milk, the effects on the breastfed infant, or the effects on milk production. Exogenous subcutaneous insulin is present in human milk, but no adverse effects in infants have been noted due to maternal insulin use. Subcutaneous administration of the carrier particle in lactating rats resulted in excretion of the carrier particle in rat milk at levels that were approximately 10% of the maternal exposure. Potential adverse reactions that are related to inhaled insulin are unlikely to be associated with potential exposure via breast milk. Insulin is degraded in the gastrointestinal tract; therefore, any insulin secreted into breast milk would not be absorbed by a breastfed infant. The American Diabetes Association encourages breast-feeding by individuals with pre-existing diabetes mellitus or gestational diabetes; accordingly, those on insulin therapy should be encouraged to breastfeed if no contraindications exist. Breast-feeding may decrease insulin requirements, despite the need for increased caloric intake. Careful observation of increased maternal caloric needs and maternal blood glucose concentrations are needed, especially in the first weeks postpartum. In postpartum individuals with pre-existing diabetes mellitus, insulin sensitivity increases with delivery of the placenta and then returns to pre-pregnancy levels over 1 to 2 weeks. For those taking insulin, particular attention should be directed to hypoglycemia prevention in the setting of breast-feeding and erratic mealtimes and sleep schedules.

Acebutolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Metoprolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Nicotine: (Minor) Nicotine may increase plasma glucose. Monitor blood sugar for needed insulin dosage adjustments in insulin-dependent diabetic patients whenever a change in either nicotine intake or smoking status occurs. In addition, the use of inhaled insulin is not recommended in patients who smoke. Smoking tobacco can alter the effect of inhaled insulin in several ways. First, nicotine activates neuroendocrine pathways (e.g., increases in circulating cortisol and catecholamine levels) and may increase plasma glucose. Second, tobacco smoking is known to aggravate insulin resistance. Finally, compared with non-smokers, insulin exposure after inhalation may be greater in patients who smoke. If inhaled insulin is used in this population, patients should be instructed to monitor blood glucose concentrations closely. If a change in smoking status or nicotine intake occur, patients should continue to monitor their blood glucose concentrations closely and clinicians should adjust the dose of insulin when indicated.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: In October 2007, Pfizer announced that Exubera® will be discontinued secondary to its limited use; a transition period where Exubera® will continue to be available is occurring so that patients can be switched to other therapies. Technosphere, another inhaled insulin that is in phase III trials, will be made available to Exubera® patients who continue to need inhaled insulin for medical reasons.Six new cases of lung cancer (i.e., secondary malignancy) have been reported in patients taking the Exubera® brand of inhaled insulin during clinical trials (0.13 cases/100 patient-years); only 1 patient in the control group was diagnosed with lung cancer (0.02 cases/100 patient-years). Additionally, a case of lung cancer has been reported in 1 patient receiving Exubera® during post-marketing surveillance. Because of the small numbers, a cause and effect relationship cannot be established. It should be noted that all patients diagnosed with lung cancer had a prior history of smoking cigarettes.Increased cough (21.5—29.2% overall incidence with 12.2—20.9% thought to be treatment related) is a commonly reported respiratory adverse event and occurs more often in patients using Exubera® inhaled insulin versus SC regular insulin. For the majority of patients, the cough is mild in severity and non-productive and decreases in prevalence over time. The cough is usually noted within seconds to minutes of inhaled insulin administration indicating that an irritant associated with inhalation of dry powder may be the underlying cause. During clinical trials, 1.2% of patients discontinued Exubera® inhaled insulin due to cough. Bronchospasm has rarely been reported during clinical trials of Exubera® inhaled insulin. If patients experience bronchospasm while taking inhaled insulin, they should discontinue the inhaled insulin and seek medical attention immediately. Re-administration of inhaled insulin requires a careful risk evaluation, and should only be done under close medical monitoring with appropriate clinical facilities available. Other respiratory adverse events that are more common in patients using inhaled insulin versus SC insulin include dyspnea (3.6—4.4% with 0.4% discontinuing treatment), epistaxis, laryngitis, respiratory tract infection, pharyngitis, rhinitis, sinusitis, and increased sputum production, other respiratory disorder.Pulmonary function tests have been performed during clinical investigations of the Exubera® brand of inhaled insulin in 1977 patients treated in controlled phase 2/3 clinical studies; the results indicate that treatment with inhaled insulin is associated with small decreases in FEV1 and carbon monoxide diffusion capacity (DLco). A decline in baseline FEV1 of 20% at last observation occurred in 1.5% if Exubera® inhaled insulin-treated patients versus 1.3% of comparator-treated patients. A decline from baseline DLco of 20% at last observation occurred in 5.1% of Exubera® inhaled insulin-treated patients vs. 3.6% of comparator-treated patients. Following 2 years of therapy, in patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes, the difference between treatment groups for the mean change in FEV1 from baseline was approximately 40 ml, favoring the comparator drug. Furthermore, the difference between treatment groups for the mean change in baseline in DLco from baseline was 0.5 ml/min/mmHg favoring the comparator group in patients with type 1 diabetes and 0.1 ml/min/mmHg favoring the inhaled insulin group in patients with type 2 diabetes. In general, these declines are small, non-progressive beyond 2 weeks of therapy, and reversible with drug discontinuation. The reversibility of the declines in lung function have been demonstrated after 3—4 years of exposure to inhaled insulin; in one study, the reversibility was demonstrated 6 weeks after discontinuation. Limited data on the use of Exubera® in patients with pulmonary diseases are available; in patients with mild asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), inhaled insulin is efficacious and well tolerated and differences in adverse events between those patients with mild lung disease and without are not evident; however, long-term safety and efficacy have not been determined and the use of Exubera® inhaled insulin in these populations is not recommended.Hypoglycemia is the most common adverse reaction of insulin therapy, including inhaled insulin therapy. The incidence is difficult to estimate as definitions of hypoglycemia in clinical trials vary. In general, both mild and severe hypoglycemia are less common in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus versus type 1, even when undergoing intensive insulin therapy. However, patients with long-standing type 2 diabetes tend to become more and more insulin deficient as the disease progresses; as this occurs, the incidence and severity of hypoglycemia approaches that of patients with type 1 diabetes. Diabetic patients who have brittle diabetes, are on intensive insulin therapy, have received an overdose of insulin, have a delayed or decreased food intake, or undergo an excessive amount of exercise relative to their usual insulin dose can develop hyperinsulinemia, resulting in hypoglycemia. In addition, in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus or long-standing type 2 diabetes mellitus, counterregulatory hormone secretion in response to hypoglycemia is impaired. It is the combination of these two mechanisms, hyperinsulinemia and impairment of counterregulatory hormone secretion, that causes patients to become severely hypoglycemic. In addition, patients with recent hypoglycemia are at increased risk of recurrent and severe hypoglycemia. Hypoglycemia also has been reported in patients who were switched from beef to pork insulin preparations and diabetic patients who develop adrenocortical insufficiency or Addison's disease may require decreased amounts of insulin. While mild hypoglycemia occurs much more frequently, severe hypoglycemic event rates (that requiring the assistance of another person) of 62—110 up to 170 episodes per 100 patient years have been reported in patients with type 1 diabetes. These numbers are approximately 10% of those for patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (3—10 up to 73 episodes per 100 patient years). Hypoglycemia is manifested as hunger, pallor, nausea/vomiting, fatigue, diaphoresis, headache, palpitations, numbness of the mouth, tingling in the fingers, tremor, muscle weakness, blurred vision, hypothermia, uncontrolled yawning, irritability, mental confusion, sinus tachycardia, shallow breathing, and loss of consciousness. Prolonged hypoglycemia can result in irreversible brain damage and even death. Patients experiencing symptoms of hypoglycemia should be instructed to ingest 20 grams glucose; if symptoms do not resolve, this can be repeated in 15—20 minutes. In addition, patients should be instructed to ingest a full snack or meal shortly thereafter. For patients who are unconscious or unwilling, parenteral glucagon can be used. In the case of severe hypoglycemia, intravenous dextrose is the treatment of choice. Description
  Text
  Insulin, inhaled is a dry powder formulation of rapid-acting recombinant human insulin indicated to improve glycemic control in individuals with diabetes mellitus. It has a rapid onset of action and is used as prandial insulin. After pulmonary absorption into the systemic circulation, its metabolism and elimination are comparable to those of regular human insulin. In 24-week studies of adults with inadequately controlled type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), inhaled insulin was compared with injectable insulin aspart. At Week 24, treatment with basal insulin and mealtime inhaled insulin provided a mean reduction in A1C that met the prespecified noninferiority margin of 0.4%; however, inhaled insulin provided less A1C reduction than insulin aspart, and the difference was statistically significant. More subjects in the insulin aspart group achieved an A1C target of 7% or less compared with the inhaled insulin group (27.1% versus 13.8%, respectively). Inhaled insulin has also been studied in adults with inadequately controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) despite treatment with maximally tolerated metformin monotherapy or 2 or more oral antidiabetic drugs (OADs). At Week 24, treatment with inhaled insulin plus OADs provided a statistically significantly greater reduction in A1C compared with inhaled placebo plus OADs (-0.82% versus -0.42%, respectively). In a 26-week, randomized, active-controlled trial in pediatric patients aged 6 to 17 years with type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus receiving basal insulin, inhaled insulin did not demonstrate noninferiority to rapid-acting insulin analogs for change in HbA1c from baseline to Week 26. The between-group difference in mean HbA1c change was 0.18% (95% CI, -0.07 to 0.44), exceeding the prespecified noninferiority margin of 0.4%. In the treatment of T1DM, insulin therapy may be initiated with a basal-bolus regimen and then further individualized to achieve treatment goals. In the treatment of T2DM, insulin is generally reserved for people who continue to have an A1C above target despite dual/triple therapy with metformin and other antidiabetic agents. GLP-1-based therapy is generally the preferred choice to insulin in T2DM in adults without severe hyperglycemia or hyperglycemic crisis. Evidence from trials comparing GLP-1-based therapy and insulin (basal, premixed, or basal-bolus) shows similar or even better efficacy in A1C reduction; GLP-1-based therapy has a lower risk of hypoglycemia and is associated with reductions in body weight compared to weight gain with insulin. In people who cannot tolerate GLP-1-based therapy, or who fail to meet glycemic targets with dual/triple therapy, insulin therapy with basal insulin should be initiated and titrated to target. If A1C remains above target, prandial insulin (injected or inhaled) should be added. Consider an initial injectable combination (i.e., GLP-1-based therapy plus basal insulin or prandial/basal insulin) if A1C is greater than 10% and 2% above target. In people who are intensified to insulin therapy, combination therapy with GLP-1-based therapy has been shown to have greater efficacy and durability of glycemic treatment effect than treatment intensification with insulin alone. Consider the early introduction of insulin if there is evidence of ongoing catabolism, if symptoms of hyperglycemia are present, or when A1C concentrations (greater than 10%) or blood glucose concentrations (300 mg/dL or more) are very high. In people who are starting on insulin therapy and are taking a thiazolidinedione or sulfonylurea, discontinue or reduce the dose of the oral medication. Inhaled insulin was FDA-approved in 2014.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Endogenous insulin regulates carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism by several mechanisms; in general, insulin promotes the storage and inhibits the breakdown of glucose, fat, and amino acids. Insulin lowers glucose concentrations by facilitating the uptake of glucose in muscle and adipose tissue and by inhibiting hepatic glucose production (glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis). Insulin also regulates fat metabolism by enhancing the storage of fat (lipogenesis) and inhibiting the mobilization of fat for energy in adipose tissues (lipolysis and free fatty acid oxidation). Finally, insulin is involved in the regulation of protein metabolism by increasing protein synthesis and inhibiting proteolysis in muscle tissue.
 
Diabetes mellitus type 1 is caused by insulin deficiency while diabetes mellitus type 2 is caused by a combination of insulin deficiency and resistance. Biosynthetic insulin is used as replacement therapy in patients with diabetes mellitus to temporarily restore their ability to use carbohydrates, fats, and proteins, and to convert glycogen to fat. Insulin administration also enables these patients to replete their liver glycogen stores. Inhaled insulin is prepared using recombinant DNA technology (non-pathogenic strain of E. coli bacteria) to create a product identical in structure and function to endogenous human insulin.
 
Proper insulin therapy, when needed, has beneficial effects besides glycemic control in patients with diabetes mellitus (DM).
In the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) patients, 13 to 39 years of age with type 1 DM were studied. Those receiving 'intensive' therapy (3 or more injections per day or use of an insulin pump) had approximately a 60% reduction in the incidence of retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy compared to 'conventional' (2 injections/day) dosing but had a greater risk of serious hypoglycemia.
In the United Kingdom prospective diabetes study (UKPDS) patients 48 to 60 years of age with type 2 DM were studied. Those receiving 'intensive therapy' with either sulfonylureas or insulin experienced an approximate 25% reduction in the incidence of microvascular complications when compared to 'conventional therapy.' The fasting blood glucose goal in the intensive therapy groups was less than 6 mmol/L (less than 109 mg/dL); in the conventional therapy group, the fasting blood glucose goal was the best achievable with diet alone; drug therapy was added if fasting blood glucose was more than 15 mmol/L (273 mg/dL) or the patient experienced symptoms of hyperglycemia. Similar to DCCT, patients in the intensive therapy group had a greater incidence of hypoglycemia.

    Description
    The deep lungs (alveoli) are attractive targets for drug administration because they have a large absorptive area with high permeability and vascularization. In order to effectively deliver inhaled drug products to the alveoli, a particle size of 1 to 3 microns (mcm) in aerodynamic diameter and a low particle velocity are necessary. Storage of opened strips that are in use: Store at room temperature (15 to 30 degrees C [59 to 86 degrees F]). Opened strips must be used within 3 days. Do not return opened strips to the refrigerator after storage at room temperature.
 
Preparation and Administration
Remove a cartridge from the blister strip and load it into the inhaler according to the manufacturer's instructions.
Before use, allow the cartridge and inhaler to reach room temperature for approximately 10 minutes.
After the cartridge has been inserted, keep the inhaler level. Do not invert, shake, or drop the inhaler; if any of these occur, replace the cartridge before administration.
Instruct the individual to fully exhale before inhalation. Place the mouthpiece in the mouth and instruct the individual to inhale deeply through the inhaler. Have the individual hold the breath as long as comfortable, then remove the inhaler and exhale normally.
Discard the used cartridge after administration. If more than 1 cartridge is required for the prescribed dose, repeat the administration process with a new cartridge.
Keep the inhaler dry; wipe with a clean, dry cloth as needed. Do not wash the inhaler. Store the inhaler closed and do not leave cartridges in the device.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  The effect of hepatic impairment on inhaled insulin has not been studied. However, patients with hepatic impairment may be at greater risk for hypoglycemia. Frequent glucose monitoring and a lower dosage may be necessary in patients with hepatic impairment. Individualize dosage based on blood glucose and other clinical parameters.
  Renal Impairment
  The effect of renal impairment on inhaled insulin has not been studied. Patients with renal impairment may be more at risk for hypoglycemia. Some studies with human insulin have shown increased circulating concentrations of insulin in patients with renal failure. Frequent glucose monitoring and a lower dosage may be necessary in patients with renal impairment. Individualize dosage based on blood glucose and other clinical parameters.
