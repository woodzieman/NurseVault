Drug Class: Bortezomib is a proteasome inhibitor that is administered intravenously or subcutaneously.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 1.3 mg/m2 per dose IV or subcutaneously; 1.6 mg/m2 per dose IV has also been studied.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): dysesthesia, hypoesthesia, paresthesias, peripheral neuropathy, weakness, ataxia, coma, cranial nerve palsies, dizziness, dysarthria, guillain-barre syndrome, headache, leukoencephalopathy, muscle paralysis, seizures, stroke, anemia, bleeding, disseminated intravascular coagulation (dic), gi bleeding, intracranial bleeding, leukopenia, lymphopenia, neutropenia, subdural hematoma, thrombocytopenia, hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, syncope, alopecia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Bortezomib is contraindicated in patients with bortezomib hypersensitivity, boron hypersensitivity, or mannitol hypersensitivity. Bortezomib is a modified dipeptidyl boronic acid, and mannitol is an inactive ingredient in the product. Emetic risk: Low Extravasation risk: Nonvesicant Hazardous drug: NIOSH 2024 List: Table 1

          Dosage Description
          Treatment is administered for nine 6-week cycles. In cycles 1 to 4, 1.3 mg/m2 IV on days 1, 4, 8, and 11 followed by a 10-day rest period (on days 12 to 21), then 1.3 mg/m2 IV on days 22, 25, 29, and 32 followed by a 10-day rest period (on days 33 to 42) in combination with melphalan (9 mg/m2 PO daily on days 1, 2, 3, and 4) and prednisone (60 mg/m2 on days 1, 2, 3, and 4); this 6-week cycle is considered 1 course. In cycles 5 to 9, bortezomib 1.3 mg/m2 IV over 3 to 5 seconds on days 1, 8, 22, and 29 in combination with melphalan (9 mg/m2 PO daily on days 1, 2, 3, and 4) and prednisone (60 mg/m2 PO daily on days 1, 2, 3, and 4); a 6-week cycle is considered 1 course. At least 72 hours should elapse between consecutive doses of bortezomib. Coadministration of certain drugs may need to be avoided or dosage adjustments may be necessary; review drug interactions. Interruption or discontinuation of therapy and/or a dose reduction may be necessary in patients who develop toxicities. At a median follow-up time of 16.3 months, the primary endpoint of median time to progression was significantly improved with bortezomib, melphalan, and prednisone (VMP) (median of 8 treatment cycles) compared with melphalan and prednisone (MP) alone (24 months vs. 16.6 months; hazard ratio [HR] = 0.48; p less than 0.001) in patients with previously untreated multiple myeloma who were transplant ineligible in a multinational, randomized, phase 3 trial (the VISTA trial; n = 682). The median overall survival (OS) time (secondary endpoint) was not reached in either study arm at this analysis; however, OS was significantly improved in the VMP arm (HR = 0.61; p = 0.0008). At a median follow-up time of 61 months, the median OS was significantly improved with VMP compared with MP alone (56.4 months vs. 43.1 months; HR = 0.695; p less than 0.001); 5-year OS rates were 46% and 34.4%, respectively.

        Route Age Groups
          Heading
          Adults
          Dosage Description
          1.3 mg/m2 IV on days 1, 4, 8, and 11 followed by a 10-day rest period (on days 12 to 21); this 3-week cycle is considered 1 course. For extended therapy of more than 8 cycles, administer bortezomib 1.3 mg/m2 IV over 3 to 5 seconds on the standard 3-week cycle or on days 1, 8, 15, and 22 followed by a 13-day rest period (on days 23 to 35). At least 72 hours should elapse between consecutive doses of bortezomib. Coadministration of certain drugs may need to be avoided or dosage adjustments may be necessary; review drug interactions. Interruption or discontinuation of therapy and/or a dose reduction may be necessary in patients who develop toxicities. The primary endpoint of median time to progression (TTP) was significantly improved with IV bortezomib compared with high-dose dexamethasone (6.22 months vs. 3.49 months; hazard ratio [HR] = 0.55; p less than 0.001) in patients with relapsed multiple myeloma who had received 1 to 3 prior therapies in a multinational, randomized, phase 3 trial (the APEX trial; n = 669). At 1 year of follow-up, the OS was significantly improved with bortezomib therapy (HR = 0.57; p = 0.001) despite 44% of patients with disease progression in the dexamethasone arm crossing over to the bortezomib arm. Due to favorable efficacy results at an interim analysis, all patients in the dexamethasone arm were offered bortezomib. At a median follow-up time of 22 months, the median OS was significantly improved with bortezomib compared with dexamethasone (29.8 months vs. 23.7 months; HR = 0.77; p less than 0.027); 1-year OS rates were 80% and 67%, respectively. More than 62% of patients in the dexamethasone arm had crossed over to the bortezomib arm at the time of this analysis. Treatment with single-agent IV bortezomib led to a median TTP of 7 months and a median OS of 17 months in patients with relapsed and refractory multiple myeloma who had received a median of 6 prior therapies (range, 2 to 15 therapies) in a single-arm, phase 2 trial (the SUMMIT trial; n = 202). Dexamethasone 20 mg PO on the day of and the day after bortezomib was permitted starting in cycle 2 in patients with disease progression or starting in cycle 4 in patients with stable disease.

          Dosage Description
          Begin treatment at the last tolerated bortezomib IV dose on days 1, 4, 8, and 11, either as a single agent or in combination with dexamethasone, every 3 weeks for a maximum of 8 cycles. Patients who had relapsed or had progressive multiple myeloma at least 6 months after bortezomib therapy and who had at least a partial response (PR) to a bortezomib-containing regimen were re-treated with the last tolerated dose of bortezomib as a single agent or in combination with dexamethasone in a multinational, open-label, phase 2 trial (n = 126). The median time from prior bortezomib treatment was 13.9 months (range, 5 to 39 months) and patients had a median of 2 prior therapies (range, 1 to 7 therapies). After a median of 7 bortezomib re-treatment cycles (range, 1 to 8 cycles), the overall response rate (ORR) was 40% (complete response rate, 1%; PR rate, 39%). The ORR was 32% in patients re-treated with single-agent bortezomib (n = 34) and 42% in patients re-treated with bortezomib and dexamethasone (n = 92). The median duration of response was 6.5 months (range, 0.6 to 19.3 months).

Administer in combination with dexamethasone (20 mg PO on days 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 11, and 12 in cycles 1 to 8 then 20 mg PO on days 1, 2, 8, and 9 in cycles 9 to 16) and panobinostat (20 mg PO on days 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, and 12). Patients with primary refractory or bortezomib refractory disease were ineligible for this study. At a median follow-up time of 6.47 months, the median progression-free survival time (primary endpoint) was significantly improved in the panobinostat arm (11.99 months) compared with the placebo arm (8.08 months; hazard ratio [HR] = 0.63; 95% CI, 0.52 to 0.76; p less than 0.0001). The overall survival (OS) time was not significantly improved in the panobinostat arm (33.64 months vs. 30.39 months; HR = 0.87; 95% CI, 0.69 to 1.1); however, OS data are not mature. Crossover from the placebo arm to the panobinostat arm is not permitted.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Bortezomib is contraindicated in patients with bortezomib hypersensitivity, boron hypersensitivity, or mannitol hypersensitivity. Bortezomib is a modified dipeptidyl boronic acid, and mannitol is an inactive ingredient in the product.
 
Bortezomib is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category D. Females of childbearing potential should avoid becoming pregnant while taking bortezomib. Although not teratogenic in animal studies, certain species experienced significant fetal loss when bortezomib was given during organogenesis. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of bortezomib in pregnant women. If bortezomib is used during pregnancy or if the patient becomes pregnant while receiving the drugs, the patient should be apprised of the potential harm to the fetus.
 
Fertility studies with bortezomib were not performed but evaluation of reproductive tissues has been performed in general animal toxicity studies. In animal studies, degenerative effects in ovary were observed at doses >= 0.3 mg/m2 (one-fourth the clinical dose), and degenerative changes in the testes occurred at 1.2 mg/m2. Bortezomib could potentially cause infertility in male or female patients; family planning options should be discussed with appropriate patients prior to beginning treatment.
 
According to the manufacturer, bortezomib or breast-feeding should be discontinued because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in nursing infants from bortezomib. Bortezomib excretion into human milk is unknown. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
Patients with pre-existing symptoms (numbness, pain, or a burning feeling in the hands or feet) and/or signs of peripheral neuropathy may experience worsening of this condition during treatment with bortezomib. Only administer bortezomib to patients with preexisting severe neuropathy after careful risk-benefit assessment. Patients should be monitored for symptoms of neuropathy such as a burning sensation, hyperesthesia, paresthesia, discomfort, or neuropathic pain. Patients should be cautioned about the use of concomitant medications that may be associated with peripheral neuropathy [e.g., amiodarone, anti-virals, isoniazid, nitrofurantoin, or HMG-CoA Reductase Inhibitors ('statins')], as the appearance of neuropathy due to these medications may be additive to the effects of bortezomib. The complete time-course of this toxicity has not been fully characterized. Patients experiencing new or worsening peripheral neuropathy may require a change in the dose and schedule of bortezomib; some patients may require bortezomib discontinuation (see Dosage).
 
Clinically significant hypotension may occur with bortezomib therapy. Caution should be used when treating patients with a history of syncope, receiving medications associated with hypotension (i.e., antihypertensive therapy), and in patients who are volume depleted or have dehydration due to the potential for exacerbation of hypotension during bortezomib therapy. Management of postural/orthostatic hypotension may include adjustment of antihypertensive medications, hydration, or administration of mineralocorticoids. In addition, because bortezomib can cause nausea, vomiting, and/or diarrhea, patients should be closely monitored for the development of dehydration; administer fluids and electrolytes should nausea/vomiting/diarrhea occur.
 
Exacerbation of existing congestive heart failure may occur with bortezomib therapy. In addition, patients may experience acute onset congestive heart failure including the onset of a decreased left ventricular ejection fraction. These adverse effects may occur in patient with risk factors for cardiac disease but have also been reported in patients with few or no risk factors. Patients with risk factors for heart failure or other cardiac disease should be closely monitored. The European Medicines Agency Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) concluded during its March 2008 meeting that the benefits of bortezomib are greater than its risks EXCEPT in patients with acute diffuse infiltrative pulmonary disease and pericardial disease. The CHMP, therefore, recommended contraindicating the use of bortezomib in patients with acute diffuse infiltrative pulmonary and pericardial disease. Also, the CHMP recommended strengthening the existing warnings on pulmonary disorders by advising physicians to perform chest X-rays and consider individual benefit-risk profiles before bortezomib initiation. New information on cardiac and pulmonary side effects observed during the post-marketing phase is also recommended to be included in the label. Recommendations will be sent to the European Commission for consideration.
 
Bortezomib can cause tumor lysis syndrome (TLS). Patients with a high tumor burden prior to treatment are at risk for TLS and should be carefully monitored during bortezomib therapy.
 
Severe bone marrow suppression, including anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia, may require bortezomib dosage adjustment. Patients with aggressive malignant hematologic disease may require treatment despite severe bone marrow suppression. Therefore, this drug should be used only by clinicians experienced in the use of cancer or immune suppression therapy. The hematological status (leukocyte and platelet counts) of the patient should be monitored closely during bortezomib therapy, and a complete blood count should be monitored prior to each dose of bortezomib. Onset of thrombocytopenia is common in cycles 1 and 2 but can continue throughout therapy. Bortezomib therapy should be temporarily discontinued in patients who experience Grade 4 thrombocytopenia or other hematological toxicity, and then reinstituted at a reduced dose once the toxicity has resolved (see Dosage). Patients with an active infection should be treated prior to receiving bortezomib. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with chemotherapy.
 
Myelosuppressive effects of bortezomib can increase the risk of infection or bleeding; therefore, dental work should be delayed until blood counts have returned to normal. Patients, especially those with dental disease, should be instructed in proper oral hygiene, including caution in use of regular toothbrushes, dental floss, and toothpicks.
 
Intramuscular injections should not be administered to patients with platelet counts 60 ml/minute (see Pharmacokinetics).
 
Bortezomib may be associated with fatigue, dizziness, syncope, hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, and blurred or double vision. Driving or operating machinery is not recommended by patients if they experience these symptoms.
 
The safety and effectiveness of bortezomib in children have not been established.
 
During phase 3 clinical trials for multiple myeloma, approximately 37% of patients were >= 65 years of age. In patients treated with bortezomib, the incidence of grade 3 and 4 adverse reactions was 64%, 78%, and 75% for patients 65 years of age, respectively. During phase 2 clinical trials for multiple myeloma, 35% of patients were >= 65 years of age. In patients receiving bortezomib, the incidence of grade 3 or higher adverse reactions was 74%, 80%, and 85% for patients 65 years of age, respectively. Although no overall differences in safety or efficacy were observed between patients >= 65 years of age and younger patients, greater sensitivity of elderly patients cannot be ruled out (see Pharmacokinetics).
 
Caution is recommended during handling and preparation of bortezomib to avoid accidental exposure. Use of gloves and other protective clothing to prevent skin contact are recommended. Bortezomib is not a vesicant; however, irritation may occur upon extravasation.

    cardiac disease
    General Information
    Use bortezomib with caution in patients with cardiac disease. Frequently monitor patients with pre-existing, or risk factors for cardiac disease. Cardiotoxicity has been reported with bortezomib therapy, including acute onset congestive heart failure, decreased left ventricular ejection fraction, and rare cases of QT prolongation. Additionally, serious and sometimes fatal pulmonary toxicity (e.g., pulmonary hypertension, acute respiratory distress syndrome, pneumonitis, interstitial pneumonia, lung infiltration) has occurred with bortezomib use. Interrupt therapy pending a diagnostic evaluation in patients who develop new or worsening cardiopulmonary symptoms.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use bortezomib with caution in patients with moderate (Child-Pugh class B) and severe (Child-Pugh class C) hepatic impairment. Hepatotoxicity has been reported with bortezomib use, including acute hepatic failure, hepatitis, elevated hepatic enzymes, and hyperbilirubinemia. A dose reduction at baseline, an interruption in therapy to assess reversibility, or discontinuation of therapy may be necessary in patients with hepatic impairment or who develop hepatotoxicity.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus
    General Information
    Use bortezomib with caution in patients with diabetes mellitus receiving oral antidiabetic agents; these patients should closely monitor their blood glucose levels. Both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia have been reported in patients receiving bortezomib.

    pregnancy
    General Information
    Based on its mechanism of action and animal data, bortezomib can cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy. There are no human data to evaluate a drug-associated risk. Administration to rabbits during the period of organogenesis at a dose approximately 0.5 times the clinical dose of 1.3 mg/m2 resulted in significant post-implantation loss, reduced number of live fetuses, and decreased fetal weight in surviving offspring. Inform people of reproductive potential about the potential risks to the fetus associated with bortezomib use during pregnancy.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with bortezomib. Bortezomib can cause fetal harm. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking bortezomib and for 7 months after the last dose. For those that can cause pregnancy in others, contraception should be used during bortezomib treatment and for 4 months after the last dose. Based on its mechanism of action and findings in animals, bortezomib may impair male and female fertility.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking bortezomib and for 2 months after the last dose. Bortezomib has the potential to cause serious side effects in the breast-fed child. There are no data on the presence of bortezomib in human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effects on milk production.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The most common GI adverse events during clinical trials with bortezomib were nausea/vomiting (55%/33%), diarrhea (52%), constipation (41%), anorexia (36%), abdominal pain (15%), dyspepsia, and dysgeusia; ileus can occur. Ischemic colitis and acute pancreatitis were noted during the post-marketing surveillance period. Grade 3 and 4 gastrointestinal (GI) events occurred in 18% and 1% of patients, respectively and were considered serious in 11% of patients. Nausea was reported more frequently in patients with multiple myeloma (57%) compared to patients with mantle cell lymphoma (44%). In a comparative trial of patients with multiple myeloma, 5% of 318 who received bortezomib monotherapy and 20% of 318 who received bortezomib and doxorubicin liposomal had mucositis or stomatitis; dosage reduction may be needed (see Dosage). Use of antiemetics and antidiarrheals may be necessary to treat GI events. Dehydration, which may be a result of nausea, vomiting, and/or diarrhea was reported in 10% of patients. Fluid and electrolyte replacement should be administered to prevent dehydration.Bortezomib causes a peripheral neuropathy that is predominantly sensory. However, cases of severe sensory and motor peripheral neuropathy have been reported. Peripheral neuropathy including peripheral sensory neuropathy, neuropathic pain, and aggravation of pre-existing peripheral neuropathy occurred in 39% of bortezomib-treated patients during clinical trials and was dose-limiting in some patients. Only administer bortezomib to patients with preexisting severe neuropathy after careful risk-benefit assessment. Peripheral neuropathy was Grade 3 in 11% of patients and Grade 4 in 70% had been previously treated with neurotoxic agents. The incidence of Grade 3 neuropathy was 5% (2 of 41 patients) in patients without baseline neuropathy. Symptoms may improve or return to baseline after discontinuation of bortezomib therapy; the time course of this toxicity has not been fully characterized. For example, improvement in or resolution of peripheral neuropathy was reported in 73% of patients who discontinued due to Grade 2 neuropathy or who had at least Grade 3 peripheral neuropathy in the phase 2 multiple myeloma studies. The long-term outcome of peripheral neuropathy has not been studied in mantle cell lymphoma. Instruct patients to report any new or worsening symptoms of peripheral neuropathy such as tingling, numbness, pain, a burning feeling in the feet or hands, or weakness in the arms or legs.Asthenia (weakness), including symptoms of malaise and fatigue, occurred in 64% of patients taking bortezomib during clinical trials. Asthenia was predominantly reported as Grade 1 or 2; however, 16% of patients experienced Grade 3 asthenia. Four percent (4%) of patients discontinued treatment due to asthenia, and asthenia was reported in 62% and 72% of patients with multiple myeloma and mantle cell lymphoma, respectively. Brain imaging techniques, preferably magnetic resonance imagining (MRI), are used to confirm the diagnosis of RPLS. Bortezomib should be discontinued if patients develop RPLS, and the safety of reinitiating bortezomib in patients that have a history of RPLS is not known. Description
  Text
  Bortezomib is a proteasome inhibitor that is administered intravenously or subcutaneously. Bortezomib is indicated for the treatment of multiple myeloma and mantle-cell lymphoma. Patients with pre-existing severe neuropathy should receive bortezomib only after careful risk-benefit assessment. The subcutaneous route may be the preferred route of administration in patients with pre-existing peripheral neuropathy or who have a high risk of developing peripheral neuropathy.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Bortezomib is a reversible inhibitor of the 26S proteasome in mammalian cells, which is part of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. A proteasome is a large multiprotein particle present in the cytosol and cell nucleus that is responsible for the regulation of protein expression and the degradation of damaged or obsolete proteins within the cell; its activity is critical to activation or suppression of cellular functions. This system regulates the expression of proteins mediating cell cycle progression (p21cip, p27kip, cyclins), oncogenes (p53), and apoptosis (Bcl, cIAP, XIAP, Bax); inhibition of the breakdown of these proteins has been associated with sensitization of the cell to apoptosis. Malignant cells are much more sensitive to the effects of proteasome inhibition than normal cells. In normal cells, checkpoint mechanisms arrest cell division when irregularities in cell cycle regulators are noted; cell division is allowed to continue only after proteasome activity has been restored. In tumor cells however, genetic changes that accompany malignant transformation disable the checkpoint mechanisms. Thus, proteasome inhibition may reverse some of the changes that allow proliferation and suppress apoptosis in the malignant cell.
 
Bortezomib has been shown to inhibit the activation of nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-kappa B) through stabilization of the inhibitor protein I kappa B kinase (IKK). Myeloma cells depend on the NF-kappa B pathway for transcription of growth factors such as interleukin-6, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) for angiogenesis, VCAM-1, a cell adhesion molecule important in the adherence of the plasma cells to the stromal tissue in bone marrow, and other proteins (e.g., Bcl-2) that enhance myeloma cell survival and resistance to chemotherapy. At low concentrations (nanomolar), bortezomib is effective in inhibiting the transcription of these genes. However, inhibition of NF-kappa B may not be the only role of bortezomib in multiple myeloma. Bortezomib appears to be synergistic with dexamethasone in cell culture due to the inhibition of insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), which is thought to responsible for resistance to dexamethasone in multiple myeloma. Additional in vitro studies have shown that bortezomib decreases the levels of several anti-apoptotic proteins and induces a dual pathway of apoptosis in myeloma cells.

The mean distribution volume of bortezomib ranged from about 498 to 1,884 L/m2, the mean elimination half-life ranged from 40 to 193 hours, and the mean total body clearance ranged from 15 to 112 L/hour in multiple myeloma patients who received single or repeat IV doses of bortezomib 1 mg/m2 (n = 12) or 1.3 mg/m2 (n = 12). In vitro, bortezomib primarily undergoes oxidative metabolism via CYP450 isoenzymes. Deboronation occurs to form 2 metabolites that then undergo hydroxylation to form several metabolites; the 2 initial metabolites appear to be inactive as 26S proteasome inhibitors. The elimination pathways of bortezomib have not been determined in humans. The maximal inhibition of 20S proteasome activity in whole blood was 73% to 83% with a bortezomib 1.3 mg/m2 dose given twice weekly and was noted 5 minutes after administration.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes: CYP3A4, CYP2C19
Bortezomib is a substrate of CYP3A4, CYP2C19 and CYP1A2; metabolism via CYP2D6 and CYP2C9 isoenzymes is minor. In vitro, bortezomib is a weak inhibitor of CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4; it is not a CYP3A4 or CYP1A2 inducer. Bortezomib may inhibit CYP2C19 and increase exposure to drugs that are substrates for this enzyme. Increased or decreased bortezomib exposure may occur with the concomitant use of CYP3A4 inhibitors or CYP3A4 inducers, respectively. Avoid the use of CYP3A4 inducers during bortezomib therapy; closely monitor patients for toxicity and consider a bortezomib dose reduction if bortezomib is administered with a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor. The co-administration of bortezomib with a weak CYP3A4 inducer, dexamethasone, did not affect bortezomib exposure in a drug interaction study (n = 7). Additionally, the co-administration of bortezomib with a strong CYP2C19 inhibitor, omeprazole, did not affect bortezomib exposure in a drug interaction study (n = 17).
