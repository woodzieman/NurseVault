    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    nephrolithiasis
    General Information
    Patients prone to oxylate nephrolithiasis should check with their physicians prior to use of cranberry tablets. Since cranberry juice and cranberry extracts have a high oxalate content, there is some concern they might increase the risk of kidney stones. Concentrated cranberry extracts might contain higher amounts of oxalate than the approximately 1.9 mg of oxalate contained in 30ml of cranberry juice. Some evidence exists that cranberry extract tablets can boost urinary oxalate concentration by as much as 43%. Instruct patients with a history of kidney stones to avoid cranberry extract products or excessive consumption of cranberry juice.

Interactions
Bleomycin: (Moderate) Monitor for decreased efficacy of bleomycin during coadministration; discontinue ascorbic acid therapy if decreased efficacy is suspected. Coadministration of ascorbic acid and bleomycin may result in decreased efficacy of bleomycin.
Castor Oil: (Moderate) Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins may be decreased with coadministration of castor oil.
Cholestyramine: (Moderate) Cholestyramine can decrease the intestinal absorption of fat and fat-soluble vitamins. If used concurrently, administration of the two agents should be staggered for the longest time interval possible.
Colesevelam: (Moderate) It is not known if colesevelam can reduce the absorption of oral vitamin supplements including fat soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K. To minimize potential interactions, administer vitamins at least 4 hours before colesevelam.
Colestipol: (Moderate) Separate administration of fat-soluble vitamins by 1 hour before or 4 hours after a colestipol dose to limit effects on oral absorption. Because it sequesters bile acids, colestipol may interfere with normal fat absorption and thus may reduce absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.
Doxycycline: (Moderate) Monitor for decreased efficacy of doxycycline during coadministration; discontinue ascorbic acid therapy if decreased efficacy is suspected. Coadministration may result in decreased efficacy of doxycycline.
Erythromycin: (Moderate) Monitor for decreased efficacy of erythromycin during coadministration; discontinue ascorbic acid therapy if decreased efficacy is suspected. Coadministration may result in decreased efficacy of erythromycin.
fluPHENAZine: (Moderate) Monitor for decreased efficacy of fluphenazine during coadministration. Coadministration of ascorbic acid and fluphenazine may result in decreased plasma concentrations of fluphenazine due to acidification of the urine by ascorbic acid and increased excretion of fluphenazine.
Lincomycin: (Moderate) Monitor for decreased efficacy of lincomycin during coadministration; discontinue ascorbic acid therapy if decreased efficacy is suspected. Coadministration may result in decreased efficacy of lincomycin.
Mineral Oil: (Moderate) Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins is reported to be decreased with prolonged oral administration of mineral oil. However, despite warnings in various texts, there is little direct evidence that the interaction is of practical/clinical importance with limited use as directed. It may be prudent for those taking dietary supplements of Vitamin A, D, E, or K to separate administration by 1 hour before or 4 hours after a mineral oil oral dosage to help limit absorption interactions. Theoretically, the effect on fat-soluble vitamin absorption may more likely occur with prolonged or chronic administration of mineral oil.
Orlistat: (Moderate) Administer vitamin E at least 2 hours before or after the administration of orlistat to limit effects on oral absorption. Orlistat has been shown to inhibit the absorption of a vitamin E acetate supplement by 60%.
Selumetinib: (Moderate) Monitor vitamin E intake in patients receiving selumetinib capsules and taking vitamin E supplements. Vitamin E intake that exceeds safe limits may increase the risk for bleeding. Selumetinib capsules contain vitamin E. Vitamin E content varies by capsule: each 10 mg capsule contains 32 mg alpha-tocopherol, each 25 mg capsule contains 36 mg alpha-tocopherol. Tolerable Upper Intake Levels of vitamin E vary by age and range from 200 to 1,000 mg alpha-tocopherol per day.
Streptomycin: (Moderate) Monitor for decreased efficacy of streptomycin during coadministration; discontinue ascorbic acid therapy if decreased efficacy is suspected. Coadministration may result in decreased efficacy of streptomycin.
Tipranavir: (Major) Tipranavir should be used with caution in patients who may be at risk of increased bleeding, such as in patients taking high doses of vitamin E. In vitro, tipranavir was observed to inhibit human platelet aggregation at concentrations consistent with human exposure at regular doses. In rats, coadministration with vitamin E increased the bleeding effects of tipranavir.
Warfarin: (Moderate) Vitamin E should be used cautiously in patients receiving warfarin. While the mechanism is unclear, it is believed that concomitant administration of large doses of vitamin E (e.g., more than 400 units per day) with warfarin potentiates hypoprothrombinemia due to the vitamin K antagonistic activity of vitamin E.

    General Information
    Cranberry products are generally well tolerated. Consumption of cranberry extract dietary supplements has infrequently caused dyspepsia (general GI upset). Gastrointestinal complaints are among the more common side effects leading to discontinuation of use of cranberry products, though they are thought to be infrequent. Cranberry juice, especially if consumed in large amounts, can cause gastrointestinal upset, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea.

Description
  Text
  NOTE: In the US, nutraceuticals are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for nutraceuticals as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since nutraceuticals are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products. Monographs on nutraceuticals are included when reliable clinical data are available. The information presented below is condensed from the best clinical data we could find.
 
The American cranberry, Vaccinium macrocarpon Ait. is commonly marketed in the US as a sweetened and diluted beverage known as cranberry juice cocktail. The applicable part of the cranberry plant for medical use is the fruit. Rich in vitamin C, cranberry fruit was used by colony settlers to prevent and treat scurvy and as a poultice to promote the healing of skin wounds. Cranberries have long been used as a home remedy for the treatment or prevention of urinary tract infections (UTIs). Several products, containing cranberry extracts, are commercially available as dietary supplements. Some trials have reported that cranberry juice consumption may decrease the number of symptomatic UTIs, particularly for women with recurrent UTIs. The data for the use of cranberry juice in other populations is not extensive. Other preparations (such as extract powders) need to be quantified to ensure potency and purity of the 'active' ingredient, before being recommended for use. Rigorous systematic reviews have stated that the available evidence does not support the routine use of cranberry products over antibiotics for the prevention of UTI.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Cranberry does not treat active urinary tract infections. The cranberry may inhibit the attachment of E.coli bacteria to the epithelial cells that line the urinary tract. Adherence of E.coli bacteria to the urinary tract epithelial cells is mediated by bacterial fimbria, called Type 1 and P adhesins. Cranberry juice inhibits the hemagglutination activity of E.coli urinary isolates expressing these adhesin types. Two components of cranberry are proposed to mediate this inhibition: 1) fructose and 2) proanthocyanidins (condensed tannins). The mechanism(s) remain unproven clinically. Potency for dietary supplement products can vary substantially from manufacturer to manufacturer. Oral dosage forms are made of dried cranberry extract. Cranberry juice cocktail is approximately 30% pure cranberry juice, sweetened with fructose or artificial sweetener. Whether the inhibitory effect on E. coli adhesion is dependent on dosage form is not clear.
 
Increased fluid intake from juice consumption encourages the flushing of bacteria from the urinary tract and bladder. While cranberry juice slightly acidifies the urine, this action is not a major contributor to the urinary tract effects, although this action may make the urine less malodorous in the presence of incontinence or active infection. Urinary concentrations of cranberry are not sufficient to achieve antibacterial activity in vivo.

  Text
  Cranberry juice or extracts are administered orally. Specific pharmacokinetic data for cranberry extracts or the active components of cranberry juice are not available.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 enzymes:
Early data suggested that cranberry juice might inhibit CYP2C9 in vitro. However, research data suggest that cranberry juice does not significantly affect CYP2C9, as evidenced by a lack of effect on the plasma levels, metabolism, or elimination of flurbiprofen, a CYP2C9 substrate. S-warfarin is a CYP2C9 substrate, and some case reports have suggested a potential clinically significant interaction. However, other data show that consumption of 250 ml/day of cranberry juice for 7 days or up to 200 ml three times daily in patients on stable warfarin regimens does not significantly increase warfarin anticoagulant activity. Additional pharmacokinetic research demonstrates that cranberry juice does not significantly affect the pharmacokinetic profiles of tizanidine or midazolam, which are substrates for CYP1A2 and CYP3A4 respectively. The data suggest that cranberry juice ingestion does not significantly alter the activity of these enzymes. There are no specific data regarding dried cranberry extract concentrates and drug interactions.
