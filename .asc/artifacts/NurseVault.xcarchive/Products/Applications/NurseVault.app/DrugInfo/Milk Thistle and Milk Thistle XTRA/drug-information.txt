        140 mg (as silymarin) PO three times per day prior to meals. The majority of clinical trials have used this dosage regimen. In one study, 87 silymarin-treated patients showed higher survival rates (58%) after 2 years versus the survival rates of those patients taking placebo (39%), but the results failed to reach statistical significance. While there was a trend to lower prevalence of major hepatic complications (e.g., encephalopathy or variceal bleeding) in the silymarin group, the sample size was too small and the results did not reach statistical significance.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  The Food and Drug Administration has not evaluated the safety and effectiveness of milk thistle, Silybum marianum. Milk thistle is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Consumers should also be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.Milk thistle is a member of the Compositae family. Cross-reactivity may occur in those individuals allergic to other plants in the Asteraceae/Compositae family. Members include ragweed, chrysanthemums, marigolds, daisies, and many other herbs (e.g., Echinacea). Allergic reactions, though infrequent, may be severe. Patients with a history of atopy may have a greater likelihood of developing reactions to milk thistle.Hepatic disease is not a self-limiting condition and should not be self-diagnosed. The decision to self-treat symptoms of hepatic disease with milk thistle may prevent proper medical treatment. Patients are advised to see their health care professional for evaluation prior to the use of milk thistle, and should not replace milk thistle for any prescribed therapies unless advised to do so by their prescriber. Patients with jaundice should consult a health care professional immediately. Milk thistle, Silybum marianum, if used for the treatment of hepatic disease, is relatively contraindicated in patients with alcoholism. Those who continue to drink ethanol while receiving adjunctive milk thistle therapy for chronic hepatic disease may increase their risk of further hepatic injury and could potentially antagonize any potential therapeutic effect of this herb. Abstinence from ethanol is recommended.Milk thistle has not been evaluated for safety or efficacy in those patients with concurrent renal disease. Based on pharmacokinetic studies only a small portion of a milk thistle dose is excreted by the kidney, and dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not likely to be needed. However, caution is warranted in these patients due to a lack of supporting clinical data.The German Commission E has not specifically contraindicated the use of milk thistle during pregnancy. However, several manufacturers have not advised its use, particularly in the first trimester, due to a lack of safety data. Until more is known, women should avoid milk thistle during pregnancy. Milk thistle has been used for intrahepatic cholestasis of late pregnancy in a very limited number of patients; safety and efficacy are uncertain. The use of milk thistle should be limited to cases where the benefit to the mother would outweigh any potential risk to the fetus.It is unknown if milk thistle is excreted in breast milk. A decision should be made whether to discontinue the herb or to discontinue breast-feeding, taking into account the importance of breast-feeding the infant. Do not confuse milk thistle (Silybum marianum or Carduus marianum) with blessed thistle (Carduus benedictus), which is a different herb used as a galactagogue.Due to a lack of available clinical data, milk thistle should be used with caution in children and infants. Manufacturers have recommended against its use in children under 12 years old. The use of milk thistle should take place under the direction of a qualified health care professional and should be limited to where the benefit of milk thistle outweighs the potential risks of therapy.

Interactions
Deferiprone: (Major) Avoid the concomitant use of deferiprone and Milk Thistle (Silymarin). Deferiprone is a UDP glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) 1A6 substrate, and milk thistle may inhibit UGT1A6. The in vitro glucuronidation of deferiprone was reduced by 78% in the presence of another UGT1A6 inhibitor. Side effects, such as nausea, increased liver enzymes, or risk for neutropenia may be increased.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: Some milk thistle preparations are a combination of several herbs. Only adverse reactions pertaining to milk thistle are discussed in this monograph.Adverse reactions to herbal products are not always documented and most clinical trials have included limited numbers of patients. Diarrhea, which is described as mild and laxative-like, is the most commonly reported side effect of milk thistle in clinical trials. Side effects reported in one clinical trial included arthralgia, headache, pruritus and urticaria; some of these side effects occurred in placebo-receiving patients. Allergic reactions to milk thistle are rarely reported. Allergic reactions may occur in subjects with known allergies to other plants of the Asteraceae/Compositae family (e.g., sunflowers, daisies, and many herbs). Contact dermatitis and atopic reactions are also possible with topical exposure to milk thistle. In addition to controlled clinical trials, one case of urticaria related to milk thistle has been published in the Russian literature. Other types of rash (unspecified) would be expected to occur on occasion given the plant-family origins of this herb.The FDA special nutritional adverse event monitoring system has not reported any adverse events due to milk thistle, except when taken in combination with other herbs. The Adverse Drug Reactions Advisory Committee of Australia has published a report of a woman who experienced an adverse reaction to milk thistle that consisted of intermittent colic-like abdominal pain, dehydration, diaphoresis, fluid diarrhea, listlessness, nausea/vomiting, weakness, and "collapse". The patient felt well between these attacks. The time-course of her symptoms was associated with her administration times of milk thistle. The patient also experienced a similar event upon rechallenge with the milk thistle product. The Australian group has reported two other events; one adverse reaction involved similar symptoms to the case described. The other case involved a report of thrombocytopenia in an elderly male. The contribution of milk thistle product impurities to the development of these adverse events is unknown. The relationship of adverse events to the use of milk thistle cannot be firmly established at this time.

Description
  Text
  NOTE: In the US, nutraceuticals are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for nutraceuticals as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since nutraceuticals are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products. Monographs on nutraceuticals are included in CP when reliable clinical data are available. The information presented below is condensed from the best clinical data we could find.Description: Silybum marianum, also known as Carduus marianum or milk thistle, is an herb that yields a crude mixture of hepatoprotective principles designated as silymarin. While silymarin is found in the entire plant, but the highest concentration (4—6%) is found within the ripe seeds (fruit). Silymarin consists of several flavonoligans: silibinin, silydianin, silychristin, and others. Silibinin (also known as silybin) is the most pharmacologically active flavonoligan of silymarin, and the measured content of silibinin is used to standardize milk thistle products. In addition to flavonoligands, the milk thistle fruits also express betaine and linoleic acid.Historically, milk thistle has been used for over 2000 years for various disorders of the liver, kidney, and spleen. Silibinin, the active flavonoligand, has been studied as a conservative treatment for alcoholic liver disease, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, and various types of viral hepatitis. Milk thistle shows promise as an adjunct treatment for drug- or toxin-induced hepatic dysfunction. Silibinin is commonly administered for Amanita phalloides (i.e., 'death-cap') mushroom poisonings in Europe, where an intravenous product (i.e., Legalon(R) SIL, Madaus and Co.) is available. Animal models suggest that silibinin may be protective against drug-induced hepatic damage from acetaminophen, halothane, methotrexate, or phenothiazines. Milk thistle is also being investigated for other therapeutic uses. Cytoprotective actions in tissues other than the liver have been noted and may indicate future use for chemoprevention of cancer or prophylaxis against certain nephrotoxins. Milk thistle may also be tested as a cholesterol-lowering agent. In 1969, the first standardized oral milk thistle extract, Legalon(R), became available in Germany, followed by the release of Legalon(R) SIL for the treatment of Amanita mushroom poisonings in 1984. In 1986, Germany's Commission E approved the use of milk thistle fruit extracts, standardized to 70% silymarin, for non-prescription supportive treatment of cirrhosis and chronic inflammation of the liver. Surveys suggest that roughly 30% of patients in the US with chronic hepatic diseases consume milk thistle in addition to their regularly prescribed medications. Results of clinical studies must be reviewed with caution due to the heterogeneity of hepatic diseases and the presence of other uncontrolled parameters. For example, the liver has a certain degree of intrinsic regenerative capacity, which cannot be quantified. Well-designed trials for specific hepatic diagnoses will be necessary to prove milk thistle's effectiveness.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: Silymarin activity is usually based on the study of the flavonoligand, silibinin, which exhibits a variety of pharmacologic actions at the cellular level.•Hepatoprotective actions: Silymarin has a variety of hepatoprotective effects. First, silymarin can protect normal, undamaged hepatocytes by altering the outer hepatocyte cell membrane to prevent intracellular entry of toxins. Second, silymarin increases protein synthesis via stimulation of RNA and DNA polymerase A, which promotes hepatocyte regeneration. Silibinin inhibits Kuppfer cell activity and free radical formation and also inhibits the 5-lipoxygenase pathway, which results in decreased leukotriene synthesis. Other anti-inflammatory effects include mast cell inhibition and decreased neutrophil migration. Based on animal studies, silibinin also appears to inhibit the conversion of hepatic stellate cells into myofibroblasts, thereby slowing fibrotic changes within the liver. Finally, silibinin is a free-radical scavenger and an antioxidant, preventing lipid peroxidation. The effects of milk thistle appear to be selective for healthy liver cells; the growth of hepatic carcinoma cells is not stimulated by silymarin. Silibinin does not have any antiviral activity against the hepatitis A virus, hepatitis B virus, or hepatitis C virus.Silibinin may inhibit the hepatic cytochrome P450 microsomal enzyme system, which reduces phase I detoxification reactions and enhances glucuronidation. Silibinin may also protect glutathione stores. These actions may explain some of the hepatoprotective effects of silymarin against drug or toxins; bioactivation by the CYP450 enzymes is necessary for some toxins to produce hepatotoxic intermediates. Also, silibinin may inhibit beta-glucuronidase, which may limit the enterohepatic recycling of some medications, including hormones like estrogens. This may explain silibinin's potential activity in treating cholestasis caused by estrogen excess.•Antilipemic actions: Silymarin inhibits the peroxidation of lipids within the hepatic cell. Silibinin may decrease the synthesis of cholesterol by the liver. Clinically, milk thistle has been shown to slightly reduce HDL fractions and total cholesterol in a small number of patients. More study is needed to delineate the mechanism of antilipemic activity.•Renal-protective actions: Preliminary animal investigations indicate that silibinin may offer cytoprotection against cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity. The effects of cisplatin on GFR and proteinuria were prevented by pretreatment with silibinin in one animal study. However, silibinin may not be protective against all nephrotoxins. Silibinin had no effect on cyclosporine-induced renal dysfunction in a different animal study. The different mechanisms by which the nephrotoxic drugs induce renal impairment may influence whether silibinin will be protective; the exact mechanism of protection has not been elucidated.•Cytoprotective actions: Silymarin suppresses tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-activation of certain nuclear transcription factors and induces various cyclin-dependent protein kinase inhibitors. Via these actions silymarin inhibits cellular inflammation and may inhibit carcinogenesis. Silymarin's anticarcinogenic activity has been noted in non-melanoma skin, breast, and prostate carcinoma cells in vitro. Silymarin appears to arrest the G1 cell-cycle phase of these tumors.

  Text
  Milk thistle is administered orally in extracts made from the seeds (fruits). Contents of available products are usually standardized to 70% or more of silymarin, calculated as silibinin, based on German Commission E standards. Due to a lack of data, extracts of milk thistle leaves or non-seed components are not considered bioequivalent to milk thistle seed extracts. Silymarin is poorly soluble in water; teas or infusions made from the herb contain < 10% (w/v) active ingredient and are only considered useful for dyspeptic complaints. Silibinin has been administered intravenously, but pharmaceutical-grade intravenous formulation (Legalon SIL) is only available outside of the US.
 
The pharmacokinetics of milk thistle are fairly well described. Distribution following entry into the bloodstream is not certain, but high concentrations are found in human hepatocytes. Studies in mice indicate silibinin distributes to the liver, lung, pancreas, prostate, skin, stomach and small bowel. Based on current data, roughly 3% to 8% of an oral dose is excreted unchanged into the urine as silibinin, and 20% to 40% is recovered from the bile as glucuronide or sulfate conjugates. Bile concentrations peak within 2 to 9 hours after an oral dose; the half-life of silibinin is 6 to 8 hours based on 24 hour excretion studies. Enterohepatic recycling may occur. A silibinin-phosphatidylcholine complex (i.e., Silibide or IdB 1016) increases biliary concentrations of silibinin from 3% to 11% as a result of improved oral absorption.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: none
Preliminary in vivo data indicate that milk thistle is unlikely to inhibit or induce the CYP 450 isozymes CYP1A2, CYP2D6, CYP2E1, or CYP3A4.
