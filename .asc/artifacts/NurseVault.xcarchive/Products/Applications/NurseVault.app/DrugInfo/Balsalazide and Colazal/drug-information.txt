Drug Class: Balsalazide is a diazo-bonded 5-aminosalicylate and is a prodrug that releases the antiinflammatory agent 5-ASA (also known as mesalamine).

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 6.75 grams/day PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): cholestasis, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatic failure, hepatic necrosis, hepatitis, jaundice, alopecia, anaphylactoid reactions, bronchospasm, eosinophilia, eosinophilic pneumonia, myocarditis, pancreatitis, pericarditis, pleural effusion, pruritus, rash, vasculitis, hematuria, increased urinary frequency, interstitial nephritis, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, nephrotic syndrome, nephrotoxicity, proteinuria, renal failure (unspecified), urine discoloration

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Balsalazide is contraindicated in patients with a history of severe salicylate hypersensitivity. Patients sensitive to sulfasalazine, mesalamine (5-ASA), or olsalazine also may demonstrate cross-sensitivity; therefore, do not use in patients with a history of 5-aminosalicylates hypersensitivity.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Balsalazide is contraindicated in patients with a history of severe salicylate hypersensitivity. Patients sensitive to sulfasalazine, mesalamine (5-ASA), or olsalazine also may demonstrate cross-sensitivity; therefore, do not use in patients with a history of 5-aminosalicylates hypersensitivity.
 
Balsalazide therapy has been associated with exacerbations of symptoms of ulcerative colitis. In adult clinical studies, 3 of 259 patients (i.e., roughly 1%) reported worsening of symptoms of ulcerative colitis. In pediatric studies, 4 of 68 patients (i.e., roughly 6%) reported excerebration of symptoms. Patients should be closely observed for worsening symptoms of disease during treatment.
 
Balsalazide is classified in FDA pregnancy-risk category B. Animal studies have revealed no evidence of harmful fetal effects. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women; it is not known if balsalazide crosses the human placenta. However, following administration of balsalazide only a small portion of a dose is absorbed systemically and then is rapidly excreted in the urine. The primary active ingredient produced my balsalazide metabolism, mesalamine, has not been associated with fetal harm. Therefore, use of balsalazide during pregnancy appears to pose no significant harm to the developing fetus and the maternal benefits of therapy appear to outweigh any potential for risk to the fetus.
 
It is unknown whether balsalazide is excreted into breast milk. However, following administration only a small portion of a dose is absorbed systemically and then is rapidly excreted in the urine. Mesalamine, the primary active ingredient produced my balsalazide metabolism, has been deemed safe for use during breast-feeding. However, diarrhea (a possible allergic reaction) has been observed in a nursing infant whose mother was on rectal mesalamine therapy. Therefore, a nursing infant of a mother undergoing balsalazide therapy should be carefully monitored for alterations in bowel function.
 
Safe and effective use of balsalazide in children under the age of 5 has not been determined. However, balsalazide has been shown to be safe and effective, and is approved for use, in adolescents and children older than 5 years as evidenced by a multicenter study in which 68 patients received either 6.75 grams or 2.25 grams of balsalazide per day for a total of 8 weeks. In this study, the side effect profile associated with balsalazide treatment was similar to that seen in adult patients. The most common adverse events reported were headache and GI symptoms, such as abdominal pain, vomiting, and diarrhea.
 
Patients with pyloric stenosis can have prolonged gastric retention of balsalazide capsules, which could delay release in the colon.
 
There have been reports of renal impairment in patients taking balsalazide; however, specific details regarding the reports are unavailable. Renal toxicity has been observed in animals and in humans taking other mesalamine products, and balsalazide is a prodrug for mesalamine, 5-ASA. Use with caution in patients with known renal impairment or renal failure or a history of renal disease.
 
Use balsalazide cautiously in patients with preexisting hepatic disease. Products that are metabolized to (or contain) mesalamine (5-ASA) have been reported in post-market use to cause rare cases of hepatitis, hyperbilirubinemia, jaundice, cholestatic jaundice, cirrhosis, and possible hepatocellular damage including hepatic necrosis and hepatic failure. The incidence of hepatic adverse events is not known. In some cases, the reactions were fatal. One case of Kawasaki-syndrome, which included hepatic changes, was also reported.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Balsalazide is contraindicated in patients with salicylate hypersensitivity. Patients sensitive to sulfasalazine, mesalamine, or olsalazine also may demonstrate cross-sensitivity; therefore, do not use in patients with a history of 5-aminosalicylates hypersensitivity. As with sulfasalazine, balsalazide-induced hypersensitivity reactions may present as internal organ involvement, including myocarditis, pericarditis, nephritis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, and hematologic abnormalities.
    Headline
    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use balsalazide with caution in people with hepatic failure or impairment. Evaluate the risks and benefits of using balsalazide in these individuals since hepatic failure has been reported in people with pre-existing hepatic impairment who have received balsalazide or other medications that release mesalamine into the gastrointestinal tract.
    Headline
    renal disease
    General Information
    Consider the risks and benefits of balsalazide therapy in people with known renal impairment or renal failure, a history of renal disease, or taking concomitant nephrotoxic drugs. Evaluate renal function in all individuals prior to initiating balsalazide and periodically during treatment. Balsalazide is known to be substantially excreted by the kidney. Renal impairment, including minimal change disease, acute and chronic interstitial nephritis, and renal failure, has been reported in balsalazide recipients and recipients of other products that release mesalamine into the gastrointestinal tract. Discontinue balsalazide if renal function deteriorates while on therapy.
    Headline
    gastric outlet obstruction
    General Information
    Avoid the use of balsalazide capsules in people with gastric outlet obstruction (e.g., pyloric stenosis or any other functional or organic upper GI obstruction). Individuals with these conditions can have prolonged gastric retention of these capsules, which could delay the release of mesalamine in the colon.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Closely monitor complete blood cell counts and platelet counts in geriatric adults during treatment with balsalazide. Reports from uncontrolled clinical studies and postmarketing reporting systems suggest a higher incidence of blood dyscrasias (i.e., agranulocytosis, neutropenia, pancytopenia) in geriatric subjects (65 years and older) receiving 5-ASA (mesalamine) products such as balsalazide vs. younger adult patients.
    Headline
    skin photosensitivity disorder
    General Information
    Use balsalazide with caution in people with a previous history of a skin photosensitivity disorder. Mesalamine, the active metabolite of balsalazide, has been reported to cause photosensitivity. People with pre-existing skin conditions, such as atopic dermatitis and atopic eczema, have reported more severe photosensitivity reactions. Advise individuals to avoid sun exposure, wear protective clothing, and use a broad-spectrum sunscreen when outdoors.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There is no evidence that balsalazide is associated with poor pregnancy outcomes; experts recommend that patients maintained on balsalazide prior to pregnancy continue their treatment regimen. The use of balsalazide during pregnancy appears to pose no significant harm to the developing fetus, and the maternal benefits of therapy appear to outweigh any potential for risk to the fetus. Data suggest increased ulcerative colitis disease activity is associated with a risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm delivery, low birth weight, and small for gestational age at birth. Mesalamine, the active metabolite of balsalazide, crosses the placental barrier. However, following administration of balsalazide, only a small portion of the dose is absorbed systemically, followed by rapid excretion in the urine. Published data from meta-analyses, case series, and cohort studies on the use of mesalamine, the active metabolite of balsalazide, during pregnancy have not reliably informed an association with mesalamine and major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. In animal studies, there were no adverse developmental effects observed after oral administration of balsalazide to rats and rabbits during organogenesis at doses up to 2.4 and 4.7 times, respectively, the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD).
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Balsalazide is compatible with breast-feeding. Mesalamine, the active metabolite of balsalazide, is present in human milk in small amounts, with a relative infant dose of 0.1% or less. After oral administration, only a small portion of a balsalazide dose is absorbed systemically, followed by rapid excretion in the urine. Diarrhea in the breastfed child has been infrequently reported with the use of mesalamine during breast-feeding. Carefully monitor the breast-fed child for alterations in bowel function, such as persistent changes in stool frequency. There are no data on the effect of balsalazide on milk production. Among the 5-ASA agents, mesalamine, balsalazide, and olsalazine are preferred over sulfasalazine due to the unknown side effects of sulfasalazine's sulfapyridine metabolite, which is excreted into milk at higher concentrations than the parent drug and has hemolytic and antimicrobial properties.

Interactions
amLODIPine; Celecoxib: (Moderate) Monitor patients for signs of worsening renal function during coadministration of balsalazide and celecoxib. Coadministration may increase the risk for drug-induced nephrotoxicity. Balsalazide is converted to mesalamine in the gastrointestinal tract; nephrotoxicity has been observed during mesalamine treatment.
azaTHIOprine: (Moderate) 5-aminosalicylates (e.g., sulfasalazine, mesalamine, olsalazine) may interact with azathioprine and increase the risk of azathioprine-related toxicity. The inhibition of thiopurine methyltransferase activity, one of the enzymes responsible for azathioprine metabolism, by 5-aminosalicylates has been described via in vitro and in vivo study. Theoretically, this interaction could result in a higher risk of bone marrow suppression or other azathioprine dose-related side effects. If concurrent therapy cannot be avoided, closely monitor platelet and complete blood cell counts.
Celecoxib: (Moderate) Monitor patients for signs of worsening renal function during coadministration of balsalazide and celecoxib. Coadministration may increase the risk for drug-induced nephrotoxicity. Balsalazide is converted to mesalamine in the gastrointestinal tract; nephrotoxicity has been observed during mesalamine treatment.
Celecoxib; Tramadol: (Moderate) Monitor patients for signs of worsening renal function during coadministration of balsalazide and celecoxib. Coadministration may increase the risk for drug-induced nephrotoxicity. Dalteparin: (Moderate) Coadministration of 5-aminosalicylates and low molecular weight heparins may result in an increased risk of bleeding (i.e., hematomas) following neuraxial anesthesia. Discontinue 5-aminosalicylates prior to the initiation of a low molecular weight heparins. If this is not possible, it is recommended to monitor patients closely for bleeding.
Enoxaparin: (Moderate) Coadministration of 5-aminosalicylates and low molecular weight heparins may result in an increased risk of bleeding (i.e., hematomas) following neuraxial anesthesia. Discontinue 5-aminosalicylates prior to the initiation of a low molecular weight heparins. If this is not possible, it is recommended to monitor patients closely for bleeding.
Heparin: (Moderate) Coadministration of 5-aminosalicylates and heparin may result in an increased risk of bleeding (i.e., hematomas) following neuraxial anesthesia. Discontinue 5-aminosalicylates prior to the initiation of heparin. If this is not possible, it is recommended to monitor patients closely for bleeding.
Low Molecular Weight Heparins: (Moderate) Coadministration of 5-aminosalicylates and low molecular weight heparins may result in an increased risk of bleeding (i.e., hematomas) following neuraxial anesthesia. Discontinue 5-aminosalicylates prior to the initiation of a low molecular weight heparins. If this is not possible, it is recommended to monitor patients closely for bleeding.
Mercaptopurine, 6-MP: (Moderate) Increased myelosuppression may occur if mercaptopurine is coadministered with balsalazide. If concomitant use is necessary, use the lowest possible doses of each drug and closely monitor the patient for bone marrow suppression. 5-Aminosalicylates, such as balsalazide, have been shown to inhibit the thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) enzyme in vitro. Mercaptopurine is inactivated via the TPMT enzyme.
Thioguanine, 6-TG: (Moderate) Use these drugs together with caution; concomitant use may result in reduced metabolism of thioguanine via TPMT and an increased risk for thioguanine-induced toxicity. Monitor patients for signs and symptoms of hematologic and hepatic toxicity. There is in vitro evidence that 5-aminosalicylate derivatives inhibit thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT), the enzyme that metabolizes thioguanine. Increased thioguanine concentrations can lead to an increased risk for severe thioguanine-induced myelosuppression. In cases of bone marrow suppression, a dose reduction of thioguanine may be necessary.
Warfarin: (Moderate) Increased prothrombin time in patients taking concomitant 5-aminosalicylates and warfarin has been reported. Closely monitor patients PT and INR during and following concomitant balsalazide therapy; dosage adjustments of anticoagulants may be necessary.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Four controlled clinical trials were used to assess adverse reactions to balsalazide. Greater than 1000 patients received balsalazide in these trials. The most frequently reported (>= 3%) adverse reactions at the usual daily dose were headache (8%), abdominal pain (6%), diarrhea (5%), nausea/vomiting (5%/4%), respiratory infection (4%), and arthralgia (4%). Withdrawal from therapy due to ADRs was comparable with placebo. Other adverse events reported by at least 1% of patients but < 3% include anorexia, back pain, constipation, cough, cramps, dizziness, dyspepsia, fatigue, fever, flatulence, flu-like symptoms, frequent stools, insomnia, myalgia, pain (unspecified), pharyngitis, rectal bleeding, rhinitis, sinusitis, urinary tract infection, and xerostomia.

The incidence of hepatic adverse events is not known. In some cases, the reactions were fatal. One case of Kawasaki-syndrome, which included hepatic changes, was also reported.

    General Information
    5-ASA (mesalamine) is known to be substantially excreted by the kidney, and the risk of adverse reactions to balsalazide, which is converted to mesalamine, may be greater in patients with impaired renal function. Albuminuria (proteinuria), increased urinary frequency, and hematuria were reported infrequently (i.e., less than 1%) during ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease trials with 5-ASA (mesalamine) products. In clinical practice, reports of renal failure (unspecified), nephropathy such as minimal change syndrome and nephrotic syndrome, and acute or chronic interstitial nephritis have been associated with products that contain (or are metabolized to) mesalamine. In addition, cases of nephrogenic diabetes insipidus have been reported during postmarketing experience with mesalamine. There are no particular signals for nephropathy; evaluate renal function in all patients prior to initiation and periodically while on mesalamine therapy. Monitor patients with known renal impairment or history of renal disease or taking nephrotoxic drugs for decreased renal function and mesalamine-related adverse reactions. The risk of nephrotoxicity may be greater in patients with pre-existing renal impairment or other risk factors for decreased renal function. Patients should be made aware that urine may become discolored reddish-brown while taking 5-ASA (mesalamine) when it comes in contact with surfaces or water treated with hypochlorite-containing bleach. If urine discoloration is observed, advise patients to observe their urine flow and report to the healthcare provider only if urine is discolored on leaving the body, before contact with any surface or water (e.g., in the toilet).

    General Information
    The use of balsalazide may lead to laboratory test interference. Spuriously elevated test results may occur when measuring urinary normetanephrine by liquid chromatography with electrochemical detection, because of the similarity in the chromatograms of normetanephrine and mesalamine's main metabolite, Nacetylaminosalicylic acid (N-Ac-5-ASA). Consider an alternative, selective assay for normetanephrine.

Description
  Text
  Balsalazide is a diazo-bonded 5-aminosalicylate and is a prodrug that releases the antiinflammatory agent 5-ASA (also known as mesalamine). Oral balsalazide is indicated for the treatment of mildly to moderately active ulcerative colitis (UC). Although there is little evidence to suggest differences in efficacy between the diazo-bonded 5- ASA preparations, balsalazide is often preferred over olsalazine in mild-to-moderate UC due to its better tolerability. Guidelines support the use of standard-dose mesalamine (2 to 3 g/day) or diazo-bonded 5-ASAs (balsalazide or olsalazine) for induction and maintenance of remission in adults with extensive mild-to-moderate UC. In adults with left-sided UC, combination therapy with oral and rectal 5-ASA is preferred over oral therapy alone, and adults with moderate disease activity or an inadequate response to standard-dose therapy may benefit from high-dose oral mesalamine (more than 3 g/day) combined with rectal mesalamine. Rectal mesalamine suppositories (1 g/day) are often recommended for maintenance of remission and are preferred over oral 5-ASA therapy. For individuals whose disease is moderate-to-severe or who fail conventional therapy, current guidelines support timely escalation to advanced therapies rather than repeated switching between the various 5-ASA formulations. Balsalazide is also effective for pediatric UC in subjects 5 to 17 years of age; the remission rates in pediatric clinical trials were 3/35 (9%) with lower-dose versus 4/33 (12%) with higher-dose balsalazide, respectively. However, preferred 5-ASA agents for the pediatric population include mesalamine (5-ASA) or sulfasalazine, given the strong evidence of efficacy of 5-ASA for both induction and maintenance of remission in mild-moderate UC in pediatric subjects and the availability of sulfasalazine in dosage forms amenable for use in younger children. Balsalazide was FDA-approved in 2000.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Balsalazide remains intact until it reaches the colon where bacterial action within the colon reduces balsalazide to the active component mesalamine (5-ASA). The exact mechanism of action of mesalamine is unknown. It is believed, however, that mesalamine blocks production of arachidonic acid metabolites in the colon. This action appears to be topical rather than systemic in nature.

    Balsalazide capsules contain acid-insoluble balsalazide disodium granules. The effect of food on the absorption of balsalazide has been studied; data indicate that both Cmax and AUC are lower, while Tmax is markedly prolonged, when balsalazide is administered along side a high-fat meal as compared to fasting.     Description
    In studies of pediatric patients with mild-to-moderate active ulcerative colitis receiving 6.75 grams/day, steady-state was reached within 2 weeks, similar to what is observed in adult patients. Likewise, the pharmacokinetics of balsalazide, 5-ASA, and N-Ac-5-ASA were characterized by very large interpatient variability, which is also similar to that seen in adult patients. The pro-drug moiety, balsalazide, appeared to exhibit dose-independent (i.e., dose-linear) kinetics in children, and the systemic exposure parameters (Cmax and AUC) increased in an almost dose-proportional fashion after the 6.75 grams/day versus the 2.25 grams/day doses. However, the absolute magnitude of these exposure parameters was greater relative to adults. The Cmax and AUC observed in pediatric patients were 26% and 102% greater than those observed in adult patients at the 6.75 grams/day dosage level. In contrast, the systemic exposure parameters for the active metabolites, 5-ASA and N-Ac-5-ASA, in pediatric patients increased in a less than dose-proportional manner after the 6.75 grams/day dose versus the 2.25 grams/day dose. Additionally, the magnitude of these exposure parameters was decreased for both metabolites relative to adults. For the metabolite of key safety concern from a systemic exposure perspective, 5-ASA, the Cmax and AUC observed in pediatric patients were 67% and 64% lower than those observed in adult patients at the 6.75 grams/day dosage level. Likewise, for N-Ac-5-ASA, the Cmax and AUC observed in pediatric patients were 68% and 55% lower than those observed in adult patients at the 6.75 grams/day dosage level.

  Text
  Balsalazide is administered orally. Systemic absorption is very low and variable in healthy subjects. However, the primary action of the drug is local, not systemic. Once in the colon, bacterial azoreductases cleave the compound to release 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA), the therapeutically active portion, and 4-aminobenzoyl-beta-alanine (4-ABA). The compounds 5-ASA and 4-ABA are further metabolized to N-acetylated metabolites (N-Ac-5-ASA and N-Ac-4-ABA). Balsalazide is 99% or more bound to human plasma proteins; 5-ASA and N-Ac-5-ASA are 43% and 78% bound, respectively. Balsalazide and its metabolites have been identified in plasma, urine, and feces. The major route of elimination is via the feces; approximately 65% of a single dose (2.25 grams/dose) has been recovered as metabolites in healthy subjects. Less than 1% of an oral dose has been recovered from the urine as parent compound. The elimination half-life cannot be determined because of large intersubject variability.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  No specific dosage adjustments are recommended for individuals with hepatic impairment; use with caution. Evaluate the risks and benefits of using balsalazide in these individuals since hepatic failure has been reported in people with pre-existing hepatic impairment who have received balsalazide or other medications that release mesalamine into the gastrointestinal tract.
  Renal Impairment
  Balsalazide is converted to mesalamine, which is substantially excreted by the kidney and the risk of adverse events may be greater in people with impaired renal function. Evaluate renal function before balsalazide initiation and periodically while on therapy. Monitor individuals with known renal impairment for drug-related adverse reactions. Discontinue balsalazide if renal function deteriorates while on therapy.
