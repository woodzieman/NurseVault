Drug Class: Rotigotine hydrochloride is a transdermal non-ergoline dopamine agonist used for the treatment of Parkinson's disease (PD) and restless leg syndrome (RLS).

Routes: topical; Common adult doses: 8 mg/24 hours transdermally for PD; 3 mg/24 hr transdermally for RLS.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): edema, erythema, hyperhidrosis, maculopapular rash, pruritus, hypertension, orthostatic hypotension, sinus tachycardia, syncope, abnormal dreams, agitation, confusion, delirium, depression, hallucinations, insomnia, nightmares, paranoia, psychosis, weight loss, dizziness, dysesthesia, fatigue, headache, lethargy, paresthesias, tremor, vertigo, antecollis, arthralgia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  NOTE: Rotigotine transdermal system (Neupro) was recalled in April 2008 due to the formation of rotigotine crystals in the patches that may have decreased efficacy of the product. Health care professionals were instructed to titrate patients off of Neupro per the guidelines in the prescribing information (see Dosage). Subsequently, marketing of the product was discontinued.
 
When possible, avoid abrupt discontinuation of the patch due to the potential for a syndrome resembling neuroleptic malignant syndrome or akinetic crises.
 
Rotigotine is contraindicated in patients with a rotigotine hypersensitivity or hypersensitivity to any inactive ingredients found in the transdermal patch formulation (i.e., ascorbyl palmitate, povidone, silicone adhesive, sodium metabisulfite, and dl-alpha-tocopherol). Anaphylactic reactions may develop to sodium metabisulfite in susceptible individuals; sulfite hypersensitivity is seen more frequently in those with asthma than nonasthmatic patients.
 
Rotigotine commonly causes drowsiness or somnolence. There have been reports of patients who have fallen asleep while performing activities of daily living, including driving (see Adverse Reactions). Excessive drowsiness has, in some cases, occurred as late as one year after the initiation of treatment. In some cases excessive drowsiness due to rotigotine has resulted in auto accidents or other harmful events in the course of daily living. Symptoms of excessive drowsiness may not be preceded by warning signs. Patients should be cautioned against driving or operating machinery, working at heights, or performing other tasks that require alertness while receiving rotigotine. Those who have experienced somnolence or a sudden episode of sleep while taking the drug should avoid these activities. Reassessment for oversedation is suggested throughout rotigotine therapy. The use of concomitant CNS depressant medications or sleep disorders may increase the risk of falling asleep while taking this medication; patients should be assessed for these risk factors prior to initiation of the drug and be advised of the additive risks for somnolence. Rotigotine should generally be discontinued in those experiencing episodes of falling asleep while engaged in activities of daily living. It is not known if a reduction in dosage will subsequently reduce or eliminate excessive somnolence or sudden episodes of sleep.
 
The backing layer of rotigotine transdermal patches contains aluminum; remove patches prior to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or defibrillation (cardioversion) to avoid skin burns. 
 
Rotigotine should be used cautiously in those with cardiac disease. Rotigotine increases heart rate an average of 2—4 bpm compared to placebo. Tachycardia (heart rate greater than 100 bpm) has occurred in 9% of those receiving rotigotine compared to 7% receiving placebo. Dopamine agonists may cause syncope. Although the incidence of syncope with rotigotine was similar to placebo in controlled trials, those with clinically relevant cardiovascular disease were excluded from evaluation. Dopamine agonists, including rotigotine, can cause orthostatic hypotension. This dose-related effect may be particularly evident during dosage titration. In addition, some patients with Parkinson's Disease may have a reduced capacity to respond to orthostasis. During clinical trials, orthostatic hypotension (systolic decrease of at least 20 mmHg at 3 minutes after rising) was reported in 5% of those receiving the 6 mg/24 hour transdermal patch compared to 4% of those receiving placebo. Therefore, patients should be monitored closely for symptoms of orthostatic hypotension; careful titration may minimize the potential for this adverse effect. Conversely, patients receiving rotigotine may have dose-related elevations in systolic and diastolic blood pressures. Systolic readings greater than 180 mmHg were reported in 4% of those receiving rotigotine versus 2% for placebo; diastolic readings greater than 105 mmHg occurred in 9% of rotigotine-treated patients versus 5% for placebo. Rotigotine should be used cautiously in those with pre-existing hypertension.
 
Weight gain of more than 10% of baseline weight occurred in 3% of patients during clinical trials with rotigotine. Weight gain was frequently associated with peripheral edema, suggesting that the drug should be used cautiously in patients with any condition pre-disposing to fluid retention such as congestive heart failure.
 
Although there are no differences in plasma concentrations of rotigotine in patients 65—80 years of age compared to younger adults, exposure of rotigotine in geriatric patients older than 80 years may be higher due to age-related skin changes (e.g., atrophy). It is advisable to carefully monitor elderly patients receiving rotigotine.
 
Hallucinations were reported in clinical trials for Parkinson's Disease in 2% of rotigotine-treated patients compared to 0.7% of those receiving placebo. Discontinuation of treatment was required in a small percentage of patients receiving the drug (0.2%). Caution is advised when using rotigotine in patients with psychosis, including schizophrenia.
 
No changes in rotigotine pharmacokinetics were observed in subjects with mild to severe impairment, including those on dialysis. In subjects with severe renal impairment not on dialysis (creatinine clearance 15—29 ml/min), exposure to rotigotine conjugates was doubled; however, no dosage adjustments are recommended. Weight gain of more than 10% of baseline weight occurred in 3% of patients during clinical trials with rotigotine. Weight gain was frequently associated with peripheral edema, suggesting that the drug should be used cautiously in patients with any condition pre-disposing to fluid retention such as severe renal insufficiency. In general, rotigotine should be used with caution in patients with renal impairment until further information becomes available.
 
Rotigotine is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. There are no studies of rotigotine in human pregnancy. In animal studies, disrupted implantation occurred at all evaluated doses. These findings were thought to be due to the prolactin lowering effects of rotigotine, since prolactin is necessary for implantation and early maintenance of pregnancy in rats, but not in humans. Carcinogenicity studies in animals suggest that rotigotine does not exhibit carcinogenic potential when used in doses up to 12 times the maximum recommended human dose of 6 mg/24 hours. Some animal data suggest mutagenic and clastogenic potential. Rotigotine should be used in pregnancy only if the potential benefits outweigh the potential risks to the fetus.
 
Breast-feeding is not recommended during therapy with rotigotine. It is not known if rotigotine is excreted in human breast milk. However, rotigotine and/or its metabolites are excreted into the milk of lactating rats. Postnatal growth was inhibited in the offspring of rats treated with rotigotine during lactation. Rotigotine decreases prolactin secretion in humans and may inhibit lactation. Because of the potential for serious adverse effects in nursing infants, a decision should be made to discontinue nursing or discontinue rotigotine in breast-feeding women.
 
Safe and effective use of rotigotine has not been established in children.
 
The effects of applying heat to the transdermal system are unknown. However, based on data from other transdermal products indicating that absorption is increased, patients should be advised to avoid exposing the patch to sources of direct heat such as a heating pad, electric blanket, heat lamp, sauna, hot tub, heated water bed, and prolonged direct sunlight.
 
Patients with Parkinson's Disease have an approximate 6-fold higher risk of developing melanoma than the general population. The contribution of antiparkinson's medications, if any, is unknown. Until further data becomes available, the manufacturer of rotigotine recommends that patients receiving rotigotine for any indication be regularly monitored for melanoma.
 
Rare cases of retroperitoneal fibrosis, pulmonary infiltrates, pleural effusion, and pleural thickening have been reported in patients treated with ergot-derived dopaminergic agents (e.g., bromocriptine, pergolide). Although these adverse events are believed to be related to the ergonine structure, whether non-ergot derived dopamine agonists such as rotigotine can cause them is unknown.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Caution is advised in people with a sulfite allergy. The rotigotine transdermal patch contains sodium metabisulfite, which may cause allergic-type reactions including anaphylactic reactions and life-threatening or less severe asthmatic episodes in susceptible individuals; sulfite hypersensitivity is seen more frequently in those with asthma than nonasthmatic patients.
    Headline
    defibrillation (cardioversion), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
    General Information
    Because rotigotine transdermal systems (e.g., Neupro) contain aluminum or other metal components, patients should be instructed to remove the patch before undergoing defibrillation (cardioversion) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Metal components contained in the backing of some transdermal systems can overheat during these procedures and cause skin burns in the area where the patch is adhered.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery, sleep disorder
    General Information
    Rotigotine has the potential to cause drowsiness or somnolence. Prior to initiating treatment, advise patients of the potential for drowsiness while taking rotigotine and inquire about factors that may increase the risk for somnolence, such as a sleep disorder (e.g., narcolepsy, sleep apnea), alcohol use, or coadministration with other CNS depressants. Patients with Parkinson disease or restless leg syndrome taking dopaminergic agents, including rotigotine, have reported sudden sleep onset while engaged in activities of daily living, such as driving. In some cases, auto accidents or other harmful events have occurred. Some patients do not perceive warning signs, such as excessive drowsiness, and believe they are alert immediately prior to the event; therefore, patients should use caution when driving or operating machinery or performing other activities requiring coordination and concentration. Reassessment for somnolence is necessary throughout therapy. Patients may not acknowledge drowsiness or sleepiness until directly questioned about drowsiness or sleepiness during specific activities. Consider discontinuing rotigotine in patients that develop significant daytime sleepiness or episodes of falling asleep during activities that require active participation (e.g., conversations, eating, etc.). If a decision is made to continue the drug under these circumstances, patients should be advised to avoid driving or other potentially dangerous activities.
    Headline
    psychotic disorder
    General Information
    In general, people with a major psychotic disorder should not be treated with rotigotine because of the risk of exacerbating psychosis. In addition, certain medications used to treat psychosis may exacerbate the symptoms of Parkinson disease and may decrease the effectiveness of rotigotine. Postmarketing reports indicate that patients may experience new or worsening mental status and behavioral changes, which may be severe, including psychotic-like behavior during rotigotine treatment or after starting or increasing the dose of rotigotine. Hallucinations have also been reported in patients receiving rotigotine and were of sufficient severity to cause discontinuation of treatment.
    Headline
    cardiovascular disease, orthostatic hypotension
    General Information
    Caution is advised when administering rotigotine to people with cardiovascular disease or conditions that would be sensitive to changes in blood pressure or heart rate. Dopaminergic agents may impair systemic regulation of blood pressure and can result in postural or orthostatic hypotension, particularly during dose escalation. Syncope has been reported in patients using dopamine agonists, and patients with severe cardiovascular disease should be asked about symptoms of syncope and pre-syncope. Rotigotine should also be used with caution in people with symptomatic orthostatic hypotension at baseline. Individuals with Parkinson disease appear to have an impaired capacity to respond to a postural challenge. Patients should be carefully monitored for signs and symptoms of postural hypotension, particularly during dose escalation. Careful titration and dose escalation may minimize the potential for orthostatic hypotension and syncope. Elevation of blood pressure and heart rate has also occurred. Use of rotigotine at maximal doses has been associated with increases in blood pressure and pulse, with increased risks of hypertension noted in clinical trials. Additionally, weight gain (10% or more) was frequently associated with fluid retention and peripheral edema during rotigotine use in Parkinson disease patients, suggesting that the drug should be used cautiously in people with conditions pre-disposing to fluid retention such as congestive heart failure.
    Headline
    conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature
    General Information
    The effects of applying heat to the rotigotine transdermal system are unknown. However, heat application has been shown to increase drug absorption several fold with other transdermal products. While using rotigotine, patients should be advised to avoid exposing the application site to external sources of direct heat and should avoid conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature, such as a heating pad, electric blanket, heat lamp, sauna, hot tub, heated water bed, or prolonged direct sunlight (UV) exposure.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are limited data regarding the clinical use of rotigotine in human pregnancy. In a prospective case series documenting outcomes of dopamine agonist use for restless legs syndrome during pregnancy, 2 pregnant women were exposed to rotigotine throughout their pregnancies. One patient received rotigotine patch 6 mg/day and delivered via cesarean section because of breech presentation; the baby girl was growth-inhibited but otherwise healthy. The other patient received 3 mg/day until week 6 followed by 1 mg/day from week 7 until her delivery of a healthy boy at week 36. In studies conducted in mice, rats, and rabbits, rotigotine was shown to have adverse effects on embryo-fetal development when administered during pregnancy at doses similar to or lower than those used clinically. Because there are no well-controlled studies documenting safe use of rotigotine during pregnancy, rotigotine should be used in pregnancy only if the potential benefits outweigh the potential risks to the fetus. The effects of rotigotine in labor and delivery are unknown.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    During clinical trials with rotigotine, weight gain of more than 10% of baseline weight occurred in early and advanced stage Parkinson disease patients. Weight gain was frequently associated with fluid retention and peripheral edema, suggesting that the drug should be used cautiously in patients with any condition pre-disposing to fluid retention such as renal insufficiency (renal impairment, renal failure).
    Headline

    General Information
    The developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding should be considered along with the clinical need of the mother and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed infant from rotigotine or the underlying maternal condition. However, inhibition of lactation may occur because rotigotine decreases secretion of prolactin in humans. Studies in rats have shown that rotigotine and its metabolites are excreted in breast milk.   General Information
  Rotigotine is administered transdermally. Application site reactions (ASRs) including erythema, edema, and pruritus localized to the patch area occurred in 37% of rotigotine-treated patients versus 14% of those receiving placebo during clinical trials. These reactions have been primarily mild to moderate in severity and appear to be dose-related. Discontinuation of treatment due to an ASR also shows a relation to dose with 3% of patients discontinuing treatment while on the 6 mg/24 hour patch versus 2% of those on the 2 mg/24 hour patch. Generalized skin reactions such as maculopapular rash and pruritus have occurred less frequently than localized effects. Rotigotine should be discontinued if a generalized reaction develops. Otherwise, an individual determination should be made for localized effects based upon such factors as increases in severity, size, and duration. Rotation of the application site reduces the incidence of ASRs compared to repeated site administration.Rotigotine commonly causes drowsiness or somnolence, occurring in 33% of those receiving the drug versus 20% of those receiving placebo. There have been reports of patients who have fallen asleep while performing activities of daily living, including driving. Excessive drowsiness has, in some cases, occurred as late as one year after the initiation of treatment. In some cases, sudden sleep onset due to rotigotine has resulted in auto accidents or other harmful events in the course of daily living. Symptoms of excessive drowsiness may not be preceded by warning signs. Patients should be cautioned against driving or operating machinery, working at heights, or performing other tasks that require alertness while receiving rotigotine. Those who have experienced somnolence or a sudden episode of sleep while taking the drug should avoid these activities. Reassessment for oversedation is suggested throughout rotigotine therapy; patients should notify their prescriber if they fall asleep during normal activities. The use of concomitant CNS depressant medications or sleep disorders may increase the risk of falling asleep while taking this medication; patients should be assessed for these risk factors prior to initiation of the drug and be advised of the additive risks for somnolence. Rotigotine should generally be discontinued in patients who experience episodes of falling asleep while engaged in activities of daily living. It is not known if a reduction in dosage will subsequently reduce or eliminate excessive somnolence or sudden episodes of sleep.Rotigotine increases heart rate an average of 2—4 bpm compared to placebo. Sinus tachycardia (heart rate greater than 100 bpm) has occurred in 9% of those receiving rotigotine compared to 7% receiving placebo. Dopamine agonists may cause syncope. Although the incidence of syncope with rotigotine was similar to placebo in controlled trials, those with clinically relevant cardiovascular disease were excluded from evaluation. Similar to other dopamine agonists, rotigotine can cause orthostatic hypotension. This dose-related effect may be particularly evident during dose titrations. During clinical trials, orthostatic hypotension (systolic decrease of at least 20 mmHg at 3 minutes after rising) was reported in 5% of those receiving rotigotine 6 mg transdermal patch compared to 4% of those receiving placebo. Careful titration may minimize the potential for this adverse effect. Patients receiving rotigotine may have dose-related elevations in systolic and diastolic blood pressures (i.e., hypertension). Systolic readings greater than 180 mmHg were reported in 4% of those receiving rotigotine versus 2% for placebo; diastolic readings greater than 105 mmHg occurred in 9% of rotigotine-treated patients versus 5% for placebo.Hallucinations were reported in clinical trials for Parkinson's Disease in 2% of rotigotine-treated patients compared to 0.7% of those receiving placebo. Discontinuation of treatment was required in a small percentage of patients receiving the drug (0.2%). Rotigotine may also potentiate the dopaminergic effects of L-dopa such as dyskinesia. During clinical trials, dyskinesia was reported in 0.5% of patients receiving rotigotine versus 0.3% of those receiving placebo.Weight gain of more than 10% of baseline weight occurred in 3% of patients during clinical trials with rotigotine. Weight gain was frequently associated with peripheral edema, suggesting that the drug should be used cautiously in patients with a condition pre-disposing to fluid retention such as congestive heart failure or renal insufficiency.Gastrointestinal (GI) complaints such as nausea/vomiting were frequently reported in clinical trials with rotigotine, at a rate over twice that for placebo (41% vs. 17%). Nausea/vomiting may be more likely to occur during initiation of therapy. A dosage reduction may be required in some instances. Xerostomia occurred more frequently in patients treated with rotigotine than placebo (3% vs. 1%). Other GI effects which occurred in at least 2% of patients receiving rotigotine in clinical trials and more frequently than placebo included constipation (5% vs. 4%), dyspepsia (4% vs. 1%), and anorexia (3% vs. 1%).Rotigotine contains sodium metabisulfite, which may cause anaphylactoid reactions in susceptible individuals.Centrally-mediated adverse effects associated with rotigotine receipt include insomnia, fatigue, headache, vertigo, and dizziness.Adverse reactions reported in at least 2% of patients receiving rotigotine in clinical trials and more frequently than placebo include increased sweating, back pain, arthralgia, abnormal dreams, urinary tract infection, and abnormal vision.Other adverse reactions which have been observed in 1% of patients during clinical trials with rotigotine include: abnormal gait, ataxia, confusion, contact dermatitis, fever, gama-glutamyl transferase increase, hypertonia, hypoesthesia, leg pain, liver and biliary disorders, malaise, neuralgia, paresthesias, purpura, and urinary incontinence.Rare cases of retroperitoneal fibrosis, pulmonary infiltrates, pleural effusion, pleural thickening, pericarditis, and cardiac valvulopathy have been reported in patients treated with ergot-derived dopaminergic agents (e.g., bromocriptine, pergolide). The clinical relevance of this finding is unknown.

    Headline
    restless legs syndrome (RLS) augmentation, restless legs syndrome (RLS) rebound
    General Information
    Restless legs syndrome (RLS) augmentation occurs when dopamine agonists, such as rotigotine, cause a worsening of RLS symptom severity above and beyond the level at the time the medication was started or may involve an earlier time of symptom onset each day compared to pre-treatment levels. Augmentation symptoms may include the earlier onset of symptoms in the evening or afternoon, increase in symptoms, or spread of symptoms to involve other extremities. Restless legs syndrome (RLS) rebound is an exacerbation of RLS symptoms and is considered to be an end-of-dose effect related to the half-life of the drug. Rebound has been observed during discontinuation of the dopaminergic agent, and in the early morning hours during ongoing treatment (also known as early-morning rebound). If augmentation or early-morning rebound occurs, the use of rotigotine should be re-assessed, and a dosage adjustment or discontinuation of treatment should be considered. When discontinuing rotigotine, a gradual taper is advisable to minimize rebound effects.

Description
  Text
  Rotigotine hydrochloride is a transdermal non-ergoline dopamine agonist used for the treatment of Parkinson's disease (PD) and restless leg syndrome (RLS). Effectiveness with and without concomitant levodopa treatment was established in advanced and early stage PD patients, respectively. In one study, median oral doses of levodopa significantly decreased from 1400 mg/day to 400 mg/day during combined therapy with rotigotine. Use of rotigotine can improve motor performance and activities of daily living in patients with PD. This dosage form can also allow the theory to be tested that continuous dopaminergic stimulation from the initiation of PD therapy will help to limit motor complications (i.e., on-off syndrome). Dopamine receptor agonists have historically been widely used in the treatment of RLS, presumably because of their longer half-lives and good tolerability compared with levodopa. However, RLS guidelines from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) suggest against the standard use of rotigotine due to the risk of RLS augmentation with long-term use. Treatment may be considered selectively when short-term RLS symptom control is prioritized over the potential long-term risks, with appropriate counseling and monitoring. Rotigotine (Neupro) was approved by the FDA in May 2007 for early stage idiopathic Parkinson's disease, and in April 2012 for advanced stage idiopathic Parkinson's disease and restless legs syndrome.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Rotigotine is a non-ergoline dopamine agonist that binds to D2 dopamine receptors within the caudate-putamen in the brain. The drug is also an agonist at D3 and D1 dopamine receptors. Although the exact mechanism in the treatment of Parkinson's Disease is unknown, it is thought to be related to the D2 receptor activity of the drug. The mechanism of action in Restless Legs Syndrome (RLS) is unknown. Dopaminergic pathways (e.g., presynaptic dopaminergic dysfunction) are thought to be involved in the pathogenesis of RLS, which presumably explains the benefits of dopamine agonists in the treatment of the syndrome.

    Transdermal Route
After application to the trunk, detectable plasma concentrations of rotigotine are achieved in an average of 3 hours (range: 1—8 hours).   Text
  Rotigotine is administered as a transdermal patch. Plasma protein binding is about 89.5%. Rotigotine is extensively metabolized by conjugation and N-dealkylation. Cytochrome P450 isoenzymes, sulfotransferases, and glucuronosyltransferases (UGT1A9 and UGT2B15) catalyze the metabolism of the drug. In vitro studies indicate that multiple CYP450 isoenzymes can catalyze the metabolism of rotigotine; however, there is no evidence of significant inhibition of metabolism during co-incubation with CYP450 inhibitors. The drug does not appear to inhibit CYP1A2, CYP2C9,or CYP3A4. There is a low risk of CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 inhibition by the drug, and no indication of enzyme-inducing effects on CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, or CYP3A4. Following removal of the patch, the terminal half-life is 5—7 hours. The primary elimination route is renal, with 71% of a dose excreted in the urine as inactive conjugates of rotigotine and its N-desalkyl metabolites. Less than 1% of a dose is excreted in the urine as the parent compound. About 11% is excreted in the feces.

    Administration Details
      Name
      Transdermal Patch Formulations
      Description
      Apply adhesive side of patch to clean, dry, intact healthy skin on the front of the abdomen, thigh, hip, flank, shoulder, or upper arm. After removal of patch, wash application site with soap and water to remove any drug or adhesive. Baby oil or mineral oil may be used if needed. Do not use alcohol or other solvents.
After application, wash hands immediately. After handling the patch, do not touch eyes or objects until hands are washed.
Avoid direct sunlight exposure to rashes or areas of irritation caused by the patch.
The effects of applying heat to the patch are unknown. Advise patients to avoid exposing the patch to sources of direct heat such as heating pads, electric blankets, heat lamps, saunas, hot tubs, heated water beds, and prolonged direct sunlight.
