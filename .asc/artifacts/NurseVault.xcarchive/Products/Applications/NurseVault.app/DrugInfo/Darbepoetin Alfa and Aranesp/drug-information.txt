Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Evaluate the iron status before and during treatment. Administer supplemental iron when the serum ferritin is less than 100 mcg/L or when serum transferrin saturation is less than 20%.
Correct or exclude other causes of anemia such as vitamin deficiency, metabolic or chronic inflammatory conditions, and bleeding before initiating darbepoetin alfa.
For lack or loss of hemoglobin response to darbepoetin alpha, search for causative factors.
Conditions that may interfere with response to erythropoietin-stimulating agents (ESAs) include bleeding, infection, inflammation, malignancy, malnutrition, hypothyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, vitamin B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, iron deficiency, hematological disease (e.g., hemolytic anemia, sickle cell disease, thalassemia, refractory anemia), and bone marrow disorders, such as myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS).
If possible, correct the etiology such as vitamin B12, folate, or iron deficiency. In the absence of another etiology, evaluate for pure red cell aplasia (PRCA), including testing for the presence of antibodies to erythropoietin.
Monitor hemoglobin (Hgb) weekly until stable and sufficient to minimize the need for red blood cell (RBC) transfusions.
Darbepoetin alfa has not been shown to improve quality of life, fatigue, or well-being.
Darbepoetin alfa is not indicated for use:
In individuals with cancer receiving hormonal agents, biologic products, or radiotherapy, unless also receiving concomitant myelosuppressive chemotherapy.
In individuals with cancer receiving myelosuppressive chemotherapy when the anticipated outcome is cure.
In individuals with cancer receiving myelosuppressive chemotherapy in whom anemia can be managed by transfusion.
As a substitute for RBC transfusions in individuals who require immediate correction of anemia.
In adults with chronic kidney disease not on dialysis, initiate when the Hgb is less than 10 g/dL, the rate of Hgb decline indicates the likelihood of requiring an RBC transfusion, and reducing the risk of alloimmunization and/or other RBC transfusion-related risks is a goal.
In adults with chronic kidney disease on dialysis, initiate when the Hgb concentration is less than 10 g/dL
For all children with chronic kidney disease, base the Hgb concentration at which to initiate therapy on individual consideration of potential benefits (e.g., improvement in quality of life, school attendance/performance, and avoidance of transfusion) and potential harms.
In pediatric populations, a target Hgb concentration of 11 to 12 g/dL is recommended.
In individuals in whom all correctable causes for lack or loss of hemoglobin response have been maximally treated but who remain hyporesponsive, ESA therapy may be continued cautiously at doses up to 4 times the initial dose to prevent a further decline in hemoglobin concentration. Red blood cell transfusions can be used to prevent or treat anemia-related symptoms and signs.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Cases of pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) and of severe anemia, with or without other cytopenias, associated with neutralizing antibodies to erythropoietin have been reported in patients treated with darbepoetin. This has been reported predominantly in patients with chronic kidney failure receiving darbepoetin by subcutaneous administration. Any patient who develops a sudden loss of response to darbepoetin, accompanied by severe anemia and low reticulocyte count, should be evaluated for the etiology of loss of effect, including the presence of binding and neutralizing antibodies to erythropoietin. If anti-erythropoietin antibody-associated anemia is suspected, withhold darbepoetin and other erythropoietic proteins. Contact Amgen (800—77AMGEN) to perform assays for binding and neutralizing antibodies. Darbepoetin should be permanently discontinued in patients with antibody-mediated anemia. Patients should not be switched to other erythropoietic proteins as antibodies may cross-react.
 
Darbepoetin alfa and other erythropoiesis stimulating agents (ESAs) may increase the risk of death or serious cardiovascular events (e.g., arterial and venous thromboembolic events, myocardial infarction, stroke, CHF, hemodialysis graft occlusion) in patients with a targeted hemoglobin concentration greater than 12 g/dl. In patients receiving an ESA, a dose reduction is indicated when the hemoglobin concentration is > 12 g/dl (chronic kidney failure), when hemoglobin exceeds the level needed to avoid blood transfusions (chemotherapy-induced anemia), or if a rise in hemoglobin > 1 g/dl over a 2-week period occurs (see Dosage). Furthermore, to reduce the risk of cardiovascular events in patients with renal disease, adjust the dose to maintain hemoglobin concentrations between 10 and 12 g/dl. In patients with chemotherapy-induced anemia, the lowest dose that will gradually increase the hemoglobin to a concentration sufficient to avoid an RBC transfusion should be used. In patients with renal impairment not yet requiring hemodialysis, epoetin alfa administered to a goal hemoglobin concentration of 13.5 g/dl was associated with an increased incidence of myocardial infarction, hospitalization for CHF without renal replacement therapy, and stroke compared to patients receiving epoetin alfa with a targeted hemoglobin concentration of 11.3 g/dl. Patients with chronic renal failure who do not respond to ESA therapy may be at even greater risk for cardiovascular events. In addition, in patients with renal failure requiring dialysis, ESAs administered to a targeted hemoglobin concentration greater than 12 g/dl have been associated with an increased risk of death. In patients with renal failure on dialysis, the increase in mortality was observed in patients with concomitant cardiac disease including angina and congestive heart failure; the reasons for the increased rate of death in this trial are not known, although the rates of non-fatal MI, vascular access thrombosis, and other thrombotic events were higher in patients with a targeted hemoglobin of 14 g/dl or hematocrit of 42%. Use ESAs with caution in patients with pre-existing cardiac disease. Furthermore, during hemodialysis, patients treated with ESAs may require increased anticoagulant therapy with heparin to prevent clotting of the dialysis machine. In patients receiving an ESA, but not prophylactic anticoagulation, prior to surgery to reduce the requirement for allogeneic RBC transfusion, an increased incidence of venous thromboembolism (VTE) has been noted; antithrombotic prophylaxis is recommended in this population, although darbepoetin alfa is not approved to reduce the necessity of allogeneic RBC transfusion. In addition, increased mortality has been demonstrated in patients receiving epoetin alfa prior to coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG), and as such, ESAs are not recommended to prevent allogeneic RBC transfusions in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Similarly, an increased incidence of thrombotic events, tumor progression, and/or death has been observed in patients with certain types of neoplastic disease (i.e., breast, head and neck, non-small cell lung cancer, cervical, and non-myeloid malignancies) receiving ESAs, primarily when the hemoglobin concentration is greater than 12 g/dl; however, these risks can not be excluded when hemoglobin concentrations are < 12 g/dl. An increased risk of death has also been reported in patients on darbepoetin alfa with non-myeloid malignancies who are not receiving chemotherapy. Conversely, a study reported at the 2007 World Conference on Lung Cancer indicates that in patients with extensive small cell lung cancer, the use of darbepoetin titrated to a goal hemoglobin concentration of 13 g/dl was not associated with an increase or decrease in overall survival as compared to placebo. Until more data are available, ESAs should only be used as indicated, which is in patients with cancer who are anemic secondary to chemotherapy titrated to the lowest hemoglobin concentration that is sufficient to avoid RBC transfusions. On March 13, 2008, an FDA Advisory Committee recommended that ESAs not be used in patients with metastatic breast cancer, head and neck cancer, or the treatment of cancers with curative potential. In July 2008, the FDA ruled that darbepoetin alfa is not indicated for use in patients receiving myelosuppressive therapy when the anticipated outcome is cure. Because the risk of thromboembolism is increased in many populations, the anticipated benefits of darbepoetin alfa therapy in any patient with a history of thromboembolic disease should be weighed against the potential risks. NOTE: For more information, see adverse reactions section.
 
Although it appears darbepoetin alfa does not have any direct pressor effects, blood pressure may rise during darbepoetin alfa therapy. Darbepoetin alfa is contraindicated in patients with uncontrolled hypertension; hypertension should be controlled prior to beginning darbepoetin alfa therapy in all patients. Special care should be taken to monitor and aggressively control blood pressure all patients receiving darbepoetin alfa, especially patients with underlying hypertension or cardiovascular disease. If blood pressure is difficult to control by pharmacologic or dietary means, the dose of darbepoetin should be reduced or withheld. During the early phase of darbepoetin alfa therapy, up to 40% of patients require initiation of or intensification of antihypertensive therapy. It is recommended that the dose of darbepoetin alfa be decreased if the hemoglobin increase exceeds 1 g/dl in any 2-week period because of the association of excessive rate of rise of hemoglobin with an exacerbation of hypertension.
 
In adult patients with chronic renal failure, there is an increased risk of seizures during the first several months of darbepoetin alfa therapy, when blood pressure and hematocrit increase rapidly, as compared with other time-points. Given the potential for an increased risk of seizures during the first several months of therapy, blood pressure and neurologic status should be carefully monitored. Patients should be advised to avoid potentially hazardous activities such as driving or operating machinery. The safety and efficacy of darbepoetin alfa in patients with a seizure disorder have not been established; these patients should be closely monitored. It is recommended that the dose of darbepoetin alfa be decreased if the hemoglobin increase exceeds 1 gm/dl in any 2-week period. The recommended target hemoglobin concentration should not exceed 12 g/dl.
 
Darbepoetin alfa should be used with caution in patients with porphyria. Although there have been no reports of exacerbations of porphyria with darbepoetin alfa, exacerbations have been reported, rarely, with epoetin alfa.
 
Patients receiving darbepoetin alfa should be encouraged to maintain compliance with dietary and dialysis prescriptions, especially as treatment with darbepoetin alfa increases the patient's sense of well-being and quality of life. Although not reported during treatment with darbepoetin alfa, treatment with epoetin alfa has been associated with hyperkalemia, which most often occurred in association with poor compliance to medication, diet, and/or dialysis. Similar outcomes would be expected with darbepoetin alfa.
 
Treatment with darbepoetin alfa may result in functional or absolute iron deficiency. Prior to and during darbepoetin alfa therapy, evaluation of iron stores, including transferrin saturation (serum iron divided by the iron binding capacity) and serum ferritin should be monitored. Transferrin saturation should be at least 20% and the serum ferritin should be at least 100 ng/ml. Virtually all patients will require iron supplementation to increase or maintain transferrin saturation levels that will support erythropoiesis. Darbepoetin alfa is not indicated in patients with iron-deficiency anemia or anemias due to acute or chronic blood loss.
 
The following conditions can interfere with the response to darbepoetin alfa: acute or chronic infection or inflammation, aluminum overload, cystic fibrosis, erythrocyte enzyme deficiency, folate deficiency, hematological disease (e.g., thalassemia, refractory anemia, or other myelodysplastic disorders), hyperparathyroidism, hypersplenism, occult blood loss, osteitis fibrosa cystica, or vitamin B12 deficiency. To date, darbepoetin alfa has not been associated with the stimulation of malignant hematologic cell lines. Nevertheless, it should be used with caution in patients with myeloid leukemia. The safety and efficacy of darbepoetin alfa have not been established in patients with sickle cell disease or coagulopathy (i.e., hypercoagulable states).
 
Darbepoetin alfa is derived from Chinese hamster ovarian cells and is contraindicated in patients with known hamster protein hypersensitivity or known albumin hypersensitivity or polysorbate 80 hypersensitivity. Aranesp (darbepoetin alfa) is supplied in 2 formulations with different excipients, one containing polysorbate 80 and another containing albumin (human). Rarely, potentially serious allergic reactions, including skin rash and urticaria, associated with darbepoetin alfa have been reported. Symptoms have recurred with rechallenge, suggesting a causal relationship exists in some instances. If a serious allergic or anaphylactic reaction occurs, darbepoetin alfa should be immediately and permanently discontinued and appropriate therapy should be administered. The needle cover of the prefilled syringes (SingleJect and the SureClick Autoinjector) contain dry natural rubber, which may cause allergic reactions in individuals with latex hypersensitivity.
 
Due to effective plasma donor screening for prior exposure to certain viruses, testing for the presence of viruses, and manufacturing processes designed to reduce the risk of transmitting viral infection, the risk of transmission of infectious agents associated with Aranesp products formulated with albumin is remote. However, none of the processes are completely effective. There is also the possibility that unknown infectious agents may be present. A theoretical risk of the transmission of Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD) is also considered remote. No cases of transmission of viral infections or CJD have ever been identified for albumin.
 
Darbepoetin alfa is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C and should be used during pregnancy only when the benefits outweigh the risks of treatment. The possibility of pregnancy and contraceptive considerations should be discussed with the patient.
 
It is not known if darbepoetin alfa is distributed into breast milk. Although it is similar to an endogenous hormone, caution should be exercised when darbepoetin alfa is administered to a breast-feeding woman.
 
Conversion to darbepoetin alfa from epoetin alfa is FDA approved in children and adolescents with renal failure aged 1—18 years. In this population, darbepoetin demonstrated comparative safety and efficacy to adults. While safety and efficacy in the initial treatment of anemia in children with chronic renal failure have not been established, limited data indicate that darbepoetin can be used as initial therapy. In a study of 33 dialysis-dependent children, both with a history of epoetin use (n=14) and those naive to epoetin therapy (n=19), darbepoetin therapy, at a dosage of approximately 0.45 mcg/kg/week SC, was associated with an average increase in hemoglobin of 1.4 g/dl at 8—12 weeks. The increase in hemoglobin was sustained at 20—28 weeks (an average increase of 1.2 g/dl). Pain at injection site, one case of hypertension, and 3 cases of asymptomatic thrombocytosis were reported.

    Headline
    red cell aplasia
    General Information
    Darbepoetin alfa is contraindicated in individuals with pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) that begins after treatment with an erythropoietin protein medication. Cases of PRCA and severe anemia, with or without other cytopenias associated with neutralizing antibodies to erythropoietin, have been reported in individuals treated with darbepoetin alfa, predominantly in individuals with chronic kidney disease receiving an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) by subcutaneous administration.
    Headline
    cardiovascular disease, coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG), mortality, orthopedic surgery, thromboembolism
    General Information
    In controlled trials, subjects with chronic kidney disease experienced greater risks for mortality, serious adverse cardiovascular reactions, and stroke when administered erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) to target a hemoglobin concentration greater than 11 g/dL. No trial has identified a hemoglobin target concentration, ESA dose, or dosing strategy that does not increase these risks. Use the lowest ESA dose sufficient to reduce the need for red blood cell transfusions. For individuals with chronic kidney disease either on or off dialysis, a hemoglobin less than 10 g/dL is advised before darbepoetin alfa initiation. In controlled clinical trials of subjects with chronic kidney disease comparing higher hemoglobin targets (13 to 14 g/dL) to lower targets (9 to 11.3 g/dL), ESAs increased the risk of death, myocardial infarction, stroke, congestive heart failure, thrombosis of vascular access, and other thromboembolism in the higher target groups. Using ESAs to target a hemoglobin concentration greater than 11 g/dL increases the risk of serious adverse cardiovascular reactions and has not been shown to provide additional benefit. Use ESAs with caution in individuals with coexistent cardiovascular disease and stroke. Individuals with chronic kidney disease and an insufficient hemoglobin response to ESA therapy may be at even greater risk for cardiovascular reactions and mortality than other populations. A rate of hemoglobin rise of greater than 1 g/dL over 2 weeks may contribute to these risks; a dose reduction is warranted. Additionally, ESAs increased the risk of death in subjects undergoing coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG) and the risk of deep venous thrombosis in individuals undergoing orthopedic surgery in controlled clinical trials. However, guidelines suggest it is reasonable to use ESAs with iron supplementation several days before cardiac surgery to increase red cell mass in individuals who have preoperative anemia, refuse blood cell transfusion, or are deemed high-risk for postoperative anemia; studies have reported no adverse effects associated with short-term ESA pretreatment.
    Headline
    hypertension
    General Information
    Darbepoetin alfa is contraindicated in individuals with uncontrolled hypertension; control hypertension before and during darbepoetin alfa therapy in all individuals. Advise individuals about the importance of compliance with antihypertensive therapy and dietary restrictions.
    Headline
    tumor progression or recurrence
    General Information
    Use of erythropoiesis stimulating agents (ESAs) shortened overall survival and/or increased the risk of tumor progression or recurrence in clinical studies of subjects with breast, non-small cell lung, head and neck, lymphoid, and cervical cancers. ESAs are not indicated for individuals receiving myelosuppressive chemotherapy when the anticipated outcome is cure. Further, use ESAs only for anemia from myelosuppressive chemotherapy, and use the lowest dose to avoid red blood cell transfusions. Discontinue the ESA after the completion of a chemotherapy course.
    Headline
    seizure disorder
    General Information
    Advise individuals with a history of seizure disorder to contact their health care provider for premonitory symptoms or a change in seizure frequency. Darbepoetin alfa increases the risk of seizures in individuals with chronic kidney disease. During the first several months after darbepoetin alfa initiation, closely monitor for premonitory neurologic symptoms.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Consider the benefits and risks of darbepoetin alfa for the mother and possible risks to the fetus when prescribing darbepoetin alfa during pregnancy. The limited available data on darbepoetin alfa use in pregnant women are insufficient to determine a drug-associated risk of adverse developmental outcomes. In animal studies, early post-implantation loss occurred when pregnant rats received darbepoetin alfa at doses approximating the clinical recommended starting doses.
    Headline

Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.

Interactions
Lenalidomide: (Moderate) Monitor for signs and symptoms of thromboembolic events if concomitant use of lenalidomide and an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) is required. Both medications have been associated with venous thromboembolism and concomitant use may increase the risk of thrombosis.
Thalidomide: (Moderate) Monitor for signs and symptoms of thromboembolic events if concomitant use of thalidomide and an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) is required. Both medications have been associated with venous thromboembolism and concomitant use may increase the risk of thrombosis.
Vadadustat: (Major) Avoid concomitant use of vadadustat and erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESA), such as darbepoetin alfa. If ESA rescue therapy with darbepoetin alfa is necessary, interrupt vadadustat treatment. Resume vadadustat at the prior dose or with a dose that is 150 mg more than the prior dose when hemoglobin concentrations are 10 g/dL or more and 7 days have elapsed after the last dose of darbepoetin alfa. Concomitant use may result in a rapid rise in hemoglobin concentrations or an increase in hemoglobin above the target concentration, which may increase the risk for thrombotic vascular events.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: In March 2007, the FDA issued a public health advisory regarding serious risks and adverse events associated with the use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs). ESAs have been shown to increase the risk of death and serious cardiovascular events (e.g., arterial and venous thromboembolic events including MI, CVA, CHF, and hemodialysis graft occlusion) in controlled clinical trials when administered to target a hemoglobin of greater than 12 g/dl. A rate of hemoglobin rise > 1 g/dl over 2 weeks may also contribute to these risks. Even though clinical trials demonstrating the increased rate of serious adverse events have used different ESAs, the FDA feels that the results can be applied to all ESAs as a class. Darbepoetin alfa should be dosed to achieve a hemoglobin concentration of 10—12 g/dl in patients with chronic kidney disease. For all other indications, darbepoetin alfa should be dosed to the lowest hemoglobin concentration possible that avoids RBC transfusions.When administered to a target hemoglobin concentration of > 12 g/dl, an increase in the rate of thrombotic events, death, and/or tumor progression has been demonstrated in several different populations including those with renal impairment, undergoing surgery, and with certain types of cancers. However, risk of tumor progression, cancer recurrence, and shortened overall survival in cancer patients cannot be ruled out with target hemoglobin concentrations < 12 g/dl. Until further data are available, the recommended target hemoglobin concentration should not exceed 12 g/dl for any patient receiving an erythropoiesis stimulating agent (ESA). Furthermore, in patients with cancer, ESAs should only be used in those patients that are anemic secondary to chemotherapy treatment, should not be used if the anticipated outcome of chemotherapy is cure, should not be initiated unless the hemoglobin < 10 g/dl, and should be dosed to achieve a hemoglobin level sufficient to avoid blood transfusions (see Dosage). In chronic renal failure patients, hypertension (20%), hypotension (20%), cardiac arrhythmias and cardiac arrest (8%), angina and chest pain (unspecified) (8%), congestive heart failure (5%), acute myocardial infarction (2%), stroke (1%), and transient ischemic attack (1%) have been reported during therapy with darbepoetin alfa. In cancer patients, hypertension was reported in 3.7% of patients. Patients with preexisting hypertension may require adjustments in their antihypertensive therapy. Cardiovascular events may be associated with higher hemoglobin levels and/or higher rates of rise of hemoglobin. In a study of patients with renal impairment defined as chronic kidney disease not requiring renal replacement therapy (CrCl of 15—50 ml/min per 1.73 m2 of body surface area using the MDRD formula), a goal hemoglobin concentration of 13.5 g/dl (12.6 g/dl was the average concentration achieved by this group of patients during the study) vs. 11.3 g/dl was associated with an increased incidence of death, myocardial infarction, hospitalization for congestive heart failure without renal replacement therapy, and stroke (13.5% for 11.3 g/dl group vs. 17.5% for 13.5 g/dl group, HR 1.34, 95% CI (1.03—1.74, P=0.03). In another trial, epoetin alfa administered to hemodialysis patients with cardiac disease resulted in a higher mortality rate (35% vs. 29%) in the patients randomized to a target hemoglobin of 14 g/dl vs. 10 g/dl. The reason for the increased serious adverse events observed in these studies is unknown; however, in the second study, the incidence of nonfatal myocardial infarction, vascular access thrombosis and other thrombotic events was higher in the group of patients randomized to a target hemoglobin of 14 g/dl. Rates of thrombotic events (e.g., vascular access thrombosis, venous thrombosis, and pulmonary embolus) with darbepoetin therapy in hemodialysis patients were similar to those observed with other recombinant erythropoietin products. In cancer patients, the incidence of thrombotic events was 6.2% for darbepoetin and 4.1% for placebo. However, the following events were reported more frequently in darbepoetin-treated cancer patients vs placebo-controls: pulmonary embolism (1.3% vs 0%), thromboembolism, thrombosis (5.6% vs 4.1%), and thrombophlebitis (deep and/or superficial). In women with breast cancer receiving chemotherapy, a 4-month interim analysis revealed that mortality was higher in women receiving epoetin alfa to a goal hemoglobin 12—14 g/dl (8.7%) compared to those receiving placebo (3.4%); fatal thrombotic events were also higher in the group receiving epoetin (1.1% vs. 0.2%). In addition, patients with head and neck cancer receiving epoetin beta with a targeted hemoglobin concentration of 14 g/dl in women and 15 g/dl in men experienced shorter progression-free survival compared to placebo (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.22—2.14, p=0.0008). Similarly, an interim analysis of patients with head and neck cancer receiving darbepoetin alfa revealed an increase in disease recurrence compared to those receiving placebo. In a study of patients with non-myeloid malignancy that were anemic secondary to the disease rather than chemotherapy, the use of darbepoetin did not reduce the requirement of RBC transfusions, but did increase the incidence of death compared to placebo (HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.08—1.55). A similar finding of decreased survival has also been demonstrated in patients with advanced, non-curable non-small-cell lung cancer receiving epoetin alfa with a targeted hemoglobin concentration of 12—14 g/dl compared to placebo (HR 1.84, p=0.04). Finally, a systematic review of trials in patients with cancer (+/- chemotherapy) receiving ESAs versus placebo or standard of care found a significantly increased risk of thromboembolic events in patients receiving an ESA (RR 1.57, 95% CI, 1.31—1.87) and a non-significant increase in mortality risk (RR 1.10, 95% CI, 1.01—1.20). In contrast to these findings, a study reported at the 2007 World Conference on Lung Cancer found that in patients with extensive small cell lung cancer, the use of darbepoetin titrated to a goal hemoglobin concentration of 13 g/dl was not associated with an increase or decrease in overall survival as compared to placebo. Illicit use of recombinant erythropoietin products by athletes, particularly cyclists, has led to a number of cases of sudden death. In these athletes, epoetin alfa caused a significant increase in hemoglobin levels that was exacerbated by fluid loss during heavy exertion and led to fatal thromboembolism.Seizures have occurred during therapy with darbepoetin alfa. A sudden rise in blood pressure, associated with increased hemoglobin, is believed to be the mechanism. A higher incidence of seizures during the first few months of therapy with darbepoetin alfa was seen in clinical trials. Regular evaluations of hemoglobin should be made at least weekly in all patients. If the hemoglobin rises by more than 1 g/dl in any 2-week period, the dose should be reduced immediately. Hypertensive encephalopathy and seizures have been observed in chronic renal failure patients treated with epoetin alfa, and similar finding may be expected with darbepoetin alfa. Headache has been reported in up to 15% of chronic renal failure patients and 12% of patients receiving chemotherapy treated with darbepoetin alfa.Flu-like symptoms can occur during initiation of darbepoetin alfa therapy. Symptoms (e.g., arthralgia (11%), fever (9%), myalgia (21%), or weakness/asthenia (5%)) generally disappear after 2—12 hours. Musculoskeletal pain (10%), muscle spasm (17%), and back pain (8%) have also been reported in some patients.GI effects of darbepoetin alfa therapy in patients with chronic renal failure and in those receiving chemotherapy, respectively, include diarrhea (14%, 22%), nausea/vomiting (14%, 2%), abdominal pain (10%) and constipation (5%, 18%).Patients report an injection site reaction (e.g., erythema, pruritus, pain, stinging or swelling) following subcutaneous administration. Bleeding at the injection site (6%) has also been reported. Phlebitis and/or thrombophlebitis at the IV injection site can be a problem during intravenous administration. Skin rash (unspecified), urticaria, and pruritus (8%) have been observed; there have been no reports of anaphylactic reactions with darbepoetin alfa. There has been no evidence of antibody development to erythropoietin during treatment with darbepoetin alfa.Adverse reactions reported in patients receiving darbepoetin alfa include bronchitis (6%), cough (10%), dehydration (5%), dizziness (8%), dyspnea (12%), infection including sepsis, bacteremia, pneumonia, peritonitis, and abscess (27%) peripheral edema (11%), fatigue (9%), and upper respiratory tract infection (14%). The underlying disease-state may have contributed to these adverse reactions. Edema of any type was reported more frequently in darbepoetin-treated cancer patients (21%) than in patients who received placebo (10%).As with all therapeutic proteins, there is a potential for immunogenicity. The presence of neutralizing antibodies (i.e., anti-erythropoietin antibodies) has been associated with the development of pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) in patients receiving recombinant erythropoietin products. Any patient experiencing a loss of response to darbepoetin should be evaluated to determine the etiology of the loss of effect. Darbepoetin should be discontinued in any patient with evidence of PRCA and the patient should be evaluated for the presence of binding and neutralizing antibodies to darbepoetin, native erythropoietin, and any other recombinant erythropoietin administered to the patient. Contact Amgen (800—77AMGEN) to perform assays for binding and neutralizing antibodies. Neutralizing antibodies to erythropoietin, in association with PRCA or severe anemia (with or without other cytopenias), have been reported in patients receiving darbepoetin during post-marketing experience. In clinical studies, the percentage of patients with antibodies to darbepoetin was examined using the BIAcore assay. Sera from 1501 chronic renal failure (CRF) patients and 1159 cancer patients were tested. At baseline, prior to darbepoetin treatment, binding antibodies were detected in 59 (4%) of CRF patients and 36 (3%) of cancer patients. While receiving darbepoetin therapy (range 22—177 weeks), a follow-up sample was taken. One additional CRF patient and eight additional cancer patients developed antibodies capable of binding darbepoetin. None of the patients had antibodies capable of neutralizing the activity of darbepoetin alfa or endogenous erythropoietin at baseline or at end of study. No clinical sequelae consistent with PRCA were associated with the presence of these antibodies. The incidence of antibody formation is highly dependent on the sensitivity and specificity of the assay. Comparison of the incidence of antibodies across products within this class (erythropoietic proteins) may be misleading.
  Adverse Reactions
    Headline
    angina, heart failure, intracranial bleeding, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, stroke, thromboembolism, thrombosis
    General Information
    Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) may increase the risk of death, cardiovascular adverse events including thromboembolism, and tumor progression or recurrence (in individuals with cancer). In pooled data from 5 randomized studies, angina (8%), vascular access complications (8%), and arteriovenous graft thrombosis (5%) were reported in 766 adult chronic kidney disease (CKD) subjects who received darbepoetin alfa. Additionally, the risk of stroke was increased almost 2-fold (2.1% vs. 1.1%) in subjects with CKD who received darbepoetin alfa compared with placebo in a randomized trial in 4,038 CKD subjects not on dialysis. Among subjects with CKD, those who received an ESA to target a hemoglobin concentration of 13 to 14 g/dL had greater risks for death, myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, congestive heart failure, thrombosis of hemodialysis vascular access, and other thromboembolic events as compared with the subjects who had a hemoglobin target of 9 to 11.3 g/dL. During hemodialysis, individuals treated with darbepoetin alfa may require increased anticoagulant therapy with heparin to prevent clotting of the dialysis machine. In pooled data from 7 randomized, placebo-controlled studies, thromboembolic adverse events (5.1%) including arterial events (1.1%) such as MI (0.6%) and venous events (4.1%) such as pulmonary embolism (1.5%) occurred in 2,888 adults with cancer who received darbepoetin alfa during chemotherapy. Cerebrovascular disorders including central nervous system/intracranial bleeding and cerebrovascular accident/stroke were reported in 1.3% of darbepoetin alfa-treated cancer subjects. Due to the effects on erythropoiesis, abuse of darbepoetin alfa may be seen in athletes. Misuse of medications that increase erythropoiesis, such as darbepoetin alfa, by healthy individuals may lead to life-threatening cardiovascular complications (e.g., stroke, myocardial infarction, and thromboembolism).

Description
  Text
  Darbepoetin alfa is an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA). It is indicated for the treatment of anemia due to chronic kidney disease (CKD) in patients aged 1 month or older or due to the myelosuppressive effects of chemotherapy in adult patients. Darbepoetin alfa is not indicated as a substitute for red blood cell transfusions in patients who require immediate correction of anemia. It is also not indicated in patients with cancer who are receiving hormonal agents, biologic products, or radiotherapy (unless they are also receiving concomitant myelosuppressive chemotherapy); myelosuppressive chemotherapy when the anticipated outcome is cure; or myelosuppressive chemotherapy in whom the anemia could be managed by transfusion. ESAs may increase the risk of death, serious adverse cardiovascular reactions, and stroke in CKD patients who achieve a hemoglobin concentration more than 11 g/dL. ESAs may shorten the overall survival time and increase the risk of tumor progression or recurrence in patients with cancer. Therefore, use the lowest darbepoetin alfa dose needed to reduce the need for red blood cell transfusions.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Darbepoetin alfa stimulates erythropoiesis by the same mechanism as endogenous erythropoietin (EPO). EPO is a glycoprotein that regulates the production of red blood cells by stimulating the division and differentiation of committed erythroid progenitor cells in the bone marrow. In adults, almost 90% of EPO is produced in the kidney with the remainder produced by the liver. During fetal development, EPO is produced in the liver, and prior to birth, at term, production is transferred to the kidney. EPO production in the kidney occurs in interstitial cells in the inner cortex that are in immediate proximity to the proximal tubules. Cells are activated to produce EPO as the hematocrit drops. Renal tubular cells may serve as oxygen sensors transmitting signals to the interstitial cells, possibly because they contain large amounts of heme protein that may function as an intracellular oxygen sensor and transducer.
 
EPO is required for the transformation of the most mature erythroid progenitor cell, erythroid colony-forming unit (CFU-E), to a proerythroblast. In the absence of EPO, this transformation cannot occur, and the CFU-E will die. EPO activates the synthesis of hemoglobin and other proteins found in normal erythroblasts. EPO also causes a shift of marrow reticulocytes into the circulation. Due to the length of time required for erythropoiesis, a clinically significant increase in hematocrit is usually not observed in less than 2 weeks and may take up to 6 weeks in some patients. EPO has little effect on early erythroid progenitor cells, erythroid burst-forming units (BFU-E), whose growth is more dependent upon interleukin-3 and granulocyte-macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF). The production and activity of EPO are linked in a negative feedback loop, which maintains optimal red cell mass for oxygen transport. Darbepoetin alfa produces a dose-dependent increase in hemoglobin. The stimulation of erythropoiesis increases the demand for iron, making iron supplementation necessary for many patients.

    Subcutaneous Route
    Description
    The bioavailability of darbepoetin alfa in adult patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) after subcutaneous administration is 37% (range: 30% to 50%); a higher bioavailability has been noted in pediatric patients with CKD after subcutaneous administration. The subcutaneous route produces peak plasma concentrations 48 hours (range: 12 to 72 hours) after administration in adult patients with CKD (receiving or not receiving dialysis). The clearance is approximately 1.4 times faster in adult patients receiving dialysis, compared to those not receiving dialysis. In adults with cancer, peak plasma concentrations occur at 71 hours (range: 28 to 120 hours) after a subcutaneous dose of 6.75 mg/kg. The half-life in adult cancer patients is 74 hours (range: 24 to 144 hours).

  Text
  Darbepoetin alfa is administered intravenously or subcutaneously. A dose-dependent response is seen with darbepoetin alfa doses of 0.45 mcg/kg once weekly. Other factors affecting response to therapy include iron stores, baseline hemoglobin, and concurrent medical conditions. As with the endogenous erythropoietin (EPO), darbepoetin alfa does not appear extravascularly in humans. Whether the drug crosses the placenta or is distributed into breast milk has not been evaluated. Metabolism and elimination of endogenous EPO or darbepoetin alfa are not fully understood. While the glycosylation of EPO does not affect its binding to target cells, it plays an important role in preventing the rapid clearance of the hormone from the bloodstream. Non-glycosylated erythropoietin has a half-life of a few minutes. About 10% of the dose appears to be excreted in the urine.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 enzymes and drug transporters: None

    Description
    Administer subcutaneously or intravenously.
Prior to and during therapy, monitor the patient's iron status including transferrin saturation and serum ferritin. Transferrin saturation should be >= 20% and ferritin should be >= 100 ng/mL. Almost all patients will require iron supplementation during therapy to maintain adequate iron stores.
Darbepoetin alfa is available as a vial and prefilled syringe. The needle shield for the prefilled syringe contains latex.
The prefilled syringe may not be appropriate for patients receiving doses that are not represented by one of the prefilled syringe formulations. If patients or caregivers have difficulty measuring or administering the required dose, consider whether the patient is an appropriate candidate for self-administration or whether the patient would benefit from an alternate darbepoetin alfa presentation.
Visually inspect parenteral products for particulate matter and discoloration prior to administration whenever solution and container permit.
Do not dilute. Do not administer with other drug solutions.
Do not shake; inactivation of darbepoetin alfa may occur upon shaking.
Protect from light during storage and until administration. Exposure to light may denature darbepoetin alfa.
Do not freeze.
Darbepoetin alfa contains no preservatives. Use one single-use vial per dose. Once a syringe has entered a single-dose vial, the sterility of the product cannot be guaranteed. Do not use the vials or prefilled syringes more than one time. Discard any unused portion. Do not pool unused portions from the vials or prefilled syringes.
