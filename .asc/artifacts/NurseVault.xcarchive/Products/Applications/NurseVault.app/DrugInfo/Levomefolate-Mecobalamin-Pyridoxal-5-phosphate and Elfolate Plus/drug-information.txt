    General Information
    Use levomefolate; mecobalamin; pyridoxal-5-phosphate with caution in individuals at risk for vitamin B12 deficiency. Description
  Text
  NOTE: In the US, medical foods are defined under 5(b) of the Orphan Drug Act (21 U.S.C. 360ee (b) (3)). Medical foods are specially formulated and processed products that must be administered enterally under the supervision of a physician and are intended for the dietary management of a disease or condition for which distinctive nutritional requirements are determined by medical evaluation. Medical foods are intended for patients who have limited or impaired capacity to ingest, digest, absorb, or metabolize nutrients, or who have medically determined nutrient requirements that cannot be met by the modification of the normal diet alone. Medical foods do not have to undergo review or approval by the FDA, and individual medical food products do not have to be registered with FDA. Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for medical foods as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for medical foods and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products. Monographs on medical foods are included in CP when reliable clinical data are available.
 
Levomefolate; mecobalamin; pyridoxal-5-phosphate is an orally administered prescription medical food. It is used for the dietary management of endothelial dysfunction in patients with diabetic peripheral neuropathy and for nutritional supplementation of patients with hyperhomocysteinemia who present with lower extremity ulceration. Levomefolate; mecobalamin; pyridoxal-5-phosphate maintains peripheral circulation, which improves the delivery of nutrients and oxygen to nerves.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Levomefolate; mecobalamin; pyridoxal-5-phosphate exerts its effect by increasing circulation to the peripheral nerves.
 
Levomefolate: Levomefolate, also known as 6(S)-5-methyltetrahydrofolate or L-methylfolate, is the primary biologically active form of folate found in the circulation. Normally, folic acid and dihydrofolate must undergo enzymatic reduction by methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) to levomefolate; levomefolate is then transported across cell membranes into peripheral tissues by receptor-mediated endocytosis. Levomefolate increases nitric oxide synthesis. Levomefolate regenerates BH4, a co-factor for eNOS (enzyme nitric oxide synthase), which facilitates the conversion of L-arginine into nitric oxide. When nitric oxide enters the smooth muscle cells, vasodilation and increased blood flow result. Levomefolate also is involved in the formation of methionine from homocysteine, lowering homocysteine serum concentrations. Hyperhomocysteinemia is recognized as an independent risk factor for arthrosclerosis of the coronary, cerebral, and peripheral vasculature and may also be important in the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease, diabetic retinopathy, and rheumatic diseases.
Mecobalamin: Mecobalamin is one of the two forms of biologically active vitamin B12. Vitamin B12 is essential to growth, cell reproduction, hematopoiesis, and nucleoprotein and myelin synthesis. Vitamin B12 is essential for the conversion of methylmalonate to succinate and synthesis of methionine from homocysteine (a reaction which also requires folate).
Pyridoxal-5-phosphate: Pyridoxal-5-phosphate is the active form of vitamine B6. Pyridoxine is converted in erythrocytes to its active moiety, pyridoxal phosphate (requiring riboflavin for the conversion). The active form acts as coenzymes for no fewer than 60 metabolic processes including the metabolism of fat, protein, and carbohydrate. Their role in protein metabolism includes decarboxylation of amino acids, conversion of tryptophan to niacin or serotonin, deamination, and transamination of amino acids.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Levomefolate: After oral administration, levomefolate is absorbed and incorporated into the body folate pool. After oral/parenteral administration to patients with coronary artery disease, peak plasma concentrations of levomefolate were reached in 1 to 3 hours and were found to be more than seven times higher than folic acid concentrations. After 5mg PO of levomefolate daily for seven days, the mean values for Cmax and Tmax were 129 mg/ml and 1.3hr, respectively. Levomefolate is transported across cell membranes into peripheral tissues by receptor-mediated endocytosis.
Mecobalamin: After oral administration, absorption depends on the presence of adequate intrinsic factor, which is secreted from gastric mucosa. Mecobalamin is also absorbed via a diffusion process, but to a much lesser extent.
Pyridoxal-5-phosphate: Pyridoxal-5-phosphate is readily absorbed by the intestine. It is converted in erythrocytes to its active moiety, pyridoxal phosphate (requiring riboflavin for the conversion). Pyridoxine, the most common form of vitamin B6, is the parent compound of pyridoxal-5-phosphate and requires reduction and phosphorylation before becoming biologically active.
  Text
  Levomefolate; mecobalamin; pyridoxal-5-phosphate is administered orally. Red blood cells appear to be a storage depot for folate, as RBC concentrations remain elevated more than 40 days after discontinuation of levomefolate supplementation. Levomefolate is 56% bound to plasma proteins. Levomefolate is water soluble and primarily excreted via the kidneys. The mean elimination half-life is approximately 3 hours.
