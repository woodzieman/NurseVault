Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Nonoxynol-9 products should not be administered rectally.
 
In general, there are no absolute contraindications to the use of nonoxynol-9. Occasionally, these products may result in irritation or damage to the vaginal or cervical epithelium, or result in a chemical/allergic vaginitis or irritation to the genitalia of the sexual partner. Allergic reactions are rare. There may be cross-sensitivity with other spermicides (e.g., octoxynol) due to chemical similarities.
 
The base ingredients of some of these azole intravaginal creams and suppositories may interact with the latex of contraceptive devices used concurrently with the spermicides, leading to breakdown of the physical barrier. Since a spermicide is commonly used with contraceptive devices, like a cervical cap or diaphragm, there is a risk of infection (i.e., toxic-shock syndrome, TSS) in the users of these devices or in those who use the contraceptive foam sponge. The risk of TSS is increased in women who keep the cervical cap or diaphragm in place beyond the recommended time (48 hours for the cervical cap and 24 hours for the diaphragm or 30 hours for the contraceptive sponge). The foreign body (i.e., cervical cap or diaphragm) can contribute to the risk of infection by occasionally disrupting the integrity of the vaginal epithelium. The risk for TSS is increased in women during menstruation, who have experienced recent childbirth (obstetric delivery), including recent abortion, or have a history of TSS associated with the cervical cap, diaphragm, tampons, or the contraceptive sponge.
 
The use of nonoxynol-9, like other FDA approved OTC spermicides, does not appear to cause teratogenesis or harmful fetal effects if a woman is unintentionally exposed to the product during early pregnancy. After several epidemiologic studies, the FDA has also concluded that there is not increased risk of spontaneous abortion from the use of these products. Excretion into breast-milk may occur, but amounts are so low (0.3% of an applied dose based on animal studies) that the use of nonoxynol-9 is not felt to pose significant risk to a breast-feeding infant.
 
The use of nonoxynol-9 does not confer protection against the transmission of sexually transmitted infections (STDs) in vivo. While early data indicated that this spermicide might be useful as a microbicide to prevent the transmission of several common sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) and resulting reproductive complications of those diseases, recent controlled data (2002) indicate that nonoxynol-9 does not sufficiently protect against the transmission of any STD, including gonorrhea or chlamydia in vivo. Dissapointingly, nonoxynol-9 also does not decrease the risk of transmission of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), according to reports from multiple clinical trials and the CDC in 2000. Nonoxynol-9 is not an effective means of protection against transmission of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. In some studies of persons with high-risk sexual behaviors, nonoxynol-9 and condom users experienced a higher rate of HIV transmission than those who used condoms alone, suggesting that the use of nonoxynol-9 might be harmful. A possible explanation for the increased transmission rate is due to vaginal or rectal irritation associated with the use of nonoxynol-9 products. The CDC has never recommended nonoxynol-9 alone for HIV prevention, but current recommendations do emphasize the consistent and correct use of condoms, with or without a spermicide. While nonoxynol-9 will not offer any additional protection against HIV, a condom lubricated with spermicide is clearly better than using no condom at all. The protection provided by the condom against HIV far outweighs the potential risk of the addition of spermicide. If given the choice, condoms without nonoxynol-9 may be a better option for HIV prevention. Patients with known HIV infection or acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) should be aware that nonoxynol-9 use will not prevent the transmission of HIV through vaginal or anal intercourse to their partners.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Potential adverse reactions that can occur with nonoxynol-9 include contact dermatitis, allergic vaginitis, vaginal irritation/burning, penile irritation/burning, and urinary tract infection. Local irritation or burning occurs in 1—2% of patients or their partners. Rectal irritation has been reported when administered rectally, and as such, should not be used rectally. While a sensation of unpleasant taste may occur when exposed to small amounts of nonoxynol-9 orally, such exposure is not expected to be harmful. Intentional internal ingestion of the product should be avoided, however.In addition, because nonoxynol-9 is commonly used in conjunction with cervical caps, sponges, or diaphragms, toxic-shock syndrome (TSS) or vulvovaginal candidiasis can develop. Since nonoxynol-9 can disrupt the integrity of vaginal epithelium, both the spermicide and the foreign body (i.e., cervical cap, diaphragm, or contraceptive sponge) can contribute to the risk of this problem. Symptoms of TSS include chills, confusion, dizziness, fever, lightheadedness, myalgia, sunburn-like rash followed by peeling of the skin, hypotension, and unusual redness of the inside of the nose, mouth, throat, vagina, or conjunctivae. With appropriate use according to label directions, the risk of TSS associated with the combined use of nonoxynol-9 and contraceptive devices is very rare.Under selected circumstances, it appears that the use of nonoxynol-9 might increase the rate of transmission of the HIV virus from infected patients to their partners, increasing the risk of HIV infection.

    Potential adverse reactions reported in the literature with nonoxynol-9 products include contact dermatitis, irritations, and allergic reactions. Description
  Text
  Nonoxynol-9 is a nonionic detergent that is used as a barrier spermicidal contraceptive agent. Nonoxynol-9 may be used alone or in conjunction with a cervical cap, diaphragm, or condom. Barrier methods for pregnancy prevention might not be appropriate for people who cannot use them consistently and correctly because of the relatively higher typical-use failure rates of these methods. Nonoxynol-9 products, when used alone, generally result in an estimated 28 pregnancies per 100 users in a year. The Centers for Disease Control's U.S. Medical Eligibility Criteria describe considerations for risk vs. benefits, including medical conditions or attributes that contraindicate use; these criteria can help prescribing practitioners in product selection for individual patients. There are few restrictions to spermicide use except in individuals at high risk for HIV infection. Spermicide use does not protect against or reduce the transmission of any sexually transmitted disease (STD), including the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Spermicides are available in many formulations for nonprescription purchase.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Nonoxynol-9 interacts causes lysis of the cell membrane causing a rapid loss of function and motility of the spermatozoa, and resulting in sperm death. In addition, the inactive vehicle in some product formulations may act as a mechanical barrier by inhibiting the passage of sperm to the cervical os. When used by itself, a spermicide is considered one of the least effective methods of contraception, and results in an estimated 28 pregnancies per 100 women in a year. Clinical results from controlled studies have demonstrated that nonoxynol 9 is not an effective vaginal microbicide, and it does not protect against the transmission of any sexually transmitted disease. A Phase III efficacy trial reported that nonoxynol-9 enhanced the likelihood of HIV-1 transmission and that there was an increased risk in women who administered the gel more than 3.5 times/day.

Pharmacokinetics
  Text
  Nonoxynol-9 is administered intravaginally. Direct human data for nonoxynol-9 (N-9) absorption via the vaginal wall and other pharmacokinetic characteristics are not available. Animal studies have shown that radiolabeled N-9 is rapidly and extensively absorbed into the systemic circulation from the vaginal mucosa of rats and rabbits. The rate of absorption was dependent upon the vehicle used in the formulation. Once in the systemic circulation, N-9 was efficiently removed by the liver and by the kidney. Most of the intravaginally administered aqueous N-9 is excreted within 3 to 4 days. There were no definitive data regarding the metabolism of N-9, but the researchers noted that other studies have indicated that a small amount of radioactivity (1.2%) is exhaled via the lungs as CO2 within 7 days. The excretion pattern of N-9 showed differences between species in the animal studies. In the rabbit, the major excretory pathway was via the kidney while in the rat it was in the feces. Excretion into the milk of lactating rats, at approximately 0.3% of an intravaginally administered dose, was noted. The applicability of these findings to humans is not completely certain.

  Route Specific Administrations
    Name
    Intravaginal Administration
    Description
    For vaginal use only. No product is to be inserted into the rectum or the urethra.
Instruct patient to carefully read the product instructions before use.
In general, instruct patient not to douche within 6 hours of intercourse to ensure maximum efficacy of the spermicide to prevent pregnancy.
 
Vaginal pre-filled gel single-dose applicators:
Insert 1 applicatorful into the vagina prior to intercourse. Each applicatorful is effective immediately and up to 1 hour after application.
May be applied used along with condoms or with a cervical cap or diaphragm.
An additional applicatorful is required prior to each act of intercourse, regardless of time since last application.
 
Vaginal film:
Be sure fingers are dry before touching the film.
Insert 1 film and place as far into the vagina as possible, against the cervix.
Insert not less than 15 minutes, and not more than 3 hours, before intercourse.
An additional film is required prior to each act of intercourse, regardless of time since last application.
 
Vaginal contraceptive sponge (e.g., Today sponge):
Moisten sponge with clean water as directed in package labeling and compress to activate contraceptive foam.
Insert 1 sponge intravaginally, against the cervix, up to, but not exceeding, 24 hours prior to intercourse. The sponge maintains activity, regardless of number of acts of intercourse, for up to 24 hours, as long as it remains in place.
Leave sponge in place for at least 6 hours after the last act of intercourse. Do not leave sponge in vagina for longer than 30 hours. Do not reuse the sponge. A new sponge should be prepared and inserted prior to the next act.
