Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Boric acid products are contraindicated for oral administration; boric acid should never be administered orally. Boric acid products containing benzalkonium chloride are contraindicated in patients with known benzalkonium chloride hypersensitivity.Patients need to remove contact lenses before administering boric acid ophthalmic solutions that contain the preservative benzalkonium chloride. Benzalkonium chloride may be absorbed by soft contact lenses. Advise patients to wait approximately 5 minutes before reinserting contact lenses.Boric acid should never be administered to infants and children younger than 3 years of age (see Adverse Reactions). Topical administration of boric acid powder to diaper dermatitis has caused toxic reactions and fatalities in infants. In one case, a 27-day-old infant presented with convulsions, irritability, oliguria, and 'lobster-red' skin after being treated with boric acid powder 10—15 times within 48 hours for diaper dermatitis. Peritoneal dialysis yielded 752 milligrams of boric acid. After 17 days, the infant was discharged with hypertonicity and diffuse abnormal electroencephalogram irregularities. Another case reported cerebral edema and fatty infiltration of the liver in a 3.5-month-old infant who died after presenting with seizures following treatment of his diaper rash with pure boric acid powder every 2 hours for 5 days.Systemic absorption of topical boric acid may result in severe poisonings and fatalities. Conditions which increase systemic absorption include use over large surface areas, prolonged use, use in areas where the epidermal barrier is disrupted (i.e., skin abrasion), and the use of an occlusive dressing.Self-treatment with topical boric acid should not extend beyond 4 weeks for dermatophytosis (athlete's foot) or ringworm or 2 weeks for tinea cruris (jock itch). If symptoms persist beyond these time periods, instruct patients to consult a health care professional.A specific FDA pregnancy risk category has not been assigned to topical boric acid products. There is inadequate evidence available to determine risk to the fetus when used in pregnant women. Topical drug preparations generally carry less risk than systemically administered medications. Until further information is known, avoid use of topical boric acid preparations in pregnant women unless the benefit outweighs the potential risk. Boric acid vaginal suppositories should not be used during pregnancy.It is not known if boric acid is excreted in breast milk; there are no reports describing the use of topical boric acid during breast-feeding. Topical boric acid products should not be used to soothe irritated skin on the breast or nipple, or to any area of the skin to which the infant could potentially be exposed, in women who are breast-feeding. Use of boric acid in breast-feeding women should be done only if the potential benefit outweighs the potential risk to the infant.

    General Information
    Boric acid suppositories are not to be used by people who have never had a vaginal yeast infection diagnosed by a doctor. Potential users should ask their health care team before nonprescription boric acid vaginal use if they have vaginal itching and discomfort for the first time, if they have lower abdominal pain, back or shoulder pain, fever, chills, nausea/vomiting, or foul-smelling vaginal discharge since these symptoms may indicate a need for medical treatment. The individual should also consult their provider before use if they have recurrent vaginal yeast infection (such as once a month or 3 infections within 6 months), or if they have diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, pelvic inflammatory disease, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection or other sexually transmitted disease. These people require medical evaluation before use as treatment durations and recommendations may differ from the nonprescription/homeopathic product labeling. Advise treated individuals to stop nonprescription use if symptoms do not get better in 3 days, symptoms last more than 7 days, or if they get a rash or hives, abdominal pain, fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, or foul-smelling vaginal discharge as these can be signs and symptoms of a more serious condition.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: Topical application of boric acid, in concentrations of 5% or less, to intact skin exerts minimal toxicity. However, when applied repeatedly, in higher concentrations, and/or large amounts to wounds, burns, abrasions, denuded, or macerated skin, or when applied to large areas of the skin, severe poisoning and fatalities have occurred. There is a higher incidence of toxicity in infants and children under the age of 3 years.Signs and symptoms of acute toxicity following ingestion or excessive percutaneous or transmucosal absorption of boric acid may include: nausea/vomiting; diarrhea; abdominal pain; hemorrhagic gastroenteritis; rash (unspecified) with erythema followed by exfoliative dermatitis; CNS stimulation (weakness, lethargy, headache, restlessness, tremor, twitching of facial muscles and extremities, and seizures) followed by CNS depression; metabolic acidosis; intravascular coagulation; and fever. Severe and fatal poisoning with boric acid may manifest as oliguria, anuria, renal tubular necrosis, hepatomegaly, jaundice, cyanosis, shock, circulatory collapse, and death.Adverse reactions following administration of boric acid ophthalmic solution include: mild ocular pain or visual impairment and ocular irritation.
  Adverse Reactions

    General Information
    Generally, boric acid vaginal products are not associated with significant adverse effects when used for short courses of treatment; some vaginal discharge is to be expected with use. A mild increase in vaginal burning, local erythema, vaginal itching or vaginal irritation may occur. Individuals may wish to wear a panty liner due to potential watery vaginal discharge with treatment. Allergic reactions such as pruritus, urticaria, rash, and skin irritation may occur with boric acid use. If these occur, discontinue use.

Description
  Text
  Boric acid is available as an ophthalmic solution and a vaginal suppository. The ophthalmic solution is used as an irrigation (eye wash) for irritated eyes and to remove foreign materials from the eyes. Vaginal suppositories are marketed without a prescription to rebalance vaginal pH and relieve symptoms such as vaginal odor. Homeopathic vaginal products that are marketed have not been formally evaluated by the FDA for safety and efficacy. Some guidelines suggest boric acid vagina suppositories may be considered for selected vulvovaginal candidiasis or bacterial vaginosis infections, particularly in refractory cases. For many decades, boric acid vaginal suppositories were compounded; commercial products were initially marketed for nonprescription use in 2019.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The alteration of vaginal pH may be the mechanism by which boric acid is purported to relieve vaginal odor. Boric acid is a weak topical anti-infective agent with fungistatic and bacteriostatic properties. The exact mechanisms of boric acids antibacterial and antifungal actions are unclear. Boric acid exerts slow-acting bacteriostatic activity against staphylococci and streptococci. There is some evidence, specific to vaginal use, suggesting that the fungistatic activity of boric acid is due to vaginal acidification, which results in fungal cell wall penetration and disruption of the fungal cell membrane. Alternatively, other evidence suggests that boric acid works at a pH similar to that of the healthy, untreated vaginal tract, and, therefore, the activity of boric acid may not be a result of increased vaginal acidity.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Other Route(s)
    Description
    Vaginal Route
Following intravaginal administration of boric acid suppositories, there is minimal systemic absorption (6%) from the vagina. After a 600 mg dose vaginally per day for 1 to 2 weeks in healthy volunteers (n=8), daily blood boron concentrations of less than 1 mcg/mL were reported, No subject had detectable boric acid levels 48 hours after the end of treatment. Boron blood levels below 200 mcg/mL are thought to be safe, with usual human blood boron levels ranging from 0.1 to 80 mcg/mL . Increased systemic absorption would be expected if the user had damaged vaginal epithelial tissue.
  Text
  Boric acid is administered vaginally. Boric acid is not significantly absorbed or metabolized following vaginal administration. About 50% of any systemically absorbed drug following vaginal administration is excreted unchanged in the urine within 24 hours. The half-life of boric acid is approximately 10.5 hours following a single vaginal 600 mg dose. Nearly total excretion is found in the urine within 96 hours.
