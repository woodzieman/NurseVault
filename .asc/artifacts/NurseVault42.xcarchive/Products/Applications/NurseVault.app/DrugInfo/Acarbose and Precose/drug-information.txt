Drug Class: Acarbose is an alpha-glucosidase inhibitor used for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in adults.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: Weight 60 kg or less: 150 mg/day PO

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): borborygmi, ileus, pneumatosis cystoides intestinalis, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatitis, jaundice, anemia, thrombocytopenia, weight loss, edema, eosinophilia, erythema, erythema multiforme, rash (unspecified), urticaria

In an Overdose: Unlike other antidiabetic medications, the mechanism of action of acarbose does not result in hypoglycemia, even in overdosage.

Indication Specific Dosing

For the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus as an adjunct to diet and exercise

Oral dosage
Adults weighing more than 60 kg


25 mg PO 3 times daily, initially, or alternately, 25 mg PO once daily to minimize gastrointestinal side effects. May increase dose to 50 mg PO 3 times daily and then 100 mg PO 3 times daily every 4 to 8 weeks based on 1-hour post-prandial glucose or HbA1c if needed. Max: 100 mg PO 3 times daily. Coadministration of certain drugs may need to be avoided or dosage adjustments may be necessary; review drug interactions.

Contraindications And Precaution
Drug Interactions

The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.

Hypersensitivity

This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.

diabetic ketoacidosis


Acarbose is contraindicated in people with diabetic ketoacidosis.


hepatic cirrhosis


Acarbose is contraindicated in individuals with hepatic cirrhosis. Perform liver function tests (LFTs) prior to the initiation of acarbose treatment, every 3 months during the first year of therapy, and periodically thereafter in all patients. If persistent elevations in LFTs, jaundice, or hepatitis are observed, a dosage reduction or the discontinuation of acarbose may be indicated.


inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal obstruction, malabsorption syndrome


Acarbose is contraindicated in patients with inflammatory bowel disease, colonic ulceration (e.g., ulcerative colitis), partial intestinal obstruction or in people predisposed to intestinal obstruction. Acarbose is contraindicated in people who have chronic intestinal diseases associated with marked disorders of digestion or absorption (malabsorption syndrome) and in people who have conditions that may deteriorate as a result of increased gas formation in the intestine.


pregnancy


Acarbose should be used during pregnancy only if clearly needed. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of acarbose during human pregnancy to determine a drug-associated risk of adverse developmental outcomes. Acarbose doses up to 9 and 32 times the human dose in rats and rabbits, respectively, were not teratogenic in either species. No embryotoxic effects have been noted. Poorly controlled diabetes during pregnancy also increases fetal risk. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommend human insulin as the standard of care in pregnant people with diabetes mellitus and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) requiring medical therapy; insulin does not cross the placenta.


breast-feeding


Acarbose is considered compatible with breast-feeding. Although it is unknown if acarbose is present in human milk, it is unlikely that any medication reaches the breast-fed child due to poor bioavailability. Less than 2% of an acarbose dose is systemically absorbed from the GI tract of treated individuals.


renal failure, renal impairment


Acarbose is not recommended in individuals with severe renal impairment or renal failure CrCl less than 25 mL/minute or serum creatinine more than 2 mg/dL) since it has not been extensively studied in these populations.

Interactions

Acebutolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

In large doses, salicylates uncouple oxidative phosphorylation, deplete hepatic and muscle glycogen, and cause hyperglycemia and glycosuria. After acute overdose, aspirin can cause either hypo- or hyperglycemia. Large doses of aspirin should be used cautiously in patients receiving antidiabetic agents.

Beta-blockers: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

After acute overdose, aspirin can cause either hypo- or hyperglycemia. Large doses of aspirin should be used cautiously in patients receiving antidiabetic agents.
Chromium: (Moderate) Chromium dietary supplements may lower blood glucose. As part of the glucose tolerance factor molecule, chromium appears to facilitate the binding of insulin to insulin receptors in tissues and to aid in glucose metabolism. Because blood glucose may be lowered by the use of chromium, patients who are on antidiabetic agents may need dose adjustments. Close monitoring of blood glucose is recommended.
Ciclesonide: (Moderate) Monitor patients receiving antidiabetic agents closely for worsening glycemic control when corticosteroids are instituted and for signs of hypoglycemia when corticosteroids are discontinued. The main risk factors for impaired glucose tolerance due to corticosteroids are the dose of steroid and duration of treatment. Ciprofloxacin: (Moderate) Monitor blood glucose carefully when systemic quinolones and antidiabetic agents, including alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, are coadministered. Discontinue the quinolone if a hypoglycemic reaction occurs and initiate appropriate therapy immediately. cloNIDine: (Minor) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when clonidine is given with antidiabetic agents. Since clonidine inhibits the release of catecholamines, clonidine may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. cloZAPine: (Moderate) Atypical antipsychotic therapy may aggravate diabetes mellitus and cause metabolic changes such as hyperglycemia. Changes in glucose tolerance occur more commonly in patients receiving 50 mcg or more of ethinyl estradiol (or equivalent) per day in combined oral contraceptives (COCs), which are not commonly used in practice since the marketing of lower dose COCs, patches, injections and rings. The presence or absence of a concomitant progestin may influence the significance of any hormonal effect on glucose homeostasis.

Garlic, Allium sativum: (Moderate) Patients receiving antidiabetic agents should use dietary supplements of Garlic, Allium sativum with caution. Constituents in garlic might have some antidiabetic activity, and may increase serum insulin levels and increase glycogen storage in the liver. Monitor blood glucose and glycemic control. Patients with diabetes should inform their health care professionals of their intent to ingest garlic dietary supplements. Some patients may require adjustment to their hypoglycemic medications over time. One study stated that additional garlic supplementation (0.05 to 1.5 grams PO per day) contributed to improved blood glucose control in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus within 1 to 2 weeks, and had positive effects on total cholesterol and high/low density lipoprotein regulation over time. It is unclear if hemoglobin A1C is improved or if improvements are sustained with continued treatment beyond 24 weeks. Other reviews suggest that garlic may provide modest improvements in blood lipids, but few studies demonstrate decreases in blood glucose in diabetic and non-diabetic patients. More controlled trials are needed to discern if garlic has an effect on blood glucose in patients with diabetes. When garlic is used in foods or as a seasoning, or at doses of 50 mg/day or less, it is unlikely that blood glucose levels are affected to any clinically significant degree.

Labetalol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

The presence or absence of a concomitant progestin may influence the significance of any hormonal effect on glucose homeostasis. (Minor) Progestins can impair glucose tolerance. Patients receiving antidiabetic agents should be closely monitored for signs indicating changes in diabetic control when therapy with progestins is instituted or discontinued.

(Moderate) Thiazide diuretics can decrease insulin sensitivity thereby leading to glucose intolerance and hyperglycemia. Diuretic-induced hypokalemia may also lead to hyperglycemia. Because of this, a potential pharmacodynamic interaction exists between thiazide diuretics and antidiabetic agents. It appears that the effects of thiazide diuretics on glycemic control are dose-related and low doses can be instituted without deleterious effects on glycemic control. In addition, diuretics reduce the risk of stroke and cardiovascular disease in patients with diabetes. Metoprolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Adverse Reaction
General Information

Unlike other antidiabetic medications, the mechanism of action of acarbose does not result in hypoglycemia, even in overdosage. However, because acarbose may be combined with other antidiabetic medications and may lower blood glucose, the other medications may need dosage adjustment if hypoglycemia occurs.
 
Mild hypocalcemia and low plasma pyridoxine (vitamin B6) levels have been associated with acarbose therapy but have been thought to have been spurious or of no clinical significance.

abdominal pain, borborygmi, diarrhea, flatulence, ileus, pneumatosis cystoides intestinalis


Most adverse reactions to acarbose are gastrointestinal, occurring with the highest incidence during initiation of therapy. Most of these symptoms abate or decrease in intensity with continued use. Side effects from acarbose rarely cause non-compliance or drug discontinuation, even though roughly 56% to 76% of patients receiving acarbose experience gastrointestinal adverse reactions. These are caused primarily by an increase in gas formation secondary to fermentation of unabsorbed carbohydrate in the large intestine. Adverse reactions include increased flatulence (up to 74% of patients vs. 29% receiving placebo), abdominal pain (reported as discomfort in 19% vs. 9% receiving placebo), and diarrhea (31% vs. 12% receiving placebo). Borborygmi may be reported along with flatulence. Additionally, in world-wide post-marketing surveillance, ileus, sub ileus, and pneumatosis cystoides intestinalis have been reported. Gastrointestinal symptoms may be worse in patients that do not follow their prescribed diabetic diets. In clinical trials, the use of antacids or fibrous substances to modify the adverse GI side effects of acarbose was not successful. Proper dosage titration may help improve patient tolerance of GI related adverse events; however, if strongly distressing symptoms develop despite adherence to a prescribed diabetes diet, the prescriber must be consulted and the dose temporarily or permanently reduced.


elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatitis, jaundice


Systemic adverse events with acarbose are relatively rare. In one U.S. study, asymptomatic elevated hepatic enzymes occurred in 3.8% of patients receiving acarbose versus 0.9% with placebo. In long-term studies (up to 12 months), treatment-emergent elevations of serum transaminases (AST and/or ALT) above the upper limit of normal (ULN), greater than 1.8 times the ULN, and greater than 3 times the ULN occurred in 14%, 6%, and 3%, respectively, of acarbose-treated patients and at greater incidences than with placebo. Although these differences between treatments were statistically significant, these elevations were asymptomatic, reversible, more common in females, and, in general, were not associated with other evidence of liver dysfunction. Serum transaminase elevations appeared to be dose related. In US studies including acarbose doses up to the maximum approved dose of 100 mg three times per day, treatment-emergent elevations of AST and/or ALT at any level of severity were similar between acarbose-treated patients and placebo-treated patients. In approximately 3 million patient-years of international post-marketing experience with acarbose, 62 cases of serum transaminase elevations more than 500 units/L (29 of which were associated with jaundice) have been reported. Forty-one of these 62 patients received treatment with 100 mg 3 times per day or greater and 33 of 45 patients for whom weight was reported weighed less than 60 kg. In the 59 cases where follow-up was recorded, hepatic abnormalities improved or resolved upon discontinuation of acarbose in 55 patients and were unchanged in two. Two patients in Japan died of fulminant hepatitis, however the relationship to acarbose is unclear. Additionally, in world-wide post-marketing surveillance, cases of fulminant hepatitis with fatal outcome and jaundice and/or hepatitis and associated liver damage, have been reported.


anemia, thrombocytopenia, weight loss


Abnormal laboratory findings have included small reductions in hematocrit, without associated reductions in hemoglobin. Anemia (either iron deficiency or unspecified) has been reported in roughly 1% of patients. Decreased iron absorption from the gut has been reported in some clinical studies. One case report has described a patient who experienced iron deficiency anemia and a 7 kg weight loss which resolved upon discontinuation of acarbose. Clinically, dramatic weight loss is not commonly associated with acarbose, but has been noted frequently in animal studies. Weight loss, if it occurs, is typically mild (i.e., 0.8 to 1.4 kg over 1 year in clinical studies). In humans, acarbose appears to offset the insulinotropic effects and weight gain associated with sulfonylurea treatment when it is added to sulfonylurea therapy. Several metabolic or pharmacologic mechanisms have been proposed for the development of acarbose-induced weight loss. During post-marketing surveillance, thrombocytopenia was reported during acarbose use.


edema, eosinophilia, erythema, erythema multiforme, rash (unspecified), urticaria


Acarbose has been associated with a rare report of serum eosinophilia associated with the presence of generalized erythema multiforme and histologic tissue eosinophilia. Rechallenge has confirmed acarbose as the mediator of this allergic event in this case. In addition, hypersensitivity reactions including rash (unspecified), edema, erythema, exanthema, and urticaria have been reported during post-marketing surveillance.


laboratory test interference


A laboratory test interference may occur during acarbose treatment. Monitoring of glycemic control with urine glucose tests and the 1,5 Anhydroglucitol assay (1,5-AG assay) is not recommended in patients receiving acarbose. Use of urine glucose tests will result in positive urine glucose tests and measurements of 1,5-AG are unreliable. Use alternative methods to monitor glycemic control.


Description

Acarbose is an alpha-glucosidase inhibitor used for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in adults. In contrast to miglitol, acarbose has minimal systemic absorption. Acarbose has been shown to effectively lower postprandial blood glucose when administered alone or in combination with insulin, metformin, or an oral sulfonylurea. At recommended doses, agents in this class have only modest effects on A1C levels (i.e., mean reduction, 0.5% to 1.5%), and they mostly modify postprandial blood glucose. Although not FDA-approved, acarbose reduces the risk of future diabetes by up to 25%. First-line T2DM therapy depends on comorbidities, individual-centered treatment factors, and management needs. In individuals with T2DM with or at high risk for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), heart failure (HF), and/or chronic kidney disease (CKD), certain glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) or sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors with proven benefit are appropriate initial therapy, with or without metformin based on glycemic needs. In individuals with T2DM who do not have ASCVD/indicators of high-risk, HF, or CKD, acarbose may be considered as a therapeutic option, particularly for individuals needing adjunct postprandial glucose control. Acarbose was initially FDA-approved in 1995.

Mechanism Of Action

Acarbose is an inhibitor of the alpha-glucosidases present in the brush-border of the enterocytes located in the proximal portion of the small intestine. Inhibition is highest for glucoamylase, followed by sucrase, maltase, and dextranase, respectively. There is also a minor inhibition of pancreatic alpha-amylase and isomaltose, but acarbose does not inhibit lactase. Acarbose inhibits sucrase, which prevents the conversion of sucrose to fructose and glucose, whereas inhibition of glucoamylase, maltase, and isomaltose results in delayed digestion of starch. Since alpha-glucosidases are responsible for breaking down carbohydrates, alpha-glucosidase inhibition results in delayed carbohydrate digestion and absorption throughout the small intestine. Clinically, due to delayed intraluminal production of monosaccharides (i.e., glucose), postprandial plasma glucose increases are delayed and prolonged, and plasma insulin response is blunted. The drug does not inhibit the absorption of glucose (D-glucose or dextrose), which is controlled by an intestinal sodium-dependent glucose transporter. Acarbose has minimal effects on fasting glucose and causes modest decreases in hemoglobin A1C. Acarbose does not enhance insulin secretion when used as monotherapy. Acarbose overdose will not result in hypoglycemia.
 
Acarbose must be administered at the start of a meal for maximal efficacy. Alpha-glucosidase inhibitors are competitive inhibitors; they must be present at the site of enzymatic action at the same time the carbohydrates are present in the small intestine. The affinity of the alpha-glucosidase inhibitors for the alpha-glucosidase receptors is much greater than that of the oligo- or di-saccharides in foods. Taking acarbose more than 15 minutes after the start of the meal reduces the impact of the medication on post-prandial blood glucose. To be clinically effective, the patient must be consuming a diet high in complex carbohydrates ( roughly 50% of calories or more).
 
Acarbose was shown not to have any significant effects on body weight. Due to polysaccharide metabolism by colonic microflora and the capacity of the large bowel to conserve calories, there is minimal calorie loss associated with acarbose therapy, although acarbose does appear to increase intestinal transit times. In some studies, alpha-glucosidase inhibitors are associated with decreases in triglyceride levels and iron absorption, however, it is not clear if these effects are clinically significant.

Pharmacokinetics

Acarbose is administered orally. At least 13 metabolites have been identified; approximately 34% of the major metabolite fractions are absorbed systemically and appear in the urine as sulfate, methyl, and glucuronide conjugates. One metabolite, which is formed by the cleavage of a glucose molecule from acarbose, has alpha-glucosidase inhibitory effects. This active metabolite, along with the parent compound, are recovered in the urine and account for only 2% of the total administered dose. Most of an acarbose dose (i.e., 51%), as expected, is excreted through the feces. Plasma elimination half-life is about 2 hours in healthy adults.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP450) isoenzymes and drug transporters: None

Route-Specific Pharmacokinetics
Oral Route

Systemic oral absorption of active acarbose is only about 2%. Low systemic absorption of acarbose is therapeutically desired, given the mechanism of action occurs locally in the GI tract. Acarbose is metabolized within the GI tract; metabolism occurs principally by intestinal microbial flora, intestinal hydrolysis, and the activity of digestive enzymes.

Hepatic Impairment

The pharmacokinetics of acarbose have not been studied in patients with cirrhosis.

Renal Impairment

Patients with severe renal impairment (i.e., CrCl less than 25 mL/minute/1.73 m2) attain systemic peak and AUC concentrations of acarbose that are roughly 5 to 6 times higher than patients with normal renal function.
