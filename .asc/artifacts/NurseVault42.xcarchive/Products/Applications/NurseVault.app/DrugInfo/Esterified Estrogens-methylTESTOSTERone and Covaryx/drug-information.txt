Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone combinations are contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to the drugs or any components of the formulations.
 
Estrogens are contraindicated in patients with known, suspected, or history of breast cancer. Estrogen and estrogen/progestin therapy in postmenopausal women has been associated with an increased risk of breast cancer. In the past, high-dose estrogen therapy was used in selected men and postmenopausal women with inoperable, progressive cancer of the breast, but this therapy is rarely used today. Since 1970, numerous epidemiological studies have examined the association of exogenous estrogen and breast cancer. Epidemiologic studies have reported an increased risk of breast cancer in association with increasing duration of postmenopausal treatment with estrogens, with or without progestin. This association was reanalyzed in original data from 51 studies that involved treatment with various doses and types of estrogens, with and without progestin. In the reanalysis, an increased risk of having breast cancer diagnosed became apparent after about 5 years of continued treatment, and subsided after treatment had been discontinued for about 5 years. Some later studies have suggested that treatment with estrogen and progestin increases the risk of breast cancer more than treatment with estrogen alone. In the estrogen-progestin sub-study of the WHI trial , there were more diagnoses of breast cancer in the HRT group compared to the placebo group, with a hazard ratio of 1.24. The breast cancers diagnosed in the HRT group had similar histology and grade to those found in the placebo group, but the women receiving HRT were more likely to be diagnosed with a more invasive cancer (hazard ratio 1.24) that was larger in size (1.7 cm HRT vs. 1.5 cm placebo), node positive (25.9% HRT vs. 15.8% placebo), and diagnosed at a more advanced stage (regional/metastatic 25.4% HRT vs. 16% placebo) compared to those who received placebo. The increased risk of breast cancer became apparent after 4 years on combined HRT. Women reporting prior postmenopausal use of estrogen and/or estrogen with progestin had a higher relative risk for breast cancer associated with combined HRT than those who had never used these hormones. In the estrogen-only sub-study of WHI, NO increased risk of breast cancer in estrogen-treated women compared to placebo was found, even in women who used estrogen replacement therapy for 25 years or longer. Furthermore, the hazards ratio for invasive breast cancer in women taking estrogen only versus placebo after a mean follow-up of 7.1 years was 0.8 (95% CI 0.62—1.04, P=0.09) in favor of estrogen therapy. In addition to the associated increase risk of breast cancer, at least 1 abnormal mammogram was discovered in a total of 31.5% of women in the combined HRT group compared to 21.2% of placebo patients, with an absolute increase in abnormal mammograms of about 4% per year in women receiving combined HRT; similarly, in women receiving estrogen only, 36.2% of women had mammograms with abnormalities during the trial, compared to only 28.1% of women receiving placebo (p 10 years) of post-menopausal administration of estrogens for HRT. Estrogens are contraindicated in women with undiagnosed abnormal vaginal bleeding. Women experiencing persistent abnormal vaginal bleeding should receive adequate diagnostic tests to rule out malignancy before being prescribed esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone combinations. All women receiving esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone treatment should have an annual pelvic examination which includes a Papanicolaou smear to screen for cervical dysplasia and other abnormalities.
 
Esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone combinations are contraindicated in the presence of hepatocellular cancer, hepatic adenoma, or in severe hepatic disease of any type. Both estrogens and androgens should be used cautiously in patients with acute intermittent, or variegate hepatic porphyria, which can be exacerbated. Exogenous orally administered estrogens may increase the risk of gallbladder disease (e.g., cholestasis, cholelithiasis and cholecystitis) in postmenopausal women. Based on evidence from the HERS trial, the risk of gallbladder disease may be more prevalent in those postmenopausal women with established coronary heart disease who receive oral estrogens. Patients with familial hyperlipoproteinemia may develop elevations in triglycerides while taking exogenous estrogens which may predispose them to pancreatitis; caution is warranted in these individuals. Methyltestosterone administration has been associated with peliosis hepatis, hepatocellular cancer, and the development of cholestatic hepatitis or jaundice. If liver function tests become abnormal during esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone therapy, discontinue the medication.
 
In some patients, blood pressure may increase during therapy with estrogen therapy. Patients with hypertension should be monitored closely for increases in blood pressure if estrogens are administered. Because both androgens and estrogens may cause fluid retention, conditions that might be affected by this factor, such as asthma, heart disease, renal disease, migraine, or seizure disorder require careful observation while on esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone treatment.
 
Although the effects appear to be minimal, altered glucose tolerance secondary to decreased insulin sensitivity has been reported in some patients receiving hormone replacement therapy with estrogens/androgens. Patients with hyperglycemia or diabetes mellitus should be observed for changes in glucose tolerance when initiating or discontinuing esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone therapy.
 
Estrogens are contraindicated in patients with an active or past history of stroke, myocardial infarction, thrombophlebitis, or thromboembolic disease, especially if related to past estrogen use. Estrogens have been associated with thromboembolic disease such as deep venous thrombosis (DVT). Because tobacco smoking increases the risk of DVT, myocardial infarction, and other thromboembolic disease, estrogen-containing treatment should be used cautiously in smokers. Risk is especially high for female smokers 35 years of age or older. Patients should be advised not to smoke. Furthermore, estrogens should not be used for the prevention of cardiac disease. The Women's Health Initiative (WHI) study reported increased risks of myocardial infarction, stroke, pulmonary emboli, and deep vein thrombosis in postmenopausal women during 5 years of treatment with estrogen-progestin combination therapy. Pre-existing heart disease or cardiac risk factors, such as coronary artery disease, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, morbid obesity, or diabetes mellitus may increase risks, and these patients should be observed closely. Estrogens should be discontinued in any woman developing signs or symptoms indicative of thromboembolic or cerebrovascular disease. Because certain major surgical procedures are associated with prolonged immobilization or increased risk of thromboembolism, estrogens should be discontinued several weeks prior to major surgery where feasible. The decision on when to resume estrogens after surgery would be based on the perceived additional thromboembolic risk from estrogen use and the need for estrogen therapy.
 
Approximately 85% of patients diagnosed with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) are females, giving support to the notion that hormonal influences, especially estrogen, contribute to the pathophysiology of SLE. Accordingly, hormone replacement therapy (HRT) has been reported to induce, unmask, and exacerbate lupus; case reports, anecdotal data, and the prospective Nurses Health Study indicate that a temporal relationship between HRT and lupus exist. However, several retrospective studies dispute a relationship between estrogens and lupus, and the SELENA trial, a large prospective, randomized clinical trial evaluating the safety of estrogen therapy (both as oral contraceptives and HRT in postmenopausal women) in patients with SLE has been completed and is being analyzed. Determining the risk of estrogen therapy in SLE patients is important as postmenopausal women with lupus can benefit from HRT; not only does it offer relief from postmenopausal symptoms (vasomotor symptoms, genital symptoms, and emotional lability), but it has the additional benefit of protecting patients from bone fracture and postmenopausal or drug-induced (i.e., chronic corticosteroid or cyclophosphamide therapy) osteoporosis. Women with hypercoagulable states are at increased risk of venous thromboembolism when taking HRT; given the increased prevalence of hypercoagulable states in patients with SLE (in particular antiphospholipid antibodies), the use of HRT in this population may be even more risky as the incidence of strokes, heart attacks, and blood clots is increased in general in women taking HRT. Unfortunately, definitive recommendations regarding the use of HRT in patients with SLE are not available. The results of the SELENA trial should provide evidence regarding the use of HRT in this population.
 
Mood disorders, like depression, may be aggravated in women taking exogenous estrogens or androgens. Women with a history of depression may need special monitoring. If significant depression occurs, the hormone replacement therapy should be discontinued.
 
Estrogens can increase the curvature of the cornea and may lead to intolerance of contact lenses. There have been clinical case reports of retinal thrombosis. Any change in vision or visual acuity should be examined by an ophthalmologist. Hormonal agents should be discontinued in patients developing any unexplained visual disturbance.
 
Estrogens and androgens are not indicated in children because they promote epiphysial closure. Use with caution in those patients in whom bone growth is not complete. In young children, overdoses of estrogen-androgen combinations have not been reported to cause serious ill effects. However, nausea is common. Vaginal withdrawal bleeding may occur in female children exposed to estrogens in large doses.
 
Esterified estrogens and methyltestosterone are contraindicated during pregnancy and are labeled FDA pregnancy risk category X. Estrone and other estrogens freely cross the placenta to the fetus. Increased risk of a wide variety of fetal abnormalities, including modified development of sexual organs, cardiovascular anomalies and limb defects, have been reported following the use of estrogens in pregnant women. Methyltestosterone may cause virilization of the female fetus, including structural changes in the external and internal female genitalia. In any patient in whom pregnancy is suspected, pregnancy should be ruled out before continuing esterified estrogen-methyltestosterone use. There is no known indication for the use of esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone during pregnancy.
 
Esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone combinations are contraindicated in lactating women who are breast-feeding their infants. Estrogens are distributed into breast milk and may interfere with proper lactation. Androgens, if distributed into breast-milk, could have unwanted effects on the nursing infant. It should be noted that estrogens are not approved by the FDA for the treatment of postpartum breast engorgement.
 
Esterified estrogen; methyltestosterone combinations are not indicated for use in male patients at this time. Methyltestosterone may stimulate the growth of cancerous tissue and should not be used in male patients with either breast or prostate cancer. Male patients with symptoms of prostatic hypertrophy that have not been medically evaluated should not be treated with methyltestosterone because of the possible presence of undiagnosed cancer.
 
Hormone replacement therapy (HRT), both estrogen/progestin combination therapy and estrogen alone therapy, has been found to fail to prevent mild cognitive impairment (memory loss) and to increase the risk of dementia in women 65 years and older. The WHIMS study, an ancillary study of the WHI trial to assess the effects of HRT on cognitive function in elderly women (65 years of age or older), found that patients receiving either active treatment or placebo had similar rates of developing mild cognitive impairment. Also, patients receiving combination or estrogen only HRT were more likely than patients receiving placebo to be diagnosed with dementia (pooled hazard ratio 1.76, 95% CI 1.19—2.60, P=0.005). In the population of patients taking combination HRT, ninety percent of the cases of dementia occurred in women older than 70 years with Alzheimer's disease being the most common classification; differences between the 2 treatment groups (combination HRT vs. placebo) were apparent after one year of treatment. When analyzed separately, the risk of dementia was increased in patients taking estrogen only therapy (hazard ratio 1.49, 95% CI 0.83—2.66) and in patients taking combination HRT (hazard ratio 2.05, 95% CI 1.21—3.48); however, this finding did not reach statistical significance in patients receiving estrogen only. An explanation for this may be because statistical power of the study was diminished as it was stopped early and fewer patients were enrolled than originally planned due to the early discontinuation of the WHI trial. Regardless, a protective effect of HRT was not found. Administration of HRT should be avoided in women 65 years of age and older, and HRT should not be used to prevent or treat dementia or preserve cognition (memory). The applicability of this finding to the typical user of HRT (i.e., younger, symptomatic women taking hormone replacement therapy to relieve menopausal symptoms) is unclear.

    Headline
    cardiovascular disease, hypercholesterolemia, obesity, thromboembolism, thrombophilia, tobacco smoking
    General Information
    Estrogens, with or without progestins, should not be used for the prevention of cardiovascular disease. An increased risk of thromboembolic and serious cardiovascular events [e.g. pulmonary embolism (PE), deep vein thrombosis (DVT), stroke, and myocardial infarction (MI)] are reported with estrogen plus progestin hormone replacement therapy (HRT). Increased risks of stroke and DVT are reported with estrogen-alone HRT. The use of high-dose estrogens for the palliative treatment of cancer of the prostate or breast has also been shown to increase risks for nonfatal MI, PE, and thrombophlebitis. Immediately discontinue the HRT regimen if any of these events occur or are suspected. Due to the potential for serious cardiovascular events, estrogen-containing HRT is contraindicated in people with active thromboembolism (e.g., DVT or PE) or a history of these conditions, or in those with active arterial thromboembolic disease (e.g., stroke and myocardial infarction) or a history of these conditions. HRT regimens are also contraindicated in people with known thrombophilia (e.g., protein C, protein S, or antithrombin deficiency). Individuals with certain risk factors for arterial vascular disease (e.g., cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, tobacco smoking, hypercholesterolemia, and morbid obesity) and/or venous thromboembolism (VTE) (e.g., personal history or family history of VTE, obesity, or systemic lupus erythematosus) should be monitored and managed appropriately during HRT use. Monitor blood pressure in all postmenopausal individuals receiving HRT and especially in those with pre-existing hypertension; in a few case reports, substantial increases in blood pressure have been attributed to idiosyncratic reactions to estrogen HRT therapy. In a large, randomized, placebo controlled clinical trial, a generalized effect of estrogens on blood pressure was not seen. Estrogens and androgens may cause some degree of fluid retention, and people with conditions that may be aggravated, such as cardiac dysfunction or heart failure, warrant careful observation when estrogens and/or androgens are prescribed.
    Headline
    immobility, major surgery, planned procedure
    General Information
    If feasible, estrogen-based hormone replacement therapy (HRT) should be discontinued at least 4 to 6 weeks before major surgery or a planned procedure associated with an increased risk of thromboembolism, or during periods of prolonged immobility. Resume the HRT regimen only after the patient is fully ambulatory. In addition, people treated with HRT should be advised to move about periodically during travel involving prolonged immobilization, such as during long-duration plane travel.
    Headline
    abnormal uterine bleeding, breast cancer, endometrial cancer, estrogen-dependent tumor, increased risk for breast cancer
    General Information
    Esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone hormone replacement therapy (HRT) products are contraindicated in people with a history of or known/suspected breast cancer or at increased risk for breast cancer, except in appropriately selected patients being treated for metastatic disease. Estrogen-based HRT is also contraindicated in people with undiagnosed abnormal uterine bleeding, endometrial cancer, or other estrogen-dependent tumor, which may be hormonally-sensitive. There is an increased risk of endometrial cancer in a person with a uterus who uses unopposed estrogens. Adding a progestin to estrogen HRT has been shown to reduce the risk of endometrial hyperplasia, which may be a precursor to endometrial cancer. Adequate diagnostic measures, including directed or random endometrial sampling when indicated, should be undertaken to rule out malignancy in postmenopausal women with undiagnosed persistent or recurring abnormal genital bleeding. Studies suggest that the use of estrogen-progestin HRT in postmenopausal women increases the risk for invasive breast cancer. The use of estrogen-alone and estrogen plus progestin HRT has been reported to result in an increase in abnormal mammograms, requiring further evaluation. During the Womens Health Initiative (WHI), estrogen-alone was not associated with an increased risk of invasive breast cancer, but an estrogen plus progestin substudy reported increased risks of invasive breast cancer. Consistent with the WHI clinical trials, observational studies have also reported an increased risk of breast cancer for estrogen plus progestin therapy, and a smaller increased risk for estrogen-alone therapy, after several years of use. It is not clear how methyltestosterone influences breast cancer risk when used for HRT. All people taking estrogen with or without a progestin as HRT should receive an annual clinical breast examination, perform monthly self-examinations, and have regular mammograms as recommended by their health care professional based on patient age, risk factors, and prior mammogram results. While estrogen or androgen therapy is rarely used for the palliative treatment of advanced breast cancer, estrogen HRT use may lead to severe hypercalcemia in patients with breast cancer and bone metastases. In patients with breast cancer, androgen therapy may also cause hypercalcemia by stimulating osteolysis. If hypercalcemia occurs, use of HRT should be stopped and appropriate measures taken to reduce the serum calcium level.

    General Information
    Because estrogens influence the growth of endometrial tissues, use esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone cautiously in people with endometriosis or uterine leiomyoma (uterine fibroids). A few cases of malignant transformation of residual endometrial growth have been reported in women treated post-hysterectomy with estrogen therapy without a progestin. For individuals known to have residual endometriosis post-hysterectomy, the addition of a progestin should be considered to reduce the risk of endometrial tissue growth.
    Headline
    acute hepatitis, Budd-Chiari syndrome, decompensated hepatic cirrhosis, hepatic disease, hepatic hemangioma, hepatocellular adenoma, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, porphyria
    General Information
    Esterified estrogens hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is considered contraindicated in people with hepatic failure, impairment or hepatic disease  , and methyltestosterone use is contraindicated in people with severe liver damage. However, expert guidance from the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD), North American Menopause Society (NAMS), and the Menopausal Hormone Therapy (MHT) Eligibility Criteria Group states that systemic estrogen HRT is not contraindicated in all individuals with hepatic disease and may be used in selected patients with appropriate monitoring and with consideration of personalized risk to benefit ratios. Experts generally agree that estrogen-based HRT should not be used in people with decompensated liver function (decompensated hepatic cirrhosis), Budd-Chiari syndrome, acute hepatitis, or hepatocellular adenoma (a benign liver tumor). Some experts state that all individuals with cirrhosis (compensated or decompensated) should avoid systemic estrogen-based HRT, since cirrhosis damages the liver. Postmenopausal individuals with selected liver conditions are at higher risk for progression of fatty liver disease, liver dysfunction, and fibrosis versus those who are premenopausal. There is some clinical trial evidence of slower fibrosis progression in postmenopausal individuals with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) or hepatitis C who were treated with estrogen HRT, presumably due to the positive effects of estrogen on hepatic cellular function and metabolic parameters, but more extensive study is needed to establish the potential benefits and risks in these conditions. Estrogens and/or progestins may be poorly metabolized in those with impaired liver function, and the risk versus benefits of using HRT should be weighed individually for any person with hepatic disease. There is concern about cholestatic effects of estrogens, particularly in people with existing cholestatic liver disease. However, estrogen-containing HRT appears to be safe for individuals with primary biliary cholangitis (PBC). For people with a history of intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy or a history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use of an estrogen, such as cholestatic jaundice, use estrogen-based HRT with caution. If there is a recurrence of cholestatic jaundice, discontinue the HRT regimen. Estrogen HRT may also cause an exacerbation of porphyria or hepatic hemangioma (a benign liver tumor); consider whether the benefits of estrogen HRT outweigh the risks in people with such conditions. Consider whether the benefits of esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone HRT outweigh the risks in people with such conditions. Consider the additional risks that may be posed by methyltestosterone therapy. Prolonged use of high doses of androgens has been associated with the development of peliosis hepatis and hepatic neoplasms, including hepatic cancer. Additionally, because of the hepatotoxicity associated with the use of 17-alpha-alkylated androgens, liver function tests should be obtained periodically in all treated patients.

    General Information
    Estrogen administration leads to increased thyroid-binding globulin (TBG) levels and androgens may cause decreased protein-bound iodine (PBI) concentrations.     General Information
    Administration of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) for menopause should generally be avoided in geriatric adults 65 years of age and older, and HRT should not be used to prevent or treat dementia or preserve cognition (memory). Both estrogen-progestin combination hormone replacement therapy (HRT) and estrogen-alone therapy fail to prevent mild cognitive impairment (memory loss) and increase the risk of dementia in females 65 years of age and older. Overall risk vs. benefit should be considered along with the goals of use of HRT for the individual patient when considering whether to continue HRT if they are over 65 years of age.  The Beers Criteria consider oral, topical patch, or other systemic forms of estrogens (with or without progestins) as potentially inappropriate medications for use in geriatric adults. Prolonged use of high doses of androgens has been associated with the development of peliosis hepatis and hepatic neoplasms, including hepatic cancer. Additionally, because of the hepatotoxicity associated with the use of 17-alpha-alkylated androgens, liver function tests should be obtained periodically in all treated patients.

(Minor) Patients receiving antidiabetic agents should be periodically monitored for changes in glycemic control when hormone therapy is instituted or discontinued.   Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: This monograph discusses the adverse reactions seen with the combined use of esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone for hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in females. Clinicians may wish to consult the monograph for either Esterified estrogens or Methyltestosterone separately for more information about adverse reactions related to the separate use of these medications.Many of the serious adverse reactions reported with the use of estrogens are similar to those reported with the use of estrogen-containing oral contraceptives. The risk of these serious events are typically lower in the postmenopausal use of these drugs, presumably due to the comparatively low estrogen doses used in this population for hormone replacement therapy versus those needed for contraception. Oral esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone combinations have been marketed in the US for menopausal indications since 1964. Post-marketing surveillance data available from the manufacturer for the period of 1989 to 1996, representing over 1-million woman-years of exposure, reveals a low incidence of serious side effects (6.1%) in cases reported. No deaths related to esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone use were reported within this time period.A variety of endocrine and dermatologic effects can occur during therapy with esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone combinations. Changes in sexuality include libido increase, a desirable outcome of combined therapy in many women. Some women will not have changes in libido. Estrogens and androgens can cause a variety of dermatological reactions. Cosmetically undesirable side effects of methyltestosterone, such as acne vulgaris, alopecia, and hirsutism, occur frequently with appropriate physiological replacement; the appearance of these side effects typically responds well to dosage reduction. Either methyltestosterone or estrogens may induce or aggravate acne. Melasma, in the form of tan or brown patches, may develop due to estrogen therapy at sites on the forehead, cheeks, temples and upper lip. These patches may persist after the drug is discontinued. Other dermatologic reactions to these hormones are infrequent and include maculopapular rash, urticaria, erythema nodosum, pruritus, or other erythematous eruptions.Methyltestosterone, if administered in high doses over prolonged periods, may induce clitoromegaly, voice hoarseness, and virilization in women; these effects are rare at normally prescribed dosages for replacement therapy, but may not always be reversible if they occur. Early recognition of virilization and discontinuation of methyltestosterone may increase the chance of reversing masculine side-effects.Serious urogenital adverse effects of estrogen; methyltestosterone therapy are relatively rare. Positive changes in libido may occur as a result of improvements in vulvar and vaginal atrophy in postmenopausal women. Dysmenorrhea and alterations in bleeding patterns are common in women with an intact uterus and include breakthrough bleeding or spotting, decreased menses duration, or menorrhagia. In post-menopausal women, changes in uterine bleeding patterns will usually taper and stabilize within 3—6 months of beginning cyclic or continuous HRT combinations. Amenorrhea is desirable in many postmenopausal women and not considered to be an adverse effect of estrogen therapy. Rarely, vaginal discharge, vaginal irritation, vaginal candidiasis, vaginitis, cervicitis, or changes in cervical erosion may appear. Estrogens may cause enlargement of uterine leiomyomatas (fibroids), if present. Unusual bleeding or spotting that persists beyond 6 months in any woman on estrogen therapy should be evaluated by a health care professional. Women should report unusual bleeding patterns to their health care professionals, and follow current recommendations for annual pelvic examinations including Papanicolaou smears to detect cervical dysplasia.Breast changes that may occur with estrogen therapy include mastalgia and breast tenderness. Women on hormone replacement therapy may also complain of breast enlargement or galactorrhea. Patients should report breast changes, lumps, or breast discharge to their health care professionals, and follow current recommendations for routine mammography and clinical breast examinations.Gastrointestinal effects (i.e., nausea) from esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone administration are frequent but usually limited. Abdominal bloating or cramping, diarrhea, vomiting, or anorexia can occur rarely. Nevertheless, abdominal pain or continued nausea/vomiting can indicate serious estrogen or androgen induced problems such as biliary obstruction, hepatitis, peliosis hepatis, hepatoma, or pancreatitis. Oral estrogens enhance hepatic lipoprotein uptake and inhibit bile acid synthesis, resulting in increased concentration of cholesterol in the bile which can lead to cholestasis and cholelithiasis. Gallbladder disease and cholecystitis are twice as frequent in women taking oral HRT compared with controls. Patients with familial hyperlipoproteinemia may be at greater risk of pancreatitis while on estrogen therapy, which may greatly increase their serum triglycerides. While most case-control studies have shown a relationship between oral contraceptives and the incidence of benign hepatoma and hepatocellular cancer, no such evidence exists at this time for estrogens used in postmenopausal replacement regimens. The incidence of hepatoma or liver cancer is expected to be extremely rare, but the addition of methyltestosterone to estrogen replacement therapy may slightly increase this risk. Esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone should be discontinued in any patient developing elevated hepatic enzymes, jaundice or severe abdominal pain, and the patient should be evaluated.Thromboembolism and thrombus formation are occasional, serious adverse reactions to estrogen therapy, and may include myocardial infarction, deep venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, or stroke. Estrogens decrease levels of antithrombin-III and increase the production of blood clotting factors VII, VIII, IX and X. A positive association has been noted between estrogen dosage and thrombotic disorders. Smoking appears to greatly increase the risk of thrombotic events, and women on estrogens should be advised not to smoke. Since 1970, at least 15 studies have evaluated the effect of estrogen use on stroke risk in women. Some of these studies showed a slightly increased risk for stroke in estrogen recipients while other studies showed a decreased risk. One analysis calculated the pooled estimate of the relative risk for stroke in estrogen recipients to be 0.96. In the Women's Health Initiative (WHI) Trial, an increase in the incidence of strokes were observed in both the estrogen-alone and the estrogen-progestin sub-studies when the active treatment groups were compared to placebo groups. While the details from the estrogen-alone sub-study are preliminary, in the estrogen-progestin sub-study, women in the active treatment group had 8 more strokes per year for every 10,000 women than those taking placebo ; this increase in risk was observed after the first year and persisted. In addition, in the estrogen-progestin sub-study of WHI , an increased risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) events (defined as non-fatal MI and CHD death) was observed with estrogen-progestin HRT compared to placebo. The increase in risk was observed in year one and persisted. In postmenopausal women with documented cardiac disease, a controlled clinical trial (i.e., HERS trial) demonstrated no cardiovascular benefit to using estrogen-progestin HRT as secondary prevention. During an average follow-up of 4.1 years, treatment did not reduce the overall rate of CHD events in postmenopausal women with established coronary artery disease. There were more CHD events in the HRT-treated group than in the placebo group in year 1, but not during the subsequent years. Over two thousand women from the original HERS trial agreed to participate in an open label extension of HERS, known as HERS II. Average follow-up in HERS II was an additional 2.7 years, for a total of 6.8 years overall. Rates of CHD events were comparable among women in the estrogen-progestin HRT group and the placebo group in HERS, HERS II, and overall. In men treated with estrogen for palliation of prostate and breast cancer, estrogens have increased the risks of nonfatal myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, and thrombophlebitis. In the WHI trial, an increase in VTE has been observed in women receiving estrogen compared to placebo. In the estrogen-progestin sub-study of WHI , a 2-fold greater rate of VTE, including DVT and PE, was observed with combined HRT compared to placebo. The increase in VTE risk was observed during the first year and persisted.Estrogens and androgens may cause sodium and fluid retention, resulting in peripheral edema or mild weight gain. This side effect is commonly reported with esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone therapy. In addition, estrogens can slightly increase blood pressure, occasionally causing hypertension. In the PEPI trial, postmenopausal women 45—65 years of age randomized to any hormone replacement therapy regimen experienced increases in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure of 3—5% after the first year of treatment, but the increases were not statistically different from placebo.The relationship of headache, specifically migraine headache, and the administration of estrogens or androgens is not clearly defined; however, headache is a commonly reported side effect with esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone treatment. A number of changes can occur when a woman initiates HRT and include 1) migraines can appear for the first time, 2) a change in frequency, severity and duration of headaches may be seen, or 3) an improvement or decrease in the occurrence of headaches. Some patients with headaches will respond to hormone dosage reduction. When initiating HRT an individual's headache pattern should be observed and, if migraines are present and worsen, consider discontinuing therapy.Emotional lability or nervous system changes occur in some women taking hormonal replacement therapy. These changes can include mental depression, anxiety, fatigue, dizziness, or insomnia. Women with a history of depression may need special monitoring. If significant depression occurs, the hormone replacement therapy should be discontinued.Some women taking esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone combinations may notice tenderness, swelling, or minor bleeding of their gums, which may lead to gingivitis. Proper attention to oral care and regular dental visits are recommended.Ocular disorders can occur during therapy with HRT. These can include optic neuritis, diplopia, loss of vision, or retinal thrombosis. Estrogens can cause keratoconus. A conical cornea develops from steepening or increased curvature of the cornea, caused by thinning of the stroma. Patients with contact lenses may develop intolerance to their lenses. Any change in vision or visual acuity should be examined by an ophthalmologist. Esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone combinations should be discontinued in patients developing any unexplained visual disturbance.Estrogens and androgens are known to cause teratogenesis during pregnancy and are in FDA category X. Increased risk of a wide variety of fetal abnormalities, including modified development of sexual organs, cardiovascular anomalies and limb defects, have been reported following the use of these hormones in pregnant women. Virilization of the female fetus may occur on exposure to androgens. The use of diethylstilbestrol, DES is well known for creating disturbances in the reproductive systems of both male and female offspring; similar disturbances are reported to occur in female offspring of rats exposed to other estrogens during gestation. In any patient in whom pregnancy is suspected, pregnancy should be ruled out before continuing combined esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone use.Esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone combinations can cause impaired carbohydrate metabolism and impaired glucose tolerance, leading to hyperglycemia in some women taking HRT. Limited clinical studies of estrogen regimens have not noted significant alterations in glucose metabolism in healthy post-menopausal women. However, altered glucose tolerance secondary to decreased insulin sensitivity may be important for patients with hyperglycemia or diabetes mellitus. Patients should be observed for changes in glucose tolerance when initiating or discontinuing combined esterified estrogens; methyltestosterone therapy.Unopposed estrogen therapy can promote endometrial hyperplasia in approximately 10% of patients with an intact uterus, increasing the risk of endometrial cancer. In the majority of studies of hormone replacement, the risk of endometrial adenocarcinoma is elevated; the risk of endometrial cancer increases with increasing dose and duration of estrogen use. The pooled estimate of the relative risk compared to women who never used estrogen is 2.31. At diagnosis, endometrial cancers in estrogen recipients are generally of an earlier stage and a lower grade and show less myometrial invasion than tumors in women who have not used estrogen. Thus, while the risk of endometrial carcinoma increases with estrogen use, the risk of dying does not. The World Health Organization, International Agency for Research on Cancer (WHO IARC) classifies combined menopausal HRT as carcinogenic to humans. When progestin therapy is taken for < 10 days per month, the risk of endometrial cancer is increased; however, if the progestin is taken daily in combination with estrogen, the risk of endometrial cancer is similar to that in women who have never used HRT. Furthermore, histologic and clinical data, as well as epidemiologic data, suggest that the addition of a progestin to estrogen therapy offsets, but does not eliminate, the risk of endometrial cancer caused by exogenous estrogen. With concurrent progestin use, the incidence of endometrial hyperplasia due to estrogen administration is estimated to be 1% or less. Women should report unusual vaginal bleeding patterns to their health care professionals, and follow current recommendations for annual pelvic examinations. In some cases endometrial biopsy will be indicated.Numerous epidemiologic studies have examined the effects of estrogen HRT and estrogen-progestin HRT on the development of secondary malignancy (e.g., breast cancer, ovarian cancer) in postmenopausal women (see Precautions/Contraindications). The Women's Health Initiative (WHI) Trial has reported slight increased risks of breast cancer in women taking estrogen-progestin HRT, but not estrogen only HRT. The WHO IARC, classifies combined menopausal HRT as carcinogenic to humans; in current and recent users, the risk of breast cancer increases with duration of use and is higher in women taking combined HRT verus estrogen-only therapy. Another study has reported an increased risk of ovarian cancer in women taking estrogen-only HRT for 10 or more years; short-term use of estrogen-progestin HRT did not increase ovarian cancer risks, but the authors caution that further investigation is needed.Hormone replacement therapy (HRT), both estrogen/progestin combination therapy and estrogen alone therapy, fails to prevent mild impaired cognition (memory loss) and is positively associated with the risk of developing dementia in women 65 years and older (see Precautions/Contraindications). The WHIMS study, an ancillary study of the WHI trial, found that patients receiving either HRT or placebo had similar rates of developing mild cognitive impairment as expected in a population of this age. However, patients receiving HRT were more likely than those receiving placebo to be diagnosed with dementia (pooled hazard ratio 1.76, 95% CI 1.19—2.60, P=0.005). The applicability of this finding to the typical user of HRT (i.e., younger, symptomatic women taking hormone replacement therapy to relieve menopausal symptoms) is unclear. Other adverse events that have been reported include leg cramps, hypocalcemia, and exacerbations of asthma and systemic lupus erythematosus.

Description
  Text
  Esterified estrogens and methyltestosterone are used together to treat moderate to severe vasomotor symptoms associated with menopause in patients not improved by estrogens alone. Esterified estrogens is a mixture of the sodium salts of the sulfate esters of estrogenic substances found in the urine of pregnant mares; the primary active estrogen is sodium estrone sulfate (approximately 80%), the other major component is sodium equilin sulfate (6 to 15%). Esterified estrogens may be produced from natural sources or synthesized. Methyltestosterone is structurally similar to testosterone, except it is methylated at the C-17 position, which increases the oral bioavailability of the hormone. Esterified estrogens and methyltestosterone were first marketed in products prior to the modern FDA drug approval process and are considered unapproved drugs. The FDA has required that all manufacturers of such products submit data supporting safety and effectiveness in order to remain on the market. Final status of these products with respect to FDA approval is pending; some products remain marketed. Guidelines do not address the safety and efficacy of adding an androgen to estrogen therapy to improve response; no long-term safety and efficacy data are available.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The following discussion of mechanism of action refers to the effects of both esterified estrogens and methyltestosterone in menopausal females. Clinicians may wish to refer to the separate monographs for each of the components of this combination in order to learn about the multiple mechanisms of action of these hormones in both sexes.
Esterified estrogens: Esterified estrogens elicit all of the actions of endogenous estrogens. Estrogens are responsible for the growth and development of female sex organs and the maintenance of sex characteristics including growth of axillary and pubic hair, and shaping of body contours and skeleton. At the cellular level, estrogens increase cervical secretions, cause proliferation of the endometrium, and increase uterine tone. Estrogens have a weak anabolic effect and also can affect bone calcium deposition and accelerate epiphysial closure. Estrogens appear to prevent osteoporosis associated with the onset of menopause; they generally do not reverse bone density loss that has already developed. Estrogens generally have a favorable effect on blood lipids, reducing LDL- and increasing HDL-cholesterol concentrations on average, by 15%. Serum triglycerides increase with estrogen administration. Estrogens increase the rate of synthesis of many proteins, including thyroid binding globulin and several clotting factors. Estrogens reduce levels of antithrombin III, and increase platelet aggregation. Estrogens also enhance sodium and fluid retention.
Methyltestosterone: Testosterone and other androgens play an important role in female sexual health; they are normally secreted in small amounts by the ovary and the adrenal cortex in the pre-menopausal female. The exact role(s) of androgens in female sexual health are not fully understood. Androgen peak concentrations in the plasma correlate with the peaks of plasma estrogens at various stages of the menstrual cycle. Disruption of normal androgen secretion in pre-menopausal women may result in menstrual abnormalities. With menopause, declining natural testosterone secretion may contribute to menopausal symptoms. In females, androgens act by either directly binding to androgen receptors or by the aromatization of androgens to estrogens and subsequent interactions at estrogen receptors. At the cellular level in target tissues, androgens stimulate RNA polymerase, increasing protein production. Methyltestosterone appears to improve fatigue, decrease hot-flashes, and increase libido when added to estrogen replacement therapy. The addition of methyltestosterone to estrogen replacement therapy appears to offset the normally beneficial effects on cholesterol fractions imparted by estrogen therapy alone. Methyltestosterone appears to decrease total cholesterol, decrease high-density lipoproteins (HDL) and decrease apolipoprotein A1. Androgens appear to play an important role in bone modeling and remodeling and thus hormone replacement may help maintain bone density post-menopause. Androgens also increase the synthesis of several anticoagulant and fibrinolytic proteins. Methyltestosterone, when administered in excessive doses for the individual woman, may induce masculine changes, such as acne, facial hair growth and hirsutism, alopecia, lowering of voice, and enlargement of the clitoris. As in men, administration of androgens results in anabolic effects, and is often associated with weight gain and fluid retention.

  Text
  Esterified estrogens and methyltestosterone combinations are administered orally. The following information represents the pharmacokinetic parameters of the individual components.
 
Esterified estrogens: Following oral administration, esterified estrogens are cleared by the liver more slowly than endogenous estradiol. Estrone becomes the primary circulating estrogen from esterified estrogens, and serves as a reservoir for the formation of other active estrogens. Some conversion of estrone to estradiol occurs via tissue metabolism. The estrogens are widely distributed and are strongly protein-bound, primarily to albumin and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG). Estrone, estradiol, and other estrogens undergo glucuronide and sulfate conjugation to a variety of minor metabolites which are excreted primarily in the urine. A portion of the conjugates are excreted into the intestine through the biliary system; hydrolysis in the gut allows for enterohepatic recirculation of the estrogens. The terminal half life of estrone is roughly 4—18.5 hours.
Methyltestosterone:  Methyltestosterone in metabolized in the liver via the same pathways as testosterone and is converted to 5alpha-dihydrotestosterone and glucuronide and sulfate conjugates. In females, a portion of testosterone gets converted to estrogen by aromatization in peripheral tissues. Methyltestosterone has an elimination half-life of 2.5—3.5 hours, somewhat longer than that of testosterone. Excretion of methyltestosterone, metabolites, and conjugates is primarily renal. Small amounts of methyltestosterone are eliminated in the feces.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Esterified estrogens are contraindicated in the presence of jaundice or in severe hepatic disease of any type.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
