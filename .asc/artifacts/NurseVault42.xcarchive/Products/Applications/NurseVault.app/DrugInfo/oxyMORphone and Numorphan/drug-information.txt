Drug Class: Oxymorphone is an opioid agonist indicated for the treatment of severe pain that requires an opioid analgesic and for which alternative treatments are inadequate.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: There is no ceiling effect for analgesia with oxymorphone. Clinically, dosage is titrated to provide adequate analgesia and may be limited by adverse reactions.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): agitation, amnesia, anxiety, confusion, depression, dizziness, drowsiness, dysphoria, euphoria, fatigue, hallucinations, headache, insomnia, lethargy, memory impairment, restlessness, seizures, weakness, dyspnea, hypoxia, respiratory depression, dehydration, ileus, opioid-induced esophageal dysfunction, weight loss, neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome, physiological dependence, psychological dependence, tolerance, withdrawal

In an Overdose: Patients may also develop respiratory depression through sub-acute overdose of an opiate agonist in which sedation builds up slowly leading to a decreased respiratory rate and then respiratory failure. Pinpoint pupils are a sign of opioid overdose but are not pathognomonic, as pontine lesions of hemorrhagic or ischemic origin may produce similar findings.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Use oxymorphone cautiously in patients with depression or psychosis. Opioids can cause CNS depression and psychosis (see Adverse Reactions).

  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Reserve opioid analgesics for use in individuals for whom alternative treatment options are ineffective, not tolerated, or would be otherwise inadequate to provide sufficient management of pain.
Individualize dosing; consider severity of pain, clinical response, prior analgesic treatment experience, and risk factors for addiction, abuse, and misuse. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration consistent with individual treatment goals. Reserve titration to higher doses for individuals in whom lower doses are insufficient and in whom expected benefits of using a higher dose opioid clearly outweigh substantial risks.
Monitor individuals closely for respiratory depression, which may occur at any time during therapy. Consider this risk when selecting an initial dosage and when adjusting the dose. Continually reevaluate individuals to assess the maintenance of pain control and the relative incidence of adverse reactions, as well as to monitor for the development of addiction, abuse, or misuse. If the level of pain increases after dosage stabilization, attempt to identify the source of increased pain before increasing the opioid dosage. If unacceptable opioid-related adverse reactions are observed, consider reducing the dosage. Adjust the dosage to obtain an appropriate balance between management of pain and opioid-related adverse reactions.
Many acute pain conditions (e.g., pain occurring with surgical procedures or acute musculoskeletal injuries) require no more than a few days of an opioid. Clinical guidelines on opioid prescribing for some acute pain conditions are available.
There is substantial interpatient variability in the relative potency of different opioid drugs and products. When adjusting oxymorphone dosages or converting from another opioid agonist, it is better to underestimate daily dose requirements and give rescue doses than to risk an adverse reaction. If an adverse event occurs, the next dose may be reduced. Refer to the Opioid Agonists Drug Class Overview for approximate equianalgesic doses.
Avoid abrupt discontinuation or rapid dosage reduction in an individual who is physically dependent on opioid analgesics. When discontinuing oxymorphone therapy in individuals who are opioid-dependent, taper the dose gradually by 10% to 25% every 2 to 4 weeks. Individuals who have been taking opioids for briefer periods of time may tolerate a more rapid taper. Ensure a multimodal approach to pain management, including mental health support if needed, is in place prior to an opioid analgesic taper.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Use oxymorphone cautiously in patients with depression or psychosis. Opioids can cause CNS depression and psychosis (see Adverse Reactions).
 
Oxymorphone use is contraindicated in patients with known opiate agonist hypersensitivity, specifically hypersensitivity to oxymorphone or other opioid agonists of the phenanthrene subclass including morphine, oxycodone, codeine, and hydromorphone. True opiate agonist hypersensitivity is rare. It may be possible to treat such patients with an opioid agonist from the phenylpiperidine subclass (meperidine or fentanyl) or the diphenylheptane subclass (methadone). Also, patients with paraben hypersensitivity may not be appropriate candidates for the extended-release tablets; the extended release tablets contain methylparaben. Some oxymorphone formulations may contain lactose; patients with lactase deficiency should take appropriate precautions with use.
 
Oxymorphone is contraindicated for use in patients with respiratory depression except in monitored settings and in the presence of resuscitative equipment. Oxymorphone is also contraindicated in patients with acute asthma. Receipt of moderate oxymorphone doses in these patients may significantly decrease pulmonary ventilation. Opiate agonists should be avoided or used with extreme caution in patients with severe pulmonary disease such as chronic asthma or upper airway obstruction such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), or patients with respiratory insufficiency due to decreased respiratory reserve such as severe obesity, kyphoscoliosis (a type of scoliosis), or paralysis of the phrenic nerve. Extreme caution is also warranted for patients with cor pulmonale, myxedema, CNS depression, or coma. Oxymorphone injection is contraindicated for use in patients with upper airway obstruction or hypercarbia. Rapid intravenous administration may result in chest wall rigidity. Patients with sleep apnea, impaired respiratory drive, or obesity are at an increased risk for the development of respiratory depression associated with oxymorphone. Obesity is a risk factor for obstructive sleep-apnea syndrome. Overweight patients may have undiagnosed sleep apnea and/or decrease respiratory reserve. Airway obstruction may worsen after the administration of an opiate, such as oxymorphone. Consider alternative non-opioid analgesics, and employ oxymorphone only under careful medical supervision at the lowest effective dose in these patients. Do not use oxymorphone in the treatment of patients with pulmonary edema secondary to a chemical respiratory irritant. Opioid analgesics cause pooling of blood in the extremities by decreasing peripheral vascular resistance. This effect results in decreases in venous return, cardiac work, and pulmonary venous pressure, and blood is shifted from the central to peripheral circulation, which would not be beneficial in the treatment of pulmonary edema.
 
Because oxymorphone is a strong opiate agonist and a schedule 2 controlled substance, it is subject to substance abuse and psychologic dependence (i.e., drug addiction) or criminal diversion. The addiction potential and abuse liability of oxymorphone appears to be about the same as for morphine. The immediate- or extended-release tablets may be abused by crushing, chewing, snorting, or injecting the product. These practices will pose a significant risk to the abuser that could result in overdose and death. Oxymorphone extended-release tablets must not be broken, chewed, dissolved, or crushed. The extended-release tablets must be swallowed whole. If the tablets are not intact, the rapid release and absorption of a potentially fatal dose of oxymorphone occurs; serious or life threatening respiratory depression may occur. Drug addiction is characterized by compulsive use, use for non-medical purposes, and continued use despite harm or risk for harm. Patients with a previous history of substance abuse may be at increased risk of relapse if treated with oxymorphone. Abuse and addiction are separate and distinct from physiologic dependence and tolerance. Physicians should be aware that psychologic dependence may not be accompanied by concurrent tolerance and symptoms of physiologic dependence. In addition, abuse of opiate agonists can occur in the absence of true psychologic dependence and is characterized by misuse for non-medical purposes, often in combination with other psychoactive substances. Health care professionals should not let concerns over psychologic dependence deter them from using adequate amounts of opiate agonists in the management of severe pain. Patients should also be counseled regarding misconceptions regarding the use of opiate agonists for pain management. Patients receiving opiate substitution therapy for opiate substance abuse will have increased tolerance to the analgesic effects of opiate agonists used for acute pain and will require higher and more frequent dosing to control their pain. Opiate substitution therapy does not adequately treat pain. If concerns about an increased risk of misuse, abuse, or diversion exist, oxymorphone may not be an appropriate choice, as it can be abused in a manner similar to other opioid agonists. Patients will require intensive monitoring for signs of misuse, abuse, or addiction. Oxymorphone is not approved for the management of substance abuse (alcohol or drug dependence). The use of oxymorphone in patients with alcohol or drug dependence, either active or in remission, is for the treatment of pain requiring opiate agonist analgesia. Cautious use of oxymorphone in patients with a history of alcoholism is warranted. Patients must not consume alcoholic beverages or prescription or nonprescription medications that contain alcohol while taking oxymorphone extended-release tablets. Ingestion of oxymorphone extended-release tablets with alcohol may result in increased plasma oxymorphone concentrations, which may be fatal (see Drug Interactions).
 
Abrupt discontinuation of prolonged oxymorphone therapy (regular use for several weeks or more) can result in withdrawal symptoms (see Adverse Reactions). Patients should be gradually tapered off oxymorphone to avoid a withdrawal reaction. Gradually reduce the dose before completely discontinuing use.
 
Unlike other formulations of oxymorphone, use of oxymorphone extended release tablets is contraindicated for analgesia in the immediate post-operative period (12—24 hours after surgery) for opioid-naive patients. The extended-release tablets are indicated for the management of moderate to severe pain when a continuous, around-the-clock opioid analgesic is needed for an extended period of time. Patients who are taking oxymorphone as part of ongoing analgesia therapy may be safely continued on the drug after surgery or dental work, if appropriate dosage adjustments are made considering the procedure, other drugs given, and temporary changes in physiology caused by the surgical intervention. It may be appropriate to use extended release oxymorphone tablets if the post-operative pain is expected to be moderate or severe and persist for an extended period of time. Monitor for decreased bowel motility in postoperative patients receiving opiate agonists.
 
Oxymorphone is contraindicated in a patient who has or is suspected of having a paralytic ileus. Due to the effects of opiate agonists on the gastrointestinal tract, oxymorphone should be used cautiously in patients with GI disease including GI obstruction, ulcerative colitis, or pre-existing constipation. Patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) or other inflammatory bowel disease may be more sensitive to the constipating effects of opiate agonists. Opiate agonists may obscure the diagnosis or clinical course in patients with an acute abdomen. Although opiate agonists are not desirable for use in patients with diarrhea secondary to poisoning or infectious diarrhea, antimotility agents have been used successfully in these patients. If possible, opiate agonists should not be given until the toxic substance has been eliminated.
 
Oxymorphone and opiate agonists increase the tone of the biliary tract causing spasms (especially in the sphincter of Oddi) increasing biliary tract pressure. Biliary effects of oxymorphone may result in plasma amylase and lipase concentrations to 2—15 times the normal values. Oxymorphone should be used cautiously in patients with biliary tract disease such as acute pancreatitis or undergoing biliary tract surgery.
 
Oxymorphone should be used with extreme caution in patients with head trauma, increased intracranial pressure (ICP), or a preexisting seizure disorder. Opiate agonists can compromise the evaluation of neurologic parameters. Opioid analgesics can produce effects on pupillary response and consciousness, which may obscure neurologic signs of further increases in intracranial pressure in patients with head injuries. Rapid administration of high-dose opiate agonists may transiently elevate intracranial pressure and reduce cerebral perfusion pressures. These events are associated with opiate-induced lowering of mean arterial pressure, which stimulates a regulatory response to increase cerebral blood flow leading to increased ICP. Opiate agonist-induced respiratory depression can produce cerebral hypoxia and raise CSF pressure, which is unrelated to but may exaggerate the injury. Opiate analgesics, especially in high doses, can precipitate seizures.
 
Oxymorphone and other opiate agonists produce cholinergic side effects (by stimulating medullary vagal nuclei) causing bradycardia and induce the release of histamine causing peripheral vasodilation. Due to potential exacerbation of hypotension and hypoperfusion, the use oxymorphone cautiously in patients with circulatory shock or hypovolemia. Bradycardia and vasodilation can cause problems in patients with cardiac disease (e.g., angina, heart failure). Oxymorphone should be used cautiously in patients with cardiac arrhythmias, hypotension, or hypovolemia. Opiate agonists can induce vasovagal syncope or orthostatic hypotension. Advise patients to get up slowly from a lying or sitting position.
 
Oxymorphone and other opiate agonists can cause urinary retention and oliguria due to increasing the tension of the detrusor muscle. Patients more prone to these effects include those with bladder obstruction, prostatic hypertrophy, urethral stricture, pelvic malignancy, or renal disease. Drug accumulation or prolonged duration of action can occur in patients with renal impairment. As greater drug exposure also occurs in patients with renal impairment (see Pharmacokinetics), administer oxymorphone cautiously and in reduced dosages to patients with a CrCl < 50 ml/min. Oxymorphone accumulates in renal failure. In ten uremic patients, the excretion of both conjugated and unconjugated forms of oxymorphone was significantly reduced.
 
Oxymorphone is contraindicated in patients with moderate or severe hepatic impairment, and likewise moderate to severe hepatic disease. As oxymorphone is dependent on hepatic metabolism, drug accumulation or prolonged duration of action is associated with hepatic impairment (see Pharmacokinetics). Use oxymorphone cautiously in patients with mild hepatic impairment; start with the lowest dose and slowly titrate the dose while carefully monitoring for side effects.
 
Safety and effectiveness of oxymorphone in children (patients less than 18 years of age) have not been established.
 
Elderly patients are more sensitive to the analgesic effects of opiate agonists, as they experience higher peak serum concentrations and a longer duration of pain relief. The plasma concentrations of oxymorphone are about 40% higher in patients at least 65 years of age as compared with younger patients. In addition, elderly patients are more susceptible to adverse reactions from opiate agonists, especially sedation, opioid induced urinary retention, and respiratory depression, probably as a result of altered distribution of the drug and decreased elimination. Dizziness, somnolence, confusion, and nausea were more frequently observed in subjects 65 and over compared to younger subjects. Initial doses of opiate agonists may need to be reduced, and doses should be carefully titrated taking into account analgesic effects and adverse reactions. Debilitated and elderly patients should receive smaller doses of oxymorphone. Lower doses or longer dosing intervals may be required.
 
Safe use of oxymorphone in pregnancy in regard to possible adverse effects on fetal development has not been established. Oxymorphone is a FDA pregnancy category C drug. No adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women have been conducted. As with other analgesics, the use of oxymorphone in pregnancy or in women of child-bearing potential requires that the possible benefits of the drug be weighed against the possible hazards to the mother and the child. Oral oxymorphone is not recommended for use in women during and immediately prior to labor, as use of shorter acting analgesics or other analgesic techniques are more appropriate. While oxymorphone has been used in the obstetric setting, caution is advised under various circumstances during labor and obstetric delivery. Sinusoidal fetal heart rate patterns may occur with the use of opioid analgesics. If used during the second stage of labor, the duration of labor can be prolonged by temporarily reducing the strength, duration, and frequency of uterine contractions. However, this effect is not consistent and may be offset by an increased rate of cervical dilation. Generally, the effect of opioids on the pregnant uterus appears to depend on the time of administration. Administration of the drugs during the latent phase of the first stage of labor or before cervical dilation of 4—5 cm has occurred may hamper the progress of labor. The risk of respiratory and CNS depression is especially important for premature infants who are particularly sensitive. A specific opioid antagonist, such as naloxone or nalmefene, should be available for reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression in the neonate. In addition to respiratory depression, withdrawal symptoms such as convulsions, irritability, excessive crying, tremors, hyperactive reflexes, fever, vomiting, diarrhea, sneezing, yawning, and increased respiratory rate at birth or within a few days may occur in neonates whose mothers have been taking oxymorphone chronically. The effect of oxymorphone, if any, on the later growth, development, and functional maturation of the child is unknown.
 
It is not known whether oxymorphone is excreted in human milk. Because many drugs, including some opioids, are excreted in human milk, caution should be exercised when oxymorphone is administered to a woman who is breast-feeding. Ordinarily, a mother who is taking oxymorphone should not breast-feed because of the possibility of sedation and respiratory depression in the infant. If breast-feeding continues, the infant should be observed for possible adverse effects. In general, the lowest effective maternal dose should be administered. Withdrawal symptoms may occur in infants whose mothers discontinue chronic opioid therapy.
 
Any patient receiving an opiate agonist should be warned about the possibility of sedation and to use caution when driving or operating machinery or performing other hazardous tasks.
 
Intrathecal administration of oxymorphone is contraindicated. Parenteral oxymorphone is only administered by the intravenous, intramuscular, or subcutaneous routes.

    asthma exacerbation, cachexia, central sleep apnea, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coadministration with certain medications, cor pulmonale, hypercapnia, hypoxia, respiratory depression, respiratory insufficiency, severe asthma
    General Information
    Oxymorphone is contraindicated in individuals with significant respiratory depression and those with asthma exacerbation or severe asthma in an unmonitored setting or in the absence of resuscitative equipment. Avoid opioid coadministration with certain medications, specifically other CNS depressants when possible, as this significantly increases the risk for profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death. Reserve concomitant prescribing of these medications for use in individuals for whom alternative treatment options are inadequate; if concurrent use is necessary, use the lowest effective dosages and minimum treatment durations needed. Consider prescribing an opioid overdose reversal agent if concomitant use is warranted. Monitor individuals closely for signs or symptoms of respiratory depression and sedation. Individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cor pulmonale, respiratory insufficiency, hypoxia, hypercapnia, or preexisting respiratory depression are at increased risk of decreased respiratory drive even at recommended doses. Individuals with advanced age, cachexia, or debilitation are also at an increased risk for opioid-induced respiratory depression. Monitor such individuals closely, particularly when initiating and titrating the opioid; consider the use of non-opioid analgesics in these individuals. Carbon dioxide retention from opioid-induced respiratory depression may also worsen opioid sedating effects. Consider decreasing the opioid dosage in individuals with central sleep apnea (CSA). Opioids increase the risk of CSA and sleep-related hypoxemia in a dose-dependent fashion.

    Headline
    ethanol ingestion, major psychiatric disorder, opioid overdose, substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    Opioids expose users to the risks of addiction, abuse, and misuse. Although the risk of addiction in any individual is unknown, it can occur in individuals appropriately prescribed an opioid. Addiction can occur at recommended dosages and if the medication is misused or abused. The risk of opioid-related overdose or overdose-related death is increased with higher doses, and this risk persists over the course of therapy. The potential for these risks should not prevent the proper management of pain in any given individual. Assess an individual's risk for opioid addiction, abuse, or misuse before prescribing an opioid, and monitor for the development of these behaviors or conditions. Risks are increased in individuals with a personal or family history of substance abuse disorder (including alcohol or drug abuse or addiction) or major psychiatric disorder (e.g., major depression). Individuals at increased risk may be prescribed opioids, but use in such individuals necessitates intensive counseling about the risks and proper use of the opioid along with frequent reevaluation for signs of addiction, abuse, and misuse. Opioids are sought for nonmedical use and are subject to diversion from legitimate prescribed use. Consider these risks when prescribing or dispensing opioids. Strategies to reduce these risks include prescribing the medication in the smallest appropriate quantity and educating about proper medication storage and disposal. Consumption of oxymorphone with alcohol may result in increased drug exposure that may cause a fatal overdose; advise individuals against ethanol ingestion, including the ingestion of alcohol contained in prescription or non-prescription medications. Keep opioids out of the reach of children, others for whom the medication was not prescribed, and pets as accidental exposure or improper use may cause respiratory failure and a fatal overdose. Accidental exposure of even a single dose of an opioid, especially by younger individuals, can result in a fatal overdose. Discuss the importance of an opioid overdose reversal agent and the options for obtaining an opioid overdose reversal agent (e.g., prescription, over-the-counter, or as part of a community-based program), especially with individuals who are at increased risk of opioid overdose, such as those who are also using other CNS depressants, who have a history of opioid use disorder (OUD), who have experienced a previous opioid overdose, or who have household members or other close contacts at risk for accidental exposure or opioid overdose. Educate individuals and caregivers on how to recognize respiratory depression, and how to use an opioid overdose reversal agent for the emergency treatment of opioid overdose. Emphasize the importance of calling 911 or getting emergency medical help, even if an opioid overdose reversal agent is administered.

Opioids can impair the mental or physical abilities needed to perform potentially hazardous activities.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Use oxymorphone with caution in geriatric adults. Use a lower initial oxymorphone dose and titrate slowly while carefully monitoring for signs of CNS and respiratory depression in geriatric adults. Geriatric adults may have increased sensitivity to opioids. In general, use cautious dose selection for opioids in geriatric adults, usually starting at the low end of the dosing range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function and concomitant disease or other medication therapy. According to the Beers Criteria, opioid agonists are considered potentially inappropriate medications in geriatric adults with a history of falls or fractures and should be avoided in these populations, except in the setting of severe acute pain, since opioids can produce ataxia, impaired psychomotor function, syncope, and additional falls. If an opioid must be used, consider reducing the use of other CNS-active medications that increase the risk of falls and fractures and implement strategies to reduce fall risk. In individuals receiving palliative care, the balance of benefits and harms of medication management may differ from those of the general population of older adults.
    Headline
    labor, obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on oxymorphone use during pregnancy are insufficient to define the risk of major birth defects or miscarriage. Opioids cross the placenta. In animal reproduction studies, reduced postnatal survival of pups and an increased incidence of stillborn pups were observed after oral administration of oxymorphone to rats during gestation and through lactation at doses 2.4 and 12 times the human daily dose (HDD) of 20 mg/day based on body surface area, respectively. Reduced fetal weights were observed with oral administration of oxymorphone to rats and rabbits during organogenesis at exposures up to 4.9 and 48.8 times the HDD, respectively. Advise pregnant individuals of the potential fetal risk. Opioids can prolong labor and obstetric delivery by temporarily reducing the strength, duration, and frequency of uterine contractions. This effect is not consistent and may be offset by an increased rate of cervical dilatation, which may shorten labor. In addition, neonates exposed to opioids in utero may present with respiratory depression at birth. Because of this, an opioid antagonist (e.g., naloxone) must be available for reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression in the neonate at delivery. Guidelines recommend early universal screening of pregnant individuals for opioid use and opioid use disorder at the first prenatal visit. Obtain a thorough history of substance use and review the Prescription Drug Monitoring Program to determine if individuals have received prior prescriptions for opioids or other high-risk drugs such as benzodiazepines. Discuss the risks and benefits of opioid use during pregnancy, including the risk of becoming physiologically dependent on opioids, the possibility for neonatal withdrawal, and how long-term opioid use may affect care during a future pregnancy. In individuals undergoing uncomplicated normal spontaneous vaginal birth, consider opioid therapy only if expected benefits for both pain and function are anticipated to outweigh risks. If opioids are used, use in combination with nonpharmacologic therapy and non-opioid pharmacologic therapy, as appropriate. Use immediate-release opioids instead of extended-release or long-acting opioids; order the lowest effective dosage and prescribe no greater quantity of opioids than needed for the expected duration of such pain severe enough to require opioids. For individuals using opioids for chronic pain, consider strategies to avoid or minimize the use of opioids, including alternative pain therapies (i.e., nonpharmacologic) and non-opioid pharmacologic treatments. Opioid agonist pharmacotherapy (e.g., methadone or buprenorphine) is preferable to medically supervised withdrawal in pregnant individuals with opioid use disorder.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with opioids. Long-term use of opioids may influence the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, leading to hormonal changes that may manifest as a libido decrease, impotence, erectile dysfunction, amenorrhea, or infertility. The causal role of opioids in the clinical manifestations of hypogonadism is unknown. It is not known whether the effects on fertility are reversible.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero may present with respiratory depression and psycho-physiologic effects at birth. Monitor exposed newborns for signs of excess sedation and respiratory depression. If necessary, administer an opioid antagonist (e.g., naloxone) to reverse the effects of opioid-induced respiratory depression. Long-term use of opioids during pregnancy can result in physical dependence in the exposed infant. Monitor newborns for symptoms of withdrawal and manage accordingly. Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome may present as irritability, hyperactivity, abnormal sleep pattern, high-pitched cry, tremor, vomiting, diarrhea, and failure to gain weight. It can be life-threatening if not recognized and treated. Onset, duration, and severity of opioid withdrawal may vary based on the specific opioid used, duration of use, timing and amount of last maternal use, and rate of elimination by the infant.

    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid the use of extended-release oxymorphone products during breast-feeding. Immediate-release formulations may be used with caution. There are no data on the presence of oxymorphone in human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effects on milk production. Monitor children exposed to opioids through breast milk for excess sedation and respiratory depression. Withdrawal symptoms may occur in the breast-fed child when maternal administration of the opioid is stopped or when breast-feeding is discontinued, particularly with chronic use. Other analgesics are preferred over oxymorphone during lactation; alternatives may include acetaminophen, ibuprofen, or morphine.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Allergic reactions can occur with oxymorphone, as is typical with opiate agonists. See other opiate analgesic monographs (e.g., morphine) for other possible side effects consistent with drugs of the opioid class.Among 557 patients who received immediate-release oxymorphone tablets, 14.2% had fever compared to 8.1% of placebo recipients. Fever also occurred in 1—10% of 2011 patients who received oxymorphone extended-release tablets in phase 2/3 controlled and open-label trials. The etiology of the fever is unknown.Opiate agonists present the potential for abuse although the true risk of this phenomenon is extremely low. Physiological dependence, however, will occur during chronic opiate agonist therapy as evidenced by a withdrawal syndrome occurring after abrupt discontinuation of oxymorphone in these patients. Symptoms of withdrawal include nausea and diarrhea, coughing, lacrimation, yawning, sneezing, rhinorrhea, profuse sweating, twitching muscles, abdominal and muscle pain/cramps, hot and cold flashes, and piloerection. Elevations in body temperature, respiratory rate, heart rate, and blood pressure may also occur. Without treatment most symptoms resolve within 5—14 days; however, there appears to be a secondary or chronic abstinence syndrome which may last 2—6 months, characterized by insomnia, irritability, and muscle aches. The shorter the onset and duration of action of the opiate agonist, the greater the intensity and rapidity of onset of withdrawal symptoms. Routine use of opiate agonists by an expectant mother can lead to depressed respiration in the newborn and a neonatal abstinence syndrome. Withdrawal symptoms in a newborn occur 1—4 days after birth and include generalized tremors, hypertonicity with any form of tactile stimuli, hyperalertness, sleeplessness, excessive crying, vomiting, diarrhea, yawning, and fever. It is important to differentiate physiological dependence, the onset of a withdrawal syndrome upon abrupt discontinuation of the drug, from psychological dependence. Psychological dependence is a behavioral syndrome characterized by drug craving, overwhelming concern with acquisition of the drug, and other drug-related behaviors such as drug selling and seeking the drug from multiple sources.Pharmacologic tolerance to the analgesic affects of opiate agonists including oxymorphone has been reported in some patients. Tolerance is the need for increasing opioid doses to maintain initial pain relief. Typically, tolerance presents as a decrease in the duration of analgesia and is managed by increasing the opioid dose or frequency. There is no limit to tolerance; thus, some patients may require very large doses of opiate analgesics to control their pain. When increasing doses of analgesia are required causes may be multi-factorial including tolerance, progression of disease, or psychological distress.Oxymorphone's effect on opiate receptors in the reticular activating system and striatum produces sedation. Patients should be warned that activity requiring mental alertness can be affected as CNS depression including drowsiness, confusion, and dizziness can occur. Among 557 patients who received immediate-release oxymorphone tablets, 9.3% had somnolence, 6.8% had headache, 6.5% had dizziness, and 2.7% had confusion. Anxiety occurred in at least 1% of patients. Among 105 opioid-naive patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets 1.9% had somnolence, 3.8% had headache, and 4.8% had dizziness. Among 70 opioid-experienced patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets, 2.9% had somnolence, 2.9% had headache, and none had dizziness. Among 1259 patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets, 17.2% had somnolence, 12.2% had headache, 17.8% had dizziness, 4% had insomnia, and 3.9% had fatigue. All of these events occurred in at least 1% more patients than they did in placebo recipients. Dysphoric reactions or toxic psychosis can occur after any dose, and flushing and mental depression occurred in at least 1% of patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets in phase 2/3 controlled and open-label trials. Tolerance to the CNS depression develops within a few days; however, if it does not resolve, excessive sedation may be managed with stimulants such as methylphenidate.The most significant adverse effect associated with opiate agonist use is respiratory depression. Among 557 patients who received immediate-release oxymorphone tablets, at least 1% of patients had hypoxia, and dyspnea occurred in at least 1% of patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets in phase 2/3 controlled and open-label trials. Respiratory depression results from a decreased sensitivity to carbon dioxide in the brainstem. Respiratory depression is more common in elderly or debilitated patients, following large initial doses in non-opioid tolerant patients, or when opioids are given with other agents that cause CNS depression. When oxymorphone is appropriately titrated, the risk of respiratory depression is generally small, as tolerance rapidly develops to this effect. Caution needs to be employed in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, with decreased pulmonary reserve, with cardiovascular disease, or who are taking other CNS depressants (see Contraindications and Interactions). Symptomatic respiratory depression should be treated cautiously with an opioid antagonist such as naloxone. Patients may also develop respiratory depression through sub-acute overdose of an opiate agonist in which sedation builds up slowly leading to a decreased respiratory rate and then respiratory failure. The risk of this is more common with methadone but may be seen with other long-acting opioid preparations or in patients with renal or hepatic impairment. This can be managed by holding 1—2 doses of the opioid and then restarting the opioid at 25% of the previous dose and slowly increasing the dose once the symptoms have resolved. Routine use of opiate agonists by an expectant mother can lead to respiratory depression in the newborn. If naloxone is used to reverse respiratory depression from oxymorphone, repeat doses may be needed. The duration of action of naloxone is considerably shorter than the duration of action of oxymorphone.All opioids may aggravate convulsions in patients with convulsive disorders, and all opioids may induce or aggravate seizures in some clinical settings.Oxymorphone may cause a variety of effects on the GI system, most commonly nausea/vomiting and constipation. Among 557 patients who received immediate-release oxymorphone tablets, 19% had nausea, 9% had vomiting, and 4.1% had constipation. Abdominal distension and flatulence occurred in at least 1% of patients. Among 105 opioid-naive patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets, 11.4% had nausea and 6.7% had constipation. Among 70 opioid-experienced patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets, 2.9% had nausea, 0% had vomiting, and 5.7% had constipation. Among 1259 patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets, 33.1% had nausea, 15.6% had vomiting, 2.9% had anorexia, 2.5% had abdominal pain, and 27.6% had constipation. All of these events occurred in at least 1% more patients than they did in placebo recipients. Nausea and vomiting is most commonly seen at the initiation of therapy or when switching agents. Opiate agonists affect the vestibular system and may cause more nausea/vomiting in ambulatory patients than in bedridden patients. There is a general relationship between increasing opioid plasma concentration and increasing frequency of nausea and vomiting. Scheduled treatment with an antiemetic during the first 1—2 days, then as needed during opiate therapy will usually control these symptoms until tolerance develops. If patients have nausea associated with movement, an antivertigo agent such as meclizine may be appropriate. Metoclopramide may be helpful in treating the symptoms of early satiety or fullness. Constipation due to decrease GI motility and secretions is common during opiate agonist therapy. In some cases patients can develop ileus or GI obstruction. Tolerance rarely develops to this effect; therefore, patients require a bowel regimen consisting of a stool softener and mild stimulant throughout opioid therapy. Monitor for decreased bowel motility in post-operative patients receiving opioids. Standard supportive therapy should be implemented.Opiate analgesics can cause spasm of the sphincter of Oddi, with morphine producing greater effects than meperidine and meperidine producing greater effects than codeine. Hyperamylasemia, secondary to drug-induced spasm of the sphincter of Oddi, has been associated with various opiate agonists. While serum amylase and lipase concentrations can rise as a result of biliary obstruction or spasm, overt pancreatitis is rare with opiate analgesics. Gastritis and hepatotoxicity are also relatively rare with these agents.Oxymorphone can cause cardiovascular adverse reactions such as sinus bradycardia, sinus tachycardia, palpitations, hypertension, hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, diaphoresis, and syncope. Among 557 patients who received immediate-release oxymorphone tablets, sinus tachycardia, diaphoresis, and hypotension occurred in at least 1% of patients. Orthostatic hypotension may arise as a secondary effect from peripheral vasodilatation. In cases of severe respiratory and/or circulatory depression, shock and cardiac arrest may occur.Oxymorphone is known to cause pruritus. Among 557 patients who received immediate-release oxymorphone tablets, 7.9% had pruritus. Among 105 opioid-naive patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets, 2.9% had pruritus, and among 1259 patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets, 15.2% had pruritus. Opiate agonist-induced pruritus is thought to be mediated through stimulation of central opiate receptors, as opiate antagonists (i.e., nalmefene or naloxone) have been shown to ameliorate pruritus associated with cholestasis. Use of H1-blockers or changing to a different opioid may lessen the pruritus.Anticholinergic effects may occur with oxymorphone. Patients can experience xerostomia (dry mouth), blurred vision, or urinary retention, which can cause oliguria. Among 557 patients who received immediate-release oxymorphone tablets, xerostomia occurred in at least 1% of patients. Among 1259 patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets, 6.4% had xerostomia. Blurred vision occurred in at least 1% of patients who received extended-release oxymorphone tablets in phase 2/3 controlled and open-label trials.Oxymorphone causes miosis, even in total darkness. Pinpoint pupils are a sign of opioid overdose but are not pathognomonic, as pontine lesions of hemorrhagic or ischemic origin may produce similar findings. Severe oxymorphone overdose can cause mydriasis once brain anoxia develops. Therapeutic doses can increase accommodation and sensitivity to light reflex and decrease intraocular tension in both normal and glaucomatous eyes.Opiate agonists can exert adverse effects on the endocrine system. Libido decrease may occur following a decrease in production of luteinizing hormone and a corresponding decrease in testosterone. Women may experience amenorrhea and infertility and men may be unable to attain or maintain an erection. Opioids also inhibit the secretion of ACTH and cortisol and stimulate prolactin, growth hormone secretion, and pancreatic secretion of insulin and glucagon in humans. Thyroid stimulating hormone has been shown to be both inhibited and stimulated by opioids.Myoclonia has been reported in some patients, especially those receiving high doses of opioids. Clonazepam has been used to treat myoclonic jerks secondary to opioids; however, caution should be used in using benzodiazepines in combination with opioids due to additive CNS depression. Hyperalgesia (excessive sensitivity to pain) may also occur in patients receiving high doses of opioids or in patients with renal dysfunction.

    Oxymorphone is a controlled substance with a high potential for abuse and psychological dependence. Abuse and addiction are separate and distinct from physiological dependence and tolerance, which can develop during chronic opioid therapy. In postmarketing studies, addiction, abuse, misuse, and fatal and non-fatal opioid overdose were observed in subjects with long-term opioid use. In a prospective, 12-month observational cohort study in individuals initiating long-term use of Schedule II opioid analgesics, 1% to 6% of subjects across 2 cohorts met the criteria for addiction. Approximately 9% and 22% of subjects newly met the criteria for prescription opioid abuse and misuse, respectively. In a retrospective, observational cohort study in individuals with new long-term use of Schedule II opioid analgesics, the 5-year cumulative incidence estimates for opioid-involved overdose or opioid overdose-related death ranged from approximately 1.5% to 4% across study sites, counting only the first event during follow-up. Approximately 17% of first opioid overdoses observed over the entire study period (5 to 11 years) were fatal. Higher baseline opioid dose was the strongest and most consistent predictor of opioid-involved overdose or opioid overdose-related death. Tolerance is characterized by a reduced response to a drug after repeated administration. Physiological dependence develops as a result of repeated drug use. It manifests with a withdrawal syndrome after abrupt discontinuation or significant dose reduction, or administration of an agent with opioid antagonist activity (e.g., naloxone). Withdrawal is characterized by restlessness, lacrimation, rhinorrhea, yawning, perspiration, chills, myalgia, and mydriasis. Other symptoms include irritability, anxiety, backache, joint pain, weakness, abdominal cramps, insomnia, nausea, anorexia, vomiting, diarrhea, or increased blood pressure, respiratory rate, or heart rate. Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) may occur in neonates and infants exposed to opioids in utero. Withdrawal symptoms may include irritability, hyperactivity, abnormal sleep pattern, high-pitched cry, tremor, vomiting, diarrhea, and failure to gain weight. Onset, duration, and severity of opioid withdrawal may vary based on the specific opioid used, duration of use, timing and amount of last maternal use, and rate of elimination by the infant. Exposed newborns should be monitored closely. NOWS can be life-threatening if not recognized and treated. Treatment should be managed by clinicians experienced with neonatal withdrawal.

    Cases of opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH) have been reported, both with short-term and longer-term use of opioids. Description
  Text
  Oxymorphone is an opioid agonist indicated for the treatment of severe pain that requires an opioid analgesic and for which alternative treatments are inadequate. Opioids are associated with serious adverse effects, which may be life-threatening, including addiction, abuse, and misuse, respiratory depression, and neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS). Concomitant use of opioids with benzodiazepines or other central nervous system depressants, including alcohol, may result in profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death; additionally, the coingestion of alcohol with oxymorphone may result in increased plasma oxymorphone concentrations and a potentially fatal overdose of oxymorphone. Oxymorphone was FDA-approved in 1959.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Oxymorphone is a full opioid agonist and is relatively selective for the mu-opioid receptor, although it can bind to other opioid receptors at higher doses. The principal therapeutic action of oxymorphone is analgesia. The precise mechanism of the analgesic action is unknown. However, specific central nervous system opioid receptors for endogenous compounds with opioid-like activity have been identified throughout the brain and spinal cord and are thought to play a role in the analgesic effects of opioids.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    The absolute oral bioavailability of oxymorphone is approximately 10%. Steady-state concentrations were achieved after 3 days of multiple dose administration of oral oxymorphone. Under both single-dose and steady-state conditions, dose proportionality has been established for 5, 10, and 20 mg doses of oral immediate-release and extended-release oxymorphone and also for 40 mg doses of oral extended-release oxymorphone, for both peak plasma concentrations (Cmax) and extent of absorption. After the administration of oral extended-release oxymorphone 20 and 40 mg to healthy volunteers, the Cmax was increased by approximately 50% in fed subjects compared to fasted subjects; a similar increase in Cmax was also observed with oxymorphone solution. The AUC was unchanged in 1 study and increased by approximately 18% in another study in fed subjects after the administration of oral extended-release oxymorphone. Examination of the AUC suggests that most of the difference between fed and fasting conditions occurs in the first 4 hours after dose administration. After oral dosing with a single dose of oral extended-release oxymorphone 40 mg, a peak oxymorphone plasma concentration of 2.8 nanograms/mL is achieved at 1 hour in fasted subjects and a peak of 4.25 nanograms/mL is achieved at 2 hours in fed subjects; beyond the 12-hour time point, there is very little difference in the curves.

    Name
    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    The bioavailability of oral oxymorphone may be markedly increased in individuals with moderate to severe liver disease. The effect of hepatic impairment on the pharmacokinetics of oral immediate-release oxymorphone have not been studied. However, the disposition of oral extended-release oxymorphone was compared in 6 subjects with mild, 5 subjects with moderate, and 1 subject with severe hepatic impairment and 12 subjects with normal hepatic function. The bioavailability of oxymorphone was increased by 1.6-fold in subjects with mild hepatic impairment and by 3.7-fold in subjects with moderate hepatic impairment. In a subject with severe hepatic impairment, the bioavailability was increased by 12.2-fold. The half-life of oxymorphone was not significantly affected by hepatic impairment.

    Description

    Description
    The steady-state plasma concentrations of oxymorphone, 6-OH-oxymorphone, and oxymorphone-3-glucuronide are approximately 40% higher in elderly subjects (65 years and older) than in young subjects (18 to 40 years). On average, age older than 65 years was associated with a 1.4-fold increase in oxymorphone AUC and a 1.5-fold increase in Cmax.     Description
    After single- and multiple-doses of oral extended-release oxymorphone in adult volunteers, there was a consistent tendency for females to have slightly higher AUC and Cmax values than males; however, sex differences were not observed when AUC and Cmax were adjusted by body weight.
  Text
  Oxymorphone is administered orally. Oxymorphone is not extensively bound to human plasma proteins; protein binding is in the range of 10% to 12%. Oxymorphone is highly metabolized, principally in the liver, and undergoes reduction or conjugation with glucuronic acid to form both active and inactive metabolites. The 2 major metabolites of oxymorphone are oxymorphone-3-glucuronide and 6-OH-oxymorphone. The mean plasma AUC for oxymorphone-3-glucuronide is approximately 90-fold higher than the parent compound. Although the pharmacologic activity of the glucuronide metabolite has not been evaluated, the 6-OH-oxymorphone metabolite does have analgesic bioactivity in animal models. The mean plasma 6-OH-oxymorphone AUC is approximately 70% of the oxymorphone AUC after single oral doses but is essentially equivalent to the parent compound at steady-state. Because oxymorphone is extensively metabolized, less than 1% of the administered dose is excreted unchanged in the urine. On average, 33% to 38% of the administered dose is excreted in the urine as oxymorphone-3-glucuronide and 0.25% to 0.62% is excreted as 6-OH-oxymorphone in subjects with normal hepatic and renal function. In animals given radiolabeled oxymorphone, approximately 90% of the administered radioactivity was recovered within 5 days of dosing. The majority of oxymorphone-derived radioactivity was found in the urine and feces. The half-life of immediate-release oxymorphone is approximately 9 to 11 hours after a single oral dose (5 to 40 mg).

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Mild hepatic impairment: Use with caution. Titrate slowly while carefully monitoring for respiratory or central nervous system depression.
Immediate-release: Start at the lowest dose (i.e., 5 mg PO).
