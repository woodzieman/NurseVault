    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Chenodiol is a bile acid agent and may be cross-sensitive to other bile acid agents.
    Headline
    absent gallbladder, acute biliary pancreatitis, acute cholecystitis, biliary obstruction, biliary-GI fistula, cholangitis, intrahepatic cholestasis, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis
    General Information
    Chenodiol products for gallstone treatment are contraindicated in the presence of known bile ductal abnormalities, such as intrahepatic cholestasis, primary biliary cholangitis or primary sclerosing cholangitis. Chenodiol is contraindicated in those with an absent gallbladder confirmed as nonvisualizing after 2 consecutive single doses of dye. Chenodiol gallstone treatment is also contraindicated in people with radiopaque gallstones as chenodiol will not dissolve calcified (radiopaque) or radiolucent bile pigment stones. Chenodiol is also contraindicated in the presence of gallstone complications or compelling reasons for gallbladder surgery, including unremitting acute cholecystitis, cholangitis, biliary obstruction, acute biliary pancreatitis, or biliary-GI fistula.
    Headline
    bile duct disease, hepatic disease
    General Information
    Chenodiol use for gallstone treatment is contraindicated in the presence of known hepatic disease. Because of the potential hepatotoxicity of chenodiol, poor response rate in some subgroups of chenodiol-treated individuals, and increased rates for cholecystectomy in other chenodiol-treated subgroups, chenodiol is not an appropriate treatment for many people with gallstones. Reserve chenodiol for carefully selected patients who do not have hepatocyte dysfunction; chenodiol treatment for gallstones must be accompanied by systematic monitoring for liver function alterations. Interruption of therapy may be needed for minor, usually transient, aminotransferase elevations. Aminotransferase elevations greater than 3 times the upper limit of normal (ULN) require immediate discontinuation and would be expected to recur upon rechallenge. Individuals with hepatic disease or bile duct disease may be at an increased risk of hepatoxocity when treated with chenodiol for  cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX). Obtain baseline liver transaminase (ALT, AST) and total bilirubin concentrations prior to the initiation of chenodiol treatment. Monitor liver transaminases and total bilirubin yearly and as clinically necessary. During treatment, if liver transaminase concentrations (ALT, AST) are more than 3 times the ULN or total bilirubin is greater than 2 times ULN, interrupt chenodiol treatment until concentrations have returned to baseline values. For persistent or recurrent liver test abnormalities, discontinuation of chenodiol should be considered in these patients.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Chenodiol is contraindicated during pregnancy when used for the treatment of cholelithiasis. Chenodiol should be used with caution during pregnancy when used for the treatment of cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX). Available data from published case reports over several decades of use during pregnancy have not identified an increased risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. However, animal data has revealed serious hepatic, renal, and adrenal lesions in fetuses of rhesus monkeys exposed to chenodiol at 1 to 2 times the recommended human dose (RHD), based on body surface area, from gestation days 21 to 45. Hepatic lesions were also observed in neonatal baboons after exposure of pregnant baboons to 0.6 to 1.4 times the RHD throughout pregnancy. These animal findings have not been demonstrated in humans. Nonhuman primates form sulfate conjugates of lithocholic acid, a known hepatotoxic bacterial metabolite of chenodiol, to a lesser extent than humans, which may exaggerate the toxicity of orally administered chenodiol in animal studies. However, evidence also suggests that hepatobiliary toxicity may be partly attributable to the parent drug, chenodiol.

    Use chenodiol with caution during breast-feeding. Chenodiol and its conjugates are naturally present in human milk. Data are not available regarding chenodiol concentrations in human milk after exogenous administration, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Because chenodiol may increase intestinal motility, monitor the breastfed children exposed to chenodiol for diarrhea. Warfarin: (Moderate) Due to its potential for hepatotoxicity, chenodiol may affect the pharmacodynamics of warfarin. Patients with hepatic impairment may require a lower dosage of warfarin due to decreased warfarin metabolism and decreased production of coagulation factors. Coadministration with chenodiol can cause prolongation of the prothrombin time and increase the risk of bleeding. Monitor patients on concomitant therapy carefully. If prolongation of prothrombin time is observed, the warfarin dosage should be adjusted as needed to produce a prothrombin time 1.5 to 2 times normal. If necessary, discontinue chenodiol therapy.

    General Information
    National Cooperative Gallstone Study (NCGS) patients with a history of biliary pain prior to treatment had higher cholecystectomy rates during the study if assigned to low dosage chenodiol (375 mg/day) than if assigned to either placebo or high dosage chenodiol (750 mg/day).     Headline
    new primary malignancy
    General Information
    Although direct evidence is lacking, epidemiologic studies suggest that bile acids might contribute to human colon cancer. Bile acids, including chenodiol and lithocholic acid, have no carcinogenic potential in animal models but have been shown to increase the number of tumors when administered with certain known carcinogens. The possibility that chenodiol therapy might contribute to a new primary malignancy of colon cancer in otherwise susceptible individuals cannot be ruled out.

    Headline
    elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatic failure, hepatitis, hepatotoxicity
    General Information
    Chenodiol administration has been associated with elevated hepatic enzymes and hepatotoxicity. Chenodiol is thought to cause elevations in serum aminotransferase because of the conversion to lithocholic acid, which has intrinsic and proven hepatotoxicity. During clinical evaluation of chenodiol for the treatment of gallstones, dose-related serum aminotransferase (mainly SGPT, also known as ALT) elevations occurred in 30% or more of subjects receiving recommended doses. Elevations were transient and minor; 1.5 to 3 times the upper limit of normal (ULN) and returned to normal within 6 months despite continued therapy. Generally, serum aminotransferase elevations were not accompanied by elevations in alkaline phosphatase or bilirubin. In 2% to 3% of treated subjects, ALT concentrations were greater than 3 times ULN, recurred upon rechallenge, and required discontinuation of chenodiol therapy. After discontinuation, hepatic enzyme concentrations returned to normal. The manufacturer recommends routine monitoring of liver function tests during treatment. During one trial for the clinical evaluation of chenodiol for the treatment of cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX), one chenodiol-treated subject (7%) had increased ALT concentrations greater than 3 times ULN, which led to treatment interruption. Individuals with pre-exisiting liver disease or bile duct abnormalities may be at higher risk for hepatotoxicity. Reports suggest that people who are poor sulfators of lithocholic acid (metabolite of chenodiol) are more likely to develop chenodiol-induced serum aminotransferase elevations. Interrupt treatment with chenodiol if liver transaminase (AST, ALT) concentrations are greater than 3 times ULN or total bilirubin is greater than 2 times ULN until concentrations return to baseline. Monitor liver transaminase and total bilirubin concentrations yearly and as clinically necessary. Consider discontinuing chenodiol for recurrent or persistent liver test abnormalities. Inform individuals of the symptoms of hepatotoxicity (e.g., abdominal pain, bruising, dark-colored urine, fatigue, bleeding, jaundice, nausea, and pruritus) and to discontinue chenodiol immediately if these symptoms occur. Although clinical and biopsy studies have not shown fulminant lesions during use of chenodiol, the possibility remains that an occasional subject may develop serious hepatic disease due to drug-induced hepatotoxicity. Three subjetcs with biochemical and histologic pictures of chronic active hepatitis while on chenodiol (375 or 750 mg/day) have been reported. The biochemical abnormalities returned spontaneously to normal in 2 subjects within 13 and 17 months; and after 17 months of treatment with prednisone in the third. Follow-up biopsies were not done; and the causal relationship of the drug could not be determined. Another biopsied subject was terminated from therapy because of elevated aminotransferase levels and a liver biopsy was interpreted as showing active drug hepatitis. Another subject with sclerosing cholangitis, biliary cirrhosis and history of jaundice died during chenodiol treatment for hepatic duct stones. Before treatment, serum aminotransferase and alkaline phosphate levels were twice the ULN; within 1 month they rose to over 10-times the ULN. Chenodiol was discontinued at 7 weeks when the subject was hospitalized with advanced hepatic failure and E. coli peritonitis; death ensued at the week 8. A contribution of chenodiol to the fatal outcome could not be ruled out.

    cholestasis
    General Information
    Morphologic studies of liver biopsies taken before and after 9 and 24 months of chenodiol treatment for gallstones show that 63% of the selected subjects had evidence of intrahepatic cholestasis prior to treatment and almost all had electron microscopic abnormalities. By the ninth month of chenodiol treatment, reexamination of two-thirds of these subjects showed an 89% incidence of the signs of intrahepatic cholestasis. Two of 89 subjects at the ninth month had lithocholate-like lesions in the canalicular membrane, although there were not clinical enzyme abnormalities in the face of continued treatment and no change in Type 2 light microscopic parameters. Hepatobiliary toxicity (e.g., cholestasis) were observed in many animal species, including rodents, non-rodents, and non-human primates at doses close to the human dose for chenodiol when used for the treatment of cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX). There is evidence that the hepatobiliary toxicity is partly due to the partner drug, chenodiol.

    General Information
    During clinical evaluation of chenodiol used for gallstones, gastrointestinal (GI) effects were among the most commonly reported adverse events. Dose-related diarrhea was reported in 30% to 40% of subjects receiving chenodiol for treatment of gallstones. Diarrhea was most often reported at the onset of treatment or during dosage increases, but can occur at any time during treatment. The diarrhea was generally mild, well-tolerated, and did not interfere with therapy. However, if symptoms persist, a temporary reduction of the chenodiol dose may be necessary. If symptoms do not resolve, a permanent reduction in dose may be required. During studies, a temporary dosage reduction was required in 10 to 15% of subjects, and about half of those subjects required a permanent reduction. Anti-diarrheal agents have proved useful in some individuals. Discontinuation of chenodiol therapy due to failure to control diarrhea occurs in approximately 3% of subjects treated with chenodiol for gallstones. Other less frequent, GI side effects reported in gallstone subjects included fecal urgency, abdominal cramps, pyrosis (heartburn), constipation, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, epigastric distress, dyspepsia, flatulence, and nonspecific abdominal pain. During clinical evaluation of chenodiol used for the treatment of cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX), GI adverse effects that were reported were diarrhea (36%), abdominal pain (14%), and constipation (14%).

Description
  Text
  Chenodiol is the oral formulation of chenodeoxycholic acid, a naturally occurring human bile acid. It is indicated for adult patients with radiolucent stones in well-opacifying gallbladders and for the treatment of cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX). In people with radiolucent stones, chenodiol is indicated for patients in whom selective surgery would be undertaken except for the presence of increased surgical risk due to systemic disease or age. Chenodiol therapy is most effective in patients whose gallstones are floatable or small. For patients with non-floatable stones, the risk of unsuccessful medical treatment should be weighed against the risks associated with postponed surgical intervention. Efficacy and many of the safety concerns are dose-dependent. In the National Cooperative Gallstone Study (NCGS), involving 305 patients in each treatment group, placebo and chenodiol dosages of 375 mg and 750 mg per day were associated with complete stone dissolution in 0.8%, 5.2%, and 13.5%, respectively, of enrolled subjects over 24 months. Uncontrolled clinical trials using higher doses than those used in the NCGS have shown complete dissolution rates of 28% to 38% of enrolled patients who received weight-based doses of 13 to 16 mg/kg/day for up to 24 months. The safety of chenodiol use beyond 24 months has not been established. It is estimated that half of chenodiol-treated patients will experience stone recurrence within 5 years. Retreatment with chenodiol has proven successful in dissolving some newly formed stones; however, the safety of re-treatment has not been established. The efficacy of chenodiol in the treatment of CTX was evaluated in a 24-week clinical trial. Treatment with chenodiol resulted in a significant reduction in plasma cholestanol and urine 23S-pentol (cholesterol metabolites that are markedly increased in CTX patients) compared to placebo. Chenodiol therapy has been associated with hepatotoxicity. During CTX treatment, obtain liver function tests before treatment initiation, annually, and when clinically indicated. When treating gallstones, periodically obtain liver function tests and oral cholecystogram or ultrasonograms. Monitor serum cholesterol at 6-month intervals. Chenodiol was originally FDA-approved in 1983. FDA-approval was expanded to include the treatment of cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX) in February 2025.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The sites of action of chenodiol are the liver, bile, and the colon. At therapeutic doses, chenodiol suppresses the hepatic synthesis of both cholesterol and cholic acid, gradually replacing the latter and its metabolite, deoxycholic acid. These actions contribute to biliary cholesterol desaturation and gradual dissolution of radiolucent cholesterol gallstones. Chenodiol has no effect on radiopaque (calcified) gallstones or on radiolucent bile pigment stones. The large majority of the chenodiol in the body is found in the enterohepatic circulation; serum and urinary bile acid concentrations are not significantly affected during chenodiol therapy.
In the treatment of cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX), chenodiol may act to replace deficient levels of the endogenous bile acid, chenodeoxycholic acid. CTX is a genetic metabolic disorder caused by a mutation in a gene called CYP27A1 which causes a deficiency of the enzyme that allows the body to break down cholesterol. Due to this mutation, cholesterol cannot be converted into bile acids but is instead converted into cholestanol and bile alcohol. This surplus of cholestanol leads to a toxic accumulation and deposition in various organs and tissues in the body. By replacing deficient levels of the bile acid, chenodeoxycholic acid, chenodiol helps to reduce the abnormal deposits of cholestanol in patients with CTX.
Although chenodiol therapy has been linked to hepatotoxicity, the mechanism by which this occurs is unclear. Lithocholic acid, a major metabolite of chenodiol and an established hepatotoxin, may be the cause of chenodiol-related hepatotoxicity; however, since humans possess an efficient mechanism for sulfating and eliminating this substance, there is some evidence that the demonstrated hepatotoxicity could be due, at least in part, to chenodiol itself. Moreover, the possible variations that may exist between individual patient's capacity to sulfate and eliminate lithocholic acid have not been clearly established. Published literature suggests that patients who develop chenodiol-induced serum aminotransferase elevations may be poor sulfators of lithocholic acid.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    After oral administration, chenodiol is well absorbed from the small intestine and taken up by the liver where it is converted to its taurine and glycine conjugates and secreted in bile. Between 60% and 80% of chenodiol undergoes first-pass hepatic clearance; therefore, the body pool of chenodiol resides mainly in the enterohepatic circulation. In cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX) patients at steady state following the three times daily dosing, Cmax and AUC (0 to 8 hours) of chenodiol were 3.7 mcg/mL (60%) and 12.5 mcg x hours/mL (60%), respectively. The median Tmax of chenodiol following oral administration in CTX patients was 3 (0.5 to 8) hours.
  Text
  Chenodiol is administered orally. The mean Vd at steady-state is 0.36 L/kg. The plasma protein binding of chenodiol is approximately 98%. Chenodiol is well-absorbed from the small intestine and taken up by the liver where it is converted to taurine and glycine conjugates and then secreted into the bile. Conjugated chenodiol is then either reabsorbed in the terminal ileum, deconjugated before excretion, or decomposed by bacteria to lithocholic acid. About 80% of the lithocholate is excreted in the feces and the remainder is absorbed and converted in the liver to its poorly absorbed sulfolithocholyl conjugates. During chenodiol therapy, there is only a slight increase in biliary lithocholate while fecal bile acids are increased 3 to 4-fold. The total apparent clearance of chenodiol in cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX) patients is 20 L/hour.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Chenodiol is contraindicated in the presence of known hepatocyte dysfunction. Safe use of chenodiol depends upon selection of individuals without pre-existing hepatic disease and upon careful monitoring of serum aminotransferase concentrations to detect drug-induced liver toxicity.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
