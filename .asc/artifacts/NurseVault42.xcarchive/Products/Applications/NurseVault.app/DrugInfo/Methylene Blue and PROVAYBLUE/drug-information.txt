Drug Class: Methylene blue is a water-soluble thiazine dye for acquired methemoglobinemia.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 2 mg/kg/day IV.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): hypertension, palpitations, sinus tachycardia, infusion-related reactions, peripheral edema, pruritus, urticaria, nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, sneezing, blurred vision, hyperemia, ocular pruritus

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Methylene blue's primary route of elimination is via the kidneys. It should not be used in patients with renal impairment or renal failure in which drug accumulation could lead to toxicity.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Methylene blue's primary route of elimination is via the kidneys. It should not be used in patients with renal impairment or renal failure in which drug accumulation could lead to toxicity. Dosage adjustment may be needed.
 
Methylene blue should not be used to treat methemoglobinemia associated with cyanide toxicity. Methylene blue increases the release of cyanide from methemoglobin, increasing the cyanide concentration in the blood. Sodium nitrite is considered a safer and more effective alternative.
 
Intrathecal administration of methylene blue is contraindicated as it can cause neural damage. Subcutaneous administration or extravasation can produce necrotic abscesses. Methylene blue can be administered either intravenously (IV) or orally. When administering intravenously, do so slowly over several minutes to prevent local high concentrations of the product from producing additional methemoglobin.  
 
Methylene blue destroys erythrocytes. Prolonged administration can result in anemia. Hemoglobin count should be determined regularly in patients receiving chronic methylene blue therapy. Patients with preexisting anemia should be treated with caution.
 
Methylene blue is not effective in the treatment of methemoglobinemia resulting from G6PD deficiency (glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency), and administration of the drug can precipitate an acute hemolytic episode in these patients.
 
Methylene blue is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C; adequate evaluations have not been carried out for safe oral or diagnostic use during pregnancy, so the potential risks to the fetus must be weighed against the potential benefits to the mother. Intra-amniotic diagnostic usage during pregnancy should be discouraged. The pregnancy risk factor for methylene blue increases to pregnancy category D with intra-amniotic diagnostic usage as adverse events to the fetus have been reported, including hemolytic anemia of the newborn. While methylene blue has been historically used during pregnancy as a diagnostic agent injected into the amniotic fluid to diagnose premature rupture of the membranes (PROM), safer dye agents (e.g., indigo carmine) are available for this purpose. The use of methylene blue in females of childbearing potential requires that potential benefits be weighed against possible risks.
 
No data are available to describe the use of methylene blue during lactation. Caution should be applied in the woman who is breast-feeding an infant.
 
Results from an in vitro study indicate that methylene blue is a potent, reversible inhibitor of the monoamine oxidase type A enzyme (MAO-A). Clinicians should be aware of a potentially fatal interaction between methylene blue and serotonergic agents such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and clomipramine. MAO-A is responsible for the metabolism of serotonin; therefore, concurrent use of an MAO-A inhibitor with a serotonergic agent may result in a clinically significant interaction. Some case reports and other clinical data suggest that serotonin syndrome may occur post-operatively following administration of standard doses of methylene blue to patients stabilized on an SSRI, SNRI, or clomipramine (see Interactions). It may be advisable to avoid concurrent use if possible. In theory, methylene blue may also interact with monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) or drugs that possess MAOI-like activity (e.g., furazolidone, isoniazid, procarbazine, linezolid); therefore, use of methylene blue during MAOI therapy should be avoided if possible. Signs and symptoms of serotonin syndrome include fever, diaphoresis, shivering, myoclonus, tremor, tachycardia, diarrhea, nausea, headache, incoordination, mental status changes (e.g., agitation, confusion), hyperreflexia, seizures, and coma.

    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Methylene blue is also contraindicated in individuals with a history of severe hypersensitivity reaction to any other thiazine dye.
    Headline
    General Information
    General Information
    Methemoglobinemia may not resolve or may rebound after response to methylene blue treatment in individuals with methemoglobinemia due to aryl amines, such as aniline, or sulfa medications, such as dapsone. Monitor response to therapy with methylene blue through resolution of methemoglobinemia. If methemoglobinemia does not respond to 2 doses of methylene blue or if methemoglobinemia rebounds after a response, consider additional treatment options.
    Headline
    G6PD deficiency
    General Information
    Methylene blue is contraindicated in individuals with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency (G6PD deficiency) due to the risk of hemolytic anemia. Additionally, individuals with G6PD deficiency may not reduce methylene blue to its active form, and therefore, methylene blue may be ineffective in individuals with G6PD deficiency.
    Headline
    coadministration with certain medications
    General Information
    Avoid methylene blue coadministration with certain medications, specifically serotonergic drugs, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs), as well as opioids. Methylene blue may cause serious or fatal serotonergic syndrome when used in combination with these medications. If coadministration cannot be avoided, choose the lowest possible dose of methylene blue and observe the individual closely for central nervous system effects for up to 4 hours after administration. Do not administer serotonergic drugs within 72 hours after the last dose of methylene blue. The mechanism is not clearly understood; however, literature suggests that methylene blue is a potent reversible inhibitor of monoamine oxidase.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Methylene blue dosage adjustment is required in individuals with moderate or severe renal impairment or renal failure (eGFR 15 to 59 mL/minute/1.73 m2). Methylene blue concentrations increased significantly in subjects with renal impairment (eGFR 15 to 89 mL/minute/1.73 m2) in clinical studies.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Use methylene blue during pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus and advise pregnant persons of the potential risk to the fetus. Methylene blue may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy. Intra-amniotic injection of methylene blue during the second trimester was associated with neonatal intestinal atresia and fetal death. Intra-amniotic injection of methylene blue hours to days prior to birth can result in hyperbilirubinemia, hemolytic anemia, skin staining, methemoglobinemia, respiratory distress, and photosensitivity in the neonate. If methylene blue is administered at term, monitor the neonate for adverse reactions and institute supportive care. Administration of oral methylene blue in rats and rabbits during organogenesis at doses at least 32 and 16 times, respectively, the clinical dose produced adverse developmental outcomes.
    Headline
    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Monitor individuals with hepatic impairment or hepatic failure for toxicities and potential drug interactions for an extended period of time after methylene blue treatment. Methylene blue is extensively metabolized in the liver.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    There is no information regarding the presence of methylene blue in human milk, the effects on the breast-fed infant, or the effects on milk production. Discontinue breast-feeding during methylene blue administration and for up to 8 days after treatment with methylene blue due to the potential for serious adverse effects, including genotoxicity.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Advise individuals to avoid driving or operating machinery or participating in activities requiring coordination and concentration until any adverse reactions due to methylene blue have resolved. Methylene blue may cause confusion, dizziness, and vision disturbances.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Use the lowest number of methylene blue doses needed to achieve a response in geriatric adults. Because methylene blue is extensively excreted by the kidney and geriatric adults are more likely to have decreased renal function, the risk of adverse reactions to methylene blue may be greater in geriatric adults.

Almotriptan: (Major) Theoretically, concurrent use of methylene blue and serotonin-receptor agonists may increase the risk of serotonin syndrome. Methylene blue is a thiazine dye that is also a potent, reversible inhibitor of the enzyme responsible for the catabolism of serotonin in the brain (MAO-A) and serotonin-receptor agonists increase central serotonin effects. Cases of serotonin syndrome have been reported, primarily following administration of standard infusions of methylene blue (1 to 8 mg/kg) as a visualizing agent in parathyroid surgery, in patients receiving selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, serotonin/norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, or clomipramine. It is not known if patients receiving other serotonergic agents with intravenous methylene blue are at a comparable risk or if methylene blue administered by other routes (e.g., orally, local injection) or in doses less than 1 mg/kg IV can produce a similar outcome. Published interaction reports between intravenously administered methylene blue and serotonergic psychiatric agents have documented symptoms including lethargy, confusion, delirium, agitation, aggression, obtundation, myoclonus, expressive aphasia, hypertonia, pyrexia, elevated blood pressure, seizures, and/or coma. Serotonin syndrome is characterized by rapid development of various symptoms such as hyperthermia, hypertension, myoclonus, rigidity, hyperhidrosis, incoordination, diarrhea, mental status changes (e.g., confusion, delirium, or coma), and in rare cases, death.

Methylene blue is a thiazine dye that is also a potent, reversible inhibitor of the enzyme responsible for the catabolism of serotonin and norepinephrine in the brain (MAO-A). Amoxapine primarily increases the activity of norepinephrine, with in vitro data suggesting an insignificant binding affinity for serotonin. Therefore, the potential for serotonin syndrome during coadministration of amoxapine and methylene blue is unclear. Monitoring for potential increases in blood pressure is advised due to the potential for additive noradrenergic activity.

Methylene blue also has the potential to interact with serotonergic agents, such as amphetamines, which may increase the risk for serotonin syndrome. Serotonin syndrome is characterized by mental status changes (e.g., agitation, hallucinations, delirium, and coma), autonomic instability (e.g., tachycardia, labile blood pressure, dizziness, diaphoresis, flushing, hyperthermia), neuromuscular symptoms (e.g., tremor, rigidity, myoclonus, hyperreflexia, incoordination), seizures, gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), and in rare instances, death. It is not known if patients receiving other serotonergic psychiatric agents with intravenous methylene blue are at a comparable risk or if methylene blue administered by other routes (e.g., orally, local injection) or in doses less than 1 mg/kg IV can produce a similar outcome. Published interaction reports between intravenously administered methylene blue and serotonergic psychiatric agents have documented symptoms including lethargy, confusion, delirium, agitation, aggression, obtundation, myoclonus, expressive aphasia, hypertonia, pyrexia, elevated blood pressure, seizures, and/or coma. If serotonin syndrome occurs, all serotonergic agents should be discontinued and appropriate medical management should be implemented.

It is not known if patients receiving other serotonergic psychiatric agents with IV methylene blue are at a comparable risk or if methylene blue administered by other routes (e.g., orally, local injection) or in doses less than 1 mg/kg IV can produce a similar outcome. Published interaction reports between IV methylene blue and serotonergic psychiatric agents have documented symptoms including lethargy, confusion, delirium, agitation, aggression, obtundation, myoclonus, expressive aphasia, hypertonia, pyrexia, elevated blood pressure, seizures, and/or coma. Signs and symptoms of serotonin syndrome include fever, diaphoresis, shivering, myoclonus, tremor, tachycardia, diarrhea, nausea, headache, incoordination, mental status changes (e.g., agitation, confusion), hyperreflexia, seizures, and coma.
Phenelzine: (Contraindicated) Avoid concomitant use with monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs); Methylene Blue injection may cause serious or fatal serotonergic syndrome when used in combination with serotonergic drugs. Methylene blue has been demonstrated to be a potent MAOI. Whenever possible, a washout period should elapse between the use of the MAOI and Methylene Blue injection. Patients treated with Methylene Blue injection should be monitored for serotonin syndrome. If symptoms of serotonin syndrome occur, discontinue use, and initiate supportive treatment. Inform patients of the increased risk of serotonin syndrome and advise them to not to take any serotonergic drugs within 72 hours after the last dose of Methylene Blue. If the IV use of Methylene Blue cannot be avoided, choose the lowest possible dose and closely observe the patient for CNS and serotonin-related effects for up to 4 hours after Methylene Blue is given.

Signs and symptoms of serotonin syndrome include fever, diaphoresis, shivering, myoclonus, tremor, tachycardia, diarrhea, nausea, headache, incoordination, mental status changes (e.g., agitation, confusion), hyperreflexia, seizures, and coma. If emergent treatment with methylene blue is required in a patient receiving an SNRI, the SNRI must be stopped immediately and the patient should be monitored for symptoms of CNS toxicity for two weeks or until 24 hours after the last dose of methylene blue, whichever comes first. During non-emergent use of methylene blue, the SNRI should be stopped at least 2 weeks prior to methylene blue treatment, but also taking into consideration the half-life of the SNRI being discontinued.
Verteporfin: (Moderate) Use caution if coadministration of verteporfin with methylene blue is necessary due to the risk of increased photosensitivity. Verteporfin is a light-activated drug used in photodynamic therapy; all patients treated with verteporfin will be photosensitive. Concomitant use of other photosensitizing agents like methylene blue may increase the risk of a photosensitivity reaction.
Vilazodone: (Contraindicated) According to the manufacturer of vilazodone, treatment initiation with vilazodone is contraindicated in patients currently receiving intravenous (IV) methylene blue due to an increased risk of serotonin syndrome. If urgent psychiatric treatment is required, interventions other than vilazodone (e.g., alternative medication, hospitalization) should be considered. Conversely, in patients requiring urgent treatment with IV methylene blue, vilazodone should be discontinued immediately and methylene blue therapy initiated only if acceptable alternatives are not available and the potential benefits outweigh the risks. The patient should be monitored for serotonin syndrome for 2 weeks or until 24 hours after the last dose of methylene blue, whichever comes first. Vilazodone may be re-initiated 24 hours after the last dose of methylene blue. Concurrent use of an MAO-A inhibitor with a serotonergic agent may result in a clinically significant interaction. It is not known if methylene blue administered by other routes (e.g., orally, local injection) or in doses less than 1 mg/kg IV can produce a similar outcome. Published interaction reports between IV methylene blue and serotonergic psychiatric agents have documented symptoms including lethargy, confusion, delirium, agitation, aggression, obtundation, myoclonus, expressive aphasia, hypertonia, pyrexia, elevated blood pressure, seizures, and/or coma. Serotonin syndrome is characterized by rapid development of various symptoms such as hyperthermia, hypertension, myoclonus, rigidity, hyperhidrosis, incoordination, diarrhea, mental status changes (e.g., confusion, delirium, or coma), and in rare cases, death. MAO-A is responsible for the metabolism of serotonin; therefore, concurrent use of an MAO-A inhibitor with a serotonergic agent may result in a clinically significant interaction. It is not known if patients receiving other serotonergic psychiatric agents with intravenous methylene blue are at a comparable risk or if methylene blue administered by other routes (e.g., orally, local injection) or in doses less than 1 mg/kg IV can produce a similar outcome. Description
  Text
  Methylene blue is a water-soluble thiazine dye for acquired methemoglobinemia. Other uses include bacteriological staining, as an indicator dye, and as a tracer in the evaluation of gastrointestinal fistulas. Methylene blue carries a black box warning for the risk of serotonin syndrome with concomitant use of serotonergic medications; concurrent use of serotonergic medications should be avoided.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Methemoglobinemia occurs when hemoglobin is oxidized to form methemoglobin. Unlike hemoglobin, methemoglobin is incapable of oxygen transport which can lead to tissue hypoxia. Methylene blue is a water soluble thiazine dye. Methylene blue is converted to leucomethylene blue by NADPH reductase. Leucomethylene blue accelerates the reduction of the ferric iron of methemoglobin to the ferrous state of normal hemoglobin, allowing for oxygen delivery to tissues.

    After a single 1 mg/kg dose of methylene blue, AUC0-96h increased by 52%, 116%, and 192% in subjects with mild (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] 60 to 89 mL/minute/1.73 m2), moderate (eGFR 30 to 59 mL/minute/1.73 m2), and severe (eGFR 15 to 29 mL/minute/1.73 m2) renal impairment, respectively.   Text
  Methylene blue is administered intravenously. Methylene blue is highly protein bound (94%) and exhibits concentration-dependent partitioning into blood cells. Methylene blue is metabolized by CYP1A2, CYP2C19, and CYP2D6; however the predominant pathway is mediated by UDP-galactose transporter (UGT) conjugation by multiple UGT enzymes, including UGT1A4 and UGT1A9. Approximately 40% of methylene blue is excreted unchanged by the kidneys. Low concentrations of methylene blue increase the conversion of methemoglobin to hemoglobin. The exposure-response or exposure-safety relationship for methylene blue is unknown.
