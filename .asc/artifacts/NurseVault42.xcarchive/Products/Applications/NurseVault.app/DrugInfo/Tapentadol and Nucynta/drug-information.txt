Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Reserve opioid analgesics for use in individuals for whom alternative treatment options are ineffective, not tolerated, or would be otherwise inadequate to provide sufficient management of pain.
Individualize dosing for each patient; consider severity of pain, patient response, prior analgesic treatment experience, and risk factors for addiction, abuse, and misuse. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration consistent with individual treatment goals. Reserve titration to higher doses for patients in whom lower doses are insufficient and in whom expected benefits of using a higher dose opioid clearly outweigh substantial risks.
Monitor patients closely for respiratory depression, which may occur at any time during therapy. Consider this risk when selecting an initial dosage and when adjusting the dose. Continually reevaluate patients to assess the maintenance of pain control and the relative incidence of adverse reactions, as well as to monitor for the development of addiction, abuse, or misuse. If the level of pain increases after dosage stabilization, attempt to identify the source of increased pain before increasing the opioid dosage. If unacceptable opioid-related adverse reactions are observed, consider reducing the dosage. Adjust the dosage to obtain an appropriate balance between management of pain and opioid-related adverse reactions.
Many acute pain conditions (e.g., pain occurring with surgical procedures or acute musculoskeletal injuries) require no more than a few days of an opioid. Clinical guidelines on opioid prescribing for some acute pain conditions are available.
There is substantial interpatient variability in the relative potency of different opioid drugs and products. When adjusting tapentadol dosages or converting from another opioid agonist, it is better to underestimate the patient's daily dose requirements and give rescue doses than to risk an adverse reaction. If an adverse reaction occurs, the next dose may be reduced. Refer to the Opioid Agonists Drug Class Overview for approximate equianalgesic doses.
Avoid abrupt discontinuation or rapid dosage reduction in an individual who is physically dependent on opioid analgesics. When discontinuing tapentadol therapy in patients who are opioid-dependent, taper the dose gradually by 10% to 25% every 2 to 4 weeks. Patients who have been taking opioids for briefer periods of time may tolerate a more rapid taper. Ensure a multimodal approach to pain management, including mental health support if needed, is in place prior to an opioid analgesic taper.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Tapentadol use is contraindicated in patients with severe respiratory depression and in patients with acute or severe asthma or hypercapnia in unmonitored care settings or in the absence of resuscitative equipment. Respiratory depression occurs more frequently in older or debilitated patients and in those suffering from conditions accompanied by hypoxemia, hypercapnia, or upper airway obstruction. This includes patients with asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or cor pulmonale, severe obesity, sleep apnea syndrome, myxedema, kyphoscoliosis, central nervous system (CNS) depression, or coma. Therapeutic doses of tapentadol may result in increases in airway pressure and decreases in respiratory drive. Concurrent administration with other respiratory depressants exacerbates this risk (see Drug Interactions). Consider non-opioid analgesic alternatives in at-risk patients.
 
Tapentadol use is contraindicated in patients with known or suspected paralytic ileus. Due to the effects of opiate agonists on the gastrointestinal tract, tapentadol should be used cautiously in patients with pre-existing biliary tract or GI disease including GI obstruction, ulcerative colitis (UC), or pre-existing constipation. Patients with UC or other inflammatory bowel disease may be more sensitive to the constipating effects of opiate agonists. Also, use with caution in patients with pancreatic or biliary tract disease, including acute pancreatitis, as mu-opioid agonists are known to cause spasm of the sphincter of Oddi. Opiate agonists may obscure the diagnosis or clinical course in patients with an acute abdomen. Although opiate agonists are not desirable for use in patients with diarrhea secondary to poisoning or infectious diarrhea, antimotility agents have been used successfully in these patients. If possible, opiate agonists should not be given until the toxic substance has been eliminated.
 
Tapentadol use is contraindicated in patients who are receiving or have received MAOI therapy within the past 14 days (see Drug Interactions). Concomitant usage of tapentadol and MAOIs may result in additive norepinephrine concentrations, and excessive norepinephrine is associated with cardiovascular adverse events including arrhythmias and cardiac ischemia. Further, the use of MAOIs with Serotonin and Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitor (SNRI) products, like tapentadol, is associated with an increased risk of seizures and serotonin syndrome. Concurrent therapy of tapentadol with another serotonergic agent (e.g., SSRI, SNRI, tricyclic antidepressant, and serotonin-receptor agonist) may also increase the risk of developing serotonin syndrome (see Drug Interactions).
 
Use with caution in patients with pre-existing CNS depression, head trauma, or increased intracranial pressure (ICP). Opiate agonists can compromise the evaluation of neurologic parameters. Rapid administration of high-dose opiate agonists may transiently elevate intracranial pressure and reduce cerebral perfusion pressures. These events are associated with opiate-induced lowering of mean arterial pressure, which stimulates a regulatory response to increase cerebral blood flow leading to increased ICP. Opiate agonist-induced respiratory depression can produce cerebral hypoxia and raise CSF pressure, which is unrelated to but may exaggerate the injury. Additive CNS depression may result from the use of tapentadol in patients receiving other CNS depressant agents (see Drug Interactions). Advise patients to avoid illicit drug use and ethanol intoxication while taking tapentadol.
 
Although tapentadol is not specifically studied in patients with seizure disorder, opiate analgesics are known to lower the seizure threshold and can precipitate seizures; use with caution in at-risk patients.
 
Any patient receiving an opiate agonist should be warned about the possibility of sedation and to use caution when driving or operating machinery.
 
Tapentadol is subject to misuse, abuse, and diversion and may be sought by those with addiction disorders. Use with caution in patients with a history of substance abuse or alcoholism. Because tapentadol is a strong opiate agonist, it is subject to substance abuse and psychologic dependence (i.e., drug addiction) or criminal diversion. Assess patients for their clinical risks of opioid abuse or addiction before opioid initiation. All patients who receive opioids should be routinely monitored for signs of misuse, abuse, and addiction. Abuse and addiction are separate and distinct from physiologic dependence and tolerance. Physicians should be aware that psychologic dependence may not be accompanied by concurrent tolerance and symptoms of physiologic dependence. In addition, abuse of opiate agonists can occur in the absence of true psychologic dependence and is characterized by misuse for non-medical purposes, often in combination with other psychoactive substances. Health care professionals should not let concerns over psychologic dependence deter them from using adequate amounts of opiate agonists in the management of severe pain. Counsel patients regarding the proper use and common misconceptions of opioid use in pain management. Tapentadol is for the treatment of pain requiring opiate agonist analgesia and is not approved for the management of substance abuse (alcohol or drug dependence). Patients receiving opiate substitution therapy for opiate substance abuse will have increased tolerance to the analgesic effects of opiate agonists used for acute pain and will require higher and more frequent dosing to control their pain. Opiate substitution therapy does not adequately treat pain.
 
Abrupt discontinuation of prolonged tapentadol therapy can result in withdrawal symptoms (see Adverse Reactions). A gradual tapering of tapentadol may reduce withdrawal reactions. In clinical study, abrupt withdrawal after 90 days of therapy resulted in withdrawal symptoms in 17% of patients receiving tapentadol vs. 29% of patients taking oxycodone IR as measured using the Clinical Opioid Withdraw Scale (COWS). Avoid use of a narcotic antagonist in an individual physically dependent on opioids, as an acute withdrawal syndrome will be precipitated by administration of the usual dose of an opioid antagonist. The severity of the withdrawal syndrome produced will depend on the degree of physical dependence and on the administered dose of the opioid antagonist. If treatment of respiratory depression in an individual physically dependent on opioids is necessary, administer the opioid antagonist with extreme care; titrate the antagonist dose by using smaller than usual doses.
 
Use with caution in patients with hepatic disease. Tapentadol undergoes extensive inactivation via first pass metabolism; therefore, greater systemic exposure can be expected with declining hepatic function. Use is not recommended in patients with severe hepatic impairment. Dose adjustment and caution are recommended if tapentadol is used in patients with moderate hepatic impairment (see Dosage).
 
Use with caution in patients with pre-existing severe renal impairment or renal failure because tapentadol has not been adequately studied in this population; minimal tapentadol accumulation or other unexpected pharmacokinetic irregularities may be possible. No tapentadol dosing adjustments are required in patients with mild to moderate renal dysfunction.
 
Use with caution in the elderly. Dosage adjustments of tapentadol may be required in elderly patients due to underlying renal or hepatic impairment (see Dosage), multiple co-therapies (see Drug Interactions), and/or declining respiratory and CNS function.
 
Safety and efficacy of tapentadol have not been established in children and adolescents less than 18 years of age; tapentadol has not been adequately studied in these populations.
 
Tapentadol is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C; there are no adequate and well controlled studies of tapentadol in pregnant women. In animal study, multiple malformations and embryofetal toxicity were noted in a study of pregnant rabbits given tapentadol subcutaneously at a range of doses varying from therapeutic to supra-therapeutic. In addition, skeletal development delays, low birth weight, and decreased rates of weight gain were noted in the pups of rats given tapentadol at doses associated with maternal toxicity. Tapentadol should be used during pregnancy only if the potential benefit to the mother justifies the potential risk to the fetus.
 
According to the manufacturer, tapentadol should be not be used by breast-feeding women. It is unknown if tapentadol is excreted in human or animal breast milk; however, the calculated volume of distribution of tapentadol is large (approximately 540 L after IV administration) and increased risk to nursing infants cannot be ruled out. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.

    General Information
    Opioids are associated with serious adverse effects, which may be life-threatening, including addiction, abuse, and misuse, respiratory depression, and neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS).
    Headline
    brain tumor, coma, head trauma, increased intracranial pressure, loss of consciousness
    General Information
    Avoid opioid use in individuals with loss of consciousness or coma; opioids may obscure the clinical course in an individual with a head trauma injury. Monitor individuals who may be susceptible to the intracranial effect of carbon dioxide retention (e.g., those with evidence of increased intracranial pressure or brain tumor) for signs of sedation and respiratory depression, particularly when initiating opioid therapy. Opioids may reduce respiratory drive and the resultant carbon dioxide retention can further increase intracranial pressure.
    Headline
    seizures
    General Information
    Opioids may increase seizure frequency in individuals with seizure disorders and may increase seizure risk in other clinical settings associated with seizures. Regularly evaluate individuals with a history of seizure disorders for worsened seizure control during opioid therapy.
    Headline
    hypovolemia, shock
    General Information
    Avoid the use of opioids in individuals with circulatory shock. Opioids may cause vasodilation that can further reduce cardiac output and blood pressure. Monitor individuals whose ability to maintain blood pressure has been compromised by hypovolemia or concurrent administration of certain CNS depressant medications (e.g., phenothiazines, general anesthetics) for hypotension after opioid treatment initiation or dosage titration due to increased risk for severe hypotension.
    Headline
    asthma exacerbation, cachexia, central sleep apnea, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coadministration with certain medications, cor pulmonale, hypercapnia, hypoxia, respiratory depression, respiratory insufficiency, severe asthma
    General Information
    Tapentadol is contraindicated in individuals with significant respiratory depression and those with asthma exacerbation or severe asthma in an unmonitored setting or in the absence of resuscitative equipment. Avoid opioid coadministration with certain medications, specifically other CNS depressants when possible, as this significantly increases the risk for profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death. Reserve concomitant prescribing of these medications for use in individuals for whom alternative treatment options are inadequate; if concurrent use is necessary, use the lowest effective dosages and minimum treatment durations needed. Monitor individuals closely for signs or symptoms of respiratory depression and sedation. Consider prescribing an opioid overdose reversal agent if concomitant use is warranted. Individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cor pulmonale, respiratory insufficiency, hypoxia, hypercapnia, or preexisting respiratory depression are at increased risk of decreased respiratory drive even at recommended doses. Individuals with advanced age, cachexia, or debilitation are also at an increased risk for opioid-induced respiratory depression. Monitor such individuals closely, particularly when initiating and titrating the opioid; consider the use of non-opioid analgesics in these individuals. Carbon dioxide retention from opioid-induced respiratory depression may also worsen opioid sedating effects. Consider decreasing the opioid dosage in individuals with central sleep apnea (CSA). Opioids increase the risk of CSA and sleep-related hypoxemia in a dose-dependent fashion.
    Headline
    acute pancreatitis, biliary tract disease, GI obstruction
    General Information
    Tapentadol is contraindicated in individuals with known or suspected GI obstruction, including paralytic ileus. Opioids may cause spasm of the sphincter of Oddi or increases in serum amylase. Monitor individuals with biliary tract disease, including acute pancreatitis, for worsening symptoms.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    The safety and efficacy of tapentadol has not been studied in individuals with severe hepatic impairment or hepatic failure (Child-Pugh class C) and use in this population is not recommended. Dosage adjustment or tapentadol is necessary in individuals with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B). Regularly evaluate individuals with moderate hepatic impairment for CNS or respiratory depression. Tapentadol has not been studied in pediatric populations with hepatic impairment; therefore, use in these populations is not recommended. Higher tapentadol concentrations have been observed in individuals with hepatic impairment compared to those with normal hepatic function.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Tapentadol is not recommended in individuals with severe renal impairment or renal failure (CrCl less than 30 mL/minute) due to accumulation of a metabolite formed during tapentadol glucuronidation. The clinical relevance of the elevated metabolite is not known. Tapentadol has not been studied in pediatric populations with renal impairment; therefore, use in these populations is not recommended.
    Headline
    ethanol ingestion, major psychiatric disorder, opioid overdose, substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    Opioids expose users to the risks of addiction, abuse, and misuse. Although the risk of addiction in any individual is unknown, it can occur in individuals appropriately prescribed an opioid. Addiction can occur at recommended dosages and if the medication is misused or abused. The risk of opioid-related overdose or overdose-related death is increased with higher doses, and this risk persists over the course of therapy. The potential for these risks should not prevent the proper management of pain in any given individual. Assess an individual's risk for opioid addiction, abuse, or misuse before prescribing an opioid, and monitor for the development of these behaviors or conditions. Risks are increased in individuals with a personal or family history of substance abuse disorder (including alcohol or drug abuse or addiction) or major psychiatric disorder (e.g., major depression). Individuals at increased risk may be prescribed opioids, but use in such individuals necessitates intensive counseling about the risks and proper use of the opioid along with frequent reevaluation for signs of addiction, abuse, and misuse. Opioids are sought for nonmedical use and are subject to diversion from legitimate prescribed use. Consider these risks when prescribing or dispensing opioids. Strategies to reduce these risks include prescribing the medication in the smallest appropriate quantity and educating about proper medication storage and disposal. Abuse or misuse of tapentadol extended-release tablets by chewing, crushing, snorting, or injecting the dissolved product will result in the uncontrolled delivery of tapentadol and can result in overdose and death. Consumption of extended-release tapentadol with alcohol may result in increased drug exposure that may cause a fatal overdose; advise individuals against ethanol ingestion, including the ingestion of alcohol contained in prescription or non-prescription medications. Keep opioids out of the reach of children, others for whom the medication was not prescribed, and pets as accidental exposure or improper use may cause respiratory failure and a fatal overdose. Accidental exposure of even a single dose of an opioid, especially by younger individuals, can result in a fatal overdose. Discuss the importance of an opioid overdose reversal agent and the options for obtaining an opioid overdose reversal agent (e.g., prescription, over-the-counter, or as part of a community-based program), especially with individuals who are at increased risk of opioid overdose, such as those who are also using other CNS depressants, who have a history of opioid use disorder (OUD), who have experienced a previous opioid overdose, or who have household members or other close contacts at risk for accidental exposure or opioid overdose. Educate individuals and caregivers on how to recognize respiratory depression, and how to use an opioid overdose reversal agent for the emergency treatment of opioid overdose. Emphasize the importance of calling 911 or getting emergency medical help, even if an opioid overdose reversal agent is administered.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Advise individuals against driving or operating machinery or participating in activities requiring coordination and concentration during opioid therapy unless they are tolerant to the effects of opioids and know how they will react to the medication. Opioids can impair the mental or physical abilities needed to perform potentially hazardous activities.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Geriatric adults may have increased sensitivity to opioids. In general, use cautious dose selection for opioids in geriatric adults, usually starting at the low end of the dosing range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function and concomitant disease or other medication therapy. Increase the opioid dosage slowly in geriatric adults and frequently reevaluate for signs of CNS and respiratory depression. According to the Beers Criteria, opioid agonists are considered potentially inappropriate medications in geriatric adults with a history of falls or fractures and should be avoided in these populations, except in the setting of severe acute pain, since opioids can produce ataxia, impaired psychomotor function, syncope, and additional falls. If an opioid must be used, consider reducing the use of other CNS-active medications that increase the risk of falls and fractures and implement strategies to reduce fall risk. In individuals receiving palliative care, the balance of benefits and harms of medication management may differ from those of the general population of older adults.
    Headline
    labor, obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on tapentadol use during pregnancy are insufficient to define the risk of major birth defects or miscarriage. Opioids cross the placenta. Animal studies show embryo-fetal mortality and structural malformations in rabbits, and skeletal maturation delays in rats at exposures during organogenesis that were equal to or below the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD), based on AUC, respectively. Increased rat pup mortality occurred at oral exposures equivalent to the MRHD during organogenesis and through lactation. Advise pregnant individuals of the potential fetal risk. Opioids can prolong labor and obstetric delivery by temporarily reducing the strength, duration, and frequency of uterine contractions. This effect is not consistent and may be offset by an increased rate of cervical dilatation, which may shorten labor. In addition, neonates exposed to opioids in utero may present with respiratory depression at birth. Because of this, an opioid antagonist (e.g., naloxone) must be available for reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression in the neonate at delivery. Guidelines recommend early universal screening of pregnant individuals for opioid use and opioid use disorder at the first prenatal visit. Obtain a thorough history of substance use and review the Prescription Drug Monitoring Program to determine if individuals have received prior prescriptions for opioids or other high-risk drugs such as benzodiazepines. Discuss the risks and benefits of opioid use during pregnancy, including the risk of becoming physiologically dependent on opioids, the possibility for neonatal withdrawal, and how long-term opioid use may affect care during a future pregnancy. In individuals undergoing uncomplicated normal spontaneous vaginal birth, consider opioid therapy only if expected benefits for both pain and function are anticipated to outweigh risks. If opioids are used, use in combination with nonpharmacologic therapy and nonopioid pharmacologic therapy, as appropriate. Use immediate-release opioids instead of extended-release or long-acting opioids; order the lowest effective dosage and prescribe no greater quantity of opioids than needed for the expected duration of such pain severe enough to require opioids. For individuals using opioids for chronic pain, consider strategies to avoid or minimize the use of opioids, including alternative pain therapies (i.e., nonpharmacologic) and nonopioid pharmacologic treatments. Opioid agonist pharmacotherapy (e.g., methadone or buprenorphine) is preferable to medically supervised withdrawal in pregnant individuals with opioid use disorder.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with opioids. Long-term use of opioids may influence the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, leading to hormonal changes that may manifest as a libido decrease, impotence, erectile dysfunction, amenorrhea, or infertility. The causal role of opioids in the clinical manifestations of hypogonadism is unknown. It is not known whether the effects on fertility are reversible.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero may present with respiratory depression and psycho-physiologic effects at birth. Monitor exposed newborns for signs of excess sedation and respiratory depression. If necessary, administer an opioid antagonist (e.g., naloxone) to reverse the effects of opioid-induced respiratory depression. Long-term use of opioids during pregnancy can result in physical dependence in the exposed infant. Monitor newborns for symptoms of withdrawal and manage accordingly. Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome may present as irritability, hyperactivity, abnormal sleep pattern, high-pitched cry, tremor, vomiting, diarrhea, and failure to gain weight. It can be life-threatening if not recognized and treated. Onset, duration, and severity of opioid withdrawal may vary based on the specific opioid used, duration of use, timing and amount of last maternal use, and rate of elimination by the infant.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Avoid the use of extended-release tapentadol products during breast-feeding. Immediate-release formulations may be used with caution. There are no data on the presence of tapentadol in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Based on its pharmacokinetic properties, tapentadol is likely present in human milk; risk to the breastfed child cannot be excluded. Monitor children exposed to opioids through human milk for excess sedation and respiratory depression. Pregnancy And Lactation
  Breastfeeding
    General Information
    Avoid the use of extended-release tapentadol products during breast-feeding. Immediate-release formulations may be used with caution. There are no data on the presence of tapentadol in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Based on its pharmacokinetic properties, tapentadol is likely present in human milk; risk to the breastfed child cannot be excluded. Monitor children exposed to opioids through human milk for excess sedation and respiratory depression. Withdrawal symptoms may occur in the breastfed child when maternal administration of the opioid is stopped or when breast-feeding is discontinued.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Tapentadol may cause a variety of effects on the GI system, most commonly nausea/vomiting. In pre-marketing clinical studies, patients taking tapentadol experienced nausea (30% vs. 13% on placebo), vomiting (18% vs. 4% on placebo), constipation (8% vs. 3% on placebo), xerostomia (4% vs. < 1% on placebo), anorexia (2% vs 0% on placebo), and dyspepsia (2% vs < 1% on placebo). The incidence of nausea and vomiting increased with increasing dosage in pooled data of pre-marketing studies. Active-controlled trial data suggest that tapentadol may have a greater gastrointestinal tolerability as compared to other opioid agonists. In a trial published in abstract form, the incidence of nausea was 40.9% among those taking tapentadol 200 mg and 57.1% in patients on morphine 60 mg; however, the incidence of vomiting was similar among the treatment groups. In patients taking tapentadol IR 50—100 mg compared to those taking oxycodone IR 10—15 mg, the incidence of nausea (18.4% vs 29.4%, respectively), vomiting (16.9% vs 30%, respectively), and constipation (12.8% vs 27.1%, respectively) was consistently lower among those taking tapentadol. Constipation is due to opioid induced-decreases in GI motility and secretions. Although not specifically reported with tapentadol use, ileus or GI obstruction may result. Tolerance rarely develops to this effect; therefore, advise patients to follow a bowel regimen consistently during opioid therapy. Increased fluid and fiber intake and the use of stool softeners and laxatives are recommended. Treat persistent symptoms of constipation as required to prevent impaction.
 
Tapentadol's effect on opiate receptors in the reticular activating system and striatum produces sedation. Patients should be warned that activity requiring mental alertness can be affected since CNS depression, including drowsiness, confusion, and dizziness can occur. Tolerance to opioid-induced CNS depression usually develops within a few days; however, if it does not resolve, excessive sedation of other opiate agonist medications has been managed with stimulants such as methylphenidate. Lethargy, headache, nervousness, sleep disturbances, abnormal dreams, dysphoria, euphoria, memory impairment, alterations of mood (feelings of floating, disorientation, apprehension), anxiety, abnormal thinking, and restlessness have been reported with tapentadol therapy. Tremor has also been reported. Dysphoric reactions may occur at doses within therapeutic range with initial and continued use. High doses of tapentadol are excitatory due to an increase in circulating catecholamine concentrations; in pre-marketing trials, seizures were reported in 1 patient with an undisclosed history of epilepsy after a single dose of tapentadol. Patients were excluded from pre-marketing clinical trials if they had any known pre-existing seizure disorder.
 
The most significant adverse effect associated with opiate agonist use is respiratory depression. This results from a decreased sensitivity to carbon dioxide in the brainstem. Respiratory depression is more common in elderly or debilitated patients, after large initial doses in non-opioid tolerant patients, or when opioids are given with other agents that cause CNS depression. The sensitivity of the respiratory center returns to normal within 2—3 hours. When opioids are appropriately titrated, the risk of respiratory depression is generally small as tolerance rapidly develops to this effect. Caution needs to be employed in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, decreased pulmonary reserve, cardiovascular disease, or who are taking other CNS depressants. Symptomatic respiratory depression should be treated cautiously with an opioid antagonist such as naloxone. Patients may also develop respiratory depression through sub-acute overdose of an opiate agonist in which sedation builds up slowly leading to a decreased respiratory rate and then respiratory failure. Hypoxia, cough, and dyspnea have been reported in patients on tapentadol in pre-marketing trials.
 
Tapentadol and related compounds can cause cardiovascular adverse reactions. These reactions include sinus bradycardia, sinus tachycardia, palpitations, hypertension, hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, diaphoresis, flushing, and syncope. Orthostatic hypotension may arise as a secondary effect from peripheral vasodilatation. In cases of severe respiratory and/or circulatory depression, shock and cardiac arrest may occur. Cardiovascular events may be symptomatic of a hyperserotonergic state.
 
Urinary system effects were reported in < 1% of patients who received tapentadol and included increased urinary frequency and urinary retention.
 
Opiate agonists present the potential for abuse although the true risk of this phenomenon is extremely low. Physiological dependence, however, will occur during chronic opiate agonist therapy as evidenced by a withdrawal syndrome occurring after abrupt discontinuation of the drug in these patients. Symptoms of tapentadol withdrawal include anxiety, sweating, insomnia, rigors, pain, nausea, tremors, diarrhea, sneezing, rhinorrhea, piloerection, and rarely, hallucinations. It is important to differentiate physiological dependence, the onset of a withdrawal syndrome upon abrupt discontinuation of the drug, from psychological dependence. Psychological dependence is a behavioral syndrome characterized by drug craving, overwhelming concern with acquisition of the drug and other drug-related behaviors such as drug selling and seeking the drug from multiple sources.
 
Similar to other opiates agonists, the development of pharmacologic tolerance to the analgesic effects of other opiate agonists may be possible in some patients treated with tapentadol. Tolerance is the need for increasing opioid doses to maintain initial pain relief. Typically, tolerance presents as a decrease in the duration of analgesia and is managed by increasing the opioid dose or frequency. When increasing doses of analgesia are required, causes may be multi-factorial including tolerance, progression of disease, or psychological distress.
 
Tapentadol and other opiate agonists are known to cause pruritus and other dermal disorders. The pruritus appears to be dose-related and may not be confined to the administration site. Opiate agonist-induced pruritus is thought to be mediated through stimulation of central opiate receptors since opiate antagonists (i.e., nalmefene or naloxone) have been shown to ameliorate pruritus associated with cholestasis. Rash (unspecified) has also been reported among patients on tapentadol. Use of H1-blockers or changing to a different opioid may lessen the pruritus and/or rash. In addition, 3% of patient receiving tapentadol in clinical trials have experienced hyperhidrosis compared to < 1% of patients receiving placebo.
 
Although extremely rare, anaphylactoid reactions including cases of anaphylaxis have been reported. Evidence of histamine release such as urticaria may occur with tapentadol use.
 
Serotonin syndrome may occur with tapentadol use, especially when used with serotonergic drugs (see Drug Interactions). Serotonin syndrome may occur with the recommended dosage range and may be life-threatening. Symptoms may include agitation, hallucinations, coma, tachycardia, labile blood pressure, hyperthermia, hyperreflexia, incoordination, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea.
 
In pre-marketing trials infection of the upper respiratory tract and urinary tract have been reported with greater frequency among patients taking tapentadol than those taking placebo; however, causality to the drug has not been established.

    General Information
    Tapentadol is a controlled substance with a high potential for abuse and psychological dependence. Abuse and addiction are separate and distinct from physiological dependence and tolerance, which can develop during chronic opioid therapy. In postmarketing studies, addiction, abuse, misuse, and fatal and non-fatal opioid overdose were observed in subjects with long-term opioid use. Higher baseline opioid dose was the strongest and most consistent predictor of opioid-involved overdose or opioid overdose-related death. Tolerance is characterized by a reduced response to a drug after repeated administration. Physiological dependence develops as a result of repeated drug use. It manifests with a withdrawal syndrome after abrupt discontinuation or significant dose reduction, or administration of an agent with opioid antagonist activity (e.g., naloxone). Withdrawal is characterized by restlessness, lacrimation, rhinorrhea, yawning, perspiration, chills, myalgia, and mydriasis. Withdrawal symptoms may include irritability, hyperactivity, abnormal sleep pattern, high-pitched cry, tremor, vomiting, diarrhea, and failure to gain weight. Onset, duration, and severity of opioid withdrawal may vary based on the specific opioid used, duration of use, timing and amount of last maternal use, and rate of elimination by the infant. Exposed newborns should be monitored closely. NOWS can be life-threatening if not recognized and treated. Treatment should be managed by clinicians experienced with neonatal withdrawal.

Description
  Text
  Tapentadol is an oral opioid agonist. Immediate-release tapentadol is indicated for acute pain severe enough to require an opioid analgesic and for which alternative treatments are inadequate in adults and pediatric individuals 6 years and older. The extended-release tablets are indicated for adults with persistent, severe pain or persistent, severe neuropathic pain associated with diabetic peripheral neuropathy requiring an opioid analgesic and that cannot be adequately treated with alternative options, including immediate-release opioids. Tapentadol activates the mu-opioid receptor and inhibits norepinephrine synaptic reuptake. Norepinephrine reuptake inhibition appears to have additive analgesic effect. Immediate-release tapentadol was effective in treating chronic pain conditions and postsurgical acute pain in adults during clinical trials. Extended-release tapentadol demonstrated significant improvements in pain intensity over 15 weeks in patients with moderate to severe chronic low back pain and better gastrointestinal tolerability compared to controlled-release oxycodone. Tapentadol may be a preferred analgesic in patients with altered cytochrome P450 (CYP450) activity or in adults with mild hepatic or mild to moderate renal impairment because no metabolic activation is necessary for analgesia and no active metabolites have been identified. Use is not recommended in adults with severe hepatic or renal impairment and in pediatric patients with renal or hepatic impairment. Tapentadol may cause serious respiratory depression and expose users to risks of addiction, abuse, and misuse, which can lead to overdose and death. Individualization of dosage regimens and close patient monitoring for adverse effects are needed.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Tapentadol is a centrally-acting synthetic analgesic. Analgesic efficacy is due to mu-opioid agonist activity and the inhibition of norepinephrine reuptake. Mu-opioid agonism decreases ascending pain transmission to the thalamus, which is involved in conscious pain processing. Opoid agonists interrupt ascending pain signals in the spinal cord and enhance activation of the descending pain modulation pathway. Inhibition of norepinephrine reuptake enhances the descending inhibition of pain via activation of inhibitory alpha-2 receptors.

    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    After absorption from the gastrointestinal tract, tapentadol undergoes extensive first pass metabolism resulting in an absolute bioavailability of approximately 32%. The drug does not require activation for analgesic effect.
Immediate-release: Time to Cmax is 1.25 hours, and there are dose proportional increases in Cmax and AUC over the dosing range of 50 to 150 mg. The absorption of the immediate-release product is expected to increase when administered with food. In a human study, tapentadol administration with a high-fat, high-calorie meal increased the AUC by 25% and Cmax by 16%.
Extended-release: Maximum serum concentrations are achieved 3 to 6 hours after administration. Dose proportional increases in AUC have been observed over the therapeutic dosing range. With twice-daily dosing, a steady-state drug concentration is attained after the third dose. Minimal accumulation has been noted with an oral dosing regimen of 250 mg twice daily. The AUC and Cmax increased by 6% and 17%, respectively, when administered after a high-fat, high-calorie breakfast. Administration with ethanol will increase drug bioavailability.

  Text
  Tapentadol is administered orally. After systemic absorption, the drug is widely distributed throughout the body; there is insufficient information on excretion into breast milk. Minimum protein binding of approximately 20% occurs. Tapentadol is extensively metabolized to inactive compounds with only 3% excreted as unchanged drug after oral administration. Metabolism routes include conjugation via Phase 2 pathways via UGT (approximately 70%), methylation via CYP2C9 and CYP2C19 (approximately 13%), hydroxylation via CYP2D6 (approximately 2%), and unstated or unknown pathways (approximately 12%). Approximately 99% of tapentadol and metabolites are eliminated in the urine, with 1% fecal elimination, and no measurable respiratory elimination. The terminal half-life is approximately 4 hours with immediate-release tablets and approximately 5 hours with extended-release tablets.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, UGT
The major pathway of tapentadol metabolism is Phase 2 metabolism via UGT (70%), while minor pathways include metabolism via CYP2C9 and CYP2C19 (13%), and CYP2D6 (2%). Drug interactions with cytochrome P450 enzyme inducers or inhibitors are not expected. Tapentadol does not induce or inhibit cytochrome P450 enzymes.

  Route Specific Administrations
    Administration Details
      Name
      Oral Solid Formulations
      Description
      Immediate-release tablets
Avoid use of immediate-release tablets in pediatric individuals who are unable to swallow tablets or who weigh less than 40 kg. The recommended dose cannot be achieved with available tablet strengths in individuals who weigh less than 40 kg. Consider the use of tapentadol oral solution in such individuals.
 
Extended-release tablets
Swallow tablets whole. Do not dissolve, crush, chew, split, or otherwise break as this could lead to the rapid release and absorption of a potentially toxic dose of tapentadol.
Take each tablet with enough water to ensure complete swallowing immediately after placing it in the mouth.
      Name
      Oral Liquid Formulations
      Description
      Oral solution
No dilution is necessary.
Measure and administer the oral solution using a calibrated measuring device. For adults, dispense the solution with the enclosed oral syringe and adapter. For pediatric individuals, dispense with a 3 mL oral syringe for doses that are 3 mL or less or dispense with a 5 mL oral syringe for doses greater than 3 mL.
The oral solution must be administered to a pediatric individual by an adult. Pediatric individuals must not have access to the oral solution.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Adults
Mild impairment (Child-Pugh score 5 to 6): No dosage adjustment necessary.
Moderate impairment (Child-Pugh score 7 to 9): For the immediate-release formulation, an initial dose of 50 mg PO every 8 hours is recommended with titration based on tolerability and efficacy. For the extended-release formulation, an initial dose of 50 mg PO every 24 hours is recommended with titration, if needed, to no more than 100 mg PO every 24 hours.
Severe impairment (Child-Pugh score 10 to 15): Use of tapentadol is not recommended in patients with severe hepatic impairment as limited data are available.
 
Pediatric patients
Avoid use in hepatic impairment.
  Renal Impairment
  Adults
No dosing adjustment is needed in patients with mild to moderate renal impairment (CrCl 30 to 90 mL/minute). Use is not recommended in patients with severe renal impairment (CrCl less than 30 mL/minute) as limited data are available.
 
Pediatric patients
Avoid use in renal impairment.
