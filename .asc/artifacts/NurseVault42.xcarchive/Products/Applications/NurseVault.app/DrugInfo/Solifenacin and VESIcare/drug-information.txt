Drug Class: Solifenacin is an oral competitive, selective muscarinic antagonist (M3) indicated to treat overactive bladder (OAB) and its symptoms in adults.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 10 mg/day PO.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Solifenacin is contraindicated in patients who have demonstrated hypersensitivity to the drug or the product ingredients.
 
Due to the anticholinergic effects, solifenacin is contraindicated in patients with urinary retention. In addition, anticholinergics may precipitate urinary retention in patients with preexisting urinary tract obstruction or prostatic hypertrophy, so caution is warranted. Altered pharmacokinetics of solifenacin occur in renal impairment (CrCl <= 30 ml/min) and renal failure; dosages should be adjusted (see Dosage). Use with caution in other patients with significant renal disease.
 
Use solifenacin with caution in patients with controlled glaucoma. Solifenacin is contraindicated in patients with uncontrolled narrow or closed-angle glaucoma. Solifenacin may increase intraocular pressure and aqueous outflow resistance in patients with closed-angle glaucoma; caution is advised in patients with treated disease. The anticholinergic effects of solifenacin may make the eyes dry and this can cause irritation for wearers of contact lenses.
 
Patients receiving other anticholinergic medications (e.g., dicyclomine, benztropine, or atropine) along with solifenacin may experience increased side effects which may limit therapy. Patients should be advised to consult their physician if they experience increased or intolerable dry mouth, dry eyes, or constipation.
 
Solifenacin has a direct antispasmodic effect on smooth muscle that can delay gastric emptying, and thusly is contraindicated for use in patients with gastric retention (e.g., gastroparesis, GI obstruction, pyloric stenosis). It is not recommended for use in the setting of ileus, toxic megacolon, or severe ulcerative colitis. In general, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) may be aggravated by the use of anticholinergic medications.
 
Hepatic disease (e.g., cirrhosis) can significantly alter the elimination of solifenacin. Patients with moderately reduced hepatic function should have a dosage modification; patients with severe liver dysfunction should not receive solifenacin (see Dosage). Also, dosage reductions may be required in patients receiving medications that impact CYP3A4 (see Drug Interactions).
 
Patients should be advised to use caution when driving or operating machinery while receiving solifenacin until the effects of the drug are known. Anticholinergic drugs in general can cause blurred vision, dizziness, or somnolence in some patients.
 
Although the pharmacokinetic parameters of solifenacin are increased by 20—25% in patients 65—80 years old compared to younger patients, in placebo controlled trials, geriatric patients exhibited similar safety and efficacy. It is recommended to use solifenacin with caution in elderly patients, however it is likely that no dosage adjustment is needed.
 
QT prolongation was studied in a multidose, double blind, placebo and positive control trial. The median difference in heart rate from baseline compared to placebo for 10 mg and 30 mg doses was -2 and 0, respectively. A significant period effect on the QTc was observed. QT interval prolongation was greater with the 30 mg dose (three times maximum dose) compared to the 10 mg dose, but less than when compared with the positive control. The manufacturer did not provide specific dosing guidelines. Avoid use in patients at risk for torsade de pointes; QT prolongation and torsade de pointes have been reported during post-marketing use, although causality to the drug has not been determined. These observations should be considered in clinical decisions to prescribe solifenacin for patients with a known history of QT prolongation, those with congenital QT prolongation syndrome), or for patients who are taking Class IA (e.g., quinidine, procainamide) or Class III (e.g., amiodarone, sotalol) antiarrhythmic medications.
 
Anticholinergics in general may exacerbate the clinical symptoms of patients with myasthenia gravis or autonomic neuropathy.
 
Solifenacin is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. There are no studies of solifenacin in pregnant women. Animal studies indicate that solifenacin crosses the placental barrier of pregnant mice. No toxicities were noted with near therapeutic doses, however fetal toxicity was noted with doses of 3.6 times and greater the maximum human dose. Solifenacin should be used during pregnancy only if the potential benefit to the mother outweighs the potential risk to the fetus.
 
The manufacturer recommends discontinuation of solifenacin during breast-feeding. It is not known whether solifenacin is excreted in human milk.
 
The safe and effective use of solifenacin in children has not been established.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. There is little evidence in the literature regarding cross-reactivity among the antimuscarinics for overactive bladder (OAB) if an allergic response has previously been noted, unless the medications share chemical similarities, like chemical structure or similar metabolites. Some publications recommend avoidance of other agents in the class if a serious allergic response has occurred.
    Headline
    bladder obstruction, prostatic hypertrophy, urinary retention
    General Information
    Solifenacin tablets are contraindicated in people with urinary retention; the oral suspension prescribing label precautions use in these patients. People with clinically significant bladder outflow obstruction (BOO) or incomplete bladder emptying, such as people with benign prostatic hypertrophy (BPH), may be at increased risk of urinary retention. Solifenacin is not recommended in patients with clinically significant bladder obstruction (BOO) in the absence of clean intermittent catheterization because of the risk of urinary retention. Monitor solifenacin recipients for symptoms of urinary retention and consider discontinuation of treatment if urinary retention occurs.
    Headline
    constipation, gastroparesis, GI obstruction
    General Information
    Solifenacin is contraindicated in people with gastric retention (e.g., gastroparesis). Solifenacin, like other anticholinergic drugs, may decrease gastrointestinal motility and should be used with caution in patients with conditions associated with decreased gastrointestinal motility, such as mechanical GI obstruction, paralytic ileus, or severe constipation.
    Headline
    bradycardia, cardiomyopathy, congenital long QT syndrome, coronary artery disease, females, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, QT prolongation
    General Information
    Use solifenacin with caution in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Patients receiving solifenacin should be advised to use caution when driving or operating machinery or completing activities requiring coordination and concentration until they know how solifenacin will affect them. Anticholinergic drugs, including solifenacin, can cause anticholinergic CNS effects, including blurred vision, dizziness, or somnolence. Patients should be monitored for these effects during treatment, particularly after initiation of treatment with solifenacin or dosage increases. If a patient experiences anticholinergic CNS effects, consider dosage reduction or discontinuation of the medication.
    Headline
    closed-angle glaucoma
    General Information
    Solifenacin is contraindicated in individuals with uncontrolled narrow-angle glaucoma (closed-angle glaucoma). Solifenacin may increase intraocular pressure and aqueous outflow resistance in individuals with controlled (treated) closed-angle glaucoma and caution is advised.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Hepatic impairment can increase solifenacin exposure and the risk of antimuscarinic adverse reactions. A dosage reduction is recommended in people with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B). Solifenacin is not recommended in patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Solifenacin plasma concentrations are increased in people with severe renal impairment (CrCl 30 mL/minute or less), which can lead to an increased risk of antimuscarinic adverse reactions. Dosage adjustments are recommended in people with severe renal impairment and renal failure. No adjustments are necessary in people with mild to moderate renal impairment.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    The Beers Criteria state that antimuscarinics with strong anticholinergic properties, such as solifenacin, are considered potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) in geriatric adults with dementia/cognitive impairment (adverse CNS effects) or delirium/high risk of delirium (new-onset or worsening delirium). In clinical trials, safety and efficacy profiles for solifenacin were similar in older and younger adults.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no studies with the use of solifenacin during pregnancy to inform a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriages, or adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. Use during pregnancy only if the potential maternal benefit outweighs the potential risk to the fetus. No adverse developmental outcomes were observed in animal reproduction studies with oral administration of solifenacin to pregnant mice during the period of organogenesis at a dose resulting in 1.2-times the systemic exposure at the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) of 10 mg/day. However, administration of doses 3.6-times and greater than the MRHD during organogenesis produced maternal toxicity and resulted in developmental toxicity and reduced fetal body weights in offspring.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Solifenacin is best avoided during breast-feeding due to a lack of safety data and the long half-life of the medication. Long-term use of solifenacin might reduce milk production. The breastfed infant may be susceptible to anticholinergic effects. Consider if an alternative would be preferred. There is no information on the presence of solifenacin in human milk, the effects on a breastfed child, or the effects on milk production. Solifenacin is present in mouse milk. When a drug is present in animal milk, it is likely that the drug will be present in human milk. The developmental and health benefits of breastfeeding should be considered along with the clinical need for solifenacin and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from solifenacin or from the underlying maternal condition.

Description
  Text
  Solifenacin is an oral competitive, selective muscarinic antagonist (M3) indicated to treat overactive bladder (OAB) and its symptoms in adults. It is also indicated for the treatment of neurogenic detrusor overactivity (NDO) in pediatric patients 2 years and older. In adults with OAB, solifenacin may be used as monotherapy or as combination therapy with mirabegron, a beta-3 adrenergic receptor agonist. Clinical trials indicated that adding mirabegron to solifenacin therapy may further reduce the number of urinary incontinence episodes versus solifenacin alone in adults with OAB. Solifenacin monotherapy improves bladder control and is comparable to tolterodine in safety and other efficacy parameters, such as reducing voided urine volume and urinary frequency. However, solifenacin monotherapy was found to significantly reduce urgency and the number of incontinence episodes when compared to both placebo and tolterodine. The efficacy of solifenacin was evaluated in two 52-week, open-label, baseline-controlled, sequential dosage titration studies in patients 2 years and older. An improvement in baseline maximum cystometric (bladder) capacity (MCC) was observed at 24 weeks of treatment in both studies. In study 1, patients 2 to 4 years (n = 17) had a mean increase in MCC of 39 mL (95% CI: 21 to 57 mL). In the second study, patients 5 to 17 years (n = 49), had a mean increase in MCC of 57 mL (95% CI: 26 to 88 mL). Reductions in spontaneous bladder contractions, bladder pressure, and number of incontinence episodes were also observed in both studies. Common side effects of solifenacin include dry mouth and constipation. Per guidelines for idiopathic OAB, either bladder-specific antimuscarinics or beta-3 adrenergic receptor agonists may be used initially as monotherapy or in combination with behavioral interventions depending on the patient's needs and treatment goals. When using a bladder-specific antimuscarinic, use of extended-release oral formulations is preferred to immediate-release products as these formulations may limit side effects such as dry mouth; transdermal therapy (i.e., oxybutynin gels or patches) may also be offered. Patients experiencing intolerance or side effects may respond well to dose reduction or selection of a different bladder-specific antimuscarinic agent. Clinicians may consider combination therapy with an antimuscarinic and beta-3 agonist for patients refractory to monotherapy with either therapy alone. Solifenacin was FDA approved in 2004.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Solifenacin is a competitive M3 selective muscarinic receptor antagonist, but has effects on all muscarinic receptors. In general, antimuscarinic drugs depress both voluntary and involuntary bladder contractions, and also control secretions in the salivary glands, gastric smooth muscle, ciliary muscle, and the CNS. The M2 and M3 muscarinic receptors are found in the human detrusor muscle. Even though there are a greater number of M2 receptors located in the detrusor muscle, it appears that M3 receptors are primarily responsible for normal micturition contraction. Muscarinic receptors that may also have a role in micturition are found on presynaptic nerve terminals (regulate transmitter release) and on bladder urothelium cells. Solifenacin has been clinically shown to inhibit bladder contraction, decrease detrusor pressure, and cause incomplete emptying of the bladder. Compared to tolterodine, solifenacin improves the volume of urine per void and reduces the frequency of incontinent and urgency episodes. Solifenacin has functional selectivity for the bladder compared to salivary muscarinic receptors, therefore, the antimuscarinic effects of solifenacin on the salivary gland are less pronounced compared to traditional anticholinergic agents.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Solifenacin is well absorbed (90% oral bioavailability), and there is no effect of food on the pharmacokinetics. Solifenacin reaches peak concentrations of 32.3 and 62.9 ng/mL for the 5 and 10 mg tablets, respectively, at steady-state. Peak concentrations are attained in approximately 3 to 8 hours after administration of the oral tablet.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    The pharmacokinetics of solifenacin are affected by hepatic impairment. There is a 2-fold increase in the half-life and 35% increase in exposure (AUC) of solifenacin in patients with moderate hepatic impairment.
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    There is a 2.1-fold increase in exposure (AUC) and a 1.6-fold increase in the half-life of solifenacin in patients with severe renal impairment.

Peak concentrations of solifenacin oral suspension at steady state were 2.5 to 29 ng/mL/mg and were attained within 2 to 6 hours after administration to pediatric patients aged 2 to 17 years with neurogenic detrusor overactivity (NDO).
    Name
    Geriatric
    Description
    Multiple dose studies of solifenacin in geriatric volunteers (65 to 80 years) showed that Cmax, AUC, and half-life values were 20% to 25% higher as compared to the younger adults (18 to 55 years). However, in placebo controlled trials safety and efficacy were similar between the 2 populations.
  Text
  Solifenacin is administered orally. Solifenacin is highly protein bound (98%) to human plasma proteins, principally to alpha1-acid glycoprotein. Solifenacin is also highly distributed to non-CNS tissues, having a mean steady-state volume of distribution (Vd) of 600 L in adults and 211 L in pediatric patients 2 to 17 years. Solifenacin is metabolized primarily in the liver by CYP3A4; however, alternate metabolic pathways exist. The primary metabolic routes of solifenacin are through N-oxidation of the quinuclidin ring and 4R-hydroxylation of tetrahydroisoquinoline ring. One pharmacologically active metabolite (4R-hydroxy solifenacin), occurring at low concentrations and unlikely to contribute significantly to clinical activity, and 3 pharmacologically inactive metabolites (N-glucuronide and the N-oxide and 4R-hydroxy-N-oxide of solifenacin) have been found in human plasma. After a 10 mg radiolabeled dose in healthy adult patients, 69.2% of the radioactivity was recovered in the urine and 22.5% in the feces over 26 days. Less than 15% of the dose is recovered unchanged in the urine. The elimination half-life of solifenacin at steady-state is approximately 45 to 68 hours in adults and 26 hours in pediatric patients 2 to 17 years.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP450) isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4
Solifenacin is metabolized primarily by CYP3A4. Drugs that potently inhibit CYP3A4 may increase concentrations. Drugs that induce CYP3A4 may alter the pharmacokinetic parameters. In vitro data show that solifenacin has no significant inhibitory effects on any CYP isoenzymes.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B): Do not exceed 5 mg PO once daily in adults. Do not exceed the recommended starting dose in pediatric patients.
Severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C): Use not recommended in adults or pediatric patients.
  Renal Impairment
  Adult patients:
CrCl 30 mL/minute or more: No dosage adjustment needed.
CrCl less than 30 mL/minute: Do not exceed 5 mg PO once daily.
 
Pediatric patients:
CrCl 30 mL/minute/1.73 m2 or more: No dosage adjustment needed.
CrCl less than 30 mL/minute/1.73 m2: Do not exceed the recommended starting dose.
