Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Because pramlintide slows the rate of gastric emptying, pramlintide is contraindicated in patients who have been diagnosed with gastroparesis.
 
Patients with moderate or severe renal impairment (CrCl 21—50 ml/min) did not show an increase in pramlintide exposure or reduced clearance when compared to subjects with normal renal function. The use of pramlintide has not been studied in patients with renal failure or dialysis.
 
Pramlintide will not cause hypoglycemia by itself. Pramlintide used in conjunction with insulin increases the risk of insulin-induced severe hypoglycemia, especially in type 1 diabetic patients. Severe hypoglycemia usually occurs within the first 3 hours after pramlintide injection. If severe hypoglycemia occurs while driving or operating machinery or while engaging in other high-risk activities, serious injuries may occur. Therefore, when introducing pramlintide therapy, appropriate precautions need to be taken to reduce the risk of severe insulin-induced hypoglycemia; the precautions include an increase in the frequency of blood glucose monitoring and a reduction in the meal time insulin dose (see Dosage). Furthermore, careful patient selection is critical to avoid hypoglycemia; patients with recurrent severe hypoglycemia that have required assistance during the past six months or have hypoglycemia unawareness should not receive pramlintide; hypoglycemia unawareness is a contraindication. In addition, patient and family education regarding hypoglycemia management is crucial; the patient and patient's family should be instructed on how to recognize and manage the symptoms of hypoglycemia. Early warning signs of hypoglycemia may be less obvious in patients with hypoglycemia unawareness which can be due to a long history of diabetes (where deficiencies in the release or response to counterregulatory hormones exist), with autonomic neuropathy, intensified diabetes control, or taking beta-blockers, guanethidine, or reserpine. Patients should be aware of the need to have a readily available source of glucose (dextrose, d-glucose) or other carbohydrate to treat hypoglycemic episodes. In severe hypoglycemia, intravenous dextrose or glucagon injections may be needed. Pramlintide has been studied in patients 65 years of age or older during clinical trials; glycosylated hemoglobin values and hypoglycemia frequencies were not different in elderly patients versus younger patients. In general, however, elderly patients are especially at risk for hypoglycemic episodes. The specific reasons identified include intensive insulin therapy, use of an excessive insulin dose, improper timing of insulin administration with regards to meals, injection of the wrong type of insulin, renal failure, severe liver disease, alcohol ingestion, defective counterregulatory hormone release, missing meals/fasting, and gastroparesis. Because hypoglycemic events may be difficult to recognize in some elderly patients, both pramlintide and insulin regimens should be carefully managed to obviate an increased risk of severe hypoglycemia. Severe or frequent hypoglycemia in any patient is an indication for the modification of treatment regimens, including setting higher glycemic goals.
 
Proper patient selection is critical to safe and effective use of pramlintide. Before initiation of therapy, the patient's glycosylated hemoglobin, recent blood glucose monitoring data, history of insulin-induced hypoglycemia, current insulin regimen, and body weight should be reviewed. Pramlintide should only be considered in patients that have failed to achieve adequate glycemic control despite individual insulin management and are receiving ongoing care under the guidance of a health care provider skilled in the use of insulin and supported by the services of diabetes educator(s). Pramlintide should not be used in patients who have poor compliance with their current insulin regimen, poor compliance with prescribed self monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG), or poorly controlled diabetes mellitus as evidenced by a glycosylated hemoglobin > 9%. In addition, pramlintide should be discontinued if recurrent unexplained hypoglycemia that requires medical assistance, persistent nausea, or noncompliance with SMBG, insulin dose adjustments, or recommended follow-up occurs during therapy.
 
Pramlintide is contraindicated for use in patients with pramlintide hypersensitivity or hypersensitivity to the excipients in the formulations. Minor, local sensitivity characterized by redness, swelling, or itching at the site of injection does not usually contraindicate therapy. Pramlintide contains metacresol and should be avoided in patients with cresol hypersensitivity; localized reactions and general myalgias have been reported with the use of cresol as an injectable excipient. Potential systemic allergic reactions were reported in 5% of pramlintide-treated patients and 4—5% of placebo-treated patients during clinical trials. No patients withdrew from clinical trials due to a potential systemic allergic reaction.
 
Diabetic patients must follow a regular, prescribed diet and exercise schedule to avoid either hypo- or hyperglycemia. Pramlintide is administered in combination with insulin; the timing of meals and exercise with pramlintide and insulin doses is extremely important, and should remain consistent, unless prescribed otherwise. Fever, thyroid disease, infection, recent trauma or surgery, diarrhea secondary to malabsorption, vomiting, and certain medications can also affect insulin and pramlintide requirements, requiring dosage adjustments. Diabetic patients should be given a 'sick-day' plan to take appropriate action with blood glucose monitoring and pramlintide and insulin therapy when acute illness is present.
 
Pramlintide is structurally related to the calcitonins and calcitonin gene-related peptide and it may interfere with bone osteoclastic and osteoblastic activity. However, pramlintide did not have an effect on bone metabolism or bone mineral density in type 1 diabetes patients treated with 30 mcg of pramlintide four times daily for 12 months. Until the effects of pramlintide on bone mineral density are fully understood, it may be prudent to use pramlintide cautiously in patients with osteopenia or osteoporosis.
 
Pramlintide is classified in FDA pregnancy risk category C. There are no adequate, well-controlled clinical studies of the use of pramlintide in pregnant women. Studies in perfused human placenta indicate that pramlintide has low potential to cross the maternal/fetal placental barrier (fetal: maternal ratio <= 0.006); however, these results may not be applicable to all gestational time periods. Increases in congenital abnormalities (neural tube defect, cleft palate, exencephaly) have been observed in fetuses of rats treated during organogenesis with 0.3 and 1 mg/kg/day (10 and 47 times the maximum recommended human dose, respectively). In rabbits, 0.3 mg/kg/day (9 times the maximum recommended human dose) had no adverse effect on embryofetal development. Because animal reproduction studies are not always predictive of human response, pramlintide should only be used in pregnant women if the benefit out weighs the risk.
 
It is unknown if pramlintide is excreted in breast milk. Because pramlintide has a short elimination half-life and a high molecular weight, if it is excreted in human milk, it will probably be in low concentrations. Furthermore, because pramlintide is a peptide, it will most likely be digested in the stomach of a nursing infant. Regardless, until more information is available, pramlintide should only be used in women who are breast-feeding their infants if it is determined that the benefit outweighs the potential risk to the infant.
 
The safety and efficacy of pramlintide in children have not been established.
 
Monitor blood glucose for needed dosage adjustments in diabetic patients whenever a change in either nicotine intake or tobacco smoking status occurs. Nicotine activates neuroendocrine pathways (e.g., increases in circulating cortisol and catecholamine levels) and may increase plasma glucose. Tobacco smoking is known to aggravate insulin resistance. The cessation of nicotine therapy or tobacco smoking may result in a decrease in blood glucose or an increase the subcutaneous absorption of insulin and/or pramlintide.

Interactions
Acarbose: (Moderate) Because pramlintide delays gastric emptying and nutritive absorption, the manufacturer recommends avoiding the use of pramlintide with other drugs that slow nutritive absorption (e.g., alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, such as miglitol and acarbose).
Acebutolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Besides low blood glucose concentrations, nausea/vomiting is the most commonly reported adverse event with an incidence of 28% in pramlintide-treated patients versus 12% of placebo-treated type 2 diabetes patients. The frequency appears to be higher in type 1 diabetes with 48% of pramlintide versus 17% of placebo-treated patients reporting nausea/vomiting. Nausea/vomiting is typically reported as mild to moderate, is dose-dependent, usually occurs at the beginning of treatment, and decreases in incidence over time. In addition, the incidence and severity of nausea/vomiting can be reduced with initial pramlintide dose titration (see Dosage). Additional gastrointestinal adverse events reported in clinical trials include abdominal pain and anorexia. Other adverse events reported with a frequency of >= 5% in pramlintide-treated patients and greater than that of placebo include arthralgia, coughing, dizziness, fatigue, headache, inflicted injury, and pharyngitis. Clinicians and patients should be aware that some of these adverse events (nausea, dizziness, fatigue, and headache) are also symptoms of low blood glucose; patients should be educated on the recognition and treatment of low blood glucose prior to starting pramlintide and insulin combination therapy.Hypoglycemia is the most frequently reported adverse reaction during clinical trials of pramlintide; the overall incidence of hypoglycemia does not differ significantly between patients treated with insulin plus placebo versus insulin plus pramlintide. Pramlintide by itself does not cause hypoglycemia; however, when pramlintide is used in combination with insulin, the risk of insulin-induced hypoglycemia is increased. While the risk of severe hypoglycemia appears to be higher in patients with type 1 diabetes, patients with type 2 diabetes can also experience severe hypoglycemia. Severe hypoglycemia with pramlintide therapy is most common during pramlintide initiation (i.e., the first 4 weeks of therapy) and in the first 3 hours following injection. The initial risk of severe hypoglycemia appears to diminish if the mealtime insulin dose is reduced when pramlintide is started (see Dosage). In addition, patients should increase the frequency of blood glucose monitoring when pramlintide is initiated to further reduce the risk of insulin-induced hypoglycemia. Signs and symptoms of hypoglycemia include hunger, pallor, nausea/vomiting, fatigue, diaphoresis, headache, palpitations, numbness of the mouth, tingling in the fingers, tremors, muscle weakness, blurred vision, hypothermia, uncontrolled yawning, irritability, mental confusion, sinus tachycardia, shallow breathing, and loss of consciousness (coma). Prolonged hypoglycemia can result in irreversible brain damage and even death. Patients experiencing symptoms of hypoglycemia should be instructed to ingest 20 grams glucose; if symptoms do not resolve, this can be repeated in 15—20 minutes. In addition, patients should be instructed to ingest a full snack or meal shortly thereafter. For patients who are unconscious or unwilling, parenteral glucagon can be used. In the case of severe hypoglycemia, intravenous dextrose is the treatment of choice.During pramlintide therapy, an injection site reaction can occur, manifested as lipohypertrophy (the accumulation of SC fat around a site of injection that has been used repeatedly) and lipoatrophy (the breakdown of adipose tissue at the injection site, causing a depression in the skin). These lipodystrophy reactions can be avoided by rotating the sites of injection so that a site is not used more than once every 1—2 months. In addition, patients may experience redness, swelling, or pruritus at the site of injection. These reactions typically resolve in a few days to a few weeks. Potential systemic reactions were reported in 5% of pramlintide-treated patients. The incidence in patients treated with placebo was similar (4—5%). No patients receiving pramlintide withdrew from a trial due to a potential systemic allergic reaction.

Patients should increase the frequency of blood glucose monitoring when pramlintide is initiated to further reduce the risk of insulin-induced hypoglycemia. Rapid reductions in blood glucose concentrations may induce symptoms of hypoglycemia regardless of glucose values. Signs and symptoms of hypoglycemia may include hunger, pallor, nausea/vomiting, fatigue, diaphoresis, headache, palpitations, numbness of the mouth, tingling in the fingers, tremors, muscle weakness, blurred vision, hypothermia, irritability, mental confusion, sinus tachycardia, shallow breathing, seizures, and loss of consciousness (coma). Prolonged hypoglycemia can result in irreversible brain damage and even death.

Description
  Text
  Pramlintide is a synthetic analog of amylin, a hormone co-secreted with insulin from pancreatic beta cells. Pramlintide is indicated as an adjunctive treatment in adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) who use mealtime insulin therapy and who have failed to achieve desired glucose control despite optimal insulin therapy. Pramlintide is synthesized from a chemical modification of amylin with proline substituted for 3 amino acid residues at positions 25, 28, and 29; amylin itself cannot be injected as it is too viscous, unstable in solution, and tends to aggregate. Amylin secretion is absent in patients with type 1 DM and decreased in patients with type 2 DM; decreased amylin concentrations contribute to persistent postprandial hyperglycemia and weight gain; accordingly, amylin replacement therapy with pramlintide complements insulin's effects in achieving glycemic control, especially in controlling postprandial hyperglycemia. Specifically, pramlintide slows gastric emptying, reduces postprandial glucagon secretion, and modulates appetite leading to decreased caloric intake. When used in combination with insulin, pramlintide further reduces A1C by up to 0.3% while not increasing the overall incidence of hypoglycemia. Unlike the weight gain that is experienced with insulin monotherapy, modest weight reductions of 0.5 to 2 kg occur during combination therapy with pramlintide. In the treatment of type 1 DM, insulin therapy may be initiated with a basal-bolus regimen of insulin and then further individualized to achieve treatment goals; the addition of pramlintide may be beneficial in these patients. In the treatment of type 2 DM, insulin is generally reserved for patients who continue to have an A1C above target despite dual/triple therapy with metformin and other antidiabetic agents. If A1C remains above target, the addition of pramlintide may be considered. Pramlintide was initially FDA approved in 2005.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Pramlintide is a synthetic analog of human amylin, a neuroendocrine hormone secreted by pancreatic beta cells. Amylin works in concert with insulin to regulate postprandial glucose concentrations; amylin is stored with insulin in secretory granules and secreted with insulin; however, amylin concentrations are lower than insulin in human plasma (1:20 molar ratio). Amylin affects glucose concentrations by 3 different mechanisms: (1) slowing of gastric emptying without altering the overall absorption of nutrients, (2) suppression of postprandial glucagon secretion, and (3) centrally-mediated modulation of appetite leading to decreased caloric intake.
 
Pancreatic beta cells are dysfunctional or damaged in patients with diabetes mellitus (DM); similar to insulin, amylin secretion is absent in patients with type 1 DM and decreased in patients with type 2 DM; the decreased amylin concentrations contribute to the inadequacies of insulin therapy in patients such as persistent postprandial hyperglycemia and weight gain. When administered in combination with insulin to patients with DM, pramlintide slows gastric emptying, reduces postprandial rise in glucagon concentrations, and causes satiety leading to decreased caloric intake and potential weight loss. Pramlintide's effects on reduced caloric intake appear to be independent of the nausea that can accompany pramlintide therapy. Clinically, these effects translate to a reduction in hemoglobin A1C and weight loss without an increase in hypoglycemia; these effects are sustained for 12 months or longer.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Subcutaneous Route
    Description
    The absolute bioavailability of pramlintide is 30% to 40% when given subcutaneously. When injected subcutaneously into the abdominal area or thigh of healthy subjects, Cmax and AUC increase in proportion to the dose; however, when injected into the arm, the pramlintide Cmax and AUC increases are not dose-proportional and are variable. The time to maximum concentration is approximately 20 minutes.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    Patients with moderate or severe renal impairment (CrCl 21 to 50 mL/minute) did not show an increase in pramlintide exposure or reduced clearance when compared to subjects with normal renal function. The use of pramlintide has not been studied in patients with renal failure or in patients receiving dialysis.
  Text
  Pramlintide is administered by subcutaneous injection. Pramlintide is not extensively bound to blood cells or albumin; approximately 40% of the drug is bound in plasma. The half-life of pramlintide is approximately 48 minutes with the therapeutic effects lasting approximately 3 hours. Pramlintide is extensively metabolized by the kidney; the primary metabolite, des-lys pramlintide has a similar half-life to the parent compound and is biologically active. AUC concentrations of pramlintide are constant with repeated dosing indicating no bioaccumulation.

Subcutaneous injection:
Administer immediately prior to each major meal (250 kcal or more, or 30 grams or more of carbohydrate).
Inject into the abdomen or the anterior and lateral aspects of the thigh; do not inject into the arm due to variable absorption.
Pramlintide is indicated for use in combination with insulin, however, per the manufacturer, they should be administered as separate injections. The site of injection should be distinct from the site of the insulin injection (i.e., at least 2 inches apart).
Double-check dosage prior to administration.
Rotate administration sites with each injection to prevent lipodystrophy.
 
Preparation and Administration Instructions for Patients:
To prepare a dose using the pen-injector: If stored in the refrigerator, allow it to warm to room temperature before injecting. If using the pen-injector for the first time, it should be primed before using. If the pen is dropped at any time, repeat the priming process to ensure that it works. Prior to injecting each dose, make sure that you are using the correct pen strength. Attach a needle to the pen-injector device. Turn the dial until the correct dose appears in the window. Pull out the knob as far as it will go; you should hear a clicking noise.
To inject a dose: Select an injection site on the stomach or thigh, and clean with an alcohol wipe. Make sure the injection site for pramlintide is at least 2 inches away from the site for your insulin injection. Pinch the skin up with your fingers about 3 inches apart, and insert the needle at an angle of 45 to 90 degrees. Release the skin and press the knob for pen-injectors all the way down to deliver the dose. When using the pen-injector, keep the needle in the skin for 10 seconds so that all of the pramlintide is injected. Remove the needle from the skin and press gently on the injection site for a moment (but do not rub or massage). Rotate your injection site so that each site is not used more than once every 1 to 2 months. Do not use injection sites that are thickened, red, or bumpy. Never inject pramlintide into a vein.
Remove the needle after each injection to help prevent leakage, keep out air bubbles, reduce needle clogs, and reduce the risk of infection.
Discard the used needles in an appropriate sharps container after injection. Do not share or re-use needles.
