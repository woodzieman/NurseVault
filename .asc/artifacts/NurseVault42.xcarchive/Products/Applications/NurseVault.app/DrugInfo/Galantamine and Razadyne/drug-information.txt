Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Galantamine is contraindicated in patients who have demonstrated hypersensitivity to the drug or its ingredients.
 
The abrupt withdrawal of galantamine in patients who had been receiving doses in the effective range is not associated with an increased frequency of adverse events. The beneficial effects of galantamine are lost, however, when the drug is discontinued. If therapy has been interrupted for several days or longer, the dose should be re-titrated (see Dosage).
 
The AUC and half-life of galantamine are increased by about 30% in those with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh Class B). The effects on metabolism in those with severe hepatic impairment (i.e., Child-Pugh Class C) are unknown. Therefore, the use of galantamine is not recommended in patients with severe hepatic disease. The manufacturer considers the drug contraindicated for use in the presence of severe hepatic dysfunction or jaundice. The drug should be used cautiously and with careful dosage titration in those with moderate hepatic impairment.
 
Because the effects of galantamine in those with severe renal impairment have not been studied, the drug is not recommended in those with a creatinine clearance less than 9 ml/min or those with renal failure. Galantamine should be used cautiously and with careful dosage titration in those with moderate renal impairment. Severe dehydration (in association with renal failure or renal impairment) as well as hypokalemia have been reported rarely in post-marketing data. Patients and caregivers should be advised to ensure adequate fluid intake during treatment.
 
Treatment should be initiated and supervised by a physician experienced in the diagnosis and treatment of Alzheimer's disease and the elderly. Diagnosis should be made according to current guidelines. Therapy with galantamine should only be started if a caregiver is available who will regularly monitor drug intake by the patient. The use of galantamine has not been investigated in patients with severe Alzheimer's disease, other types of dementia, or in patients with other types of memory impairment (e.g., age related cognitive decline).
 
Although patients with Alzheimer's disease and other dementias commonly lose weight, cholinesterase inhibitors including galantamine have been associated with anorexia and weight loss in these patients. During galantamine therapy, weight must be monitored periodically.
 
Dizziness and somnolence may occur during treatment with galantamine. Therefore, patients should be cautioned about engaging in tasks requiring mental alertness such as driving or operating machinery until they know how the drug will affect their cognition.
 
Because galantamine potentiates the actions of acetylcholine, an increase in gastric acid secretion should be anticipated. Gastrointestinal disorders such as nausea and vomiting may occur, particularly when initiating treatment and/or increasing the galantamine dose. These adverse events occur more commonly in females, and may require dose reduction or temporary discontinuation. Care should be exercised in treating patients with active peptic ulcer disease or patients predisposed to these conditions. Patients with a history of peptic ulcer disease or those receiving NSAIDs concurrently should be monitored closely for symptoms of active or occult GI disease. Other GI symptoms, such as diarrhea, can be increased by the use of cholinesterase inhibitors. Cholinergic effects may also exacerbate conditions involving GI obstruction or ileus. Discontinue use in cases of active GI bleeding.
 
Galantamine should be used with caution in patients with asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), or other obstructive-type pulmonary disease. Potentiation of the effects of acetylcholine by cholinomimetics, such as galantamine, may result in increased bronchoconstriction and bronchial secretion. Respiratory signs and symptoms should be monitored in patients with pulmonary disease as safety has not been demonstrated.
 
Use galantamine with caution in patients with cardiac disease, such as sick sinus syndrome, severe cardiac arrhythmias, or cardiac conduction disturbances (e.g., sino-atrial block, AV block). However, post-marketing surveillance suggests that all patients should be considered at risk for adverse cardiac effects due to cholinesterase inhibitors, since bradycardia and heart block have occurred in patients without previously diagnosed cardiac conduction abnormalities. Because of their pharmacological action, cholinesterase inhibitors have vagotonic effects on the sino-atrial and atrioventricular nodes, leading to bradycardia and AV block which may exacerbate hypotension or syncope. The vagotonic effects of galantamine may be increased when given with other medications known to cause bradycardia such as digoxin or beta blockers. In clinical trials, however, bradycardia was rarely reported as severe. The overall frequency of bradycardia was 2—3% for galantamine doses up to 24 mg/day compared with <1% for placebo. Deaths have occurred during randomized controlled trials evaluating galantamine in mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Individuals with mild cognitive impairment demonstrate isolated memory impairment greater than expected for their age and education, but do not meet current diagnostic criteria for Alzheimer's disease. In two randomized, placebo-controlled trials of 2 years duration in subjects with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), a total of 13 subjects on galantamine (n=1026) and 1 subject on placebo (n=1022) died. The deaths were due to various causes which could be expected in an elderly population; about half of the galantamine deaths appeared to result from various vascular causes (myocardial infarction, stroke, and sudden death). Although statistically this mortality difference was significant, causality is difficult to assign as pooled studies evaluating Alzheimer's and other dementia patients (as opposed to MCI patients) have noted higher death rates in placebo vs. galantamine groups.
 
Cholinomimetics may induce or exacerbate urinary tract obstruction/bladder obstruction. Caution is recommended when administering galantamine to patients predisposed to these disorders.
 
Although seizure activity is a possible manifestation of Alzheimer's disease, cholinomimetics may also induce or exacerbate seizures in this patient population. In general, caution is recommended when administering galantamine to patients predisposed to seizure disorder (e.g., head trauma, increased intracranial pressure, or unstable metabolic conditions). In clinical trials, there was no increase in the incidence of convulsions with galantamine compared to placebo.
 
Increased cholinergic effects of galantamine in the CNS may exacerbate symptoms of Parkinson's disease.
 
Galantamine is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category B. It is not known whether galantamine causes harm to the human fetus or influences reproductive capacity in pregnant females. In animal studies, a slight delay in fetal development and skeletal variations have been observed at doses 3—6 times the recommended human dose. Galantamine should be used during pregnancy only if the potential maternal benefit justifies the potential fetal risk.
 
Safety in breast-feeding has not been established. It is unknown if galantamine is excreted in breast milk; therefore, galantamine should be avoided during breast-feeding.
 
There is no accepted use for galantamine in children.
 
Galantamine is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor and therefore is likely to exaggerate muscle relaxation under anesthesia. If used during surgery, extended respiratory depression could result from prolonged neuromuscular blockade. In addition, the use of galantamine is not recommended in those recovering from gastrointestinal surgery due to various effects on the GI tract resulting from the cholinergic action of the drug.

    Hypersensitivity

    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Galantamine is also contraindicated in patients who have demonstrated hypersensitivity to benzgalantamine.
    Headline
    AV block, sinus node dysfunction
    General Information
    Cholinesterase inhibitors, including galantamine, may have vagotonic effects on the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes, leading to bradycardia or heart block. Bradycardia and all types of heart block have been reported in people taking cholinesterase inhibitors, both with and without known underlying sinus node dysfunction or other supraventricular cardiac conduction abnormalities (e.g., AV block, sinoatrial block).
    Headline
    peptic ulcer disease
    General Information
    Use galantamine with caution in people with a history or risk factors for developing gastrointestinal ulcers (e.g., those with a history of peptic ulcer disease or those receiving concurrent NSAIDs). Cholinesterase inhibitors, such as galantamine, are expected to increase gastric acid secretion due to increased cholinergic activity. Galantamine recipients should be closely monitored for symptoms of active or occult GI bleeding.
    Headline
    bladder obstruction
    General Information
    Cholinomimetics may cause bladder obstruction (bladder outflow obstruction). Although not observed in clinical trials with galantamine, caution is advised when administering galantamine to patients predisposed to these disorders.
    Headline
    asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
    General Information
    Due to cholinomimetic actions, galantamine should be used with caution in people with a history of severe asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).
    Headline
    seizures
    General Information
    Use galantamine with caution in people with a history of seizures or a seizure disorder. Cholinomimetics, such as galantamine, are believed to have the potential to cause generalized seizures. Seizure activity may also be a manifestation of Alzheimer disease; monitor these patients for seizures during galantamine treatment.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Dosage adjustment is recommended for people with renal impairment with a CrCl of 9 to 59 mL/minute. Galantamine is not recommended for use in people with renal failure (CrCl less than 9 mL/minute) due to the increased exposure to galantamine and risk of adverse effects.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Galantamine is not recommended for use in severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure due to reduced clearance and increased exposure to the drug. Dosage adjustment is recommended for people with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B) due to a reduction in clearance of galantamine that was observed in clinical studies. No adjustment is necessary in mild impairment (Child-Pugh class A).
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    According to the Beers Criteria, acetylcholinesterase inhibitors such as galantamine may cause bradycardia and are considered potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) in geriatric adults with an underlying history of syncope and should be avoided in those whose syncope may be due to bradycardia. During 2 randomized controlled trials evaluating galantamine in mild cognitive impairment (MCI), there was a statistically higher mortality rate in the geriatric adults receiving galantamine versus placebo. The deaths were due to various causes which could be expected in an older population; about half of the galantamine deaths appeared to result from various vascular causes (myocardial infarction, stroke, and sudden death). Although the difference in mortality between galantamine- and placebo-treated groups in these MCI studies was significant, the results are highly discrepant with other studies of galantamine. Pooled studies evaluating Alzheimer and other dementia patients have noted higher death rates in placebo versus galantamine groups.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate data on the developmental risk associated with the use of galantamine during human pregnancy. In animal reproductive studies, administration of galantamine resulted in developmental toxicity (increased incidence of morphological abnormalities and decreased growth in offspring) at doses similar to or greater than those used clinically. The no-effect dose for embryo-fetal developmental toxicity, as well as for pre- and postnatal developmental toxicity, in rats (2 mg/kg/day) is approximately equal to the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) of 24 mg/day on a body surface area (mg/m2) basis. The no-effect dose for embryo-fetal developmental toxicity in rabbits (28 mg/kg/day) is approximately 20-times the MRHD on a mg/m2 basis.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use galantamine with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of galantamine in human milk, the effects on the breastfed infant, or the effects on milk production. The developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding should be considered along with the clinical need for galantamine and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed infant from galantamine or from the underlying maternal condition.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Many dose-limiting side effects are related to the cholinergic effects of galantamine. Such side effects are more likely to develop during galantamine dosage titration and usually resolve with continued treatment. To reduce the incidence of intolerable side effects, administration of galantamine should always occur with food and plenty of fluid. Also, following the recommended slow titration schedule while advancing the dosage should help limit common adverse reactions. Gastrointestinal symptoms are among the most frequently reported adverse reactions of galantamine and include abdominal pain (5%), diarrhea (up to 12%), dyspepsia (5%), and nausea/vomiting (13%/6%). Results from clinical trials indicate that females are more likely than males to experience nausea/vomiting. The majority of these adverse events occurred during the dose-escalation period. In those patients who experienced the most frequent adverse event, nausea, the median duration of the nausea was 5—7 days. Anti-emetics may be helpful during this time. Adverse effects may respond to the holding of a few doses or dosage reduction to the previously tolerated dosage level. Those who have interrupted galantamine therapy for longer than several days should have their treatment reinitiated with the lowest daily dose and titrated slowly back to their tolerated maintenance dose.Although patients with Alzheimer's disease and other dementias commonly lose weight, cholinesterase inhibitors have been associated with anorexia and weight loss in these patients. Anorexia occurred in 7—9% and weight loss occurred in roughly 5% of patients receiving galantamine during clinical trials, a frequency twice that reported with placebo. Females were found to be more susceptible to anorexia than males.Commonly reported central nervous system effects occurring with galantamine include dizziness (9%), headache (8%) and tremor (3%). Psychiatric events included depression (7%), fatigue (5%), insomnia (5%) and somnolence (4%, also noted as drowsiness or sedation). Description
  Text
  Galantamine is a reversible cholinesterase inhibitor (ChEI) approved for the symptomatic management of mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease. The drug is a natural alkaloid originating from the bulbs of the common snowdrop flower, also known as the daffodil or Galanthus nivalis. Small improvements in cognition and activities of daily living may be observed during treatment with ChEIs; however, the drugs do not prevent or delay progression of the disease. According to an effectiveness review from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), the evidence suggests that galantamine is associated with improvements in cognition in patients with mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease, but there is an increased risk for withdrawals due to serious adverse events. The evidence is insufficient to determine the effects of galantamine on individual cognitive domains, function, quality of life, and clinical impression of change in mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease as well as all efficacy and harm outcomes in moderate to severe Alzheimer's disease. The evidence is insufficient to evaluate the efficacy and harms of galantamine in treating behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD). There are no drugs with established efficacy in vascular dementia, although patients with mixed vascular dementia and Alzheimer's disease may benefit from treatment with a ChEI. Most treatment guidelines support a trial of a ChEI for improving cognition in dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB); however, the data evaluating the benefits of galantamine in DLB are inconsistent and further study is needed to determine the efficacy and harms of galantamine in DLB patients. Studies of ChEIs for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or frontotemporal dementia (FTD) have failed to show benefit in these populations, and ChEIs may cause agitation in patients with FTD. Similar to other cholinesterase inhibitors, galantamine requires a slow dosage titration to limit GI side effects. Galantamine was initially FDA approved in 2001.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Alzheimer's disease may cause cognitive problems through a complex process involving many factors. One key characteristic of Alzheimer's disease is decreasing cognition, which is believed to be related to the degeneration of acetylcholine-producing neurons in the brain. Studies have found that the degree of cholinergic loss can be correlated to the degree of cognitive impairment and density of amyloid plaques, which are a neuropathological hallmark of Alzheimer's disease. Galantamine is a tertiary alkaloid that is a competitive and reversible inhibitor of acetylcholinesterase (AChE). This action is believed to help maintain acetylcholine levels, potentially reducing the decline in memory and daily function by compensating for the loss of these neurons. However, this mechanism requires that intact cholinergic neurons be present. As Alzheimer's disease progresses, fewer intact cholinergic neurons remain, and cholinesterase inhibitors become less effective. Moreover, these medications have not been shown to impact the actual progression of the disease or brain changes. Brain abnormalities seen in Alzheimer's include damage to the neurons that connect the forebrain to the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, areas crucial for memory. Galantamine is unique among similar AChe inhibitor drugs because it also acts on nicotinic receptors, which may be due to its attachment to a specific part of the receptor. However, there's no proof that this additional action provides any greater clinical benefit over other drugs in its class.

    Description
    Immediate-release galantamine is rapidly and completely absorbed following oral administration with time to peak concentration about 1 hour. Bioavailability of the immediate-release tablet is the same as an oral solution; bioavailability is roughly 90%.   Text
  Galantamine is administered orally. The mean volume of distribution of galantamine is 175 L. The plasma protein binding is 18% at therapeutically relevant concentrations. In whole blood, it is mainly distributed to blood cells (roughly 53%). Multiple metabolic pathways and renal excretion are involved in elimination, so no single pathway appears predominant. Hepatic isoenzymes CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 are involved in the formation of the metabolites O-desmethylgalantamine and N-oxide-galantamine, respectively. The elimination half-life averages 7 hours. Approximately 20% of a dose is excreted in the urine as unchanged galantamine after 24 hours. In studies of radiolabeled oral galantamine, unchanged galantamine and its glucuronide accounted for most of the plasma radioactivity. By 7 days, 93% to 99% of the total radioactivity was recovered, with about 95% in urine and about 5% in the feces.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2D6, CYP3A4
Galantamine is partially metabolized by isoenzymes CYP3A4 and CYP2D6. Strong inhibitors or inducers of CYP3A4 and/or CYP2D6 may alter serum concentrations of galantamine. Galantamine does not appear to be a significant inhibitor and CYP450 enzymes.
