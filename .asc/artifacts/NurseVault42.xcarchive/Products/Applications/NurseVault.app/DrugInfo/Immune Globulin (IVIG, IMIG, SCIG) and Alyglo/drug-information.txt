Drug Class: Immune globulin products are human plasma-derived protein solutions composed primarily of immunoglobulin G (IgG), with trace amounts of IgA and IgM depending on the formulation.

Routes: intravenous, subcutaneous, intramuscular; Common adult doses: Dosage Description

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): hypoxia, pulmonary edema, transfusion-related acute lung injury, angina, bradycardia, cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction, pallor, paresis, pulmonary embolism, stroke, thromboembolism, thrombosis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ards), apnea, bronchospasm, cough, cyanosis, dyspnea, epistaxis, pharyngitis, rhinitis, sinusitis, wheezing, enterocolitis, asthenia, chest pain (unspecified), chills, edema, fatigue

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Immune globulin IV (IGIV) is only for intravenous administration. Other routes of administration such as intramuscular administration have not been evaluated.

Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Approved indications, populations, and dosing recommendations vary among immune globulin products. Refer to individual product labeling for product-specific details.
Immune globulin products are not pharmaceutically equivalent but are considered to have comparable efficacy. For individuals receiving chronic immune globulin therapy, consider product changes carefully; begin with conservative infusion times and increase monitoring if products are substituted.
In general, immune globulin should be dosed based on actual body weight in individuals weighing up to 100 kg with a body mass index (BMI) less than 30 kg/m2. Some experts recommend using adjusted body weight for dosing immune globulin if the individual exceeds these parameters or is greater than 20% over their ideal body weight.

        Heading
        Subcutaneous dosage (General conversion dosing, all products)
        Route Age Groups
          Heading
          Adults
          Dosage Description
          Initiate subcutaneous dosing based on the previously established immune globulin regimen. When transitioning from IVIG, initiate subcutaneous therapy approximately 1 week after the last IV infusion and administer at intervals ranging from daily to biweekly per product labeling. Convert the IVIG dose to a weekly equivalent using the product-specific conversion factor (e.g., multiply the prior IVIG dose in grams by 1.3 (Cutaquig, Cuvitru) or 1.37 (all other products) and divide by the number of weeks between IV doses). For more frequent dosing, divide the weekly dose by the number of administrations per week; for biweekly dosing, multiply the weekly dose by 2. When transitioning from another subcutaneous product, maintain the same total weekly dose (1:1 weekly equivalent) unless otherwise specified in the product labeling. Obtain a baseline IgG trough concentration prior to conversion and recheck approximately 2 to 3 months after the switch and periodically thereafter. Adjust dose and interval primarily based on clinical response, using the difference between measured and target IgG trough concentrations and the product-specific adjustment table according to body weight.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Immune globulin IV (IGIV) is only for intravenous administration. Other routes of administration such as intramuscular administration have not been evaluated. IGIV should be used cautiously in patients with a history of human immunoglobulin hypersensitivity. Carimune NF, Gammagard Liquid, Gammar-P I.V., Privigen, and Gamunex are contraindicated in patients who have had a severe systemic reaction to IVIG or IMIG whereas Octagam is contraindicated in patients with intolerance to homologous immunoglobulins. Use caution in patients with maltose hypersensitivity, fructose or sorbitol intolerance, or sucrose hypersensitivity because IGIV products can contain these sugars. Gammar-P I.V. is contraindicated in patients with albumin hypersensitivity, and Flebogamma 5% should not be administered to individuals with a history of severe or anaphylactic reactions to blood or blood-derived products.
 
Privigen is contraindicated in patients with hyperprolinemia; the product contains the stabilizer L-proline.
 
Patients with agammaglobulinemia or severe hypogammaglobulinemia are at an increased risk of developing immune-mediated adverse reactions to the initial infusion if they have not received immune globulin IV (IGIV) in the preceding 8 weeks or if they have never received immunoglobulins. Immune globulin IV should, in general, be avoided in patients with IgA deficiency. These patients often develop antibodies against IgA and are more likely to have anaphylactic or immune-mediated adverse reactions to pooled immunoglobulin products such as IGIV. Anaphylaxis can occur even if a product contains low amounts of IgA. The use of Carimune NF and Flebogamma 5% are contraindicated in patients with selective IgA deficiency and antibodies to IgA whereas Polygam S/D and Gammagard S/D are contraindicated in patients with selective IgA deficiency where the IgA deficiency is the only abnormality of concern. Gammar-P I.V. and Privigen are contraindicated in patients with isolated IgA deficiency because these patients have the potential for developing antibodies to IgA and could have anaphylactic reactions to subsequent administration of blood products that contain IgA. Octagam is contraindicated in patients with IgA deficiency when the patient has IgE mediated antibodies. Gamunex should only be given with utmost cautionary measures to patients with known antibodies against IgA and severe, selective IgA deficiencies because of the risk of severe immediate hypersensitivity reactions including anaphylaxis. For Gammagard Liquid, patients with severe selective IgA deficiency (IgA < 0.05 g/L) should be treated only if their IgA deficiency is associated with an immune deficiency for which therapy with intravenous immune globulin is clearly indicated because they may develop anti-IgA antibodies that can result in a severe anaphylactic reaction. Such patients should only receive intravenous immune globulin with utmost caution and in a setting where supportive care is available for treating life-threatening reactions.
 
The safety and efficacy of IGIV have not been evaluated in neonates or infants. Ninety-four pediatric subjects, including 15 who were diagnosed with primary humoral immunodeficiency, have received Flebogamma 5% in the course of clinical studies or post-marketing studies over a 14-year period. Although preliminary safety data in children and adolescents with primary humoral immune deficiency who received IGIV products have not revealed differences between the safety profiles of the product(s) in pediatric and adult patients, the experience has been too limited to consider the safety and efficacy to be established in children.
 
Immune globulin IV (IGIV) is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. No complications to the fetus have been reported, but IGIV has not been well studied in pregnant women. IGIV should be given to a pregnant woman only if clearly needed.
 
Immune globulin IV (IGIV) should be used with caution in women who are breast-feeding because it is not known if IGIV is excreted in breast milk.
 
In isolated cases and mostly with the use of high doses, administration of immune globulin IV (IGIV) has been associated with aseptic meningitis, regardless of lot or commercial source. Patients with a history of migraine developed aseptic meningitis at a higher rate.
 
Immune globulin IV (IGIV) is a derivative of human blood. As with other products derived from or purified with human blood components, the remote possibility of contamination with hepatitis, Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD), and other bacterial or viral infections exists in patients receiving the IGIV. Screening plasma donors for prior exposure to certain viruses, testing for the presence of viruses, and inactivating and/or reducing viruses has reduced the risk of transmission of infectious agents. The manufacturing processes are designed to reduce the risk of transmitting viral infection; however, none of the processes are completely effective. There is also the possibility that unknown infectious agents may be present in this product. Some viruses, such as parvovirus B19, are particularly difficult to remove or inactivate. Parvovirus B19 most seriously affects pregnant women and immune compromised individuals and symptoms include fever, drowsiness, chills, and rhinitis followed in about 2 weeks with rash and joint pain. Patients should be encouraged to call their health care provider if they develop these symptoms. All infections thought by a physician to have been possibly transmitted by IGIV should be reported to the manufacturer. The health care provider should discuss the risks and benefits of this product with the patient.
 
There is clinical evidence of a possible association between immune globulin IV (IGIV) administration and thrombotic events. The exact cause of this is unknown; therefore, caution should be used in the prescribing and infusion of IGIV in patients with a history of cardiac disease or thromboembolic disease. Elderly patients and patients with coronary artery disease, heart failure, or known or suspected hyperviscosity may be at risk for thrombotic events. Assessment of blood viscosity may be warranted for patients at risk for hyperviscosity such as those with cryoglobulins, fasting chylomicronemia, hypertriglyceridemia, or monoclonal gammopathies. The FDA states that rapid infusion rates and high doses of IGIV are potential risk factors for this complication in patients who are already at risk for thrombotic events. In one study, 89 adverse events were associated with 341 rapid infusions in 50 patients with neuromuscular disorders, 3.5% of which were considered major or a major event in 11 out of 50 patients (22%). These major events (see Adverse Reactions) were related to the rapid infusion protocol (up to 800 ml/hour) in a potentially 'at-risk' population. It is strongly recommended that in patients at risk for thromboembolic events that the infusion concentration be no more than 5% and the infusion rate should be initiated no faster then 0.5 ml/kg/hour and advanced slowly only if well tolerated to a maximum of 4 ml/kg/hour.
 
Immune globulin IV (IGIV) products have been associated with acute renal failure. Approximately 55% of the reported cases of renal dysfunction involved patients treated for idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) and fewer than 5% of the cases involved patients with primary immune deficiency. Preliminary evidence indicates that IGIV products containing sucrose may present a greater risk for this complication than non-sucrose containing IGIV. It is not known whether age or baseline glomerular filtration rate differences could be factors explaining the greater proportion of reports of renal dysfunction after administration of IGIV for ITP. To reduce the risk of acute renal failure, caution should be undertaken in patients at increased risk (e.g., patients with any degree of renal impairment or renal disease, diabetes mellitus, elderly, dehydration or hypovolemia, sepsis, paraproteinemia, or concomitant nephrotoxic drug therapy) and periodic monitoring of renal function tests and urinary output are particularly important. If renal function deteriorates, discontinuation of the product should be considered. Physicians should carefully weigh the potential benefits of sucrose-containing IGIV administration against the potential risks of renal damage. Prior to the initiation of the infusion of IGIV, patients should be adequately hydrated. Recommended doses should not be exceeded. Usage of the minimum available concentration and administration at the minimum practicable rate of infusion is recommended, especially for patients at risk for acute renal failure.
 
The immune globulins present in immune globulin IV (IGIV) products may interfere with live virus vaccination, such as measles immunization. Vaccinations should be delayed until 3 months after IGIV administration and it may be necessary to revaccinate persons who receive IGIV shortly after live virus vaccination. If necessary due to imminent exposure to disease, the measles, mumps, and rubella virus vaccine live can be administered simultaneously with immune globulin preparations, however the vaccine-induced immunity may be reduced. Tetanus toxoid may be given at the same time as immune globulin, but at a different injection site.
 
Blood glucose testing systems based on the glucose dehydrogenase pyrroloquinolinequinone or on the glucose-dye-oxidoreductase methods falsely interpret the maltose contained in Octagam as glucose. Falsely elevated glucose readings (false hyperglycemia) led to life-threatening hypoglycemia because of inappropriate administration of insulin. Falsely elevated glucose readings could also mask true cases of hypoglycemia. Measurement of blood glucose must be done with a glucose-specific method if a patient takes a parenteral product that contains maltose. Read the product information of the blood glucose testing system including the information about the test strips to determine if the system is appropriate for use with maltose-containing parenteral products. If any uncertainty exists, contact the manufacturer of the testing system.

    General Information
    Thrombosis may occur after treatment with immune globulin products. Risk factors for thromboembolism may include advanced age (geriatric adults), prolonged immobility, hypercoagulable conditions (thrombophilia), history of venous or arterial thrombosis, use of estrogens, indwelling central venous catheter, hyperviscosity, and cardiovascular disease or risk factors. Thrombosis may also occur without known risk factors. In individuals at risk for thrombosis, ensure adequate hydration prior to immune globulin administration and monitor for signs and symptoms of thrombosis. For intravenous or subcutaneous administration, use the lowest practical dose and infusion rate. Consider a baseline blood viscosity assessment in patients at risk for hyperviscosity, such as those with cryoglobulinemia, fasting chylomicronemia or hypertriglyceridemia, or monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (paraproteinemia).
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, hypovolemia, obesity, renal failure, renal impairment, sepsis
    General Information
    For intravenous and subcutaneous administration, administer immune globulin at the lowest practical infusion rate in individuals at increased risk of renal injury, including those with renal impairment or renal failure, diabetes mellitus, age older than 65 years, hypovolemia, sepsis, paraproteinemia, obesity, or those receiving nephrotoxic medications. Ensure adequate hydration prior to immune globulin administration. Acute renal failure, acute tubular necrosis, proximal tubular nephropathy, osmotic nephrosis, and death have been reported with immune globulin use. Assess renal function, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine, and urine output, prior to initiation of therapy and periodically during treatment. If renal function deteriorates, consider discontinuing immune globulin.
    Headline
    blood group phenotype A, blood group phenotype AB, blood group phenotype B, inflammation
    General Information
    Monitor individuals receiving intravenous or subcutaneous immune globulin for clinical signs and symptoms of hemolysis, particularly those at increased risk, such as individuals with non-O blood types (blood group phenotype A, blood group phenotype B, blood group phenotype AB) or those receiving high doses (2 g/kg or more). Although the contribution of underlying inflammation to hemolysis risk is not fully understood, it may be a contributing factor. Consider appropriate laboratory testing in higher risk individuals, including measurement of hemoglobin or hematocrit prior to infusion and within approximately 36 to 96 hours post infusion. If signs and/or symptoms of hemolysis are present after immune globulin infusion, perform appropriate confirmatory laboratory testing. Immune globulin products may contain blood group antibodies that act as hemolysins and induce in vivo coating of red blood cells (RBCs) with immunoglobulin, causing a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT, Coombs' test). Delayed hemolytic anemia secondary to immune globulin can develop due to enhanced red blood cell sequestration, and acute hemolysis consistent with intravascular hemolysis has been reported.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no available data on immune globulin use during pregnancy to inform a drug associated risk. It is not known if immune globulin can cause fetal harm. Immune globulins cross the placenta increasingly as pregnancy progresses beyond 30 weeks of gestation.  When given to pregnant individuals with idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura prior to delivery, platelet response and clinical effect were similar in the individual and neonate. According to the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), fetal adverse events have not occurred after administration of immune globulin preparations during pregnancy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use immune globulin with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of immune globulin in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production.  Endogenous immune globulin is a normal component in breast milk. Case reports of 2 breast-feeding individuals receiving intravenous immune globulin therapy suggest transfer of IgG and IgM into the colostrum and milk. There is emerging consensus that immune globulin may be the preferred treatment for postpartum individuals with multiple sclerosis who are breast-feeding, although 1 retrospective study failed to find a decrease in relapse rate among those who received IgG postpartum. In a retrospective study of people with relapsing remitting multiple sclerosis, 73% of neonates were breastfed for periods of 3 to 12 weeks with no adverse effects. Individuals received immune globulin through 12 weeks postpartum with regimens of 400 mg/kg/day for 5 days after delivery with additional booster doses at 6 and 12 week postpartum (n = 41) or 0.4 mg/kg/day for 5 days during weeks 6 to 8 of gestation with booster doses every 6 weeks until 12 weeks postpartum.

Measles Virus; Mumps Virus; Rubella Virus; Varicella Virus Vaccine, Live: (Major) Do not give immune globulin including varicella zoster immune globulin concurrently with the varicella-zoster virus vaccine, live. Because of the potential inhibition of the immune response to vaccination by passively transferred antibodies, it is advisable not to give varicella-zoster virus vaccine, live to any patient who has received blood (except washed red blood cells), plasma transfusions, or immunoglobulins within the previous 5 months.     Headline
    laboratory test interference
    General Information
    Intravenous and subcutaneous immune globulin administration may result in laboratory test interference. After infusion of immunoglobulins, a transitory rise in passively transferred antibodies in the blood may yield false-positive serologic test results, with the potential for misleading interpretation. Passive transmission of antibodies to erythrocyte antigens (e.g., A, B, D) may cause a positive direct or indirect antiglobulin (Coombs') test. In addition, false-positive results have been reported in assays that detect beta-D-glucans for the diagnosis of fungal infections; this interference may persist for several weeks following immune globulin infusion.  Perform blood glucose measurements using glucose-specific methods in individuals receiving parenteral immune globulin products containing maltose (e.g., Octagam, Cutaquig). Blood glucose testing systems that use glucose dehydrogenase pyrroloquinoline quinone (GDH-PQQ) or glucose-dye-oxidoreductase methods may falsely interpret maltose as glucose, resulting in falsely elevated glucose readings (false hyperglycemia). Such errors have led to inappropriate insulin administration, resulting in life-threatening hypoglycemia, and may also mask true hypoglycemia. Consult the blood glucose testing system and test strip product information to confirm compatibility with maltose-containing parenteral products; if uncertainty exists, contact the manufacturer.

Description
  Text
  Immune globulin products are human plasma-derived protein solutions composed primarily of immunoglobulin G (IgG), with trace amounts of IgA and IgM depending on the formulation. They are available for intravenous, subcutaneous, and intramuscular administration and provide passive immunity by supplying a broad spectrum of antibodies obtained from pooled plasma of screened donors. Manufacturing includes testing for blood-borne pathogens and viral inactivation and/or removal steps. Immune globulin is used primarily as replacement therapy in individuals with primary immunodeficiency and is also indicated for selected immune-mediated and neurologic conditions (e.g., immune thrombocytopenic purpura, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP), Guillain-Barre syndrome, Kawasaki disease in pediatric patients). When administered intramuscularly, immune globulin is used for postexposure prophylaxis of hepatitis A, measles, and varicella. Pharmacokinetic profiles differ by route of administration: intravenous administration results in higher peak and lower trough IgG concentrations, whereas more frequent subcutaneous administration produces lower peaks, higher troughs, and more stable steady-state serum IgG concentrations; intramuscular products provide short-term passive immunity for specific indications. Although immune globulin products have comparable clinical efficacy, they are not pharmaceutically equivalent, as formulations vary with respect to IgG subclass distribution, antigen-specific antibody titers, concentration, stabilizing agents, osmolality, and IgA content. Across all routes of administration, immune globulin products are associated with infusion- or administration-related adverse reactions and a risk of thrombosis, and product-specific dosing and administration guidance should be followed. Intramuscular immune globulin was FDA-approved in the 1940s, followed by intravenous immune globulin, which was first approved in 1979; subcutaneous immune globulin formulations were approved subsequently.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Immunoglobulins are antibodies synthesized by B lymphocytes. Immune globulin is derived from pooled human plasma collected from thousands of donors. Most preparations consist primarily of intact immunoglobulin G (IgG) molecules, with trace amounts of IgA, IgM, soluble CD4 and CD8, human leukocyte antigens (HLA), and cytokines. The pooled, heterogeneous IgG contained in immune globulin provides a broad spectrum of antibodies capable of opsonizing and neutralizing a wide range of toxins and microorganisms, as well as activating the complement system. Although the relative distribution of IgG subclasses in parenteral immune globulin products is similar to that of normal human plasma, antibody titers against specific antigens vary among manufacturers. The Fc fragment of the IgG molecule enables interaction with Fc-gamma receptors expressed on B lymphocytes and other cells of the phagocytic system. In addition, the Fc fragment binds to Fc-binding plasma proteins, which are essential for complement activation and efficient clearance of microorganisms. Through these mechanisms, immune globulin confers passive immunity that can attenuate or prevent infectious diseases and mitigate harmful responses to toxins, mycoplasma, parasites, bacteria, and viruses.
 
In immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory conditions, the therapeutic effects of immune globulin are believed to result largely from interactions between the IgG Fc fragment and Fc-gamma receptors on target cells, including macrophages, B cells, natural killer cells, plasma cells, eosinophils, neutrophils, platelets, and other immune effector cells. In autoimmune cytopenias and inflammatory neurologic disorders, blockade of Fc-gamma receptors on macrophages reduces the clearance of opsonized target cells and suppresses antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity, respectively. Immune globulin may also modulate inflammatory responses by inhibiting complement-mediated tissue injury and regulating the production of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines and cytokine antagonists, such as interleukin-1 beta, interleukin-1 receptor antagonist, and tumor necrosis factor alpha. Additional immunomodulatory mechanisms may include the effects of anti-idiotypic antibodies on B cells and autoantibodies, modulation of helper T-cell activity, and induction of apoptosis or altered immune gene expression.
 
In the treatment of idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, immune globulin typically produces a rapid increase in platelet count, with effects most often lasting several weeks; however, sustained platelet stabilization for up to 1 year following administration has been reported.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Intravenous Route
    Description
    Peak serum IgG concentrations occur immediately after intravenous immune globulin (IVIG) infusion and are dose dependent, followed by a biphasic decline in serum concentrations. The initial phase is characterized by rapid distribution and equilibration between the intravascular and extravascular compartments, resulting in an expected rapid decrease in serum IgG concentrations; equilibrium is generally achieved within approximately 6 days, after which IgG is distributed evenly between these compartments. The second phase reflects a slower, more constant rate of elimination. Plasma clearance is low, ranging from 1.3 to 2.5 mL/kg/day, and the serum half-life of IgG is approximately 18 to 25 days, with substantial interindividual variability. Fever, infection, or high serum IgG concentrations have been associated with a shortened IgG half-life, whereas immunodeficiency states may be associated with a prolonged half-life, reported to range from 18 to 40 days. In a pharmacokinetic study of 61 adult patients with humoral immunodeficiency receiving intravenous (IV) Gammagard Liquid (median dose: 455 mg/kg/dose every 4 weeks), the mean peak and trough IgG concentration for IV Gammagard Liquid was 2,240 +/- 536 mg/dL and 1,050 +/- 260 mg/dL, respectively. The AUC was 9,958 +/- 2,274 mg x days/dL.
    Name
    Intramuscular Route
    Description
    The half-life of intramuscular immune globulin (IMIG) is approximately 23 days. Absorption after IM administration is slow, with peak serum concentrations occurring at approximately 2 days.
    Name
    Subcutaneous Route
    Description
    The systemic availability of subcutaneous immune globulin (SCIG) can be affected by several factors, including the site of administration and the rate of IgG catabolism. Catabolism of IgG is affected by the size and timing of a dose. After a large IVIG infusion, high plasma concentrations can saturate the Fc receptors and lead to increased IgG catabolism. SCIG is administered more frequently and results in lower serum IgG peaks and, hence, decreased IgG catabolism. The reduction in IgG catabolism leads to higher serum IgG concentrations, which may be beneficial for some individuals. Across studies in pediatric and adult populations with primary immunodeficiency, switching from IVIG to SCIG results in lower peak IgG concentrations, higher trough concentrations, and more stable steady-state serum IgG concentrations compared with IVIG administered every 3 to 4 weeks. To achieve systemic IgG exposure that is noninferior to IVIG, SCIG doses are typically increased to approximately 137% to 153% of the IVIG dose, depending on the product and dosing regimen. Following subcutaneous administration, peak IgG concentrations generally occur 2 to 4 days post-dose. Weekly SCIG administration produces relatively constant serum IgG concentrations, while biweekly or more frequent dosing regimens provide comparable overall exposure (AUC) with modest differences in peak and trough concentrations. Pharmacokinetic parameters are generally similar across age groups, and systemic IgG exposure is comparable across weekly, biweekly, and more frequent SCIG dosing schedules when the total dose is appropriately adjusted.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Pediatrics
    Description
    Neonates
The Vd of intravenous immune globulin (IVIG) in neonates has been reported to vary widely, ranging from 0.04 to 0.25 L/kg. The elimination half-life of IVIG in neonates appears comparable to that observed in adults, with reported values ranging from 18 to 29 days. In a study of 15 neonates (32 to 41 weeks gestation; birth weight more than 1,500 g), IVIG administered at doses of 500 mg/kg and 1,000 mg/kg produced mean peak serum IgG concentrations of 2,171 mg/dL and 2,734 mg/dL, respectively, measured 15 minutes after infusion. The 500 mg/kg dose resulted in significantly elevated IgG concentrations for more than 8 days, whereas the 1,000 mg/kg dose maintained significant elevations for more than 11 days. Serum IgG concentrations demonstrated a biphasic decline, with an initial rapid distribution phase within 24 hours after infusion followed by a slower elimination phase over approximately 6 weeks. Plasma clearance was estimated at 3 mL/kg/day. Gestational age, birth weight, administered dose, and baseline serum IgG concentrations had minimal impact on pharmacokinetic parameters.
 
Infants, Children, and Adolescents
The pharmacokinetics of intravenous and subcutaneous immune globulin in pediatric populations are similar to those observed in adults. Both routes of administration are effective in pediatric populations, with trough IgG concentrations comparable to those achieved in adults. Pediatric-specific dose adjustments are not required to attain target serum IgG concentrations. As in adults, dosing should be individualized and adjusted over time based on achieved trough IgG concentrations and clinical response.

      Description
      Administer intramuscular immune globulin (IMIG) by intramuscular injection only. Do not administer intravenously.
IMIG injections should be made into the deltoid muscle or into the anterolateral muscles of the thigh.
In order to avoid injury to the sciatic nerves, do NOT inject into the central gluteal region; injection into the upper, outer quadrant of the gluteus maximus should be used only if large volumes are administered or when large doses must be divided into multiple IM injections.
Dosages greater than 10 mL should be divided and injected into different sites.

General Subcutaneous Administration
Do not administer subcutaneously for the treatment of immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) due to the risk of hematoma formation.
Cleanse each injection site with an alcohol wipe or appropriate antiseptic using a circular motion from the center outward; allow to dry completely. The number and location of sites depend on the total dose volume and specific product.
Acceptable injection sites include the abdomen, thighs, upper arms, upper leg/hip area, and lower back. Avoid bony prominences, visible blood vessels, and areas that are tender, bruised, erythematous, indurated, scarred, tattooed, inflamed, or infected. Rotate sites with each weekly administration. New sites and simultaneous infusion sites should be at least 2 inches apart.
Select the number of infusion sites based on total dose volume. Multiple sites may be used simultaneously with a multi-needle set, Y-site tubing, or more than one infusion device, as appropriate.
Pinch the skin and insert the needle into the subcutaneous tissue, avoiding intravascular placement. Attach a sterile syringe and gently aspirate to confirm absence of blood. If blood is present, remove and discard the needle and tubing, and repeat at a new site with new sterile components. Secure the needle with sterile tape or a transparent dressing.
Prepare and prime the pump and administration tubing per manufacturer instructions to remove air. Initiate infusion according to the pump manufacturer's directions.
Upon completion, remove the dressing and needle(s). Apply gentle pressure with sterile gauze if needed and cover with a protective dressing.
After infusion completion, remove clear bandage and needle(s). If needed, gently press a small piece of gauze over the injection site and cover with a protective dressing.
 
Product specific administration
Cutaquig
May infuse in up to 6 sites (at least 2 inches apart) simultaneously.
The maximum infusion volume and rate are dependent on age:
Adults and Adolescents 17 years: For the first 2 infusions, do not exceed 25 mL per site and 20 mL/hour per site. For subsequent infusions, if tolerated, may gradually increase infusion volume by approximately 10 mL per site and infusion rate by 10 mL/hour per site every 2 to 4 weeks to a maximum infusion volume of 40 mL per site and a maximum infusion rate of 52 mL/hour per site.
Children and Adolescents 7 to 16 years: For the first 2 infusions, do not exceed 15 mL per site and 15 mL/hour per site. For subsequent infusions, if tolerated, may gradually increase infusion volume by approximately 5 to 10 mL per site and infusion rate by 5 to 10 mL/hour per site every 2 to 4 weeks to a maximum infusion volume of 29 mL per site and a maximum infusion rate of 25 mL/hour per site.
Children 2 to 6 years: For the first 2 infusions, do not exceed 10 mL per site and 15 mL/hour per site. For subsequent infusions, if tolerated, may gradually increase infusion volume by approximately 5 to 10 mL per site and infusion rate by 5 to 10 mL/hour per site every 2 to 4 weeks to a maximum infusion volume of 15.5 mL per site and a maximum infusion rate of 25 mL/hour per site.
 
Cuvitru
May infuse in up to 4 sites (at least 4 inches apart) simultaneously.
The maximum infusion volume and rate are dependent on weight:
Patients weighing 40 kg or more: Do not exceed 60 mL per site. For the first 2 infusions, do not exceed 10 to 20 mL/hour per site. For subsequent infusions, if tolerated, may increase to maximum of 60 mL/hour per site. For patients utilizing 4 infusion sites, the maximum infusion rate for all sites combined is 240 mL/hour.
Patients weighing less than 40 kg: For the first 2 infusions, do not exceed 20 mL per site and 10 to 20 mL/hour per site. For subsequent infusions, if tolerated, may increase to maximum of 60 mL per site and 60 mL/hour per site. For patients utilizing 4 infusion sites, the maximum infusion rate for all sites combined is 240 mL/hour.
 
Gammagard Liquid, Gammagard Liquid ERC
Patients weighing 40 kg and greater: Initial subcutaneous infusion rate is 20 mL/hour/site. If tolerated, the infusion rate may be increased to a maximum of 30 mL/hour/site. If multiple sites are used, the rate set on the pump should be the rate per site multiplied by the number of sites. For example, if the rate is 30 mL/hour/site, then 30 mL multiplied by 4 sites would be 120 mL/hour. Do NOT exceed a total of 8 infusion sites or maximum infusion rate of 240 mL/hour.
Patients weighing less than 40 kg: Initial subcutaneous infusion rate is 15 mL/hour/site. If tolerated, the infusion rate may be increased to a maximum of 20 mL/hour/site. If multiple sites are used, the rate set on the pump should be the rate per site multiplied by the number of sites. For example, if the rate is 20 mL/hour/site, then 20 mL multiplied by 4 sites would be 80 mL/hour. Do NOT exceed a total of 8 infusion sites or maximum infusion rate of 160 mL/hour.
 
Gammaked, Gamunex-C Vial
Adults: Initial, maintenance, and maximum subcutaneous infusion rate is 20 mL/hour/infusion site. Do not exceed a total of 8 simultaneous infusion sites.
Pediatric patients weighing 25 kg and greater: Initial subcutaneous infusion rate is 15 mL/hour/infusion site. If tolerated, the infusion rate may be increased to a maximum of 20 mL/hour/infusion site. Do not exceed a total of 6 simultaneous infusion sites.
Pediatric patients weighing less than 25 kg: Initial, maintenance, and maximum subcutaneous infusion rate is 10 mL/hour/infusion site. Do not exceed a total of 6 simultaneous infusion sites.
 
Hizentra
May infuse in up to 8 sites (at least 2 inches apart) simultaneously.
The maximum infusion volume and rate are dependent on indication:
Primary immunodeficiency: For the first infusion, do not exceed 15 mL per site and 15 mL/hour per site. For subsequent infusions, if tolerated, may increase to a maximum of 25 mL per site and 25 mL/hour per site.
Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP): For the first infusion, do not exceed 20 mL per site and 20 mL/hour per site. For subsequent infusions, if tolerated, may increase to a maximum of 50 mL per site and 50 mL/hour per site.

Adults and Pediatric patients 10 to 17 years: Do not exceed 25 mL per site and 35 mL/hour per site.
Pediatric patients 2 to 9 years: Do not exceed 25 mL per site and 25 mL/hour per site.

    Description
    Titers against specific antigens vary between manufacturers. Due to this variance, some clinicians feel strongly that IVIG products are not equivalent.
Visually inspect parenteral products for particulate matter and discoloration prior to administration whenever solution and container permit; do not use if solution has discoloration, cloudiness, or particulate matter. Do not use any solution that has been frozen, heated, or shaken.
Storage: Containers are for single use only; use promptly and discard any unused portion immediately.

  Intravenous administration
