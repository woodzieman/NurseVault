        Dosage Description
        Initially, 4 mg PO once daily for 2 weeks, then increase to 8 mg PO once daily if tolerated. A large randomized placebo-controlled trial (EUROPA, N=12,218) has reported that perindopril reduces the risk of cardiac events by 20% in patients with stable coronary artery disease. Approximately 65% of the study population had a history of myocardial infarction (MI). The primary study endpoint (8% perindopril vs. 10% placebo) includes a composite of cardiovascular death, non-fatal MI, or cardiac arrest with successful resuscitation. Non-fatal MI is significantly reduced (22% risk reduction) which contributes to the change in the composite primary endpoint. The EUROPA trial reported systolic and diastolic blood pressure reductions for perindopril of 5 mmHg and 2 mmHg, respectively, which potentially could have contributed to the reduction of cardiac events. Although this trial excluded patients with uncontrolled hypertension (SBP > 180 mmHg or DBP > 100 mmHg), 27% of patients studied had hypertension at entry to the study (defined as blood pressure > 160/95 mmHg or receiving antihypertensive therapy).

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE inhibitors) hypersensitivity usually manifests as a result of alterations in kinin generation in sensitive individuals; there is no evidence of a specific immune-mediated reaction. However, such reactions can be potentially life-threatening, even if they are not true 'allergic' reactions. Perindopril is contraindicated in patients with a history of ACE inhibitor-induced angioedema, and should not be used in patients with a history of hereditary angioedema or idiopathic angioedema. If angioedema occurs, ACE inhibitor therapy should be halted and appropriate treatment instituted (see Adverse Reactions). The incidence of ACE inhibitor-induced angioedema is higher in Black patients than non-Black patients. In addition, ACE inhibitors are less effective in lowering blood pressure in Black patients, including the African-American population.
 
Treatment with ACE inhibitors may increase the risk of anaphylactoid reactions in patients undergoing hymenoptera venom (insect sting) allergy desensitization. Two patients undergoing desensitizing treatment with hymenoptera venom while receiving ACE inhibitors sustained life-threatening anaphylactoid reactions. In the same patients, these reactions were avoided when ACE inhibitors were temporarily withheld, but they reappeared upon inadvertent rechallenge. However, a retrospective analysis of 79 patients who underwent hymenoptera venom (insect sting) allergy desensitization did not show an association between ACE inhibitor therapy and increased frequency of systemic reactions to venom immunotherapy. Of 17 patients taking an ACE inhibitor while undergoing desensitization, none experienced a systemic reaction to venom immunotherapy; whereas, 13 of 62 patients not taking an ACE inhibitor experienced a systemic reaction during venom immunotherapy. Anaphylactoid reactions have been reported in patients taking ACE inhibitors (enalapril) who were receiving dialysis with high-flux membranes; the mechanism is unknown. When anaphylactoid symptoms such as nausea, abdominal cramps, burning, angioedema, shortness of breath or low blood pressure are recognized, the dialysis should be stopped and the patient should receive aggressive treatment for the hypersensitivity reaction. Anaphylactoid reactions have also occurred in patients undergoing low-density lipoprotein apheresis with dextran sulfate absorption (a procedure dependent upon devices not approved in the United States). Although a causal relationship to ACE inhibitor therapy has not been firmly established, treatment with perindopril may increase the risk for anaphylactoid reactions during membrane exposure. ACE inhibitors may also precipitate low blood pressure in dialysis patients who are volume-depleted.
 
Perindopril should not be used in patients with unilateral or bilateral renal artery stenosis. Because the affected kidneys depend on the renin-angiotensin system to maintain GFR, inhibition of this system can lead to renal failure. Increases in blood urea nitrogen and serum creatinine are estimated to occur in about 20% of patients receiving ACE inhibitors. Azotemia is usually reversible upon discontinuation of ACE inhibitor and/or diuretic therapy. Since renal artery stenosis may not be identified prior to therapy, renal function should be monitored closely during the first 2 weeks after initiating therapy. Perindopril therapy should be discontinued if blood urea nitrogen and serum creatinine increase. Other types of renal disease (e.g. diabetic nephropathy) can actually improve during ACE inhibitor therapy. The dose of perindopril should be adjusted in patients with renal impairment (see Dosage). Greater sensitivity to the hypotensive effects of perindopril is possible in elderly patients, who may have reduced renal function. Neutropenia and agranulocytosis have been reported in patients receiving ACE inhibitor therapy, especially in patients who have preexisting renal disease, are receiving concomitant immunosuppression, or have collagen-vascular disease or autoimmune disease. Data from clinical trials of perindopril are insufficient to show that the drug does not cause agranulocytosis; therefore, complete blood counts should be established before and during perindopril therapy whenever the drug is administered to these patient populations. Perindopril should be used with caution in patients with pre-existing bone marrow suppression.
 
The systemic availability of perindoprilat may be increased in patients with hepatic disease. In patients with hepatic disease, the perindoprilat plasma concentrations are about 50% higher than that seen in healthy subjects or hypertensive patients with normal liver function. Dose adjustment is not required in patients with compensated hepatic cirrhosis; however, the dosage should be adjusted based on clinical response. Severe hepatic disease has not been adequately studied.
 
Perindopril should be used with caution in patients with hyperkalemia. ACE inhibitors can elevate serum potassium concentrations and could worsen pre-existing condition. Hyperkalemia may be associated with serious cardiac arrhythmias. Risk factors for the development of hyperkalemia include renal insufficiency, diabetes mellitus, and the concomitant use of potassium-sparing diuretics, potassium supplements, and/or potassium-containing salt substitutes (see Drug Interactions).
 
Perindopril is relatively contraindicated in patients who exhibit hypotension. Hypotension can occur if perindopril is administered to patients with hypovolemia or hyponatremia, or to patients receiving dialysis (see Dosage). Patients with intermittent hemodialysis) or high dose diuretics. Before initiating therapy with perindopril, diuretics should be stopped if possible for the 2 to 3 days prior to perindopril therapy, and volume and sodium balance corrected. If the diuretic cannot be discontinued, a reduced dose of perindopril can be used with careful medical supervision for several hours and until blood pressure has stabilized (see Dosage). After the first dose of perindopril, the patient should be followed closely for two weeks and whenever the dose of perindopril and/or diuretics is increased. In patients who are currently being treated with a diuretic, symptomatic hypotension occasionally can occur following the initial dose of perindopril. To reduce the likelihood of hypotension, the dose of diuretic, if possible, can be adjusted to diminish the likelihood of hypotension. The appearance of hypotension after the initial dose of perindopril does not preclude subsequent careful dose titration of perindopril, following effective management of the hypotension. Perindopril should be used cautiously in patients with congestive heart failure (initial doses should be lower than in the treatment of hypertension) because of a greater risk of developing hypotension. Hypotension may aggravate ischemia in patients with coronary artery disease or cerebrovascular disease, which may precipitate a myocardial infarction or cerebrovascular accident. Perindopril should be used with caution in patients with aortic stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. As with all vasodilators, ACE inhibitors should be given with caution to patients with obstruction in the outflow tract of the left ventricle.
 
In patients undergoing major surgery or during anesthesia with agents that lower blood pressure, perindopril may block angiotensin II formation secondary to compensatory renin release. Therefore, perindopril should be used with caution prior to surgery. If hypotension occurs during surgery and/or anesthesia and is considered to be due to blockade of angiotensin II formation, it can be corrected by volume expansion.
 
Perindopril is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category D. ACE inhibitors have been associated with fetal and neonatal morbidity and mortality when administered to pregnant women (see Adverse Reactions). In addition, use of ACE inhibitors during the first trimester of pregnancy has been associated with neurological malformations, prematurity, intrauterine growth retardation, patent ductus arteriosus and other structural cardiac malformations. In women planning to become pregnant, ACE inhibitors should not be used. Women of child-bearing age should be made aware of the potential risk and ACE inhibitors should only be given after careful counseling and consideration of individual risks and benefits. When pregnancy is detected, ACE inhibitors should be discontinued as soon as possible and monitoring of the fetal development should be performed on a regular basis. Rarely (probably less often than once per every thousand pregnancies), no alternative to ACE inhibitors will be found. In these rare cases, the pregnant women should be apprised of the potential hazards to their fetuses, and serial ultrasound examinations should be performed to assess the intraamniotic environment. The reported adverse fetal and neonatal effects (e.g., hypotension, neonatal skull hypoplasia and craniofacial deformation, fetal limb contractures, hypoplastic lung development, anuria, oligohydramnios, reversible or irreversible renal failure, and death) have been reported during ACE inhibitor exposure during the second and third trimesters. In addition, a recent observational study based on Tennessee Medicaid data has reported that the risk of congenital malformations is significantly increased during first-trimester exposure to ACE inhibitors. Further evaluation of teratogenicity data associated with ACE inhibitor exposure during pregnancy is ongoing.
 
Caution should be exercised when perindopril is given to a nursing mother. It is not known whether perindopril or perindoprilat are excreted in human breast milk. Because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in the infant, a decision should be made either to discontinue breast-feeding or perindopril, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother.
 
The safety and effectiveness of perindopril in children have not been established.

    General Information
    Perindopril is contraindicated in individuals with hereditary or idiopathic angioedema or a history of angioedema related to previous angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor treatment. Individuals with a history of angioedema unrelated to ACE inhibitors may be at increased risk for angioedema while receiving an ACE inhibitor. Other risk factors for ACE inhibitor-induced angioedema may include a history of ACE inhibitor induced cough, females, tobacco smoking, and those older than 65 years. ACE inhibitors have also been associated with a higher rate of angioedema in Black patients and Hispanic patients. In addition, ACE inhibitors are less effective in lowering blood pressure in Black patients. Hence, a higher initial dose for the ACE inhibitor or alternative initial therapy with a thiazide diuretic or long-acting calcium channel blocker may be considered in Black patients with hypertension.

    acute myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular disease, coronary artery disease, hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, hyponatremia, hypovolemia, severe aortic stenosis
    General Information
    Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors can cause symptomatic hypotension that may be complicated by oliguria, progressive azotemia, and rarely acute renal failure, or death. Risk factors for the development of excessive hypotension include heart failure with systolic blood pressure below 100 mmHg, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, hyponatremia, high dose diuretic therapy, dialysis, or severe hypovolemia and/or salt depletion of any etiology. Avoid use of ACE inhibitors in individuals who are hemodynamically unstable after an acute myocardial infarction. Hypotension may worsen symptoms or cause complications in individuals with severe aortic stenosis or hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy. Monitor individuals closely during the first 2 weeks of ACE inhibitor therapy and whenever the dose of ACE inhibitor (and/or diuretic) is increased.

    autoimmune disease, collagen-vascular disease, diabetes mellitus, low-density lipoprotein apheresis, renal disease
    General Information
    Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors can cause hyperkalemia. Risk factors for the development of hyperkalemia include renal insufficiency, diabetes mellitus, and concomitant use of potassium-sparing diuretics, potassium supplements, and/or potassium-containing salt substitutes. Individuals whose renal function may depend on the activity of the renin-angiotensin system (e.g., individuals with renal artery stenosis, chronic renal disease, severe heart failure, post-myocardial infarction, or hypovolemia) may be at risk for developing acute renal failure on ACE inhibitor therapy. Consider withholding or discontinuing ACE inhibitor therapy in individuals who develop a clinically significant decrease in renal function while receiving an ACE inhibitor. Monitor renal function and potassium periodically in individuals receiving ACE inhibitor therapy. Dosage adjustment of perindopril may be necessary in individuals with renal impairment. Anaphylactoid reactions have been reported in individuals taking angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors who are receiving dialysis with high-flux membranes (e.g., AN69). Consider an alternative dialysis membrane or an alternative medication in individuals with a history of reaction. Dialysis is also a risk factor for excessive hypotension with ACE inhibitor therapy. Anaphylactoid reactions have also been reported in individuals receiving ACE inhibitor therapy who are undergoing low-density lipoprotein apheresis with dextran sulfate adsorption. Additionally, ACE inhibitors may cause agranulocytosis and/or neutropenia, especially in individuals with renal failure or autoimmune disease, including collagen-vascular disease. Frequent hematologic monitoring is recommended in individuals with renal insufficiency or autoimmune disease who are receiving ACE inhibitors.

    geriatric
    General Information
    Initiate perindopril at a lower dose and titrate the dose slowly as needed in geriatric adults. Experience with perindopril doses exceeding 8 mg/day in older adults is limited. Monitor for dizziness because of the potential for falls in older adults. Plasma concentrations of both perindopril and perindoprilat were increased in older adults compared to concentrations in younger subjects.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Once pregnancy is detected, discontinue perindopril as soon as possible. Use of medications that affect the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), such as ACE inhibitors and angiotensin II receptor antagonists, are generally not recommended for use during pregnancy. Use of RAS inhibitors during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy is associated with reduced fetal renal function and increased risks of fetal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. Exposure to these agents in pregnancy may result in adverse outcomes, including fetal death, hypotension, neonatal skull hypoplasia, craniofacial abnormalities, fetal limb contractures, hypoplastic lung development, anuria, fetal growth restriction, and both reversible and irreversible renal failure. Additionally, cases of oligohydramnios and anhydramnios have been reported. Development of oligohydramnios may be associated with fetal lung hypoplasia and skeletal deformations. Use of RAS inhibitors during the first trimester of pregnancy may increase the risk of fetal harm, such as congenital malformations; however, available data are conflicting. In rare cases when another antihypertensive agent cannot be used to treat a pregnant patient, serial ultrasound examinations should be performed to assess the intra-amniotic environment. If oligohydramnios is observed, discontinue perindopril unless it is considered life-saving for the mother. Oligohydramnios may not appear until after the fetus has sustained irreversible injury.

    General Information
    Use perindopril with caution in breast-feeding patients. It is not known whether perindopril is excreted in human milk. Alternative therapies may be considered. Due to low levels in breast milk, guidelines generally consider captopril and enalapril to be compatible with breast-feeding unless high doses are required. In addition, benazepril and quinapril are excreted in low quantities into breast milk and have been suggested as options during breast-feeding.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Adverse reactions associated with the use of perindopril are usually mild and transient, requiring discontinuation in clinical trials in about 6.5% treated versus 6.7% placebo patients. The most common reasons for discontinuation of perindopril were cough (1.3% vs. 0.4% placebo), headache , asthenia/fatigue, and dizziness. Dizziness occurred in only 8.2 % of patients taking perindopril compared with 8.5 % taking placebo; however, dizziness occurred more frequently with perindopril at higher doses and in elderly patients. The most frequent adverse events (>1%) reported with perindopril which were greater than placebo and rated at least possibly drug-related included cough (6% vs 1.8%) and proteinuria (1% vs. 0.5%). In controlled trials, the overall incidence of cough (regardless of causality) was 12% in perindopril patients (vs. 4.5% placebo). ACE inhibition can result in the accumulation of kinins in the respiratory tract, sometimes causing a persistent, nonproductive cough. This cough may occur more frequently in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and is often overlooked as a potential adverse effect of ACE inhibitor therapy. Eosinophilic pneumonitis and dyspnea (0.3—1% with perindopril) have also occurred rarely.ACE inhibitors have been associated with anaphylactoid reactions, likely due to their inhibitory effect on eicosanoid and polypeptide metabolism, including bradykinin metabolism. Angioedema, or angioneurotic edema, of the face, edema of the extremities, mucous membranes, tongue, lips, larynx (laryngeal edema), and glottis has occurred rarely during ACE inhibitor therapy but is reversible following discontinuance of the drug. Involvement of the upper respiratory tract can induce acute respiratory distress. The onset usually occurs within hours or at most 1 week after starting ACE inhibitor therapy, but may occur at any time during therapy. The mechanism is unknown but may involve drug-induced auto-antibodies, bradykinin accumulation, dysregulation of the complement system, or histamine. Black patients receiving ACE inhibitors have been reported to have a higher incidence of angioedema compared to non-Black patients. If angioedema occurs during therapy with an ACE inhibitor, the drug should be discontinued immediately. Appropriate monitoring and therapy should be provided until signs and symptoms have resolved completely. Angioedema associated with laryngeal edema or tongue edema may be fatal. If there is involvement of the tongue, glottis or larynx, appropriate therapy (e.g., 0.3 to 0.5 ml subcutaneous epinephrine 1:1000) and/or measures to ensure a patent airway should be promptly provided. Even in those instances where swelling of only the tongue is involved, without respiratory distress, patients may require prolonged observation since treatment with antihistamines and corticosteroids may not be sufficient. Patients with involvement of the tongue, glottis or larynx are likely to experience airway obstruction, especially those with a history of airway surgery. Rarely, intestinal angioedema has been reported during post-marketing experience with ACE inhibitors. Patients with intestinal angioedema may present with abdominal pain (with or without nausea or vomiting). In some cases there is no prior history of facial angioedema and C-1 esterase levels are normal. Intestinal angioedema can be diagnosed by procedures including abdominal CT scan or ultrasound, or at surgery. Symptoms resolve after stopping the ACE inhibitor. Intestinal angioedema should be included in the differential diagnosis of patients receiving ACE inhibitors who present with abdominal pain.Symptomatic hypotension (0.8%), orthostatic hypotension, and syncope can occur with perindopril therapy. If hypotension occurs, the patient should be placed in a supine position and should receive appropriate correctional therapy. Symptomatic hypotension is more likely to occur in hypovolemic or hyponatremic patients. Patients with congestive heart failure are more likely to get excessive hypotension. Previous prolonged diuretic therapy, dialysis, diarrhea/vomiting, or salt-restricted diet could be contributing factors for hypotension. Volume and salt balance should be corrected, if possible, and diuretic therapy discontinued for a few days before perindopril therapy is started (see Dosage). If the diuretic cannot be discontinued, a reduced dosage of perindopril can be used with careful medical supervision for several hours and until blood pressure has stabilized (see Dosage). After the first dose of perindopril, the patient should be followed closely for two weeks of treatment and whenever the dose of perindopril and/or diuretics is increased. In patients who are currently being treated with a diuretic, symptomatic hypotension occasionally can occur following the initial dose of perindopril. To reduce the likelihood of hypotension, the dose of diuretic, if possible, can be adjusted to diminish the likelihood of hypotension. The appearance of hypotension after the initial dose of perindopril does not preclude subsequent careful dose titration of perindopril, following effective management of the hypotension.Reversible minor increases in blood urea nitrogen and serum creatinine were observed in patients treated with perindopril. However, more marked increases have been reported and are more likely to occur in patients receiving ACE inhibitor therapy with compromised renal function, renal artery stenosis, heart failure, or in patients who are hypovolemic (See Precautions). Potential serious renal adverse events of ACE inhibitor therapy include: acute renal failure, progressive azotemia, uremia, anuria and oliguria. If acute azotemia (elevated serum creatinine or BUN) occurs, or if renal artery stenosis is suspected, perindopril therapy should be discontinued.Hyperkalemia (serum potassium concentrations > 5.7 mEq/L) occurred in 1.3% of hypertensive patients receiving perindopril vs. 2.3% placebo during clinical trials. Serum potassium concentrations should be monitored closely in patients at risk for developing hyperkalemia. Risk factors for hyperkalemia during ACE inhibitor therapy include renal insufficiency, diabetes mellitus, and the concomitant use of potassium-sparing diuretics, potassium supplements, and/or potassium-containing salt substitutes.A maculopapular rash can develop during ACE inhibitor therapy, including perindopril therapy (2.3% incidence of rash). This rash could be part of a hypersensitivity reaction and accompanied by urticaria, pruritus, pemphigus, or photosensitivity. Myalgia has also been reported and could be an indicator of serum sickness. Myalgia has been reported infrequently (>=1%) with perindopril therapy.Leukopenia (including neutropenia) occurred in 0.1% of perindopril-treated patients.Serum ALT concentrations increased in 1.6% of perindopril-treated patients. Hepatotoxicity (hepatitis, hepatic failure) has been reported rarely in patients receiving ACE inhibitors. Although not completely understood, hepatotoxicity has included elevated hepatic enzymes, cholestasis with jaundice, fulminant hepatic necrosis, and death. Patients who develop jaundice should discontinue perindopril therapy and receive appropriate treatment.ACE inhibitors have been associated with teratogenesis and are classified as FDA pregnancy risk category D. ACE inhibitors have resulted in fetal and neonatal morbidity and mortality when administered to pregnant women. In women planning to become pregnant, ACE inhibitors should not be used. Women of child-bearing age should be made aware of the potential risk and ACE inhibitors should only be given after careful counseling and consideration of individual risks and benefits. Rarely (probably less often than once per every thousand pregnancies), no alternative to ACE inhibitors will be found. In these rare cases, the pregnant women should be apprised of the potential hazards to their fetuses, and serial ultrasound examinations should be performed to assess the intraamniotic environment. Adverse fetal and neonatal effects (e.g., hypotension, oligohydramnios, renal failure, skull hypoplasia, and death) have been reported during ACE inhibitor exposure, primarily during the second and third trimesters. In addition, a recent observational study based on Tennessee Medicaid data has reported that the risk of congenital malformations is significantly increased during first-trimester exposure to ACE inhibitors. Based on this trial, the reported risk ratio for development of birth defects is 2.71 (95% CI, 1.72—4.27) for infants with ACE inhibitor exposure limited to the first trimester when compared to infants who had no exposure to antihypertensive agents. An increased risk of developing malformations of the cardiovascular system (risk ratio 3.72; 95% CI, 1.89—7.3) and central nervous system (risk ratio 4.39; 95% CI, 1.37—14.02) has been observed. A post hoc analysis has reported a risk ratio of 9.32 for developing congenital malformations of the renal system (95% CI, 1.79—48.5). Although findings from one epidemiologic study do not establish causality between first trimester ACE inhibitor exposure and congenital malformations, the observations introduce the possibility that ACE inhibitors could be associated with teratogenic effects during early pregnancy. Further evaluation of teratogenicity data associated with ACE inhibitor exposure during pregnancy is ongoing. When pregnancy is detected, ACE inhibitors should be discontinued as soon as possible and monitoring of the fetal development should be performed on a regular basis. Infants with histories of in utero exposure to ACE inhibitors should be closely observed for hypotension, oliguria, and hyperkalemia. If oliguria occurs, attention should be directed toward support of blood pressure and renal perfusion. Exchange transfusion or dialysis may be required as means of reversing hypotension and/or substituting for disordered renal function. There have been occasional reports of benefit with such methods to remove ACE inhibitors from the neonatal circulation, but experience is limited. Specific adverse fetal and neonatal effects of ACE inhibitor exposure during the last six months of pregnancy have included hypotension, neonatal skull hypoplasia, anuria, reversible or irreversible renal failure (e.g., renal tubular dysplasia), oligohydramnios, and death. Oligohydramnios has been attributed to decreased fetal renal function and has been associated with fetal limb contractures, craniofacial deformation, and hypoplastic lung development. If oligohydramnios is observed, ACE inhibitors should be discontinued unless it is considered life-saving for the mother. Contraction stress testing (CST), a nonstress test (NST), or biophysical profiling (BPP) may be appropriate, depending upon the week of pregnancy. Patients and physicians should be aware that oligohydramnios may not appear until after the fetus has sustained irreversible injury. Prematurity, intrauterine growth retardation, and patent ductus arteriosus have also been reported, although it is not clear whether these occurrences are due to ACE inhibitor exposure.

    General Information
    Adverse reactions associated with the use of perindopril are usually mild and transient, requiring discontinuation in clinical trials in about 6.5% treated versus 6.7% placebo patients.     General Information
    Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors have been associated with anaphylactoid reactions, likely due to their inhibitory effect on eicosanoid and polypeptide metabolism, including bradykinin metabolism. Angioedema of the face, extremities, lips, tongue, glottis, and larynx has been reported in individuals treated with ACE inhibitors; angioedema was reported in 0.1% of perindopril-treated subjects in clinical trials. If there is involvement of the tongue, glottis or larynx, appropriate therapy (e.g., 0.3 to 0.5 mL subcutaneous epinephrine 1 mg/mL) and/or measures to ensure a patent airway should be promptly provided. Even in those instances where swelling of only the tongue is involved, without respiratory distress, individuals may require prolonged observation since treatment with antihistamines and corticosteroids may not be sufficient. The onset of angioedema usually occurs within hours or at most 1 week after starting ACE inhibitor therapy, but may occur at any time during therapy. The mechanism is unknown but may involve drug-induced auto-antibodies, bradykinin accumulation, dysregulation of the complement system, or histamine. Intestinal angioedema has been reported with ACE inhibitors. Individuals with intestinal angioedema presented with abdominal pain (with or without nausea or vomiting). In some cases, there was no prior history of facial angioedema and C1 esterase concentrations were normal. Intestinal angioedema was diagnosed by procedures including abdominal CT scan or ultrasound, or at surgery. Symptoms resolved after stopping the ACE inhibitor. Treatment with ACE inhibitors may increase the risk of anaphylactoid reactions in individuals undergoing hymenoptera venom (insect sting) allergy desensitization. Life-threatening anaphylactoid reactions occurred in 2 individuals undergoing desensitizing treatment with hymenoptera venom while receiving ACE inhibitor therapy. These reactions abated when ACE inhibitors were temporarily withheld, but reappeared upon rechallenge. However, a retrospective analysis of 79 subjects who underwent hymenoptera venom (insect sting) allergy desensitization did not show an association between ACE inhibitor therapy and increased frequency of systemic reactions to venom immunotherapy. Of 17 subjects taking an ACE inhibitor while undergoing desensitization, none experienced a systemic reaction to venom immunotherapy; comparatively, 13 of 62 subjects not taking an ACE inhibitor experienced a systemic reaction during venom immunotherapy.

    Symptomatic hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, and syncope can occur with perindopril therapy. To minimize the possibility of hypotensive effects, decrease the dose of or discontinue the diuretic or increase the salt intake prior to initiation of treatment with perindopril. If diuretic therapy cannot be altered, close medical supervision should be provided with the first dose of perindopril for at least two hours and until blood pressure has stabilized for another hour. The appearance of hypotension after the initial dose of perindopril does not preclude subsequent careful dose titration of perindopril, following effective management of the hypotension.

Description
  Text
  Perindopril is an oral angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor used for the treatment of hypertension and to reduce the risk of cardiovascular mortality or nonfatal myocardial infarction in patients with stable coronary artery disease. It is a prodrug that is hydrolyzed to perindoprilat. Perindopril is an intermediate-acting ACE inhibitor, allowing for once or twice daily dosing. In contrast to captopril, perindopril does not contain a sulfhydryl group, which has implications regarding the drug's adverse reaction profile. Perindopril has also been used in heart failure, although it is not FDA approved for this use. Perindopril was approved by the FDA December 1993, but did not become available in the US until mid-1999.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: Perindoprilat, the active moiety of perindopril, competes with angiotensin I for its binding site on the angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE). As a result, the drug blocks the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II. Angiotensin II is a potent vasoconstrictor and a negative feedback mediator for renin activity. Thus, when perindoprilat lowers angiotensin II plasma levels, blood pressure decreases and plasma renin activity increases. In addition, baroreceptor reflex mechanisms are stimulated in response to the fall in blood pressure. Perindoprilat inhibits both ACE and kininase II. Kininase II, identical to ACE, is an enzyme that degrades bradykinin, a potent vasodilator, to inactive peptides. Whether increased bradykinin levels play a part in the therapeutic effects of ACE inhibitors is presently unclear. Bradykinin-induced vasodilation is thought to be of secondary importance in the blood-pressure lowering effect of ACE inhibitors. A bradykinin mechanism may, however, contribute to ACE-inhibitor-induced angioneurotic edema and cough.ACE-inhibiting drugs can act locally to reduce vascular tone by decreasing local angiotensin II-induced sympathetic and/or vasoconstrictive activity. ACE inhibitors also can inhibit presynaptic norepinephrine release and postsynaptic adrenergic receptor activity, thus decreasing vascular sensitivity to vasopressor activity; however, this action may not be clinically evident at usual doses. Decreases in plasma angiotensin II levels also result in a reduction in aldosterone secretion, with a subsequent decrease in sodium and water retention. As antihypertensives, ACE inhibitors reduce LVH, do not worsen insulin resistance or hyperlipidemia, and generally do not cause sexual dysfunction.Perindoprilat causes arterial dilation, thereby lowering total peripheral vascular resistance. In hypertensive patients, blood pressure is decreased with little or no change in heart rate, stroke volume, or cardiac output. Both standing and supine blood pressure are reduced following administration of perindopril, and although symptomatic hypotension is rare, it occurs more commonly in patients who are hypovolemic or hyponatremic. Perindopril is about one-half as effective in reducing blood pressure in black versus non-black patients.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    After oral administration, perindopril is rapidly absorbed with peak plasma concentrations occurring in about 1 hour. Peak plasma perindoprilat concentrations occur 3 to 7 hours after oral perindopril administration. Food does not significantly decrease the rate or extent of perindopril absorption. However, biotransformation to perindoprilat is reduced by about 43% resulting in an approximate 20% reduction in the plasma ACE inhibition curve, which is probably not clinically significant. In clinical trials, perindopril was typically administered in a non-fasting state. The AUC and Cmax of both perindopril and perindoprilat increase in a dose-proportional manner following single and multiple daily dosing. Perindopril and perindoprilat exhibit multicompartment pharmacokinetics including a deep tissue compartment related to ACE binding sites. Although a majority of perindoprilat elimination occurs within a mean half-life of 3 to 7 hours, there is a prolonged terminal elimination half-life of 30 to 120 hours due to the slow disassociation of perindoprilat from plasma and tissue ACE binding sites. Following multiple, once daily perindopril dosing, perindoprilat has a 1.5- to 2-fold accumulation and reaches steady-state plasma levels in 3 to 6 days.

  Text
  Perindopril is an orally administered prodrug. Perindopril erbumine undergoes hydrolysis via the hepatic esterase to perindoprilat, the active drug. Perindopril is 60% bound to plasma proteins; perindoprilat is 10 to 20% protein-bound. Perindopril is extensively metabolized, with only 4 to 12% of the dose recovered unchanged in the urine. The absolute oral bioavailability of perindopril is 75% and approximately 30 to 50% of systemically available perindopril is hydrolyzed to the active agent perindoprilat. Perindoprilat has a mean overall systemic bioavailability of about 25%. Peak perindopril concentrations occur at approximately 1 hour and perindoprilat concentrations occur 3 to 7 hours after oral administration. Five inactive metabolites of perindopril form following glucuronidation and cyclization via dehydration. The inactive metabolites are perindopril and perindoprilat glucuronides, dehydrated perindopril, and the diastereoisomers of dehydrated perindoprilat. Perindoprilat and its metabolites are eliminated primarily by the renal route. The mean elimination half-life ranges for perindopril and perindoprilat are 0.8 to 1 hour and 3 to 10 hours, respectively.
