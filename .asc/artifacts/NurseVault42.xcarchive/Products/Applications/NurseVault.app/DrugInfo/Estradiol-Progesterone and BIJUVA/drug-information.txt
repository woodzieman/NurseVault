      Route Age Groups
        Heading
        Adults who are menopausal and postmenopausal
        Dosage Description
        1 capsule (estradiol 0.5 mg with progesterone 100 mg) PO each evening with food. Adjust dosage based on clinical response. Max: 1 capsule (estradiol 1 mg with progesterone 100 mg) per day. Reassess and attempt to taper or discontinue hormone therapy at 3 to 6-month intervals. The North American Menopause Society (NAMS) Guidelines support the initiation of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) around the time of menopause if no contraindications exist, and use is acceptable to the individual patient, as hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for vasomotor and genitourinary symptoms and has been shown to prevent bone loss and fracture. When prescribed for isolated genitourinary symptoms, topical vaginal products should be considered. Early initiation of HRT and continuation of use at/or until the median age of menopause (52 years) is recommended in women with premature natural or surgically induced menopause. The timing of initiation of HRT can affect the overall risk-benefit profile. Clinicians may consider HRT for vasomotor symptoms and increased risk for bone loss around the time of menopause in women younger than 60 years of age or who are less than 10 years from menopause onset. For women who initiate HRT more than 10 or 20 years from menopause onset or are 60 years of age or older, the benefit-risk ratio is less favorable due to the known risks of HRT (e.g., stroke, myocardial infarction, venous thromboembolism, dementia, urinary incontinence), and guidelines generally recommend against use in these women. Decisions regarding whether to continue systemic HRT in women older than 60 years should be made on an individual basis for quality of life, persistent vasomotor symptoms, or prevention of bone loss and fracture, with consideration given to alternative treatments for prevention of bone loss and other health issues.

    Headline
    abnormal uterine bleeding, breast cancer, endometrial cancer, estrogen-dependent tumor, increased risk for breast cancer
    General Information
    Estradiol; progesterone hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is contraindicated in people with a history of or known/suspected breast cancer or at increased risk for breast cancer. Estrogen-based HRT is also contraindicated in people with undiagnosed abnormal uterine bleeding of unknown etiology, endometrial cancer, or other estrogen-dependent tumor, which may be hormonally-sensitive. There is an increased risk of endometrial cancer in a person with a uterus who uses unopposed estrogens. Adding a progestin to estrogen HRT has been shown to reduce the risk of endometrial hyperplasia, which may be a precursor to endometrial cancer. Adequate diagnostic measures, including directed or random endometrial sampling when indicated, should be undertaken to rule out malignancy in postmenopausal women with undiagnosed persistent or recurring abnormal genital bleeding. The use of estrogen-alone or estrogen plus progestin HRT has been reported to result in an increase in abnormal mammograms, requiring further evaluation and observational studies. During the Womens Health Initiative (WHI), estrogen-alone was not associated with an increased risk of invasive breast cancer, but an estrogen plus progestin WHI substudy reported increased risks of invasive breast cancer. Consistent with the WHI clinical trials, observational studies have also reported an increased risk of breast cancer for estrogen plus progestin therapy, and a smaller increased risk for estrogen-alone therapy, after several years of use. All people taking estrogen with or without a progestin as HRT should receive an annual clinical breast examination, perform monthly self-examinations, and have regular mammograms as recommended by their health care professional based on patient age, risk factors, and prior mammogram results. While estrogen therapy is rarely used for the palliative treatment of advanced breast cancer, estrogen HRT use may lead to severe hypercalcemia in patients with breast cancer and bone metastases. If hypercalcemia occurs, use of HRT should be stopped and appropriate measures taken to reduce the serum calcium level.
    Headline
    endometriosis, uterine leiomyoma
    General Information
    Because estrogens influence the growth of endometrial tissues, use estrogen-based hormone replacement therapy (HRT) cautiously in people with endometriosis or uterine leiomyoma (uterine fibroids). A few cases of malignant transformation of residual endometrial growth have been reported in women treated post-hysterectomy with estrogen-alone HRT. For individuals known to have residual endometriosis post-hysterectomy, the addition of a progestin, such as in the use of estradiol; progesterone products, should be considered to reduce the risk of endometrial tissue growth.
    Headline
    cardiac disease, hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, obesity, thromboembolism, thrombophilia, tobacco smoking
    General Information
    Estradiol; progesterone is contraindicated in people with active thromboembolism [e.g. pulmonary embolism (PE), deep vein thrombosis (DVT)] or a history of these conditions, or in those with active arterial thromboembolic disease [e.g., stroke and myocardial infarction (MI)] or a history of these conditions. Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) regimens are also contraindicated in people with known thrombophilia (e.g., protein C, protein S, or antithrombin deficiency). Individuals with certain risk factors for arterial vascular disease (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, tobacco smoking, hypercholesterolemia, and morbid obesity) and/or venous thromboembolism (VTE) (e.g., personal history or family history of VTE, obesity, or systemic lupus erythematosus) should be monitored and managed appropriately during HRT use. The provider should be alert to the earliest manifestations of thrombotic disorder (thrombophlebitis, MI, cerebrovascular disease such as stroke or focal headache with symptoms consistent with cerebral ischemia, PE, or unexplained visual disturbance with ocular pain, which might indicate retinal thrombosis). The safety and efficacy of estradiol; progesterone for the prevention of cardiovascular disorders has not been established. The Women's Health Initiative (WHI) estrogen plus progestin HRT substudy reported overall increased risks of deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, stroke and myocardial infarction in postmenopausal women (50 to 79 years of age) receiving an estrogen with progestin HRT regimen relative to placebo. Monitor blood pressure in all postmenopausal individuals receiving HRT and especially in those with pre-existing hypertension; in a few case reports, substantial increases in blood pressure have been attributed to idiosyncratic reactions to estrogen HRT. In a large, randomized, placebo controlled clinical trial, a generalized effect of estrogens on blood pressure was not seen. Estrogens may cause some degree of fluid retention, and people with conditions that may be aggravated, such as cardiac disease / dysfunction, warrant careful observation when estrogens are prescribed.
    Headline
    immobility, major surgery, planned procedure
    General Information
    If feasible, estrogen-based hormone replacement therapy (HRT) should be discontinued at least 4 to 6 weeks before major surgery or a planned procedure associated with an increased risk of thromboembolism, or during periods of prolonged immobility. Resume the HRT regimen only after the patient is fully ambulatory. In addition, people treated with HRT should be advised to move about periodically during travel involving prolonged immobilization, such as during long-duration plane travel.
    Headline
    acute hepatitis, Budd-Chiari syndrome, decompensated hepatic cirrhosis, hepatic disease, hepatic hemangioma, hepatocellular adenoma, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, porphyria
    General Information
    The FDA-approved labeling states that estradiol; progesterone hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is contraindicated in people with hepatic failure, impairment or hepatic disease. However, expert guidance from the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD), North American Menopause Society (NAMS), and the Menopausal Hormone Therapy (MHT) Eligibility Criteria Group states that systemic estrogen HRT is not contraindicated in all individuals with hepatic disease and may be used in selected patients with appropriate monitoring and with consideration of personalized risk to benefit ratios. Experts generally agree that estrogen-based HRT should not be used in people with decompensated liver function (decompensated hepatic cirrhosis), Budd-Chiari syndrome, acute hepatitis, or hepatocellular adenoma (a benign liver tumor). Some experts state that all individuals with cirrhosis (compensated or decompensated) should avoid systemic estrogen-based HRT, since cirrhosis damages the liver. Postmenopausal individuals with selected liver conditions are at higher risk for progression of fatty liver disease, liver dysfunction, and fibrosis versus those who are premenopausal. There is some clinical trial evidence of slower fibrosis progression in postmenopausal individuals with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) or hepatitis C who were treated with estrogen HRT, presumably due to the positive effects of estrogen on hepatic cellular function and metabolic parameters, but more extensive study is needed to establish the potential benefits and risks in these conditions. Estrogens and/or progestins may be poorly metabolized in those with impaired liver function, and the risk versus benefits of using HRT should be weighed individually for any person with hepatic disease. There is concern about cholestatic effects of estrogens, particularly in people with existing cholestatic liver disease. However, estrogen-containing HRT appears to be safe for individuals with primary biliary cholangitis (PBC). For people with a history of intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy or a history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use of an estrogen, such as cholestatic jaundice, use estrogen-based HRT with caution. If there is a recurrence of cholestatic jaundice, discontinue the HRT regimen. Estrogen HRT may also cause an exacerbation of porphyria or hepatic hemangioma (a benign liver tumor); consider whether the benefits of estrogen HRT outweigh the risks in people with such conditions.

    Headline
    hypothyroidism
    General Information
    Estrogen administration leads to increased thyroid-binding globulin (TBG) levels. People with hypothyroidism dependent on thyroid hormone replacement therapy who are also receiving estrogens may require increased doses of their thyroid replacement therapy. Monitor thyroid function in these individuals to ensure maintenance of their free thyroid hormone levels in an acceptable range. People with normal thyroid function can compensate for the increased TBG by making more thyroid hormone, thus maintaining free T and T serum concentrations in the normal range.

    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    An individual's overall risk vs. benefit of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) should be considered for the geriatric adult when continuing HRT after 65 years of age. The Women's Health Initiative (WHI) studies showed higher risk for some adverse events in older versus younger menopausal adults. In the WHI estrogen plus progestin substudy versus placebo, there was a higher relative risk of nonfatal stroke and invasive breast cancer in women greater than 65 years of age. In the Women's Health Initiative Memory Study (WHIMS), combination estrogen-progestin HRT was found to increase the risk of developing probable dementia in geriatric adults who were 65 to 79 years of age. The Beers Criteria consider oral, topical patch, or other systemic forms of estrogens (with or without progestins) as potentially inappropriate medications for use in geriatric adults. Avoid use due to evidence of carcinogenic potential (i.e., breast and endometrium) and the lack of cardiovascular or cognitive protective effects. Avoid oral or transdermal estrogen in geriatric adult women with any type of urinary incontinence due to lack of efficacy for this condition. The use of vaginal estrogens is acceptable for the management of dyspareunia and vaginal/vulvar symptoms.

    Estradiol; progesterone is not indicated for use during pregnancy. There appears to be little or no increased risk of birth defects in children born to people who have used oral combined hormonal contraceptives inadvertently during early pregnancy. In any patient in whom pregnancy is suspected, pregnancy should be ruled out before continuing hormonal therapy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use estradiol; progesterone products with caution during breast-feeding. In general, these products should not be used during lactation. Estrogens and progestogens are present in human milk. Estrogen administration has been shown to decrease the quantity and quality of breast milk. This reduction can occur at any time but is less likely to occur once breast-feeding is well established. The developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding should be considered along with the clinical need for hormone therapy and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the medication or from the underlying maternal condition.

The anticonvulsants mentioned may cause oral contraceptive failure, especially when low-dose estrogen regimens (e.g., ethinyl estradiol is = 50 mcg/day) or a second contraceptive method are typically suggested if women use an enzyme-inducing anti-epileptic drug or a barbiturate. Proper intake of folic acid should also be ensured. (Major) Felbamate can accelerate the clearance of the progestin component of some oral contraceptives. Patients who experience breakthrough bleeding while receiving these drugs together should notify their prescribers. An alternate or additional form of contraception should be used during concomitant treatment. Additionally, patients taking non-oral combination contraceptives or progestins for hormone replacement therapy may also experience reduced clinical efficacy; dosage adjustments may be necessary.

    This monograph discusses the adverse reactions seen with the combination use of estradiol; progesterone for hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in menopausal and postmenopausal women.
    Headline
    amenorrhea, breakthrough bleeding, candidiasis, cervical dysplasia, cervicitis, dysmenorrhea, hot flashes, leukorrhea, libido decrease, libido increase, night sweats, pelvic pain, vaginal bleeding, vaginitis
    General Information
    A variety of endocrine and urogenital effects can occur during therapy with estradiol; progesterone therapy. Changes in sexuality include libido increase or libido decrease. Positive changes in libido may occur as a result of improvements in vulvar and vaginal atrophy in postmenopausal women. Vaginal discharge, vaginal irritation, vaginal candidiasis, vaginitis, cervicitis, or changes in cervical erosion (e.g., cervical ectropion) may appear. Vaginal discharge (leukorrhea) was reported in 1.9% (0.5 mg estradiol) and 3.4% (1 mg estradiol) of women taking estradiol; progesterone therapy compared to 0.7% receiving placebo. Estrogens may also cause enlargement of uterine leiomyomatas (fibroids) if present. A cystitis-like syndrome has also been reported. Changes in vaginal bleeding pattern and breakthrough bleeding or spotting have been noted with estrogens with or without progestins and are commonly reported. Pelvic pain or dysmenorrhea are infrequent. Pelvic pain was reported in 2.8% (0.5 mg estrogen) and 3.1% (1 mg estrogen) of women taking estradiol; progesterone therapy compared to 0% receiving placebo. Vaginal bleeding was reported in 2.4% (0.5 mg estrogen) and 3.4% (1 mg estrogen) of women taking estradiol; progesterone therapy compared to 0% receiving placebo. In postmenopausal women, changes in uterine bleeding patterns will usually taper and stabilize within 3 to 6 months of beginning cyclic or continuous HRT combinations. Amenorrhea is desirable in many postmenopausal women and not considered to be an adverse effect of estrogen therapy, and occurred in roughly 56.1% of women within 1 year of use of oral estradiol; progesterone. Unusual vaginal bleeding, metrorrhagia, or spotting that persists beyond 6 months in any woman on estrogen therapy should be evaluated by a healthcare professional. For women who have a uterus, adequate diagnostic measures such as endometrial sampling, when indicated, should be undertaken to rule out malignancy in cases of undiagnosed persistent or recurring abnormal vaginal bleeding. Women who take estrogens should follow current recommendations for annual pelvic examinations including regular Papanicolaou smears to detect cervical dysplasia. Postmarketing, night sweats and hot flashes have been reported in patients receiving estradiol; progesterone.

    abdominal pain, biliary obstruction, bowel ischemia, cholecystitis, cholestasis, colitis, diarrhea, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatitis, hypertriglyceridemia, jaundice, nausea, pancreatitis, peliosis hepatis, vomiting
    General Information
    Stomach/abdominal pain or cramps, bloating, and nausea (3.5% with 0.5 mg estrogen; 2.2% with 1 mg estrogen; 0.7% with placebo) sometimes associated with vomiting are common adverse effects of estrogens and may occur in patients receiving estradiol; progesterone. Consider benign hepatic adenoma if abdominal pain/tenderness, abdominal mass, or hypovolemic shock is present, as the adenoma may rupture and cause intraabdominal hemorrhage. Benign hepatic adenomas appear to be associated with the use of oral contraceptives, and enlargement of hepatic hemangiomas has been reported with estrogen and/or progestin therapies. Numerous cases of bowel ischemia (ischemic colitis) have been reported during combined estrogen and progesterone use; mesenteric vein thrombus due to hypercoagulability is the proposed mechanism. Pancreatitis and colitis have also been reported. In patients with preexisting hypertriglyceridemia, estrogen therapy may be associated with elevations of plasma triglycerides leading to pancreatitis and other complications. Estrogens enhance hepatic lipoprotein uptake and inhibit bile acid synthesis, resulting in increased concentration of cholesterol in the bile which can lead to cholelithiasis, biliary obstruction, and cholestasis. Cholestatic jaundice and an increased incidence of gallbladder disease have also been reported. A 2- to 4-fold increase in the risk of gallbladder disease requiring surgery (e.g., cholecystitis) in postmenopausal women receiving estrogens has been reported. Similar increases in gallbladder disease have not been reported with transdermally administered estradiol, perhaps due to the fact that estradiol does not appear to increase saturation of cholesterol in the bile when administered by this route. Estrogens may be poorly metabolized in patients with impaired liver function. For patients with a history of cholestatic jaundice associated with past estrogen use or with pregnancy, use estradiol cautiously. If cholestatic jaundice recurs, discontinue the estrogen. Rare adverse reactions include hepatitis (and elevated hepatic enzymes). Estrogens may induce peliosis hepatis, a very rare consequence of taking estrogens and combined oral contraceptives that is characterized by the presence of blood-filled spaces. Persistent or severe abdominal symptoms should be evaluated by a medical professional. Postmarketing reports of abdominal distention and diarrhea have been reported in patients receiving estradiol; progesterone.

Description
  Text
  Estradiol and progesterone are used together in an oral preparation for hormone replacement therapy in women with an intact uterus to treat moderate to severe vasomotor symptoms associated with menopause. Estradiol is the principal intracellular human estrogen and is substantially more active than its metabolites, estrone and estriol, at the cellular level. Like other estrogens, estradiol also improves genitourinary symptoms associated with menopause. Progesterone is a naturally occurring progestin. In the female human body, progesterone is synthesized in the ovaries, placenta, and the adrenal cortex. The addition of a progestin to the estrogen replacement therapy substantially reduces, but does not eliminate, the risk for endometrial hyperplasia. However, combination HRT may add additional health risks for some women, and hormone replacement regimens are recommended to be used for the shortest duration consistent with the treatment goals of the individual patients. An estradiol; progesterone oral combination product was FDA-approved in October 2018.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The administration of estrogen and progestin hormones results in different pharmacologic effects in individual females dependent upon the underlying estrogen balance and age of the individual patient. This monograph describes the basic mechanism of action of estradiol and progesterone in the postmenopausal woman.
 
Estradiol: Estradiol is the principal intracellular human estrogen and is substantially more active than its metabolites, estrone and estriol, at the cellular level. Once estrogens enter the cells of responsive tissues (e.g., female organs, breasts, hypothalamus, pituitary), they increase the rate of synthesis of DNA, RNA, and some proteins. Exogenous estrogens elicit all of the actions of endogenous estrogens. Estrogens are responsible for the growth and development of female sex organs and the maintenance of sex characteristics including growth of axillary and pubic hair and shaping of body contours and skeleton. At the cellular level, estrogens increase cervical secretions, cause proliferation of the endometrium, and maintain the elasticity and tone of the urogenital structures. In postmenopausal women, estrogen replacement therapy acts to reduce the elevated levels of the gonadotropins (e.g., luteinizing hormone and follicle stimulating hormone) via a negative feedback mechanism on the pituitary. Estrogens have a weak anabolic effect and also can affect bone calcium deposition and accelerate epiphysial closure. Estrogens appear to prevent osteoporosis associated with the onset of menopause as opposed to reversing disease that has already developed, primarily by preserving bone mineral density (BMD) in the spine and in the hip. Estrogens generally have a favorable effect on blood lipids, reducing LDL- and increasing HDL-cholesterol concentrations, on average, by 15%. Serum triglycerides increase with estrogen administration. Estrogens increase the rate of synthesis of many proteins, including thyroid binding globulin and several clotting factors. Estrogens reduce levels of antithrombin III, and increase platelet aggregation. Estrogens also enhance sodium and fluid retention.
Progesterone: Progesterone is a naturally occurring steroid that is secreted by the ovary, placenta, and adrenal gland. Progestins convert a proliferative endometrium into a secretory one, thus reducing endometrial growth and the risk of endometrial carcinoma in post-menopausal women with an intact uterus. The mode of action against endometrial cancer has not been determined, but may be related to decreased numbers of nuclear estradiol receptors and suppression of epithelial DNA synthesis in endometrial tissues. Progestins, when added to postmenopausal estrogen therapies, attenuate some of the positive effects of estrogens on HDL-cholesterol.

    Description
    The oral absorption of both estradiol and progesterone is subject to first pass metabolism. After multiple doses of the estradiol; progesterone capsules, the time to maximum concentrations (Tmax) for estradiol and progesterone are approximately 3 to 6 hours and 3 hours, respectively.   Text
  Estradiol; progesterone products are administered orally. Estrogens undergo first pass metabolism after oral administration and are metabolized in the GI mucosa during absorption and in the liver. Progesterone also is subject to first pass metabolism with oral absorption. Estrogens are strongly protein-bound, primarily to albumin and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG). Progesterone is approximately 96% to 99% bound to serum proteins, primarily to serum albumin (50% to 54%) and transcortin (43% to 48%). Estrogens are widely distributed in the body and are generally found in higher concentrations in the sex hormone target organs. Estradiol is rapidly transformed by the liver to estrone by 17beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase. Circulating estrogens exist in a dynamic equilibrium of metabolic interconversions. These transformations take place mainly in the liver. Estradiol is converted reversibly to estrone, and both can be converted to estriol, the major urinary metabolite. Estrogens also undergo enterohepatic recirculation via sulfate and glucuronide conjugation in the liver, biliary secretion of conjugates into the intestine, and hydrolysis in the gut followed by reabsorption. In postmenopausal women, a significant portion of the circulating estrogens exist as sulfate conjugates, especially estrone sulfate, which serves as a circulating reservoir for the formation of more active estrogens. In-vitro and in-vivo studies have shown that estrogens and progestins are metabolized partially by CYP3A4. Estradiol, estrone, and estriol are excreted in the urine along with glucuronide and sulfate conjugates. Progesterone is metabolized primarily by the liver mostly to pregnanediols and pregnanolones, which are conjugated in the liver to glucuronide and sulfate metabolites; these metabolites are excreted in the bile and urine. Progesterone metabolites are eliminated mainly by the kidneys. Progesterone metabolites that are excreted in the bile may undergo enterohepatic recycling or may be excreted in the feces. Following repeat dosing with estradiol; progesterone capsules, the half-lives of estradiol and progesterone were 26 hours and 10 hours, respectively.
