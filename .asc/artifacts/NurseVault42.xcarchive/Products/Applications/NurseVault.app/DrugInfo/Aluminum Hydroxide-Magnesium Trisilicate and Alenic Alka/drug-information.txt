Contraindications And Precaution
Drug Interactions

NOTE: All products associated with this monograph are off-market. The content is retained for historical reference only and is no longer actively maintained.
The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.

magnesium restriction, renal failure, renal impairment


Use aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate with caution in individuals with renal failure or renal impairment or those following a diet with magnesium restriction because of the increased risk of developing hypermagnesemia and magnesium and/or aluminum toxicity.


Hypersensitivity

This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.

Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use

People with trouble swallowing, symptoms of or known peptic ulcer disease, or GI bleeding should consult a care provider before nonprescription (OTC) antacid use, as these conditions may need professional management. Patients should discontinue nonprescription antacid use and consult a doctor if symptoms last more than 2 weeks.

sodium restriction


Use aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate with caution in individuals following sodium restriction diets as these products contain sodium and sodium intake may exceed dietary restrictions.


pregnancy


When used occasionally at recommended doses, aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate products appear to be safe and effective for the relief of dyspepsia and heartburn or gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) during pregnancy, provided the pregnant individual does not have renal dysfunction. Magnesium trisilicate can lead to fetal nephrolithiasis, hypotonia, and respiratory distress if used chronically during pregnancy and in high doses. Guidelines recommend a trial of lifestyle modifications as first-line therapy for heartburn and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), followed by antacids. For ongoing symptoms, histamine type 2-receptor antagonists (H2RAs) can be used to control heartburn symptoms in pregnancy. Proton pump inhibitors should be reserved for patients who fail H2RA therapy.


breast-feeding


Aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate is compatible with breast-feeding. When chronic use and high doses are avoided, it appears to be a safe and effective treatment option for the relief of dyspepsia and heartburn/gastroesophageal reflux (GERD) during breast-feeding. No problems have been reported in the breastfed infant and these antacids do not concentrate in breast milk.

Adverse Reaction
General Information

constipation, dehydration, diarrhea, diuresis, nausea, vomiting


Aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate is associated with diarrhea, constipation, chalky taste, diuresis, dehydration, nausea, and vomiting.


GI obstruction, hemorrhoids


Patients who are dehydrated, fluid restricted, or have preexisting decreased bowel motility are predisposed to developing GI obstruction due to the aluminum in aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate. Hemorrhoids, fecal impaction, and/or fissures are other complications associated with antacid use in patients with preexisting conditions.


hypercalcemia


With prolonged use, the aluminum in aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate causes an increase in bone resorption and an increase in the intestinal absorption of calcium, possibly leading to hypercalcemia.


anorexia, encephalopathy, hypermagnesemia, hypotension, impaired cognition, osteomalacia, weakness


If used in patients with impaired renal function or at high doses for extended periods of time, patients are at risk for developing hypermagnesemia and accumulating aluminum in the bones, lungs, and nerve tissues. Hypermagnesemia results in a depressant effect on the central nervous system (causing anorexia and nausea) and the neuromuscular system (causing decreased deep tendon reflexes and skeletal muscle weakness). Hypotension, muscle weakness, and electrocardiographic changes are indicative of magnesium toxicity. The accumulation of aluminum can lead to dialysis encephalopathy, dialysis osteomalacia, or "dialysis dementia" (impaired cognition).


hypophosphatemia, malaise, nephrolithiasis, osteoporosis


Hypophosphatemia can occur in patients receiving long-term aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate therapy, due to the aluminum component. This reaction is especially likely to occur in patients who have an inadequate dietary intake of phosphate. Hypophosphatemia is characterized by anorexia, malaise, and muscle weakness. Prolonged aluminum hydroxide administration also can cause nephrolithiasis, osteomalacia, and osteoporosis.


Description

NOTE: All products associated with this monograph are off-market. The content is retained for historical reference only and is no longer actively maintained.
 
Aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate is used as an antacid for temporary relief of symptoms associated with gastric acidity, such as dyspepsia and heartburn. Aluminum and magnesium combinations are the most commonly used antacids. The side effects of constipation from aluminum and diarrhea from magnesium are dose related; combination antacids are formulated to balance the risk of either constipation or diarrhea. However, in some patients, a slight laxative type effect due to the magnesium component may occur. Antacids may interact with certain prescription drugs. Antacids containing aluminum hydroxide with magnesium trisilicate were first marketed in 1972.

Mechanism Of Action

NOTE: All products associated with this monograph are off-market. The content is retained for historical reference only and is no longer actively maintained.
 
Both aluminum hydroxide and magnesium trisilicate react with hydrochloric acid in the stomach. Aluminum hydroxide is slowly solubilized in the stomach and reacts with hydrochloric acid producing aluminum chloride and water. Magnesium trisilicate is also slowly solubilized in the stomach and reacts with hydrochloric acid to form magnesium chloride, water, and silicone dioxide. These chemical reactions neutralize gastric acid secretions and increase the gastric pH. The increases in gastric pH inhibit the proteolytic action of pepsin, an effect which is particularly important in patients with peptic ulcer disease. The increased pH and decreased pepsin production help in the healing of peptic ulcers. While gastric acids are neutralized, the actual secretion of acid is not effected. The chloride salts of aluminum and magnesium produced in the stomach react with bicarbonate in the small intestine to minimize the risk of systemic alkalosis. In addition, aluminum complexes to form the insoluble aluminum phosphate, which is excreted in the feces. This reduces the amount of phosphate that is available for absorption.
 
In the management of esophageal reflux, the increase in gastric pH produced by antacids, including aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate, causes an increase in the lower esophageal sphincter pressure. This increased pressure minimizes the amount of reflux to the esophagus. Alginic acid, listed as an inactive ingredient in aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate products, is also purported to be useful in GERD. Alginic acid works by reacting with sodium bicarbonate and saliva to form a viscous solution of sodium alginate. This viscous solution floats on the surface of gastric contents so that when reflux occurs sodium alginate, rather than gastric contents, is refluxed and esophageal irritation is minimized. The antacid combination product should be administered with a full glass of water so that the sodium alginate can float on the water in the stomach. The presence of sodium alginate is only useful when the patient is in the upright position.

Pharmacokinetics

NOTE: All products associated with this monograph are off-market. The content is retained for historical reference only and is no longer actively maintained.
 
Aluminum hydroxide; magnesium trisilicate is administered orally.
Aluminum hydroxide reacts with hydrochloric acid in the stomach to produce aluminum chloride and water. About 17-30% of the aluminum chloride is absorbed and is rapidly excreted by the kidneys in patients with normal renal function. In the small intestine, aluminum chloride is rapidly converted to insoluble, poorly absorbed basic aluminum salts. Aluminum also combines with dietary phosphate in the intestine forming insoluble, nonabsorbable aluminum phosphate which is excreted in the feces. Magnesium trisilicate is slowly solubilized and reacts with hydrochloric acid in the stomach to form magnesium chloride, silicon dioxide, and water. Approximately 15-30% of the magnesium chloride is absorbed and rapidly excreted by the kidneys in patients with normal renal function. The aluminum and magnesium that are not absorbed remain in the GI tract and are excreted in the feces.
