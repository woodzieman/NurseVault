Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Ginseng has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. Products containing ginseng are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Consumers should also be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.Due to limited scientific data, ginseng should be used cautiously in neonates, infants, young children, during pregnancy or during breast-feeding.Because ginseng may have estrogenic activity, this herb should probably be avoided in patients with estrogen-dependent malignancies such as breast cancer. Caution is also advised in women with other estrogen-dependent conditions such as uterine leiomyomata (fibroids), fibrocystic breast disease, or endometriosis.In 1979, it was reported that ginseng caused hypertension. Although this report is controversial since there was no analysis to determine what types of ginseng were being used and subjects were taking relatively large doses of the root (3 g/day) for up to 2 years, ginseng should probably be used with caution in patients with uncontrolled hypertension.Patients taking ginseng inform their surgeons and anesthesiologist that they are taking this herb prior to scheduling surgery. Because ginseng has been reported to make people susceptible to blood pressure and heart rate changes during surgery, as well as platelet inhibition, it may be wise to discontinue this herb 2—3 weeks prior to an elective procedure, where possible. As with other platelet inhibitors, discontinue ginseng supplements at least 7 days prior to the surgical procedure to limit surgical risk.

    endometriosis, estrogen-dependent tumor, uterine leiomyoma
    General Information
    Because ginseng may have estrogenic activity, its use should be avoided in patients with an estrogen-dependent tumor or malignancy, such as breast cancer, endometrial cancer, or ovarian cancer. Caution is also advised in women with other estrogen-sensitive conditions, such as uterine leiomyoma (fibroids), fibrocystic breast disease, or endometriosis.

    Headline
    planned procedure
    General Information
    Advise patients to discontinue ginseng supplements 2 weeks prior to a planned procedure (e.g., surgery). Ginseng may increase the risk of bleeding due to its antiplatelet effects and interfere with perioperative blood glucose control.
    Headline
    schizophrenia
    General Information
    Use ginseng with caution in patients with schizophrenia, since ginseng might cause sleep problems and agitation in people with schizophrenia.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus
    General Information
    Use ginseng with caution in patients with diabetes mellitus because ginseng might lower blood sugar concentrations.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Ginseng should be avoided during pregnancy. Limited data exists describing the use of this herb in people who are pregnant. In vitro evidence of teratogenicity with exposure to ginsenosides (active constituents) in animal embryos exists; however, this data may not be applicable to humans since exposure to the ginsenosides were at a much higher levels than that of normal human consumption. Additionally, conflicting evidence exists as to whether ginseng may exhibit estrogenic activity and therefore, more information is needed in order to determine the safety of ginseng in people who are pregnant.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise individuals to avoid breast-feeding while taking ginseng, due to the potential for serious adverse effects in the breast-fed child. It is unknown whether ginseng is excreted into human milk since no data exists describing the use of this herb in people who are breast-feeding. There is a possibility of the ginsenosides (active constituents) having negative effects on the breast-fed child. Additionally, conflicting evidence exists as to whether ginseng may exhibit estrogenic activity and therefore, more information is needed in order to determine the safety of ginseng in people who are breast-feeding.

Warfarin: (Major) Interactions have been reported clinically between ginseng and warfarin. With regard to warfarin, one case report is noted of a decreased INR (reduced anticoagulant effect) after the addition of ginseng (Ginsana) in a patient stabilized on warfarin, followed by a return to the desired INR after ginseng was discontinued. In another report, ginseng was implicated in a life-threatening case of valve thrombosis in a patient with an inability to maintain a therapeutic INR on warfarin after he began using a commercial ginseng product. The effect of ginseng on warfarin has been evaluated in a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 4 weeks duration in healthy volunteers. The subjects (n= 20) received warfarin (5 mg/day PO x 3 days/week). Beginning in week two, 12 of the subjects took ginseng powder (2 g/day PO in capsules); 8 subjects took placebo capsules. Compared with the placebo group, the ginseng group had significantly reduced INR values, warfarin AUCs, and peak plasma warfarin concentrations after 2 weeks. Concurrent use of ginseng and warfarin is not recommended; clinicians should discuss ginseng use with patients. Ginseng (Panax ginseng) also exerts antiplatelet activity and theoretically may interact with other drugs that exhibit antiplatelet effects or anticoagulant activity; however, data are not available to confirm or deny clinical interactions.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Because several studies have shown that many ginseng products are adulterated or mislabeled, adverse effects attributed to ginseng may be due to contaminants rather than the herb. In addition, other ingredients such as ephedrine and caffeine are sometimes added to ginseng products. Considering the long history of ginseng use and the relative infrequency of reported adverse reactions, it appears that ginseng is generally safe and not usually associated with serious adverse reactions. One case of severe headache and cerebral arteritis was reported in a woman who ingested extremely large doses of ginseng. Abnormal vaginal bleeding and mastalgia have also been reported; several reports have implicated ginseng and/or adulterants as having estrogenic activity in women. In 1979, it was reported that ginseng caused hypertension, anxiety, and irritability. However this report is controversial since there was no analysis to determine what types of ginseng were being used, and subjects were taking relatively large doses of the root (3 g/day) for up to 2 years. In addition, this was an open study and the intake of concomitant drugs (e.g., caffeine) confounded interpretation of the data.Rare cases of hypoglycemia have been reported with the use of ginseng. In patients with non-insulin-dependent diabetes, ginseng therapy has been associated with a reduction of fasting blood glucose. Five glycans in Panax ginseng have been discovered to exert hypoglycemic effects or stimulate insulin secretion in diabetic and glucose-loaded normal mice. Although no cases of serious reactions have been reported in humans, blood glucose should be monitored closely in patients with diabetes mellitus upon initiation of ginseng.The efficacy and safety of standardized ginseng extract Ginsana G 115 was evaluated in 227 volunteers for a period of 12 weeks. Only 9 adverse events were reported, the most common being insomnia.

Description
  Text
  Although several species of plants from the genus Panax are used in products described as ginseng, their effects are similar. American ginseng (P. quinquefolius L.) is used very little in the U.S. and is primarily exported to Asia. Asian ginseng (Panax ginseng C.A. Meyer) is the species best studied and most frequently available in the U.S. The dried root of Asian ginseng contains at least 13 different ginsenosides. 'Ginsenosides' is the name given to the active principles which are triterpenoid saponin glycosides. The quantities of these ginsenosides vary according to: age of the root, the location where grown, the season when harvested, and the method of drying. The claimed benefits of ginseng are so broad that it has been termed a 'tonic,' however the term 'adaptogen' has been used to describe ginseng in herbal literature. Adaptogens are promoted to reduce biological, chemical, and physical stress, and improve general vitality, including mental and physical capacity for work. Asian ginseng is considered more of stimulant than American ginseng. Despite the fact that Asian ginseng has been used for centuries in China and that purported uses are numerous, there is little scientific documentation of its clinical effects in humans. Ginseng has been evaluated for the treatment of menopausal vasomotor symptoms (VMS) in a randomized, placebo-controlled trial; however, ginseng did not reduce the number of hot flashes. The North American Menopause Society (NAMS) does not recommend that ginseng be used for VMS due to menopause.
 
Siberian ginseng (Eleutherococci radix, eleuthero) differs from Panax ginseng. Siberian ginseng was introduced from the former U.S.S.R. as a cheaper substitute for Panax ginseng. Many of the studies on Siberian ginseng are published in the Russian language, lack controls, and are not double-blinded. According to the German Commission E, the roots of Siberian ginseng also contain lignans and coumarin derivatives. Because Siberian ginseng contains different active components than Asian or American ginseng, Siberian ginseng is not addressed in this monograph.
 
NOTE: In the US, herbal and dietary supplement products are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for these products as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since dietary supplements are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The major active components of ginseng are the ginsenosides (also called panaxosides), which are triterpenoid saponin glycosides. Some of the 13 ginsenosides contained in Asian ginseng exert actions that directly oppose the actions of other ginsenosides. For example, some components cause CNS depression while other cause CNS stimulation. Tests in small animals have shown that ginseng can prolong exercise time, stimulate protein synthesis, inhibit platelet aggregation, stimulate the proliferation of hepatic ribosomes, prevent stress-induced ulcers, and increase the activity of the immune system possibly by increasing natural killer cell activity and enhancing the production of interferons. Panaxynol and ginsenosides Ro, Rg1, and Rg2 have been identified as the main antiplatelet components in Panax ginseng. Panaxynol is thought to exert its antiplatelet effect by inhibiting thromboxane formation. Studies have suggested that the actions of ginsenosides are due to enhanced nitric oxide synthesis in the endothelium of lung, heart, and kidney, and in the corpus cavernosum. In patients with non-insulin-dependent diabetes, ginseng therapy has been associated with a reduction of fasting blood glucose. Five glycans in Panax ginseng have been discovered to exert hypoglycemic effects or stimulate insulin secretion in diabetic and glucose-loaded normal mice. Although claims have been made of the ergogenic properties of ginseng, there is a lack of controlled research demonstrating the ability of ginseng to improve or prolong performance in humans.

Pharmacokinetics
  Text
  Ginseng is administered orally. Because ginsenosides are present in the dried root of the plant in relatively small amounts, only the whole root, or a concentrated extract of it, is used in herbal preparations. Potency of ginseng preparations can vary substantially from manufacturer to manufacturer. A standardized extract is available. Ginseng is also available commercially as capsules, tablets, teas, cigarettes, chewing gum, and candies. Experiments carried out in the late 1970s showed that 60% of 54 ginseng products were worthless and 25% contained no ginseng at all. A report in the November 1995 issue of Consumer Reports revealed enormous variance in the potencies of 10 different brands of ginseng capsules. The total ginsenoside concentration per capsule ranged from 0.2—7.6% in this study of ginseng products.
