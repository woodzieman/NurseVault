Drug Class: Duloxetine is an oral serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) used for the treatment of major depressive disorder, diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain, and chronic musculoskeletal pain (e.g., chronic low back pain and chronic pain due to osteoarthritis) in adults, fibromyalgia in adults...

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 120 mg/day PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): asthenia, dizziness, drowsiness, dysarthria, dysgeusia, dyskinesia, fatigue, headache, hypoesthesia, insomnia, lethargy, malaise, paresthesias, tremor, trismus, yawning, abnormal dreams, agitation, anxiety, confusion, hallucinations, irritability, nightmares, restlessness, colitis, dehydration, eructation, halitosis, pancreatitis, peptic ulcer

In an Overdose: Serotonin syndrome has been reported during use of SNRIs alone, during concurrent use of other medications known to increase serotonin levels, or during SNRI overdose. Symptoms that have been reported in association with duloxetine overdose include serotonin syndrome, somnolence, vomiting, and seizures.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Avoid abrupt discontinuation of duloxetine if possible. Gradual tapering is recommended during discontinuation of any serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) drug to decrease or prevent the occurrence of potential withdrawal symptoms.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Avoid abrupt discontinuation of duloxetine if possible. Gradual tapering is recommended during discontinuation of any serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) drug to decrease or prevent the occurrence of potential withdrawal symptoms. Withdrawal symptoms have been reported with abrupt discontinuation or tapering of duloxetine and have included abnormal dreams, headache, insomnia, dizziness, nausea/vomiting, paresthesia, irritability, fatigue, diarrhea, anxiety, vertigo, hyperhidrosis, and a small increase in recumbent heart rate. More severe symptoms such as confusion, hypomanic episode, and seizures have been reported following discontinuation of other SNRIs or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). Patients should be carefully monitored if abrupt discontinuation of duloxetine becomes necessary.
 
Duloxetine has not been studied in patients with renal impairment or renal failure. The drug should be used with caution in these patients. Reduced dosages of duloxetine may be necessary.
 
Use duloxetine with caution in patients with pre-existing urinary retention. Duloxetine is in a class of drugs known to affect urethral resistance. Urinary hesitancy (or urinary retention) was an infrequently reported adverse effect in duloxetine clinical trials. Urinary retention has also occurred during post-marketing use; some cases have required hospitalization and/or catheterization.
 
Duloxetine has not been studied in patients with cardiac disease, including those with a recent history of myocardial infarction or unstable coronary artery disease. Duloxetine has caused small increases in heart rate during clinical studies but has not been associated with significant increases in blood pressure. Mean increases in systolic and diastolic pressures of up to 2.1 mm Hg and 2.3 mm Hg, respectively, have occurred in clinical trials. Orthostatic hypotension and syncope have been reported during duloxetine administration, particularly during the first week of treatment or following dose increases. Other factors which may increase the risk of hypotension include use of dosages above 60 mg/day, and concurrent administration of antihypertensives or potent CYP 1A2 inhibitors (see Drug Interactions). Patients with preexisting cardiac disease, hypotension, or hypertension should be monitored closely if duloxetine therapy is initiated. It is advisable to evaluate blood pressure prior to initiating treatment and periodically thereafter during maintenance therapy.
 
Pharmacokinetic data from a single dose study indicate that the AUC of duloxetine is approximately 25% higher in healthy elderly females (65—77 years of age) compared to younger females (32—50 years of age), the half-life is about 4 hours longer in elderly females, and the Cmax is comparable between the groups. The significance of these findings is unknown. Results from clinical trials and other clinical experience indicate that there are no overall differences in safety and efficacy between elderly patients and younger adults. It should be noted that the elderly may have an increased risk of developing low serum sodium levels compared to other age groups. In addition, some geriatric patients may experience an increased sensitivity to the adverse effects of antidepressants including duloxetine.
 
Serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) may cause hyponatremia, which is frequently the result of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). In some cases, serum sodium levels less than 110 mmol/L have been reported; however, the adverse effect appeared reversible upon discontinuation of the causative SNRI. Elderly patients, those receiving diuretics or prone to dehydration, and those who are otherwise volume depleted (e.g., hypovolemia) appear to be at greatest risk. Hyponatremia may manifest as headache, difficulty concentrating, memory impairment, confusion, weakness, and unsteadiness which may result in falls. Severe manifestations include hallucinations, syncope, seizure, coma, respiratory arrest, and death. Symptomatic hyponatremia may require discontinuation of the SNRI, as well as implementation of the appropriate medical interventions.
 
Duloxetine is contraindicated for use in patients with uncontrolled closed-angle glaucoma. Duloxetine was associated with an increased risk of mydriasis during clinical trials. The drug should be used with caution in patients who have increased intraocular pressure or controlled closed-angle glaucoma.
 
The use of duloxetine is not recommended in patients with any degree of hepatic impairment. Post-marketing reports suggest patients with pre-existing liver disease who take duloxetine may be at an increased risk for further liver damage including hepatitis, hepatomegaly, elevated transaminases, and cholestatic jaundice. Cases of hepatic failure, sometimes fatal, have presented as hepatitis with abdominal pain, hepatomegaly, and elevated transaminase levels (> 20 times the upper limit of normal) with or without jaundice. Cholestatic jaundice with minimally elevated transaminase levels has also occurred. Post-marketing reports also indicate that patients with chronic liver disease or cirrhosis may experience elevated transaminases, bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase. Duloxetine is extensively metabolized; therefore, clearance may also be reduced resulting in an increase in plasma concentrations of duloxetine compared to patients without liver disease. Some reports suggest elevated hepatic enzymes may be dose-related. Because it is possible that duloxetine and alcohol may interact to cause liver injury or that duloxetine may aggravate pre-existing liver disease, duloxetine should be avoided if possible in patients with substantial alcohol use, ethanol intoxication, alcoholism, and/or evidence of chronic hepatic disease. Discontinue duloxetine in patients who present with pruritus, dark urine, jaundice, right upper quadrant tenderness, unexplained flu-like symptoms, or other evidence of significant hepatic dysfunction. Duloxetine should not be reinstituted unless another cause can be established.
 
There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of duloxetine use in children. Safety and efficacy have not been established. In October 2004, the FDA directed manufacturers of all antidepressants to include a boxed warning detailing the risk of suicide in pediatric patients. A causal role has been established for antidepressants in inducing suicidality in pediatric patients. The risk of suicidality for these drugs was identified in a pooled analysis of 24 placebo-controlled trials (n=4400) lasting up to 16 weeks in pediatric patients with major depressive disorder (MDD), obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), or other psychiatric disorders. The analysis showed a greater risk of suicidality during the first few months of treatment in those receiving antidepressants (SSRIs and others). The average risk of such events on drug was 4% and 2% for placebo; however, no suicides occurred in these trials. Pooled analysis of short-term clinical trials during early phase treatment with SSRIs and other antidepressants in young adults (18 to 24 years) also showed an increased risk of suicidal thinking and behavior. The clinical need for an antidepressant in children or young adults for any use must be weighed against the risk of increased suicidality; patients who are started on therapy should be observed closely for clinical worsening, suicidality, or unusual changes in behavior, particularly within the first few months of starting therapy or at the time of dose increase or decrease; such observation would generally include at least weekly face-to-face contact with patients during the first 4 weeks of treatment, then every other week visits for the next 4 weeks, then at 12 weeks, and as clinically indicated beyond 12 weeks; additional contact by telephone may be appropriate between visits. It is unknown if the suicidality risk in children or young adults extends to longer-term therapy (i.e., beyond several months). The possibility of a suicide attempt is inherent in patients with depressive symptoms, whether these occur in primary depression or in association with another primary disorder. All patients with a history of suicidal ideation or behaviors and those with a prominence of suicidal ideation prior to treatment are considered at an increased risk for suicidal ideation or attempts, and should be closely monitored during treatment with duloxetine. In patients who exhibit changes in symptoms, worsening of depression or emergent suicidality, a decision should be made to change or discontinue treatment. If discontinuing, medication should be tapered as rapidly as possible, but with recognition that abrupt discontinuation can also cause adverse symptoms. All antidepressants should be prescribed in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management in order to reduce the risk of overdose.
 
All effective antidepressants can transform depression into mania in predisposed individuals. It is generally believed (though not established in controlled trials) that treating such an episode with an antidepressant alone may increase the likelihood of precipitation of a mixed/manic episode in patients at risk for bipolar disorder. If a patient develops manic symptoms, duloxetine should be withheld, and appropriate therapy initiated to treat the manic symptoms. Additionally, depression may be the presenting symptom of a mixed/manic episode of bipolar disorder. Patients should be adequately screened for bipolar disorder prior to initiating an antidepressant. Such screening should include a detailed psychiatric history, including a family history of suicide, bipolar disorder, and depression. Patients with depression or comorbid depression in the setting of other psychiatric illness being treated with antidepressants should be observed for clinical worsening and suicidality, especially during the initial few months of a course of drug therapy, or at times of dose changes. Caregivers should be advised to closely observe the patient on a daily basis and to communicate immediately with the prescriber the emergence of agitation, irritability, unusual changes in behavior, or suicidality. It should be noted that duloxetine is not approved for use in treating bipolar depression. Analysis of short-term studies in geriatric patients aged 65 years and older showed a decline in the risk of suicidal thinking and behavior during early phase treatment with SSRIs and other antidepressants, contrary to the data obtained in children and young adults.
 
During clinical trials, duloxetine was associated with anorexia, however, weight loss was minor. Duloxetine should be used with caution in patients with anorexia nervosa or in other patients where weight loss is undesirable.
 
The effects of altered motility on the stability of the enteric coating of duloxetine is not known; however, rapid hydrolysis of the drug occurs in an acidic environment. Therefore, the manufacturer advises caution when using duloxetine in those with delayed gastric emptying, such as in patients with diabetes mellitus. Treatment with duloxetine has also been shown to worsen glycemic control in some diabetics. During long-term studies (up to 52 weeks), the mean blood glucose levels increased by 12 mg/dL in the duloxetine group and decreased by 11.5 mg/dL in the routine care group. In addition, the HbA1c increased by 0.5% in those treated with duloxetine and 0.2% in those receiving routine care.
 
Duloxetine is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. There are no available reports on the use of duloxetine during human pregnancy. Duloxetine should be used in pregnancy only where the benefit to the mother clearly outweighs any potential risk to the fetus. Alternative agents should be considered. Neonates exposed to serotonergic antidepressants late in the third trimester have developed complications requiring prolonged hospitalization, respiratory support, and tube feeding. Such complications can arise immediately upon delivery. Features are consistent with either a direct toxic effect of serotonergic agents or, possibly, a neonatal abstinence syndrome. It should be noted that, in some cases, the clinical picture is consistent with serotonin syndrome. When treating a pregnant woman with an SNRI or other serotonergic agent during the third trimester, the physician should carefully consider the potential risks and benefits of treatment. If clinically feasible, gradual tapering of the medication (in the mother) prior to delivery may be considered.
 
Duloxetine is excreted into the breast milk of lactating women. The estimated daily infant dose is approximately 0.14% of the maternal dose. The clinical significance of this finding is unknown. SSRI-type drugs (e.g., fluoxetine) which are excreted in breast milk have been observed to cause increased irritability, colic, vomiting, and decreased sleep in infants. American Academy of Pediatrics has suggested that the use of serotonin reuptake inhibitors during breast-feeding may be of concern. Duloxetine is a dual serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor. Because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in nursing infants, a decision should be made whether to discontinue breast-feeding or to discontinue duloxetine, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother.
 
Monitor patients taking an SNRI for signs and symptoms of bleeding. Platelet aggregation may be impaired by serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) due to platelet serotonin depletion, possibly increasing the risk of a bleeding complication (e.g., gastrointestinal bleeding, ecchymoses, epistaxis, hematomas, petechiae, hemorrhage). Concurrent use of anticoagulant therapy, thrombolytic therapy, or other medications that enhance bleeding potential may increase this risk (see Drug Interactions). Patients taking duloxetine should be instructed to promptly report any bleeding events to the practitioner.
 
Duloxetine is a serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor and should not be used concurrently with MAOI therapy (see Drug Interactions).

    adolescents, children, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Antidepressants increased the risk of suicidal thoughts and behavior in children, adolescents, and young adults in short-term studies. Prescribe duloxetine in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management to reduce the risk of overdose. Monitor all patients receiving antidepressants closely for clinical worsening, suicidal ideation, and unusual changes in mood or behavior, especially during the first few months of therapy and after any dosage adjustment. Instruct caregivers and patients to immediately notify the prescriber of changes in behavior or suicidal ideation. Consider changing the therapeutic regimen or discontinuing the medication in patients with persistent or worrisome symptoms, especially if these symptoms are severe, abrupt in onset, or were not part of the patient's presenting symptoms. In a pooled analysis of placebo-controlled trials of antidepressants (n = more than 4,400 pediatric patients and 77,000 adult patients), there was an increased risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors in patients 24 years of age and younger receiving an antidepressant versus placebo, with considerable variation in the risk of suicidality among drugs. Short-term studies did not show an increase in the risk of suicidality with antidepressants compared to placebo in adults older than 24 years; there was a reduction with antidepressants compared to placebo in adults aged 65 and older. The difference in absolute risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors across different indications was highest in those with major depression. No suicides occurred in any of the pediatric trials. There were suicides in the adult trials, but the number was not sufficient to reach any conclusion about the drug effect on suicide. It is unknown if the suicidality risk extends to long-term use (i.e., more than 4 months); however, there is substantial evidence from placebo-controlled maintenance trials in adults with depression that the use of antidepressants can delay the recurrence of depression, a known risk factor for suicidal thoughts and behaviors.

If an individual taking duloxetine develops symptoms consistent with mania or hypomania, the medication should be discontinued, and appropriate therapy should be initiated.

    obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate and well-controlled studies on the use of duloxetine during human pregnancy. Animal studies do not identify evidence of teratogenicity, but this data may not be reflective of human outcomes. Consider the risks of untreated conditions during pregnancy as well as the potential risks of fetal exposure. Data from published observational studies have reported that exposure to SSRIs, particularly in the month before obstetric delivery, has been associated with a less than 2-fold increase in the risk of postpartum hemorrhage.     Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero during the third trimester may experience poor neonatal adaptation, resulting in complications such as prolonged hospitalization, respiratory support, and tube feeding upon delivery.     Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use duloxetine with caution during breast-feeding. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for duloxetine, and any potential adverse effects on the breast-fed child from the medication or from the underlying maternal medical condition. Monitor the child for sedation, poor feeding, and poor weight gain. Duloxetine is present in human milk. The disposition of duloxetine was studied in 6 lactating people who were at least 12 weeks postpartum and had elected to wean their infants. The amount of duloxetine in breast milk was approximately 7 mcg/day while they received a dose of 40 mg PO twice daily; the estimated daily infant dose was less than 1% of the maternal dose. The presence of metabolites in breast milk was not examined. A pooled analysis found that maternal use of sertraline, nortriptyline, and paroxetine usually produced undetectable or low drug concentrations in infant serum and these agents may be considered as treatment alternatives during lactation.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Use duloxetine with caution in geriatric adults, as use has resulted in orthostatic hypotension, syncope, and falls, leading to bone fractures and hospitalizations in some instances. The risk of falls is associated with the degree of orthostasis, and any other factors that may increase the likelihood of falling (e.g., age-related risks, concomitant medications with orthostatic effects). Older adults are more likely than younger adults to have a regimen of multiple medications, concomitant illnesses, and gait disturbances that may increase the underlying risk of falls. As with other SNRIs, several cases of hyponatremia and syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) have been reported, and geriatric adults may be at increased risk. Some geriatric adults may experience increased sensitivity to other adverse effects of antidepressants, including duloxetine. The Beers Criteria state that SNRIs are potentially inappropriate medications in older adults with a history of falls or fractures; SNRIs can produce ataxia, impaired psychomotor function, syncope, and additional falls. If an SNRI must be used, consider reducing other CNS-active medications and implement strategies to reduce fall risk. SNRIs may also cause hyponatremia and SIADH; closely monitor sodium concentrations when initiating treatment or changing doses in older adults. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates antidepressant use in long-term care facilities. When used to manage behavior, stabilize mood, or treat a psychiatric disorder, tapering as outlined in the OBRA guidelines should be attempted unless clinically contraindicated. Dosages and durations of treatment used should align with prescribing labels, published literature recommendations, and expert guidelines.

Benzoic Acid; Hyoscyamine; Methenamine; Methylene Blue; Phenyl Salicylate: (Contraindicated) Concurrent use of methylene blue and serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) (e.g., venlafaxine, duloxetine, desvenlafaxine, milnacipran) should generally be avoided due to the potential for serotonin syndrome. Results from an in vitro study indicate that methylene blue is a potent, reversible inhibitor of the monoamine oxidase type A enzyme (MAO-A). MAO-A is responsible for the metabolism of serotonin; therefore, concurrent use of an MAO-A inhibitor with serotonergic agents such as SNRIs may result in a clinically significant interaction. Cases of serotonin syndrome have been reported, primarily following administration of standard infusions of methylene blue (1 to 8 mg/kg) as a visualizing agent in parathyroid surgery, in patients receiving selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, SNRIs, or clomipramine. It is not known if patients receiving other serotonergic psychiatric agents with intravenous methylene blue are at a comparable risk or if methylene blue administered by other routes (e.g., orally, local injection) or in doses less than 1 mg/kg IV can produce a similar outcome. One case report suggests that serotonin toxicity may have occurred post-operatively following administration of standard infusions of methylene blue in a patient receiving duloxetine. The patient experienced disorientation, a mildly elevated temperature, tachycardia, elevated blood pressure, mild agitation, and nystagmus. In a separate case, a patient who had been receiving venlafaxine developed expressive aphasia, confusion, and disinhibition following a methylene blue infusion. The authors concluded that methylene blue toxicity had occurred; however, they did not exclude the possibility of a drug interaction based upon previous reports of an interaction between injectable methylene blue and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). Published interaction reports between intravenously administered methylene blue and serotonergic psychiatric agents have documented symptoms including lethargy, confusion, delirium, agitation, aggression, obtundation, myoclonus, expressive aphasia, hypertonia, pyrexia, elevated blood pressure, seizures, and/or coma. Signs and symptoms of serotonin syndrome include fever, diaphoresis, shivering, myoclonus, tremor, tachycardia, diarrhea, nausea, headache, incoordination, mental status changes (e.g., agitation, confusion), hyperreflexia, seizures, and coma. Results from an in vitro study indicate that methylene blue is a potent, reversible inhibitor of the monoamine oxidase type A enzyme (MAO-A). MAO-A is responsible for the metabolism of serotonin; therefore, concurrent use of an MAO-A inhibitor with serotonergic agents such as SNRIs may result in a clinically significant interaction. Cases of serotonin syndrome have been reported, primarily following administration of standard infusions of methylene blue (1 to 8 mg/kg) as a visualizing agent in parathyroid surgery, in patients receiving selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, SNRIs, or clomipramine. One case report suggests that serotonin toxicity may have occurred post-operatively following administration of standard infusions of methylene blue in a patient receiving duloxetine. The patient experienced disorientation, a mildly elevated temperature, tachycardia, elevated blood pressure, mild agitation, and nystagmus. In a separate case, a patient who had been receiving venlafaxine developed expressive aphasia, confusion, and disinhibition following a methylene blue infusion. The authors concluded that methylene blue toxicity had occurred; however, they did not exclude the possibility of a drug interaction based upon previous reports of an interaction between injectable methylene blue and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  In clinical trials, duloxetine safety was evaluated in roughly 2400 patients with depression and roughly 1100 patients with diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN). The maximum dose evaluated was 120 mg/day for up to 12 months of exposure. Roughly 10% of patients being treated for depression (n=1139) stopped duloxetine treatment due to an adverse event (vs. 4% receiving placebo; n=777). Of patients being treated for DPN (n=568), approximately 14% stopped therapy due to an adverse event (vs. 7% on placebo; n=223). In clinical trials for fibromyalgia, 19.5% of patients discontinued duloxetine treatment due to an adverse event versus 11.8% of those on placebo. For all indications, nausea (1—3%) was the most frequently reported drug-related adverse event resulting in discontinuation of treatment.
 
Muscle cramps and myalgia (4% vs 1—3% placebo) have been noted during use of duloxetine. Muscle spasm and restless legs syndrome have been reported during post-marketing use of duloxetine; however, the frequencies are unknown and causality to the drug has not been established. Rigors has been reported in at least 1% of patients receiving duloxetine.
 
Treatment with duloxetine has been shown to worsen glycemic control in some patients with diabetes. During long-term studies (up to 52 weeks), the mean blood glucose levels increased by 12 mg/dl in the duloxetine group and decreased by 11.5 mg/dl in the routine care group. In addition, the HbA1c increased by 0.5% in those treated with duloxetine compared to 0.2% in those receiving routine care. Hypoglycemia with increased appetite has been reported in at least 1% of patients. Hyperglycemia has been documented in post-marketing reports, although the overall incidence is unknown.
 
Centrally-mediated ADRs that resulted in therapy discontinuation in clinical trials included dizziness (1.6% duloxetine vs. 0.4% placebo). Dizziness ranged from 6—17% in a dose-related manner. In clinical trials for fibromyalgia, dizziness occurred in 11% of those on duloxetine versus 7% receiving placebo.
 
In duloxetine clinical trials, tremor was not reported with placebo or at the 20 mg/day dose, but with 60 mg/day or 60 mg twice daily the incidence of tremor was 1 and 5%, respectively. In clinical trials for fibromyalgia, tremors occurred in 4% of those on duloxetine versus 1% receiving placebo.
 
Central nervous system (CNS) disorders in duloxetine clinical trials with rates compared to placebo included: asthenia (2—8% vs. 1%); headache (14% vs. 10%); and anxiety (3% vs. 2%). In clinical trials for fibromyalgia, headache occurred in 20% of those on duloxetine versus 12% receiving placebo.
 
In clinical trials for fibromyalgia, psychiatric effects occurring in at least 2% of patients receiving duloxetine and more commonly than placebo included agitation (6% vs 2%), sleep disorder (3% vs 2%), and abnormal dreams (3% vs 1%). Nightmares and irritability have been reported in at least 1% of patients.
 
Extrapyramidal disorder (unspecified), trismus, aggression, anger, and hallucinations have been documented in post-marketing reports. The overall incidences of these effects are unknown, and causality to duloxetine has not been established. Aggression and anger have been most noted early during treatment or after treatment discontinuation.
 
Centrally-mediated adverse reactions that resulted in therapy discontinuation in clinical trials included fatigue/somnolence/drowsiness (2.7% vs. 0%). Fatigue/somnolence was noted in general ADRs as occurring in up to 21% of patients in a dose-related manner. Insomnia was a common ADR, reported in 8—13% of patients (6—7% placebo). In controlled trials for the treatment of generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), centrally-mediated adverse effects which occurred in at least 5% of patients receiving duloxetine, and with an incidence at least twice that of the placebo group, included fatigue, somnolence, and insomnia. In patients being treated for stress incontinence, fatigue was among the most commonly reported side effects. In clinical trials for fibromyalgia, CNS effects occurring in at least 2% of patients receiving duloxetine and more commonly than placebo included insomnia (16% vs 10%), fatigue (15% vs 8%), and somnolence (11% vs 3%).
 
Hypoesthesia, lethargy, and nervousness have been reported in at least 1% of patients receiving duloxetine. Tinnitus and poor quality sleep were reported in 0.1—1% of patients during pre-marketing and post-marketing clinical trials. Tinnitus has also been reported upon treatment discontinuation. Gait disturbance has occurred rarely ( 20 times the upper limit of normal (ULN). In clinical trials, elevation of liver enzymes to > 3 times the ULN occurred in roughly 1.1% of duloxetine-treated patients, vs. 0.3% of placebo-treated patients. There appears to be a dose-response relationship with elevation of ALT and AST. Duloxetine should be avoided in patients with substantial alcohol use, alcoholism, hepatic necrosis and/or chronic liver disease. Duloxetine is not recommended for treatment in any patient with any degree of hepatic impairment or hepatic failure.
 
Pharyngitis has been reported frequently during duloxetine administration (9% vs. 5% placebo). In clinical trials for fibromyalgia, cough was reported in 4% of those on duloxetine versus 3% of those receiving placebo.
 
In clinical trials with duloxetine for major depression and generalized anxiety disorder, reported ophthalmic disorders include blurred vision (3% vs 2% placebo) and mydriasis. Glaucoma has been documented in post-marketing reports. In clinical trials for fibromyalgia, blurred vision occurred in 2% of those on duloxetine versus 1% receiving placebo.
 
Hyponatremia has been reported during administration of duloxetine, with some cases possibly the result of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) secretion. Serum sodium concentrations lower than 110 mmol/L have been reported. Although specific risk factors have not been identified, most cases have occurred in the elderly, those taking diuretics, or in patients with hypovolemia. Signs and symptoms of hyponatremia include headache, difficulty concentrating, memory impairment, confusion, weakness, and unsteadiness. More severe cases may cause hallucinations, syncope, seizures, coma, respiratory arrest, and death. Discontinuation of duloxetine may be necessary in those with symptomatic hyponatremia.
 
All effective antidepressants can precipitate mania in predisposed individuals suffering from depression. If mania occurs, the antidepressant should be held and appropriate therapy to treat the manic symptoms initiated.
 
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome-like symptoms have occurred during the use of serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), although the incidence is unknown. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a potentially life-threatening complication that may include symptoms such as hyperthermia, muscle rigidity, autonomic instability, and mental status changes. Concurrent use of antipsychotics or other dopamine antagonists with SNRIs may increase the potential for NMS-like reactions to occur. If an NMS-like reaction becomes evident during treatment, the SNRI and any nonessential antidopaminergic agents should be discontinued and appropriate medical treatment should be initiated.
 
Serotonin syndrome has been reported during use of SNRIs alone, during concurrent use of other medications known to increase serotonin levels, or during SNRI overdose. Serotonin syndrome is a range of signs and symptoms that can rarely, in its most severe form, resemble neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Symptoms may include nausea/vomiting, sedation, dizziness, diaphoresis (sweating), facial flush, mental status changes, myoclonia, restlessness, shivering, and hypertension. If serotonin syndrome becomes evident during treatment, the SNRI and any other serotonergic agents should be discontinued and appropriate medical treatment should be initiated. A drug discontinuation syndrome, resembling serotonin syndrome, has been reported in neonates exposed to SSRIs or SNRIs late in the third trimester.
 
Suicidal ideation has been reported in patients participating in clinical trials with antidepressants. A causal role has been established for antidepressants in inducing suicidality in children and young adults (18 to 24 years of age) during the early phase of treatment. Symptoms including aggressiveness, akathisia (psychomotor restlessness), agitation, anxiety, insomnia, irritability, hostility, mania, hypomania, impulsivity, and panic attacks have been reported in adult and pediatric patients being treated with antidepressants for major depressive disorder as well as for other indications, both psychiatric and nonpsychiatric. Although a causal link between the emergence of such symptoms and either the worsening of depression and/or the emergence of suicidal impulses has not been established, there is concern that such symptoms may represent precursors to emerging suicidality. Consideration should be given to changing the therapeutic regimen, including possibly discontinuing the medication, in patients whose depression is persistently worse or who are experiencing emergent suicidality or symptoms that might be precursors to worsening depression or suicidal ideation. Changing or stopping the therapeutic regimen may be especially important if these symptoms are severe, abrupt in onset, or were not part of the patients presenting symptoms. Many of these adverse symptoms have been reported in clinical trials and have not always been associated with suicidality. Rapid discontinuation of many antidepressants may also result in some of these adverse CNS events.
 
Platelet dysfunction (i.e., impaired platelet aggregation) may occur during treatment with serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) due to platelet serotonin depletion, possibly increasing the risk of a bleeding complication (e.g., gastrointestinal bleeding, ecchymoses, epistaxis, hematomas, petechiae, hemorrhage). During duloxetine clinical trials, an increased tendency to bruise was reported in 0.1—1% of patients. Ecchymosis was reported in less than 0.1% of patients. An increased risk of bleeding complications is possible in patients receiving antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications concurrently with duloxetine (see Drug Interactions). Gynecological bleeding has been reported during post-marketing use; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
Withdrawal symptoms have been reported with abrupt discontinuation or tapering of duloxetine and have included abnormal dreams, headache, insomnia, dizziness, nausea/vomiting, paresthesia, irritability, fatigue, diarrhea, anxiety, vertigo, hyperhidrosis, and a small increase in recumbent heart rate. Seizures have occurred upon treatment discontinuation during post-marketing use of duloxetine; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.
 
A neonatal abstinence syndrome has been reported in infants exposed to serotonergic agents in utero. After birth, symptoms consistent with withdrawal (i.e., poor feeding, hypoglycemia, hypothermia, lethargy or irritability, vomiting, etc.) were noted. Such complications can arise immediately upon delivery. Other symptoms have included respiratory distress, cyanosis, apnea, seizures, temperature instability, feeding difficulty, hypotonia, hypertonia, hyperreflexia, tremor, jitteriness, and constant crying. Serum concentrations of the serotonergic agent were measurable in the infants affected. Several other symptoms (bloody stools, necrotizing enterocolitis) may have been attributable to rebound platelet activation on withdrawal of the exposure to the SSRI. Neonatal symptoms generally improved over several days. Additionally, a cohort study of 55 women revealed that 22% (12/55) of neonates exposed to an SSRI in the third trimester had complications requiring treatment or extended hospitalization compared with 6% in comparison groups. Complications included respiratory distress (n=9), hypoglycemia (n=2), and jaundice (n=1). The incidence of prematurity in the third trimester SSRI group was significant at 20% vs. 3.7% of controls. These features are consistent with either a direct toxic effect of serotonergic agents, or, possibly, a drug discontinuation syndrome. It should be noted that, in some cases, the clinical picture is consistent with serotonin syndrome. When treating a pregnant woman with an SSRI or other serotonergic agent during the third trimester, the physician should carefully consider the potential risks and benefits of treatment. If clinically feasible, and taking the drug half-life into consideration, tapering of the serotonergic agent prior to delivery may be considered as an alternative. A case-controlled epidemiologic report has been published that suggests a significant association between maternal use of SSRIs after 20 weeks of pregnancy and the development of persistent pulmonary hypertension (PPHN) of the newborn (odds ratio (OR) 5.1; 95% CI, 1.9—13.3). The study population consisted of 377 women whose infants had PPHN and 836 matched control women and their infants. There was no increased risk of PPHN when SSRI use was restricted to the first half of the pregnancy (OR = 0.3; 95% CI, 0.1—1.1). Additionally, the use of non-SSRI antidepressant drugs at any time during pregnancy was not associated with an increased risk of PPHN. The SSRIs that were used by women > 20 weeks gestation in the study included fluoxetine, paroxetine, and sertraline. However, the numbers were too small to permit examination of the effects of dose size, specific SSRI used, or reduction of the length of exposure before delivery.
 
Symptoms that have been reported in association with duloxetine overdose include serotonin syndrome, somnolence, vomiting, and seizures.

Description
  Text
  Duloxetine is an oral serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) used for the treatment of major depressive disorder, diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain, and chronic musculoskeletal pain (e.g., chronic low back pain and chronic pain due to osteoarthritis) in adults, fibromyalgia in adults and adolescents 13 years and older, and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) in adults and pediatric patients 7 years and older. Efficacy was not demonstrated in clinical trials of pediatric patients with major depressive disorder (MDD). Duloxetine has demonstrated a modest benefit in treating female stress urinary incontinence (SUI) and is used off-label in the U.S. for this condition; the drug is approved for SUI treatment in several European countries. Product labels for all antidepressants contain a boxed warning related to an increased risk of suicidality in children, adolescents, and young adults when treating depression or other conditions; monitor for worsening and emergence of suicidal thoughts and behaviors in all patients being treated with duloxetine.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Duloxetine inhibits the reuptake of both serotonin (5-HT) and norepinephrine (NE). The exact mechanism of its antidepressant, central pain inhibitory, and anxiolytic effects are not known, but is thought to be related to the increase of serotonin and norepinephrine. The potency of duloxetine to inhibit reuptake is greater for 5-HT than for NE, however, inhibition of the NE reuptake process occurs at a considerably lower dose than for venlafaxine, a similar 5-HT/NE reuptake inhibitor. The ability of duloxetine to affect NE reuptake appears to occur at doses of at least 60 mg/day. Duloxetine also inhibits 5-HT uptake in human platelets, and this effect has been associated with potentiation of bleeding in some reports. Similar to the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), duloxetine does not exert significant activity at dopaminergic, histaminergic, muscarinic, alpha-1 adrenergic receptors, 5-HT1A, 5-HT1B, 5-HT1D, 5-HT2A, 5-HT2C, or opioid receptors in vivo or in vitro. Duloxetine does not inhibit monoamine oxidase (MAO). The major circulating metabolites of duloxetine do not appear to contribute to its pharmacologic activity. In the treatment of stress urinary incontinence, inhibition of 5-HT and NE reuptake by duloxetine increases the activity of the bladder detrusor muscle and the urethral sphincter thereby promoting continence.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Duloxetine is well-absorbed following oral administration. There is a median 2-hour lag before absorption begins, with Cmax occurring at 5 to 6 hours post-dose. When administered in the evening, there is a 3-hour delay in absorption and a one-third increase in apparent clearance compared to a morning dose; however, it does not appear that duloxetine pharmacodynamics are altered. Steady-state concentrations typically occur after 3 days of dosing.
Delayed-released capsules: Food, while not affecting Cmax, can delay the time to reach peak concentration by 4 hours and may decrease the AUC by 10%.
Delayed-release sprinkle capsules: When administered with a high-fat, high-calorie meal, the Cmax and AUC of duloxetine were not affected; however, the Tmax of duloxetine hydrochloride was delayed by approximately 1.7 hours. When duloxetine delayed-release sprinkle capsules were administered under fasting conditions to healthy adults by sprinkling the entire contents on 1 tablespoon (15 mL) of applesauce, the Tmax, Cmax, and AUC of duloxetine were not significantly affected.

    Description

    Description
    After a single 60 mg dose of duloxetine in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) receiving chronic, intermittent hemodialysis, the Cmax and AUC values were doubled compared to patients with normal renal function. The AUCs of the major circulating metabolites were approximately 7- to 9-fold higher and would be expected to increase further with multiple dosing. Population pharmacokinetic studies suggest that mild to moderate renal impairment (creatinine clearance of 30 mL/minute or more) has no significant effect on duloxetine apparent clearance.

  Text
  Duloxetine is administered orally. It is distributed widely throughout the body, with a Vd of 1,640 L. Duloxetine is highly protein-bound (greater than 90%), primarily to albumin and alpha1-acid glycoprotein. Plasma protein binding is not affected by renal or hepatic impairment. The drug undergoes extensive hepatic oxidation to numerous inactive metabolites with only 3% of the parent drug remaining in the plasma. Duloxetine is metabolized by CYP2D6 and CYP1A2, with a moderate potential for drug interactions involving CYP2D6. Only trace amounts of unchanged duloxetine are present in the urine; 70% of the dose appears as metabolites in the urine and 20% is excreted in the feces. The mean elimination half-life is 12 hours (range: 8 to 17 hours).
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP450) isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2D6, CYP1A2
Duloxetine is metabolized by CYP2D6 and CYP1A2. Duloxetine is a CYP1A2 inhibitor and a moderate CYP2D6 inhibitor. In two clinical studies, the average increase in AUC of the CYP1A2 substrate theophylline was 7% (range: 1% to 15%) and 20% (range: 13% to 27%) when coadministered with duloxetine 60 mg twice daily. When duloxetine was administered at a dose of 60 mg twice daily with a single 50 mg dose of desipramine, a CYP2D6 substrate, the AUC of desipramine increased 3-fold.
