Drug Class: Hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated is used to confer immunity against hepatitis A, with primary immunization typically administered in children 12 to 23 months of age.

Routes: intramuscular; Common adult doses: Adults 19 years and older: 1 mL/dose IM.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Prior to administration, health care personnel should inform the patient, parent, guardian, or responsible adult of the vaccine's benefits and risks. This should include the provision of the vaccine information statement from the manufacturer. The responsible adult should report any adverse reaction following vaccine administration to the health care provider. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services has established a Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) to accept all reports of suspected adverse events after the administration of any vaccine. This includes, but is not limited to, the reporting of events required by the National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act of 1986. The toll-free number for VAERS is (800) 822—7967.
 
Hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated is contraindicated in patients with a previous or known hypersensitivity to any component of the vaccine. Patients who have had a hypersensitivity reaction to any hepatitis A-containing vaccine should not be vaccinated with hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated. Also, patients with latex hypersensitivity may not be appropriate candidates for VAQTA or Havrix receipt. Both the vial stopper and the syringe plunger of VAQTA contain dry, natural latex rubber. The syringe plunger and tip of Havrix contain dry, natural latex rubber. Patients with albumin hypersensitivity may not be appropriate candidates for VAQTA; a 50 U dose of the vaccine contains less than 0.001 mcg of bovine albumin. Also, neomycin sulfate is used in the manufacturing of Havrix. Administer Havrix with caution to patients with a history of neomycin hypersensitivity or aminoglycoside hypersensitivity. Antibiotics are not used in the manufacturing process for VAQTA. Anaphylaxis has been reported with the use of the hepatitis A, inactivated vaccine. As with any biologic product, the prescriber or health care professional should take precautions to prevent allergic reactions. The health care professional should have immediate availability of epinephrine (1:1000) injection and other agents used in the treatment of severe anaphylaxis in the event of a serious allergic reaction to the vaccine.
 
For known exposures to HAV, immune globulin for intramuscular administration (IGIM) should be given in addition to the hepatitis A vaccine in accordance with the ACIP recommendations. Hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated may not prevent HAV in those with an unrecognized illness at the time of vaccination. Additionally, vaccination may not prevent HAV if adequate antibody titers are not achieved.
 
Do not give hepatitis A vaccine via intravenous administration. Patients with thrombocytopenia, vitamin K deficiency, a coagulopathy (e.g., hemophilia), or receiving anticoagulant therapy should be monitored closely when given hepatitis A vaccine because bleeding can occur at the IM injection site. The vaccine should be given only if the potential benefits clearly outweigh the risk of administration. If the decision is made to administer the vaccine in such persons, the vaccine should be given with caution. Steps should be taken to avoid the risk of bleeding and hematoma formation following intramuscular administration.
 
Any serious active infection including a fever is reason for delaying use of the hepatitis A vaccine except when in the opinion of the prescriber, withholding the vaccination entails a greater risk. Caution and appropriate care should be exercised in administering the vaccine to individuals with severe cardiac disease or to others in whom a febrile or systemic reaction could pose a significant risk.
 
Patients with immunosuppression may respond to hepatitis A vaccine with lower antibody titers than non-immunosuppressed patients. Lower antibody titers are particularly a concern in patients receiving chemotherapy or in patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. Hepatitis A vaccine using a standard dose and schedule is immunogenic for children and adults with HIV infection. All 32 HIV-infected children with a mean CD4 count of 1483 cells/mm3 (range, 572—2717) had protective antibody concentrations (> 20 mIU/ml) after the second Havrix dose, which was given 6 months after the first dose. Patients with CD4 counts greater than 300 cells/mm3 respond nearly as well as persons who are not immunocompromised. Adults with lower CD4 counts are less likely to acquire protective antibody concentrations. Protective antibody concentrations developed after vaccination in 61—87% of HIV-infected adults. In a study, response to vaccination was directly related to the CD4 cell count at vaccination: the higher the CD4 cell count, the higher the likelihood of detectable anti-HAV IgG. Patients with a CD4 cell count less than 200 cells/mm3 were 16 times more likely to be nonresponders, and a CD4 cell count of 200—500 cells/mm3 was associated with a 2.5 increased risk of non-response. The relationship between the CD4 count and vaccination response was independent of the nadir CD4 cell count and viral load. Thus, the data suggest that patients will respond to vaccination after immunologic reconstitution with highly active antiretroviral therapy. According to the guidelines for the prevention and treatment of opportunistic infections in HIV-infected adolescents and adults, assess the IgG antibody response to the hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated one month after vaccination, and revaccinate nonresponders.
 
Both inactivated hepatitis A vaccine products (e.g., Havrix and VAQTA) are not indicated for use in infants (< 1 year of age). Safety and effectiveness has not been established in this patient group.
 
Hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated products (e.g., Havrix and VAQTA) are classified in FDA pregnancy risk category C. Neither vaccine has been well studied in pregnant women. The ability of the vaccine to affect reproductive capacity or cause fetal harm is unknown. Administration of either vaccine to a pregnant woman should be done only if clearly needed.
 
Hepatitis A vaccine products (e.g., Havrix and VAQTA) should be used with caution in women who are breast-feeding because it is unknown if the vaccine is excreted in breast milk. However, hepatitis A vaccine is an inactivated vaccine and should be harmless. The ACIP states that breast-feeding should not be considered a contraindication for immunization with inactivated or killed vaccines.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Use of this vaccine is contraindicated in individuals with a neomycin hypersensitivity; the vaccines contain a residual amount of neomycin from the manufacturing process. Individuals who develop symptoms suggestive of hypersensitivity should not receive further injections of the vaccine. Further, individuals with latex hypersensitivity may not be appropriate candidates for vaccination with Vaqta as the vial stopper contains dry natural latex rubber that may cause allergic reactions.
    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    Deferral of hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated administration may be appropriate for a patient with a moderate or severe acute illness with or without fever. The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices recommends that vaccinations be delayed during the course of a moderate or severe acute febrile illness and administered after the acute phase of illness has resolved. All vaccines can be administered to persons with minor illnesses such as diarrhea, mild upper-respiratory infection with or without low-grade fever, or other low-grade febrile illness. Vaccinate persons with moderate or severe febrile illness as soon as they have recovered from the acute phase of the illness.
    Headline
    immunosuppression
    General Information
    Individuals with immunosuppression may respond to hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated with lower antibody titers than non-immunosuppressed individuals. Immunosuppressed persons may include individuals with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection; severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID); hypogammaglobulinemia; agammaglobulinemia; altered immune states due to generalized neoplastic disease; or an immune system compromised by radiation therapy or drug therapy (e.g., chemotherapy or corticosteroid therapy with greater than physiologic doses). Short-term (less than 2 weeks) corticosteroid therapy or intra-articular, bursal, or tendon injections with corticosteroids should not be immunosuppressive. Individuals vaccinated with hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated within 2 weeks before starting immunosuppressive therapy or while receiving immunosuppressive therapy should be considered unvaccinated and should be revaccinated at least 3 months after therapy is discontinued if immune competence has been restored. Lower antibody titers are particularly a concern in individuals with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, as the CD4 count at the time of vaccination has been associated with reduced development of anti-HAV IgG antibodies; however, data suggest that individuals will respond to vaccination after immunologic reconstitution with highly active antiretroviral therapy. In a study, response to vaccination was directly related to the CD4 count at vaccination: the higher the CD4 count, the higher the likelihood of detectable anti-HAV IgG. Individuals with a CD4 count less than 200 cells/mm3 were 16 times more likely to be nonresponders, and a CD4 cell count of 200 to 500 cells/mm3 was associated with a 2.5 increased risk of non-response. The relationship between the CD4 count and vaccination response was independent of the nadir CD4 cell count and viral load. The guidelines for the prevention and treatment of opportunistic infections in adults and adolescents with HIV recommend vaccinating individuals with a CD4 count less than 200 cells/mm3 if they have ongoing risks for hepatitis A. For HIV individuals without risk factors, waiting to vaccinate until CD4 count is 200 cells/mm3 or higher is an option. Assess the antibody response (total or anti-HAV IgG) to the hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated 1 to 2 months after completing the series. If negative, revaccinate, preferably after the CD4 count is 200 cells/mm3 or higher.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    No adequate and well-controlled studies have been conducted with the hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated during pregnancy. In pre- and post-licensure clinical studies and post-approval reports, pregnant individuals who were administered hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated had rates of miscarriage and major birth defects that were consistent with estimated background rates. Administration of inactivated or recombinant virus vaccines during pregnancy has not resulted in adverse effects on the fetus. It is recommended to administer a vaccine during pregnancy when the likelihood of disease exposure is high, when the potential infection would cause harm to the pregnant person or fetus, and when the vaccine is unlikely to cause harm.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    There is no information regarding the presence of hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated in human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effects on milk production. Inactivated and recombinant vaccines pose no risk to breast-feeding individuals or their children. Additionally, breast-feeding does not adversely affect immunization; limited data suggest breast-feeding may enhance the immune response to certain vaccine antigens.

High-dose corticosteroid therapy is generally defined as a dose of at least 20 mg/day of prednisone or equivalent (or 2 mg/kg/day for patients weighing less than 10 kg) for at least 14 consecutive days.   Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Most adverse reactions to hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated that have been reported are mild in nature and are self-limiting. Most adverse reactions did not last for more than 24 hours. The most frequently reported adverse reaction was an injection site reaction, manifested as induration, redness, and swelling. Description
  Text
  Hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated is used to confer immunity against hepatitis A, with primary immunization typically administered in children 12 to 23 months of age. The vaccine may also be used in children and adolescents desiring immunity, prior to expected exposure to the hepatitis A virus, or for postexposure prophylaxis as an alternative to immune globulin. There are currently 2 different products available; both require the administration of a series of at least 2 doses, although substantial protection is afforded by the initial (i.e., primary) dose.  Havrix was FDA-approved in 1995 and Vaqta was FDA-approved in 1996.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Injection of hepatitis A vaccine produces antibodies that confer protection against hepatitis A infection. Stimulation of specific antibodies takes place without producing any disease symptoms. During the course of natural infection with the hepatitis A virus, the initial antibody response is predominantly of the IgM class. This response lasts for several months, but during convalescence antibodies of the IgG class become dominant. Patients with anti-HAV of the IgG class are immune to reinfection. The IgG antibodies remain detectable indefinitely. Two years after immunization with hepatitis A vaccine IgG levels remained relatively high in the serum of immunized patients. The duration of protection from a course of hepatitis A vaccine is as yet unknown. Long term follow-up studies will determine the necessity for booster doses of HAV.

    Description

    Description
    Children and Adolescents 2 to 19 years
Seroconversion was examined in children and adolescents (n = 314) immunized with 2 doses of hepatitis A vaccine (Havrix, 0.5 mL/dose) 6 months apart. Two weeks after the initial dose, seroconversion occurred in 91.6% to 96.1% of patients. After 2 doses, 100% of patients converted (geometric mean titers = 2,495 to 3,644 milli-international units/mL). Combined data from 11 randomized trials (n = more than 2,000) indicated that hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated (Vaqta) is highly immunogenic. Among patients who received a 0.5 mL dose, 97% were seropositive 1 month after the first dose and 100% after the second dose when given 6 months apart, with geometric mean titers of 43 milli-international units/mL and 10,007 milli-international units/mL, respectively.
 
Children 11 to 25 months
Immunogenicity to the hepatitis A vaccine (Havrix, 0.5 mL/dose) was similar among children 11 to 13 months of age, children 15 to 18 months of age, and children 23 to 25 months of age who received 2 doses of the vaccine 6 months apart. One month after the second dose, the geometric mean titer was 1,461 milli-international units/mL for children 11 to 13 months of age at the time of the first dose, 1,635 milli-international units/mL for children 15 to 18 months of age at the time of the first dose, and 1,911 milli-international units/mL for children 23 to 25 months of age at the time of the first dose. Vaccination response rates were 99% to 100%.
  Text
  Hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated is administered intramuscularly. The distribution, metabolism, and excretion of the vaccine have not been well defined. The lowest anti-HAV antibody titer needed to confer protection is unknown. Antibody levels of 10 to 33 milli-international units/mL, using different assays, have been proposed as protective thresholds; because no absolute protective level has been defined, postvaccination studies commonly use greater than 10 milli-international units/mL as the minimum protective level. In individuals aged 2 to 18 years, 97% to 100% had protective antibody levels 1 month after the first dose, and 100% had protective levels 1 month after a booster dose. Although the total duration of protection is unknown, 100% of children at least 2 years of age and adolescents who received 2 doses of Vaqta 6 months apart had anti-HAV antibody titers of at least 10 milli-international units/mL for at least 10 years, with GMTs of 819, 505, and 574 milli-international units/mL at 2.5 to 3.5 years, 5 to 6 years, and 10 years, respectively. Among adults who received 2 doses of Vaqta 6 months apart, antibody response persisted for at least 6 years; at least 99.1% had detectable anti-HAV antibody levels of 10 milli-international units/mL or more through 6 years, with GMTs ranging from 605 to 1,734 milli-international units/mL. HAV antibodies (immunoglobulin G [IgG] anti-HAV) produced in response to HAV infection persist for life and protect against reinfection; IgG anti-HAV produced after vaccination confer long-term immunity.

Administration
  General Information
  For storage information, see the specific product information within the How Supplied section.
Verify immunization status in the state or local immunization information system in addition to the health care electronic record. If this vaccine has been previously given, question the patient, parent, or guardian about any symptoms or signs of an adverse reaction.
Advise individuals on the benefits and risks of the vaccine. Provide a copy of the Vaccine Information Statement (VIS).
Record manufacturer, lot number, administration date, and the name and address of the person administering the vaccine.
Ensure supportive care, including epinephrine 1 mg/mL injection, is readily accessible in the event of a clinically significant hypersensitivity reaction.
Complete a Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) report if adverse events have been identified. The VAERS toll-free information line is 1-800-822-7967 and email address is info@vaers.org.

      Preparation
The vaccine should be used as supplied; no dilution or reconstitution is necessary.

Off-label storage information: According to a 2007 published article, storage of Havrix (GlaxoSmithKline) at room temperature for up to 72 hours is acceptable, and Vaqta (Merck) can be stored at 37 degrees C (98.6 degrees F) for up to 12 months. Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available; no dosage adjustment is expected to be needed for hepatitis A vaccine. Consult current immunization schedules for recommended vaccines in young dialysis patients. Guidance notes that chronic renal disease can cause altered immunocompetence; non-live vaccines can be administered safely to persons with altered immunocompetence, and routine vaccines are likely effective in chronic renal disease. However, vaccine effectiveness may be reduced, and some vaccines may need to be repeated if given during significant immunosuppression. Hepatitis A vaccine may be used in renal dialysis patients or patients with chronic renal disease if otherwise indicated.
