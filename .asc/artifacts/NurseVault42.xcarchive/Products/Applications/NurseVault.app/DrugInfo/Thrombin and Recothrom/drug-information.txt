Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  As with other products derived from or purified with human blood components, it is possible that recombinant human thrombin (Evithrom™) may be contaminated with hepatitis or another viral or bacterial infection including Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD). The risk of transmitting an infectious agent has been reduced by screening plasma donors for prior exposure to certain viruses, by testing for the presence of certain current virus infections, and by inactivating and removing certain viruses. Despite these measures, such products can still potentially transmit disease. There is also the possibility that unknown infectious agents may be present in such products. All infections thought to be caused by human thrombin should be reported by the physician or other health care professional to ETHICON Customer Support Center at 877—384—4266.The use of topical bovine thrombin preparations has occasionally been associated with abnormalities in hemostasis ranging from asymptomatic alterations in laboratory determinations, including the prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), to severe bleeding or thrombosis (e.g., thromboembolic disease), which rarely have been fatal. These hemostatic effects appear to be related to the formation of antibodies against bovine thrombin and/or factor V, which in some cases may cross react with human factor V, potentially resulting in factor V deficiency. Repeated exposure to topical bovine thrombin increases the likelihood of antibody formation. An expert in coagulation disorders should be consulted if a patient exhibits abnormal coagulation parameters, abnormal bleeding, or abnormal thrombosis following the use of topical thrombin. Any interventions should consider the immunologic basis of this condition. Patients with antibodies to bovine thrombin preparations should not be reexposed to these products. In addition, thrombin should not be used to treat severe or brisk arterial bleeding.When used topically for hemostasis, thrombin is contraindicated for parenteral administration (including intravenous administration). Care should be taken to avoid entry into large blood vessels because intravascular clots can prove fatal. Thrombin is increasingly being used off-label as an intravascular injection to thrombose pseudoaneurysms via ultrasound-guided percutaneous injection; thrombin is injected directly into the pseudoaneurysm. Care should be taken to administer thrombin only into the pseudoaneurysm and to avoid systemic accidental exposure; thromboembolism with extremity ischemia has been reported after percutaneous injection of thrombin into pseudoaneurysms. Mechanisms to prevent systemic exposure and to thus prevent thromboembolism include minimizing the volume of thrombin injected and injecting the thrombin away from the neck of the pseudoaneurysm. Additionally, some authors advocate using a more dilute concentration of 100 units/ml to minimize the amount of thrombin available systemically should accidental exposure occur. Thrombin is available as a bovine derived source (Thrombin-JMI®), a human source from pooled plasma (Evithrom™), and from recombinant DNA technology (Recothrom™). All three thrombin products are contraindicated in patients with a known thrombin hypersensitivity. In addition, all three thrombin products contain mannitol and should not be used in patients with mannitol hypersensitivity. Bovine-derived thrombin (e.g., Thrombin-JMI®) is contraindicated in patients with a known bovine protein hypersensitivity. Human thrombin (e.g., Evithrom™) is made from pooled human plasma and is contraindicated in patients known to have hypersensitivity to human blood or blood products including albumin hypersensitivity. Recombinant human thrombin (Recothrom™) is derived from chinese hamster ovary host-cell protein, and is contraindicated in patients with a known hamster protein hypersensitivity. There also is the potential for an allergic reaction if recombinant thrombin is used in patients with a known hypersensitivity to snake proteins. Hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylactoid reactions, have been rarely reported following topical application and percutaneous injection of thrombin in the treatment of pseudoaneurysms. The risk of such reactions may be higher in those patients previously exposed to thrombin (topically or via intravascular injection). In those patients with a history of previous exposure, a skin-prick test to rule out hypersensitivity prior to intravascular injection is recommended.The use of intravascular thrombin off-label for the treatment of pseudoaneurysms has not been extensively studied in children or infants. In one report, thromboembolism and ischemia of the brachial artery requiring immediate thrombectomy occurred in a 10-month old infant. It is thought that the small size of the underlying artery may have played a role in the thrombotic event. However, successful treatment of femoral pseudoaneurysm have been reported in 3 children (ages 8 months, 13 years, and 17 years); additionally, successful thrombosis of a brachial artery pseudoaneurysm in a 3-week old infant and an epigastric artery pseudoaneurysm in a 15-year old child have been reported. When performing percutaneous injections into infants or small children the use of a small syringe, such as a tuberculin syringe, to slowly inject thrombin into the pseudoaneurysm is recommended.During clinical trials of human thrombin, 63 patients >= 65 years of age received Evithrom™. No differences in efficacy or safety were noted between elderly and younger patients, although greater susceptibility of elderly patients to adverse reactions cannot be ruled out. During clinical trials of recombinant human thrombin (Recothrom™), 38% of patients were over 65 years of age and 16% were over 75 years of age. No differences in safety or efficacy were observed between elderly and younger patients.Thrombin is classified as pregnancy risk category C. It is not known if thrombin has any untoward effect on the fetus. Thrombin should only be given to a pregnant woman where clearly indicated.Topical thrombin has no specific precautions for use in breast-feeding women.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Intravascular clotting is the most dangerous possible adverse reaction. When used topically, care must be taken not to allow entry of thrombin into any large blood vessel; thrombosis can occur and could prove fatal (see also discussion on formation of antibodies below). Rarely thromboembolism causing extremity ischemia distal to the site of injection has been reported after intravascular injection of thrombin; care should be taken to avoid entry of thrombin to areas outside of the pseudoaneurysm. Peripheral pulses and ankle-brachial index should be monitored before and after intravascular injection; changes in these parameters post-injection may indicate thromboembolism. If ischemic thromboembolism occurs, intra-arterial thrombolytics should be administered; some minor thromboembolisms may resolve without intervention. The data regarding the use of intravascular thrombin in children is limited; successful thrombosis of the brachial, femoral, and epigastric arteries has been reported. In contrast, thromboembolism of the brachial artery requiring an emergent thrombectomy occurred in a 10-month old infant; the authors postulate that the small size of the underlying artery may have played a role in thromboembolism for this patient. The use of topical bovine thrombin preparations has occasionally been associated with abnormalities in hemostasis ranging from asymptomatic alterations in laboratory parameters (e.g., aPTT, INR) to severe bleeding or thrombosis that have sometimes been fatal. These abnormalities appear to be related to antibody formation against bovine thrombin and/or factor V/Va, which in some cases may react to human factor V possibly resulting in factor V deficiency. Baseline elevated antibody levels to bovine coagulation proteins have been observed most frequently in patients with a prior history of a surgical procedure frequently associated with bovine thrombin use. During a clinical study of patients receiving either bovine thrombin or recombinant human thrombin, the development of antibodies to bovine thrombin, bovine factor V/Va, human thrombin, and human factor V/Va at baseline and 5 weeks post surgery was evaluated. In those patients treated with bovine thrombin, 7.94% developed antibodies to bovine thrombin and 9.52% developed antibodies to bovine factor V/Va. None of the patients treated with recombinant human thrombin developed antibodies to human thrombin or human factor V/Va. The detection of antibody formation is highly dependent on the sensitivity and specificity of the assay. The observed incidence of a positive signal in an assay may be influenced by timing of sampling, sample handling, concomitant medications, or underlying disease. Therefore, direct comparison of the incidence of antibody development can be misleading, and the clinical significance of these findings is unknown. If a patient experiences abnormal coagulation parameter or abnormal bleeding or clotting following the use of topical thrombin, an expert in coagulation disorders should be consulted. Furthermore, patients with antibodies to bovine thrombin should not be reexposed to these products.Hypersensitivity (e.g., fever, allergic reactions, anaphylactoid reactions) to topical thrombin has occurred in some patients. In addition, generalized urticaria and anaphylactoid reactions have been reported after intravascularly administered thrombin. The patient with the anaphylactoid reaction had end stage renal disease and had been repeatedly exposed to topical thrombin. Because bovine thrombin is highly immunogenic, it may be prudent to test patients with a prior history of exposure to thrombin (topical or intravascular) for hypersensitivity via a skin prick test prior to intravascular thrombin administration (see Administration).An injection site reaction, including cellulitis or abscess formation, has also been reported after intravascular use of thrombin.

Description
  Text
  Thrombin is a hemostatic agent. It is a component of the clotting cascade and is known as factor IIa. Thrombin is derived from prothrombin, an alpha-2-globulin produced by the liver. Bovine and recombinant thrombin are commercially available. Potency of the thrombin products is measured in terms of the amount of thrombin required to form a clot. Solutions of different strengths may be prepared for use, depending on the severity of bleeding. Topical thrombin is used during surgery to control incisional or surgical bleeding. Thrombin is increasingly being used off-label to treat post-catheterization pseudoaneurysms; when used for this indication, thrombin is administered intravascularly, directly into the pseudoaneurysm via ultrasound-guided percutaneous injection. Most data regarding the use of thrombin intravascularly involve pseudoaneurysms of the femoral artery; however, successful thromboses have been demonstrated in many other anatomical locations including the abdominal aorta, brachial artery, epigastric artery, hepatic artery, radial artery, subclavian artery, and superior mesenteric artery. The use of thrombin for pseudoaneurysms is gaining popularity because it is less invasive than surgery and more successful than other less invasive modalities, such as ultrasound-guided compression; the success rate of thrombin for the treatment of pseudoaneurysms of the femoral artery is more than 90%, with some reports indicating an even higher rate of more than 97%. In addition, thrombin is effective in anticoagulated patients, unlike ultrasound-guided compression. Furthermore, the complication rates, including thromboembolism and hypersensitivity reactions, have been reported to be 4% or lower.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Thrombin is a proteolytic enzyme which catalyzes the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin. Thrombin removes 2 low-molecular-weight peptides from fibrinogen to form a fibrin monomer, which automatically polymerizes with other fibrin monomers, generating long fibrin threads. These fibrin threads serve as the framework for the creation of a clot. This polymerization process occurs within seconds. Topically administered thrombin provides a direct clotting effect on the exposed blood. The rate of the reaction is dependent on the concentration of thrombin, with higher concentrations providing faster clotting rates.

Pharmacokinetics
  Text
  Thrombin is administered topically. Typical pharmacokinetic parameters are not applicable. Thrombin is formed from prothrombin, an unstable protein that is continuously produced by the liver. Lack of vitamin K or hepatic failure causes plasma concentrations of prothrombin (and subsequently thrombin) to decline within 24 hours to levels too low to maintain normal hemostasis.

Preparation
Bovine Thrombin Topical Solution (liquid)
No reconstitution is necessary.
Remove the flip-off plastic cap from the vial to expose the rubber stopper.
Withdraw the solution from the vial using a sterile needle and syringe, or alteratively, remove the rubber stopper and transfer the solution aseptically to a sterile container.
Storage: This drug product contains no preservative and is for single use only.
 
Bovine Thrombin Topical Solution (powder for topical solution)
The powder for solution can be used in a dry form on oozing surfaces.
Reconstitute with sterile isotonic saline at a recommended concentration of 1,000 to 2,000 International Units/mL.
Prepare the desired strength to suit the needs of the case by diluting the contents of the container with an appropriate volume of sterile isotonic saline. A concentration of 1,000 International Units/mL may be required where bleeding is profuse. A concentration of 100 International Units/mL is frequently used for plastic surgery, dental extractions, skin grafting, etc.
Storage: Use solutions promptly upon removal from the container; however, the solution may be refrigerated at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) for up to 24 hours or stored at room temperature for up to 8 hours after reconstitution.
 
Recombinant Thrombin Topical Solution
Recombinant Thrombin 5,000 units Topical Solution
Insert the sharp end of the needle-free transfer device into the center of the vial stopper.
Attach the prefilled diluent syringe to the needle-free transfer device.
Inject the 5 mL of diluent from the syringe into the product vial; keep the syringe plunger depressed.
Remove and discard the diluent syringe; do not use the diluent syringe for transfer of reconstituted product.
Gently swirl and invert the vial until the powder is completely dissolved; avoid excessive agitation. The powder should dissolve in less than 1 minute at room temperature.
Attach the empty syringe (provided) to the needle free transfer device and draw up the necessary amount of solution required for application.
Apply the pre-printed "do not inject" label to the sterile, empty transfer syringe provided. Draw up the recombinant thrombin solution.
Recombinant Thrombin 20,000 units Topical Solution
Attach a needle-free transfer device to both the recombinant thrombin and diluent vials.
Open the sterile, empty 20 mL syringe package and apply the pre-printed "do not inject" label to the syringe.
Attach the labeled 20 mL syringe to the needle-free transfer device on the diluent vial (injection of air into the diluent vial may facilitate withdrawal of the diluent).
Draw up 20 mL of diluent from the vial into the syringe. Remove the diluent-filled syringe from the diluent vial and attach it to the transfer device on the recombinant thrombin vial.
Transfer the 20 mL of diluent from the syringe into the recombinant thrombin vial; the vacuum in the vial facilitates transfer.
Leave the syringe attached and gently swirl and invert the recombinant thrombin vial until the powder is completely dissolved. Avoid excessive agitation. The powder should dissolve in less than 1 minute at room temperature.
Apply the pre-printed "do not inject" label to the syringe. Draw up the recombinant thrombin solution.
 
Topical Administration
Sponge (not wipe) the surface to which thrombin is to be applied free of blood prior to thrombin application.
Apply thrombin directly to the bleeding site surface or in conjunction with an absorbable gelatin sponge. The amount required depends upon the area of tissue to be treated and the method of application.
A spray may be used, or the surface may be flooded using a sterile syringe. The most effective hemostasis occurs when the thrombin mixes freely with the blood as soon as it reaches the surface.
Avoid sponging the treated surface to assure the clot remains securely in place.
When using an absorbable gelatin sponge, immerse sponge strips of the desired size into the solution to completely saturate the sponge. Knead the sponge strips vigorously with moistened, gloved fingers to remove trapped air. Remove the sponge and gently squeeze to remove the excess thrombin solution. Apply sponge to the bleeding site in a single layer. Hold in place with a pledget of cotton or small gauze sponge until hemostasis occurs.
