Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Advise patients to take personal precautions to reduce exposure to mosquito bites. Protective clothing, insect repellents, and outdoor activity avoidance, especially during twilight periods and at night may be appropriate.
Immunization is advised for all laboratory workers with potential exposure to infectious Japanese encephalitis (JE) virus. Infection may occur via inhalation of aerosolized virus or via percutaneous delivery such as from a needle stick. Vaccine-derived immunity from such exposures is not established but is presumed for percutaneous exposure.
Consider immunization for persons planning to reside in areas where JE is endemic or epidemic, longer-term (greater than 1 month) travelers to JE-endemic areas, and frequent travelers to JE-endemic areas. Consider the JE vaccine for shorter-term (less than 1 month) travelers with an increased risk for JE based on planned travel duration, season, location, activities, and accommodations.
Immunization is not recommended for short-term travelers whose visit is restricted to urban areas or times outside of a well-defined JE virus transmission season.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Ixiaro is contraindicated for use by patients with a previous severe allergic reaction such as anaphylaxis to the vaccine, and JE-VAX is contraindicated for use by patients with generalized urticaria and angioedema to a prior Japanese encephalitis vaccine dose. Ixiaro contains protamine sulfate, which may cause a hypersensitivity reaction. Ixiaro also contains sodium metabisulfite and bovine serum albumin and may be inappropriate for use by patients with either sulfite hypersensitivity or albumin hypersensitivity. JE-VAX contains 0.007% thimerosal and is contraindicated for use by patients with thimerosal hypersensitivity. Patients with gelatin hypersensitivity, polysorbate 80 hypersensitivity, or murine protein hypersensitivity may not be appropriate JE-VAX candidates, as each 1 ml dose contains approximately 500 mcg of gelatin, less than 0.0007% volume/volume polysorbate 80, and less than 50 ng of mouse serum protein. JE-VAX is prepared by inoculating mice intracerebrally with Japanese encephalitis virus. Persons with a past history of urticaria after hymenoptera envenomation, drugs, physical or other provocations, or idiopathic causes appear to have a greater risk of developing reactions to JE-VAX vaccine; the relative risk is 9.1 (95% CI 1.8—50.9). In a case-control study, 4 variables were associated with an increased likelihood of reactions to JE-VAX: urticaria, rhinitis, asthma and/or rhinitis, and any allergy. Consider the presence of a risk factor when considering the risk to benefit ratio for a patient, and inform the patient of their increased risk for a reaction. Data are unavailable to support the efficacy of prophylactic antihistamines or steroids to prevent vaccine-related allergic reactions. Monitor patients for at least 30 minutes after administration of the JE-VAX vaccine for generalized urticaria or angioedema. Due to the possibility of delayed adverse reactions, instruct vaccine recipients to remain in areas where they have ready access to medical care and to not embark on international travel for 10 days after receiving a dose of JE-VAX vaccine. Instruct patients to seek medical attention immediately upon onset of any reaction.
 
Neither Ixiaro nor JE-VAX is for intravenous administration. JE-VAX is only given subcutaneously. Ixiaro is only given intramuscularly and may be inappropriate for patients with thrombocytopenia, vitamin K deficiency, or coagulopathy such as hemophilia. Patients taking anticoagulant therapy also may not be appropriate candidates for Ixiaro receipt.
 
Patients with alcoholism may be inappropriate candidates for JE-VAX. Patients who had an unusual alcohol consumption during the 2 days after vaccination had an increased risk of hypersensitivity reactions to the JE-VAX vaccine. Instruct patients who get a dose of JE-VAX to avoid more than the usual alcohol intake for 48 hours after vaccine administration.
 
Patients with immunosuppression such as acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) may not have an adequate antibody response to vaccination with the Japanese encephalitis virus vaccine. No safety or efficacy data exist regarding the use of Ixiaro in immunocompromised individuals. Consider the patient's degree of altered immunocompetence. Inactivated vaccines administered during the period of altered immunocompetence might need to be repeated after immune function has improved.
 
Ixiaro is an FDA pregnancy category B drug; no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women exist. Administration of Ixiaro to a pregnant women is advised only if clearly needed. JE-VAX is classified in FDA pregnancy risk category C. The JE-VAX vaccine should be given to pregnant women only if, in the opinion of a physician, withholding the vaccine poses even greater risk. Infection with the Japanese encephalitis virus during pregnancy may cause intrauterine infection and fetal death.
 
According to the manufacturer, caution is advised if the Japanese encephalitis virus vaccine is administered to a woman who is breast-feeding her infant. Japanese encephalitis virus vaccine excretion into human milk is unknown. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally administered drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
Limited data indicate that the elderly have a similar response to Ixiaro. Among 24 patients at least 65 years of age, 95.8% seroconverted. Vaccination may be desirable, as advanced age may be a risk factor for developing symptomatic illness after infection.
 
No data are available regarding the use of the JE-VAX vaccine in infants. If possible, defer vaccination until at least 1 year of age. Safety and efficacy of Ixiaro have not been established in patients less than 17 years of age such as children.

    General Information

    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Data are insufficient to advise a drug-associated risk of Japanese encephalitis virus vaccine use during pregnancy. No adequate and well-controlled studies have been conducted in pregnant individuals, and the ability of the vaccine to cause fetal harm is unknown. During a developmental toxicity study performed in rats, there was no evidence of fetal harm. According to the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), administration of inactivated virus vaccines to pregnant women have not resulted in adverse effects in the fetus. The ACIP recommends vaccination during pregnancy when the likelihood of disease exposure is high, potential infection would cause harm to pregnant person or fetus, and when the vaccine is unlikely to cause harm. Infection with the Japanese encephalitis virus during pregnancy may cause intrauterine infection and fetal death. Health professionals caring for patients who receive the Japanese encephalitis virus vaccine during pregnancy are encouraged to contact the manufacturer by telephone at 844-349-4276.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: The US Department of Health and Human Services has established a Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) to accept all reports of suspected adverse events after the administration of any vaccine. This includes, but is not limited to, the reporting of events required by the National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act of 1986. The toll-free number for VAERS is (800) 822—7967.
 
In clinical trials of Ixiaro, the most common systemic adverse events among 1993 Ixiaro recipients were headache (27.9%), myalgia (15.6%), fatigue (11.3%), and influenza-like illness (12.3%). However, a similar percentage of 657 patients who received a phosphate buffered saline containing 0.1% aluminum hydroxide control had headache (26.2%), myalgia (15.5%), fatigue (11.7%), or influenza-like illness (11.7%). Approximately 10% of JE-VAX recipients had systemic side effects such as fever, headache, malaise, rash (unspecified), flu-like symptoms, chills, dizziness, myalgia, nausea/vomiting, and abdominal pain. The rate of adverse events was 15 per 100,000 JE-VAX doses, as determined from events reported through the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System from 1993—1998. The most frequently reported symptoms were headache, pruritus, rash, fever, nausea, hives, vasodilatation, and dizziness.
 
In clinical trials, about half of Ixiaro or control recipients had an injection site reaction after either dose 1 or 2. The control was a phosphate buffered saline containing 0.1% aluminum hydroxide given intramuscularly. Tenderness and pain was most commonly reported, but erythema was noted in about 10% of patients, and induration, edema, and pruritis were noted in 3.8—7.5% of Ixiaro recipients. Any injection reaction was reported by fewer patients after dose 2 (32.6% of 1951) as compared with after dose 1 (48.5% of 1963). For JE-VAX, injection site reactions such as tenderness, redness, swelling, and other local effects have been reported in about 20% of vaccinees (< 1% to 31%). At least one severe local symptom such as pain, itching, tenderness, hardening, swelling, or erythema within a week of vaccination on day 0 or 28 occurred in 59 of 427 JE-VAX recipients. In contrast, 9 of 421 Ixiaro recipients had at least one severe local symptom. In this study, hardening, swelling, and erythema were the most common severe, local symptoms associated with both Ixiaro and JE-VAX.
 
Urticaria or angioedema may occur after receipt of JE-VAX. Respiratory distress, dyspnea, or syncope due to hypotension or other causes has led to hospitalization in several cases. Most reactions were treated successfully with antihistamines or oral steroids; however, some patients were hospitalized for parenteral steroid therapy. Some patients developed an erythema multiforme or erythema nodosum, and some patients have had joint swelling or generalized itching without objective evidence of a rash. The rate of urticaria and/or edema was 6.3 per 100,000 JE-VAX doses, as determined from events reported through the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System from 1993—1998. Of 51 vaccinees, 18 had onset on the day of vaccination, and 25 had onset within 1—3 days after vaccination. Three vaccinees had bradycardia, cyanosis, or hypotension without urticaria or edema on the day of vaccination. In another group of vaccinees, the hypersensitivity rate was 16.1 per 10,000 JE-VAX doses after dose 1, 10.3 per 10,000 JE-VAX doses after dose 2, and 2 per 10,000 JE-VAX doses after dose 3. Most (26 of 38 patients) had urticaria and/or angioedema (mostly facial), but 11 only had pruritus (6 also had a nonurticarial rash), and 1 only had wheezing. Urticaria was generalized in all but 2 vaccinees. Generalized urticaria or angioedema including extremities, face, and oropharynx, especially of the lips, may occur within minutes after vaccination with JE-VAX, but a possibly related reaction has occurred as late as 17 days after vaccination. Among 10,000 JE-VAX doses administered, 74% of reactions after dose 1 occurred in the first 48 hours. In contrast, reactions after dose 2 occurred at a median of 96 hours (range, 20—336 hours); only 31% of the reactions occurred within 48 hours. Reactions have occurred after a second or third dose, when preceding doses were received uneventfully. Risk factors for hypersensitivity reactions may be unusual alcohol consumption in the 48-hour period after vaccination; receipt of other vaccines within the 7 days before JE-VAX receipt; and a history of urticaria after hymenoptera envenomation, drugs, physical or other provocations, or of idiopathic cause (see Contraindications). Availability of appropriate medical care including epinephrine and other medicines and equipment used to treat anaphylactic shock is needed in the event of a severe allergic reaction. Observe patients for 30 minutes after JE-VAX receipt. Also, tell patients about the possibility of delayed, generalized urticaria; of angioedema of the extremities, face, and oropharynx; and of the need to remain in an area where they have access to medical care for at least 10 days after a JE-VAX dose (no international travel). Instruct patients to get immediate medical attention at the onset of any reaction. Adverse reactions such as generalized urticaria and angioedema to a prior dose of JE-VAX are considered to be contraindications to further vaccination; a subsequent hypersensitivity reaction occurred within 15 minutes to 72 hours of an inadvertent second JE-VAX dose. Similarly, a severe allergic reaction such as anaphylaxis after a previous dose of Ixiaro is a contraindication to further administration of Ixiaro.
 
Receipt of JE-VAX may be associated with the development of severe neurological disorders such as acute disseminated encephalomyelitis. Several reports exist of possible acute disseminated encephalomyelitis after vaccination with JE-VAX. In 3 adults, symptoms developed within a month of a dose and included headache, neck stiffness, fatigue, cognitive dysfunction (confusion), paresthesias, double vision (visual impairment), gait instability, oculogyral instability, and dysarthria. Symptoms of acute disseminated encephalomyelitis developed 14 days after a second dose in 1 child and 17 days after a booster dose in another child. Symptoms in 1 child included diplegia, urinary incontinence, and paresthesias, which suggested transverse myelitis. According to the manufacturer, neurologic events such as encephalitis, encephalopathy, seizures, and peripheral neuropathy may occur in 1—2.3 per million vaccinees. One case of Guillain-Barre syndrome and one case of febrile seizure were reported through the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System from 1993—1998; the rate for all neurological adverse events was 0.2/100,000 JE-VAX doses.

Description
  Text
  Japanese encephalitis virus vaccine is a parenteral viral vaccine that works by inducing antibodies that neutralize live virus. The arboviral Flavivirus is spread by mosquitos and is the leading cause of viral encephalitis in Asia. Two inactivated vaccines are licensed for use in the United States: JE-VAX and Ixiaro. JE-VAX was prepared by inoculating mice intracerebrally with the virus and harvesting the brains whereas Ixiaro is prepared by propagating a viral strain in Vero cells. Ixiaro, FDA approved in March 2009, is now the only vaccine commercially available in the US to help prevent Japanese encephalitis. The Research Foundation for Microbial Diseases of Osaka University, the manufacturer of JE-VAX vaccine, has discontinued production of JE-VAX.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Receipt of the Japanese encephalitis virus vaccine induces antibody production. The antibodies neutralize live Japanese encephalitis virus. Neutralizing antibodies are measured by a 50% plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT50), with a PRNT50 titer of at least 1:10 being a reasonable correlate for protection. As determined 28 days after the last dose, a titer of at least 1:10 (seroconversion) was obtained by 96.4% of adult Ixiaro recipients and 99.2—100% of pediatric recipients (depending on age). Two doses are required for most patients; 28 days after the first dose, only 39.8% of adults had seroconverted whereas 97.3% seroconverted 7 days and 28 days after dose 2.
 
In most patients, neutralizing antibodies persist for at least 6 months after completion of the 2-dose Ixiaro series. In one study, the percentage of adults who maintained the titer threshold was 95% at 6 months, 83.4% at 12 months, 81.8% at 24 months, and 84.9% at 36 months. In another study the results were not as favorable, with only 58.3% and 48.3% of adults maintaining a titer of at least 1:10 at 11 and 23 months, respectively, after completion of the primary series. Based on this data, a booster dose is recommended for patients ages 17 years and older who have completed the primary series more than 1 year prior and who continue to be at risk of exposure; immunogenicity of the booster has not been established in patients less than 17 years of age.

Administration
  General Information
  For storage information, see the specific product information within the How Supplied section.
Verify immunization status in the state or local immunization information system in addition to the health care electronic record. If this vaccine has been previously given, question the patient, parent, or guardian about any symptoms or signs of an adverse reaction.

Record manufacturer, lot number, administration date, and the name and address of the person administering the vaccine.
