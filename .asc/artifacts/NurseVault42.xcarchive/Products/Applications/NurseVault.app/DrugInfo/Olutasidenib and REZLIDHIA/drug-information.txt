Drug Class: Olutasidenib is an isocitrate dehydrogenase-1 (IDH1) inhibitor indicated for the treatment of adult patients with relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukemia with a susceptible IDH1 mutation as detected by an FDA-approved test.

        Route Age Groups
          Heading
          Adults
          Dosage Description
          150 mg orally twice daily until disease progression. Treat patients without disease progression for a minimum of 6 months to allow time for clinical response. Therapy interruption, dose reduction, or drug discontinuation may be necessary in patients who develop toxicity. Coadministration of certain drugs may need to be avoided or dosage adjustments may be necessary; review drug interactions. At a median follow-up of 10.2 (range, 0.2 to 38.1) months, treatment with olutasidenib resulted in a complete remission (CR) or a CR with partial hematologic recovery (CRh) rate of 35% in 147 patients with relapsed or refractory IDH1-mutated acute myeloid leukemia in a multicenter, multi-cohort, 1/2 (Study 2102-HEM-101) trial. In patients who achieved a CR/CRh, the median time to first response and duration of response were 1.9 months (range, 0.9 to 5.6 months) and 25.9 months, respectively. The median overall survival time was 11.6 months. Of the 86 patients who were dependent on red blood cell or platelet transfusions at baseline, 34% of patients became transfusion independent during any 56-day post baseline period. Before enrolling in this trial, patients (median age, 71 years; range, 32 to 87 years) had received a median of 2 (range, 1 to 7) prior therapies; 12% of patients had undergone prior allogeneic hemopoietic stem-cell transplantation.

  Contraindications And Precautions
    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    differentiation syndrome
    General Information
    Differentiation syndrome, characterized as a rapid proliferation and differentiation of myeloid cells, with or without concomitant leukocytosis has been reported with olutasidenib therapy; some cases were life-threatening or fatal. Monitor patients for signs and symptoms such as dyspnea, pulmonary infiltrates, pleural or pericardial effusion, kidney injury, fever, edema, and weight gain; patients with hepatic impairment may be at an increased risk of differentiation syndrome. If differentiation syndrome is suspected, hold olutasidenib therapy, start corticosteroid therapy and monitor hemodynamic parameters until symptom resolution. Differentiation syndrome occurred as soon as 1 day and up to 18 months after starting olutasidenib.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class A, Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use olutasidenib with caution in patients with severe (Child-Pugh class C) hepatic impairment (total bilirubin greater than 3 times ULN with any AST), including hepatic failure; a recommended dosage has not been established in these patients. Patients with mild (Child-Pugh class A) or moderate (Child-Pugh class B) hepatic impairment may be at an increased risk of differentiation syndrome; closely monitor these patients. Hepatotoxicity including elevated hepatic enzymes and/or hyperbilirubinemia has been reported with olutasidenib therapy. Therapy interruption, dose reduction, or drug discontinuation may be necessary in patients who develop hepatotoxicity.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Olutasidenib may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy, based on animal studies. Discuss the potential hazard to the fetus if olutasidenib is used during pregnancy or if a patient becomes pregnant while taking this drug. Reduced maternal weight gain and embryo-fetal toxicities including an increase in fetal supernumerary rib and increased post-implantation loss were observed when olutasidenib was administered to pregnant rabbits during organogenesis at doses resulting in exposures of approximately 0.7 time the exposure in humans (at the recommended dose). Additionally, an increase in fetal supernumerary rib and an increase in post-implantation loss occurred in pregnant rats who received olutasidenib doses resulting in exposures up to 10 times the exposure in humans.
    Headline
    contraception requirements, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel patients about the reproductive risk and contraception requirements during olutasidenib treatment. Consider pregnancy testing prior to starting olutasidenib therapy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    It is not known if olutasidenib is secreted in human milk or if it has effects on the breast-fed child or on milk production. Because there is a potential for adverse reactions in a nursing child from olutasidenib, patients should avoid breast-feeding during olutasidenib therapy and for 2 weeks after the last dose.

Acetaminophen; Codeine: (Moderate) Monitor for reduced efficacy of codeine and signs of opioid withdrawal in patients who have developed physical dependence if coadministration with olutasidenib is necessary; consider increasing the dose of codeine as needed. It is recommended to avoid this combination when codeine is being used for cough. If olutasidenib is discontinued, consider a dose reduction of codeine and frequently monitor for signs of respiratory depression and sedation. Codeine is primarily metabolized by CYP2D6 to morphine, and by CYP3A to norcodeine; norcodeine does not have analgesic properties. Olutasidenib is a weak CYP3A inducer.     General Information

    General Information
    Differentiation syndrome has been reported with olutasidenib therapy. Monitor patients for signs of differentiation syndrome (e.g., leukocytosis, dyspnea, pulmonary infiltrates, pleural effusion or pericardial effusion, kidney injury, fever, edema, and weight gain); evaluate complete blood counts (CBC) and other blood chemistries at baseline, at least once weekly for the first 2 months, once every other week for the third month, once in the fourth month, and once every other month thereafter during therapy; manage laboratory abnormalities promptly. If concomitant leukocytosis is observed, start hydroxyurea as clinically indicated. If differentiation syndrome is suspected, hold olutasidenib therapy, start corticosteroids (e.g., dexamethasone 10 mg IV every 12 hours for at least 3 days), and monitor hemodynamic parameters until symptoms resolve. Taper corticosteroids and hydroxyurea after symptoms resolve. Differentiation syndrome may recur if corticosteroids and/or hydroxyurea treatment is stopped too early. Therapy interruption, dose reduction, or drug discontinuation may be necessary in patients who develop differentiation syndrome. Differentiation syndrome (16%; grade 3 or 4, 8%), leukocytosis (25%; grade 3 or 4, 9%), and lymphocytosis/increased lymphocyte count (26%; grade 3 or 4, 3%) occurred in patients with relapsed or refractory acute myelogenous leukemia and an IDH1 mutation who received olutasidenib in a clinical trial (n = 153); 1 patient died due to complications from differentiation syndrome. In this trial, the onset of differentiation syndrome occurred from 1 day to 18 months after starting olutasidenib; 76% of patients who developed differentiation syndrome recovered after treatment or after dosage interruption.

Description
  Text
  Olutasidenib is an isocitrate dehydrogenase-1 (IDH1) inhibitor indicated for the treatment of adult patients with relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukemia with a susceptible IDH1 mutation as detected by an FDA-approved test. Olutasidenib has a black box warning for differentiation syndrome; this syndrome can be fatal if untreated. Corticosteroid therapy and hemodynamic monitoring are indicated if differentiation syndrome is suspected. Hepatotoxicity has also occurred in patients treated with olutasidenib; monitor liver function tests during therapy.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Olutasidenib is an oral isocitrate dehydrogenase-1 (IDH1) inhibitor that targets the mutant IDH1 variants, including R132H, R132L, R132S, R132G, and R132C proteins (in vitro). Susceptible IDH1 mutations are those that lead to increased levels of 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG) in leukemia cells. Decreasing 2-HG levels may restore normal myeloid cell differentiation. IDH1 inhibition by olutasidenib led to decreased 2-HG levels in vitro and in xenograft models.

    In patients with acute myelogenous leukemia who received olutasidenib 150 mg PO twice daily, the mean steady-state Cmax and AUC(0-12h) values were 3,573 nanograms (ng)/mL (coefficient of variation (CV), 45.6%) and 43,050 ng x hour/mL (CV, 34%), respectively. Effects of Food
In healthy subjects, the mean Cmax and AUC(inf) values increased by 191% (CV, 20.6%) and 83% (CV, 18.3%), respectively, following a single dose of olutasidenib 150 mg PO administered with a high-fat meal (e.g., about 800 to 1,000 calories, with 50% of total calories from fat) compared with the fasted state.

  Text
  Olutasidenib is administered orally. It is approximately 93% bound to plasma proteins. The mean apparent volume of distribution was 319 L (coefficient of variation (CV), 28.1%), mean apparent oral clearance was 4 L/hour (CV, 60.5%), and the mean half-life was about 67 hours (CV, 51.2%) in patients with acute myelogenous leukemia who received olutasidenib 150 mg PO twice daily. Olutasidenib is metabolized via N-dealkylation, demethylation, oxidative deamination followed by oxidation, mono-oxidation with subsequent glucuronidation; 90% of olutasidenib is metabolized by CYP3A4. Metabolism occurs to a lesser extent via CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP1A2, and CYP2C19. In healthy subjects, 75% of the drug was recovered in the feces (35% unchanged) and 17% of the drug was recovered in the urine (1% unchanged) following a single radiolabeled olutasidenib dose of 150 mg PO.
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and transporters: CYP3A4
Olutasidenib is a CYP3A4 substrate and a weak CYP3A4 inducer. In vitro, it is an inducer of CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP1A2, CYP2C8, and CYP2C9 and an inhibitor of P-gp, BCRP, OATP1B1, OATP1B3, OAT3, OCT2, MATE1, and MATE2K. Concomitant use of olutasidenib with multiple doses of a strong CYP3A and P-gp inhibitor did not result in a clinically significant change in the pharmacokinetic parameters of olutasidenib.
