    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    Use folic acid with caution in patients with undiagnosed anemia. Folic acid may mask the hematologic signs of pernicious anemia, allowing neurologic complications to progress and potentially become irreversible. Avoid administering folic acid in doses greater than 0.4 mg/day until vitamin B12 deficiency or pernicious anemia has been ruled out. Folic acid alone is not appropriate therapy for pernicious anemia, megaloblastic anemia due to vitamin B12 deficiency, or other conditions associated with cobalamin deficiency.

    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression
    General Information
    Use folic acid; Lactobacillus with caution in individuals with diabetes mellitus or in those with immunosuppression, as there is a potential risk of bacteremia, including Lactobacillemia. Case reports have documented such events in individuals with severe underlying conditions, including acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) and other forms of significant immunocompromise.
    Headline
    children, infants, neonates, premature neonates
    General Information
    Folic acid; lactobacillus supplements should not be given to infants and children younger than 12 years of age. Premature neonates who receive probiotics, such as Lactobacillus, are at risk of invasive, potentially fatal disease caused by the bacteria or fungi contained in probiotics. Microorganisms contained in probiotics have been reported in the medical literature as causing bacteremia or fungemia, sometimes with a severe clinical course, in very preterm or very low birth weight (VLBW) neonates. A preterm neonate, birth weight less than 1,000 grams, who was administered a probiotic as part of in-hospital care developed sepsis caused by the bacterium Bifidobacterium longum and subsequently died. Genomic sequencing data demonstrated the bacterium that caused sepsis in this neonate was a genetic match to the bacteria contained in this probiotic.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Folic acid supplementation is recommended during pregnancy. Adequate intake of folic acid is essential during pregnancy and helps prevent neural tube defects (NTDs). Available study data indicate that probiotic use appears to pose a low risk to the healthy pregnant person and that use during pregnancy does not result in adverse fetal or infant outcomes; many of the studies in pregnancy have been conducted using various Lactobacillus spp. probiotic supplements.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Folic acid; lactobacillus is compatible with breast-feeding. Appropriate folic acid intake is important during lactation, and no problems have been identified with supplementation to achieve adequate intake goals during breast-feeding. Available data do not indicate risks with the use of Lactobacillus to the healthy breast-fed child. The addition of probiotic products directly to pumped human milk or infant formula is not recommended due to a potential risk for infection, particularly if the infant is preterm. Natural human milk has many components, and an important element of human milk is the probiotic microflora, including various species of Lactobacillus. The health and medical conditions of the lactating individual may affect the composition of the milk microflora.

  Adverse Reactions
    Headline
    abdominal pain, colic, constipation, diarrhea, dyspepsia, flatulence, nausea, rash
    General Information
    Controlled trials of probiotics have primarily reported on gastrointestinal (GI) adverse events. Diarrhea, flatulence, nausea, constipation, abdominal pain or discomfort, dyspepsia, colic, and abdominal fullness are the potential GI adverse events reported most commonly across studies that included use of probiotics of various species. Allergy sensitization (e.g., rash) and pain on micturition have also been reported. Allergic reactions (e.g., rash, itching, bronchospasm) are rarely reported with oral folic acid use. Higher folic acid doses of 15 mg/day or more may produce GI (e.g., flatulence, nausea) and neurologic side effects (e.g., altered sleep patterns or irritability).

Description
  Text
  Folic acid; Lactobacillus casei is a dietary supplement that contains folic acid and the probiotic, Lactobacillus casei (KE-99). Per the manufacturer, the supplement is intended for folic acid supplementation where enhancement of gut bacteria with Lactobacillus casei may be helpful for symptoms of constipation, gas, bloating, and diarrhea. These statements have not been evaluated by the FDA. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease. In the U.S., dietary supplements are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for dietary supplements as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since they are not regulated as drugs.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Folic Acid: Folic acid is one of the important hematopoietic agents necessary for proper regeneration of the blood-forming elements and their function; a deficiency of folic acid results in megaloblastic anemia. Additionally, folic acid increased jejunal glycolytic enzymes and is involved in the desaturation and hydroxylation of long-chain fatty acids in the brain.
Lactobacillus casei: The healthy gastrointestinal (GI) microbiome is composed of a variety of microbes that reside in the gut; these microbes may be altered by diet, lifestyle, and exposure to medications or toxins. Changes in the GI microbiota may influence health and disease and there is some evidence that probiotics may help maintain immunologic equilibrium through direct interaction with immune cells. The microbiome diversity in the GI tract is likely important to health, but the mechanisms are complex and are not likely due to one species alone.
