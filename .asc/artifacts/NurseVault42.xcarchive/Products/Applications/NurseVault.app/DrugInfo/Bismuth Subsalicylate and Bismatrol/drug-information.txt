Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Because systemic concentrations of salicylic acid are detectable during therapy with bismuth subsalicylate, its use is contraindicated in patients with salicylate hypersensitivity. Repeated doses of bismuth subsalicylate for durations in excess of recommendations should be avoided to avoid salicylate toxicity. Advise patients to discontinue use of the product and seek medical attention if tinnitus or hearing impairment occur during treatment as these may be signs of toxicity. In addition, avoid concurrent treatment with bismuth subsalicylate and other salicylates, including aspirin.
 
Advise patients to consult a healthcare professional if while taking bismuth subsalicylate GI symptoms get worse or if diarrhea lasts longer than 2 days.
 
Bismuth preparations, antimicrobials, lansoprazole, and omeprazole suppress H. pylori. Ingestion of these substances within four (4) weeks prior to performing urease or breath-tests for H. pylori detection may lead to false negative results. In the four weeks prior to performing the test, the patient must avoid the use of bismuth subsalicylate and other agents which are known to suppress H. pylori.
 
A FDA rule, effective April 17, 2004, eliminated labeling for the non-prescription (OTC) administration of bismuth subsalicylate in children < 12 years old due to lack of adequate data to support use in this age group. Because systemic concentrations of salicylate are detectable during therapy with bismuth subsalicylate, any use of bismuth subsalicylate should be avoided in febrile pediatric patients until the possibility of influenza-like or varicella infection has been eliminated due to the risk of Reye's syndrome. Current evidence suggests that acetylated salicylates such as aspirin are associated with this complication; aspirin has been associated with the occurrence of Reye's syndrome when given to pediatric patients with varicella zoster (chickenpox) or influenza (flu). Although a causal relationship has not been confirmed, most authorities advise against the use of salicylates in pediatric patients with varicella, influenza, or other viral infection. In addition, it is recommended that pediatric patients not be given salicylates for 6 weeks after receiving varicella vaccination.
 
Due to an increased risk of bleeding, bismuth subsalicylate therapy is contraindicated in patients with  hematological disease (i.e., conditions that increases risk of bleeding) for example: hypoprothrombinemia, vitamin K deficiency, coagulopathy (e.g., hemophilia), severe hepatic impairment due to hepatic disease, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), or in patients receiving warfarin anticoagulant therapy (see Drug Interactions). Significant systemic absorption of salicylic acid may occur during chronic treatment or with large doses of bismuth subsalicylate. However, the risk of bleeding with non-acetylated salicylates is less than that of aspirin due to the lack of platelet effects. Since salicylates may cause or aggravate hemolysis in patients with pyruvate kinase deficiency or rare variants of G6PD deficiency, these drugs should be avoided in these patients.
 
Bismuth subsalicylate is contraindicated in the presence of GI bleeding and in peptic ulcer disease. Chronic or high-dose bismuth subsalicylate therapy should be given with caution in patients with a history of or active GI disease including erosive gastritis and esophagitis. Such patients should be monitored closely, with special caution in tobacco smoking patients or in patients with alcoholism.
 
Prolonged use, or large doses of bismuth subsalicylate can increase the hypoglycemic effect of insulin, or oral hypoglycemics. Patients with diabetes mellitus should be treated with caution and warned of possible changes in blood glucose evaluations.
 
Patients with renal impairment (renal disease, renal failure) may be at risk for developing salicylate toxicity when receiving large doses of or chronic treatment with bismuth subsalicylate. Elderly patients may be at increased risk of salicylate toxicity possibly due to decreased renal function associated with increased age.
 
Although bismuth subsalicylate has been shown to be effective in the treatment of traveler's diarrhea, it is not suitable as a single agent for the treatment of dysentery. Diarrhea accompanied by bloody stools and fever can indicate dysentery and patients should be referred to a physician.
 
Patient suffering from dehydration should be treated with caution. Bismuth subsalicylate will not address the problem of dehydration associated with diarrhea. Adequate administration of fluids is essential, especially to children.
 
Patients with gout should receive bismuth subsalicylate with caution. The salicylate content of bismuth subsalicylate can interfere with uricosuric antigout agents, and can also affect serum uric acid concentrations.
 
Bismuth subsalicylate is classified in FDA pregnancy risk category C; however, salicylates, including bismuth subsalicylate, should be avoided in the 3rd trimester due to the potential for premature closure of the ductus arteriosis, fetal or maternal hemorrhage, and jaundice (due to competition with bilirubin for albumin binding). Early in pregnancy, occasional use of small doses of salicylates (60—80 mg/day) may be safe, but the risk for bleeding is still present. In general, salicylates should be avoided if possible in pregnancy. Cardiac teratogenic effects of salicylates have not been substantiated. Excessive use of bismuth subsalicylate during the third trimester may also result in prolonged labor and delivery complications.
 
Salicylates are excreted into breast-milk and are potentially harmful to the infant. Bismuth subsalicylate should be used with caution during breast-feeding, although there is no evidence of harmful effects from the recommended dosage in occasional use.
 
Bismuth preparations can be used in combination with certain antibiotics to eradicate Helicobacter pylori. When bismuth is used with antimicrobials, clinicians should be cognizant of possible pseudomembranous colitis occurring. Almost all antibacterial agents have been associated with pseudomembranous colitis (antibiotic-associated colitis), which may range in severity from mild to life-threatening. In the colon, overgrowth of Clostridia may exist when normal flora is altered subsequent to antibacterial administration. The toxin produced by Clostridium difficile is a primary cause of pseudomembranous colitis. It is known that systemic use of antibiotics predisposes patients to development of pseudomembranous colitis. Consideration should be given to the diagnosis of pseudomembranous colitis in patients presenting with diarrhea following antibacterial administration. Systemic antibiotics should be prescribed with caution to patients with inflammatory bowel disease such as ulcerative colitis or other GI disease. If diarrhea develops during therapy, the drug should be discontinued. Following diagnosis of pseudomembranous colitis, therapeutic measures should be instituted. In milder cases, the colitis may respond to discontinuation of the offending agent. In moderate to severe cases, fluids and electrolytes, protein supplementation, and treatment with an antibacterial effective against Clostridium difficile may be warranted. Products inhibiting peristalsis are contraindicated in this clinical situation. Practitioners should be aware that antibiotic-associated colitis has been observed to occur over two months or more following discontinuation of systemic antibiotic therapy; a careful medical history should be taken.

Do not use nonprescription bismuth subsalicylate without medical advice if there is fever or mucus in the stool.

    General Information
    Bismuth subsalicylate should not be used in people with peptic ulcer disease, a bleeding disorder, or bloody or black stools (GI bleeding).
    Headline
    geriatric, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    People with renal impairment or renal failure should check with their health care provider before nonprescription use of bismuth subsalicylate for travelers' diarrhea prophylaxis and other indications. People with renal dysfunction may be at risk for developing salicylate toxicity when receiving bismuth subsalicylate. Geriatric adults with chronic kidney dysfunction or on medications that can impair renal function are particularly susceptible to adverse effects of salicylates, even if not taken in overdose.
    Headline
    labor, obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    Avoid the use of bismuth subsalicylate during pregnancy, particularly after 20 weeks of gestation, due to the salicylate component. As a drug class, salicylates are readily absorbed. Salicylates have been associated with premature closure of the ductus arteriosus, fetal renal dysfunction, oligohydramnios, decreased birth weight, perinatal mortality, and alterations in maternal and neonatal hemostasis mechanisms. Avoid the use of salicylates near term and during labor and obstetric delivery due to the potential for excessive blood loss at delivery. Prolonged gestation and labor due to prostaglandin inhibition have been reported. Teratogenic effects are possible; fetotoxicity has been observed in animal studies with bismuth exposure.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, infants
    General Information
    The Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) list includes bismuth subsalicylate as a potentially inappropriate medication for use in infants, children, and adolescents with suspicion of a viral illness and use should be avoided. If avoidance is not possible, use bismuth subsalicylate with caution and appropriate monitoring.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use bismuth subsalicylate with caution during breast-feeding; alternative agents are preferred. Although bismuth salts are poorly absorbed from the GI tract, salicylate from bismuth subsalicylate products may be absorbed. Potential salicylate exposure through human milk is inferred from aspirin data. In individuals taking less than 324 mg/day of aspirin, the relative infant dose (RID) was less than 0.5%. However, RIDs have been reported to increase to approximately 10% with high-dose aspirin. If more than 2 doses of bismuth subsalicylate are taken in one day, some experts recommend considering temporary pumping and discarding of milk for 6 hours after the dose to limit infant exposure.

Acarbose: (Moderate) Salicylates, by inhibiting prostaglandin E2 synthesis, can indirectly increase insulin secretion. Thus, salicylates can decrease blood sugar. In large doses, salicylates uncouple oxidative phosphorylation, deplete hepatic and muscle glycogen, and cause hyperglycemia and glycosuria. After acute overdose, aspirin can cause either hypo- or hyperglycemia. Large doses of aspirin should be used cautiously in patients receiving antidiabetic agents.

Two mechanisms could cause increased acetazolamide concentrations, resulting in CNS depression and metabolic acidosis: first, competition with aspirin for renal tubular secretion and, second, displacement by salicylates from plasma protein binding sites. Description
  Text
  Bismuth subsalicylate (BSS) is an oral colloidal bismuth salt belonging to the salicylate class. It is used to relieve occasional diarrhea or heartburn and stomach upset. Products for non-prescription (OTC) use are approved for adults and children 12 years of age and older. Use of BSS in younger children is not recommended or approved OTC; consult a qualified health care provider. As with other salicylates, including aspirin, individuals who are pregnant, breast-feeding, or with bleeding risks should also not use BSS without health care provider approval. BSS is part of established prevention and treatment strategies for traveler's diarrhea. BSS can also be used as a component of quadruple-drug therapy to eradicate Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) gastrointestinal infection, and usually includes BSS plus 2 antimicrobials and a proton pump inhibitor.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Bismuth subsalicylate (BSS) has antiinfective actions, a direct mucosal protective effect, and weak antacid properties. Hydrolysis of bismuth subsalicylate in the stomach produces salicylic acid, which can, in turn, inhibit prostaglandin synthesis. Because secretory diarrhea can be adversely affected by prostaglandins but may improve after administration of indomethacin, the antidiarrheal action of bismuth subsalicylate may be a result of its actions on prostaglandin synthesis. BSS has been shown to prevent attachment of microorganisms to intestinal mucosa, to inactivate enterotoxins, and to exert a direct antimicrobial effect. Antimicrobial actions of bismuth are short-lived and, since bismuth salts are highly insoluble, limited to the lumen of the GI tract. Thus, both bismuth and salicylic acid are responsible for the action of BSS, depending on the clinical setting.
 
Laboratory studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of bismuth subsalicylate in blocking the effect of toxins from E. coli and from V. cholera at the mucosal surface. Bismuth subsalicylate has apparent activity against both enterotoxigenic bacteria and the heat-labile toxin they produce. Normalization of fluid movement in the GI tract by BSS may be one mechanism by which diarrhea is alleviated. In vitro data showed that bismuth subsalicylate failed to protect against fluid accumulation once the toxin was bound to the intestinal mucosa. Bismuth subcarbonate did not exert a protective effect on isolated bowel mucosa as did bismuth subsalicylate, implicating salicylic acid as a significant part of the effectiveness of BSS. Thus, antidiarrheal effects could be due to the effect of salicylic acid on prostaglandin metabolism and/or the antibacterial actions of bismuth.
 
In peptic ulcer disease, bismuth subsalicylate exerts microbicidal activity against H. pylori. The pharmacologic actions of bismuth salts against H. pylori can be attributed specifically to bismuth. Changes in H. pylori are seen soon after administration of bismuth. Deposits, believed to be bismuth complexes, appear on the external surface and beneath the cell wall of the organism. The loss of adherence property loosens the organism from the antral epithelium. Distortion and vacuolization of the bacterial cell and its contents completes the process. It has been shown in vitro that bismuth can lyse H. pylori within 30 to 90 minutes. Adding certain antimicrobial agents produces a synergistic effect, and studies have demonstrated the efficacy of combination therapy in treating gastritis and healing duodenal ulcers by suppressing Helicobacter pylori. Ulcer healing also may depend on the cytoprotective effect of bismuth subsalicylate. Bismuth compounds might stimulate the production of prostaglandins, alter GI mucus, and/or modulate the immune response.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Hydrolysis of bismuth subsalicylate occurs in the stomach, producing bismuth oxychloride and salicylic acid. The salicylic acid is rapidly and almost completely (about 90%) absorbed. Following a single-60 mL dose of BSS, peak salicylate levels were about 40 +/- 17 mcg/mL. However, due to the non-linear pharmacokinetics of salicylic acid, BSS doses of 30 mL every 30 minutes for 8 doses produced mean peak plasma salicylate levels of 137 +/- 20 mcg/mL in adults. Accumulation of salicylic acid is possible with repeated dosing or with concurrent administration of other salicylates (e.g., aspirin). Absorption of bismuth is negligible with recommended doses.

NOTE: Clinical studies of bismuth have utilized different salts, each containing differing amounts of bismuth. In the US, bismuth subsalicylate is commercially available. Each tablet or caplet or tablespoonful (i.e., 15 mL) contains 262 mg of bismuth subsalicylate (BSS) which is equivalent to 151 mg elemental bismuth. One tablespoonful (15 mL) of maximum strength liquid contains 525 mg of bismuth subsalicylate (BSS) which is equivalent to 303 mg elemental bismuth.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available; use intermittently with caution since any absorbed salicylate component is dependent on renal excretion.
