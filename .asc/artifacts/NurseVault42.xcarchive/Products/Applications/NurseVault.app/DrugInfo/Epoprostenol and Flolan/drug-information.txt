Drug Class: Epoprostenol is the pharmaceutical form of a naturally-occurring prostaglandin commonly referred to as prostacyclin (other names include prostaglandin I2, PGI2, prostaglandin X, PGX).

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: Absolute maximum dosage limits have not been established; maximum doses should be based on individual clinical response.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): chills, fever, myalgia, rash, skin ulcer, tremor, urticaria, anemia, heart failure, hyperthyroidism, pancytopenia, splenomegaly

Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
When discontinuing epoprostenol, gradually reduce the dose in increments of 2 nanograms/kg/minute every 15 minutes or longer until resolution of the dose-limiting effects. Avoid abrupt discontinuation or sudden large reductions in dosage of epoprostenol therapy, as this may result in symptoms of rebound pulmonary hypertension (e.g., dyspnea, dizziness and asthenia).
Avoid treatment interruptions. All individuals receiving epoprostenol should have immediate access to a back-up infusion pump and intravenous infusion set.

        Dosage Description
        2 ng/kg/minute continuous IV infusion, initially; use lower initial dose if not tolerated. Titrate by 2 ng/kg/minute every 15 minutes or more until a tolerance limit to the drug is established or further increases are not clinically warranted. Adjust the chronic infusion rate by 1 to 2 ng/kg/minute every 15 minutes or more based on persistence, recurrence, or worsening of symptoms and occurrence of adverse events. Incremental increases in dose occurred at intervals of 24 to 48 hours or longer in clinical trials. Avoid abrupt withdrawal or sudden large reductions in infusion rates. Typical dose range at 1 year is 16 to 30 ng/kg/minute, with wide individual variability. Studies establishing effectiveness included predominantly (97%) patients with NYHA Functional Class III to IV symptoms and etiologies of idiopathic or heritable pulmonary hypertension (49%) or pulmonary hypertension associated with connective tissue diseases (51%).
        Heading
        Infants†, Children†, and Adolescents†
        Dosage Description
        1 to 2 ng/kg/minute continuous IV infusion, initially. Titrate by 1 to 2 ng/kg/minute increments based on clinical response. Final doses are frequently higher than those used in adult patients, especially in very young children. Maximum dose is not known. A stable dose is usually 50 to 80 ng/kg/minute; however, infusion rates more than 150 ng/kg/minute have been used. A retrospective report describes the use of calcium channel blockers and/or IV epoprostenol in 77 patients ages 7 months to 13 years with primary pulmonary hypertension. Long-term epoprostenol infusions were initiated in 31 of those patients (mean age 8 +/- 4 years) after failing to improve on conventional therapy, including digitalis, diuretics, and supplemental oxygen as needed. After initiation and titration of epoprostenol, mean infusion rates were 78 +/- 38 ng/kg/minute at 1 year, 116 +/-48 ng/kg/minute at 2 years, and 122 +/- 36 ng/kg/minute at 3 years. Conventional therapy was continued in all patients, and calcium channel blockers were used inconsistently. Of the 31 patients, 24 were non-responders to acute vasodilator testing, 6 were acute responders, and 1 was too unstable to test prior to therapy initiation. The authors reported clinical improvement in all 31 patients and an improvement in pulmonary artery pressure, cardiac index, and pulmonary vascular resistance compared to baseline values. An improvement in survival compared to the conventional therapy alone group was also reported.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Abrupt discontinuation of epoprostenol therapy, including sudden reductions in dosage and interruptions in drug delivery may result in symptoms of rebound pulmonary hypertension. Symptoms can include dyspnea, dizziness and asthenia. One patient death was attributed to the interruption of epoprostenol therapy during clinical trials.Since epoprostenol can cause hypotension, it should be used cautiously with other hypotensive drugs to avoid additive effects.The chronic use of epoprostenol is relatively contraindicated in patients with CHF due to severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction. A large study intending to evaluate the use of epoprostenol in patients with NYHA class III and IV congestive heart failure was terminated after an analysis of 471 patients showed that patients receiving epoprostenol had a higher mortality rate than patients receiving standard therapy only.Epoprostenol is a potent inhibitor of platelet aggregation. Pulmonary edema in these patients may be associated with veno-occlusive disease. Epoprostenol should not be used chronically in patients who develop pulmonary edema during dose ranging.Unless contraindicated, anticoagulant therapy should be administered to patients receiving epoprostenol to reduce the risk of thromboembolic disease, including pulmonary thromboembolism or systemic embolism through a patent foramen ovale.Given the indications for use, epoprostenol would not be expected to be used during pregnancy.     General Information
    Epoprostenol is a potent inhibitor of platelet aggregation. Therefore, an increased risk for hemorrhagic complications should be considered, particularly for individuals with other risk factors for bleeding. Risk factors for bleeding in individuals with pulmonary arterial hypertension include anemia, collagen-vascular disease, congenital heart disease, esophageal varices, GI angiodysplasia, hemoptysis, hepatic disease, renal impairment, renal failure, peptic ulcer disease, and geriatric age.
    Headline
    pulmonary veno-occlusive disease
    General Information
    Some individuals with primary pulmonary hypertension have developed pulmonary edema during dose initiation. Pulmonary edema in these individuals may be associated with pulmonary veno-occlusive disease. Pulmonary vasodilatory therapies, such as epoprostenol, may cause life-threatening pulmonary edema in individuals with pulmonary veno-occlusive disease. If an individual develops pulmonary edema during initiation of epoprostenol, discontinue therapy and do not re-initiate epoprostenol.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Limited data on the use of epoprostenol during pregnancy have not established an increased risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. Untreated pulmonary arterial hypertension is associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes, including maternal and fetal death. In animal reproductive studies, there was no evidence of fetal harm at exposures 2.5 and 4.8 times the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) in rats and rabbits, respectively. It is not known if epoprostenol crosses the placenta; however, because it is rapidly hydrolyzed in the plasma after administration, it is unlikely that the fetus would be exposed to a clinically significant amount of drug. In addition, epoprostenol is a naturally-occurring prostaglandin in the fetus, and therefore, it is unlikely that administration to a pregnant person would result in direct adverse effects on the embryo or fetus. There is limited experience with the use of epoprostenol in the treatment of severe preeclampsia. Fetal bradycardia, fetal heart rate deceleration, and death were reported and were not attributed to the drug but rather to the severity of the disease in the pregnant individual. In addition, administration did not result in changes in the pulse of the pregnant individual, fetal pulse rate, or uterine contractility in a study of 13 individuals with either superimposed preeclampsia or preeclampsia. There was also no change in placental or fetal blood flow. Epoprostenol has also been used in pregnant individuals with pulmonary hypertension. One individual was successfully treated with both intravenous and inhaled epoprostenol with no adverse effects reported on the infant. In another case report, 3 individuals with pulmonary arterial hypertension received epoprostenol throughout their pregnancy and delivered with no adverse effects noted in the infants. The authors concluded that its use in stable individuals may enable successful completion of pregnancy, but caution that pregnancy in those with pulmonary hypertension poses high risk to both the pregnant individual and child and should be avoided.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use epoprostenol with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of epoprostenol in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Due to the drug's very short half-life, it is unlikely to enter breast milk in significant amounts, persist for an extended period, or remain stable in the infant's gastrointestinal tract. Oral absorption by the breastfed infant is therefore unlikely. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition.

Description
  Text
  Epoprostenol is the pharmaceutical form of a naturally-occurring prostaglandin commonly referred to as prostacyclin (other names include prostaglandin I2, PGI2, prostaglandin X, PGX). Analogs of prostacyclin (e.g., iloprost, treprostinil), have also been developed. Epoprostenol (prostacyclin) is a vasodilator and an inhibitor of platelet aggregation. Its major use is for the long-term treatment of primary pulmonary hypertension (PPH) where it improves symptoms and prolongs survival. Intravenous epoprostenol has been reported to significantly improve long-term survival over 3 years for PPH patients compared to historical data. Epoprostenol has been investigated for use in ischemic vascular diseases and congestive heart failure. Epoprostenol was approved for IV use in the treatment of PPH by the FDA in December 1995. Continuous IV epoprostenol therapy also improves exercise capacity and cardiopulmonary hemodynamics in patients with pulmonary hypertension due to scleroderma. Nebulized epoprostenol solutions have been effective in clinical trials, but more study is needed. The FDA approved epoprostenol for the treatment of pulmonary hypertension in scleroderma patients in April 2000.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The actions of epoprostenol, vasodilation of pulmonary and systemic arterial vascular beds, and platelet aggregation, are in direct opposition to thromboxane A2, another prostaglandin. The inhibitory action of epoprostenol on platelet aggregation is mediated by the stimulation of adenylate cyclase and subsequent increase in cyclic AMP in platelets. Hemodynamic effects of epoprostenol are due to increases in cyclic AMP in vascular smooth muscle and subsequent vasodilation. The hemodynamic effects of epoprostenol include decreased pulmonary vascular resistance, increased cardiac index, and increased oxygen delivery. Hypotension, however, is also a hemodynamic effect.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Intravenous Route
    Description
    Hemodynamic changes due to epoprostenol returned to baseline within 10 minutes of ending 60-minute intravenous infusions of 1 to 16 ng/kg/minute.
  Text
  Epoprostenol is administered intravenously. However, it has also been used for inhalation successfully in the treatment of primary pulmonary hypertension. Epoprostenol is rapidly hydrolyzed at neutral pH in blood and also subject to enzymatic degradation. Epoprostenol is extensively metabolized. It is metabolized to 2 primary metabolites, both of which have pharmacological activity of orders of magnitude less than epoprostenol in animal studies, and 14 additional minor metabolites have been isolated from urine. After tritium-labeled epoprostenol administration to humans, the recovery of radioactivity in urine and feces over 1 week was 82% and 4% of the administered dose, respectively. The in vitro half-life of epoprostenol in human blood at 37 degrees C and pH of 7.4 is approximately 6 minutes; therefore, the in vivo half-life of epoprostenol is expected to be no more than 6 minutes.

Reconstitute epoprostenol according to the FDA-approved product labeling. Only prepare Flolan with the provided pH 12 Sterile Diluent. Prepare Veletri with Sterile Water for Injection or 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection.
Select a concentration for the epoprostenol solution that is compatible with the infusion pump being used in respect to flow rates and reservoir capacity. Veletri:
For use at room temperature (25 degrees C [77 degrees F]): Reconstituted solutions with a concentration of 3,000 to less than 15,000 ng/mL are stable for 48 hours if used immediately and 24 hours if the solution was stored refrigerated; reconstituted solutions with a concentration of 15,000 to less than 60,000 ng/mL are stable for 48 hours if used immediately or if stored refrigerated; reconstituted solutions with a concentration of 60,000 ng/mL or more are stable for 72 hours if used immediately and 48 hours if the solution was stored refrigerated.
For use at temperatures 26 to 30 degrees C (78 to 86 degrees F): For solutions with a concentration less than 60,000 ng/mL, change the pump reservoir every 24 hours. For solution concentrations 60,000 ng/mL or more, a single reservoir may be administered up to 48 hours (immediately after reconstitution or after up to 8 days of refrigeration).
For use at temperatures 31 to 40 degrees C (87 to 104 degrees F): Solutions with a concentration of 60,000 ng/mL can be immediately administered for up to 24 hours.
 
IV Infusion
Administer epoprostenol continuously via a central venous catheter using an ambulatory infusion pump. Temporary peripheral IV infusion may be used until central access is established.
The infusion pump used should be 1) small and lightweight, 2) be able to adjust infusion rates in 2 ng/kg/minute increments, 3) have alarms for occlusion, end of infusion, and low battery, 4) be accurate to +/- 6% of the programmed rate, and 5) be positive-pressure driven with intervals between pulses no more than 3 minutes apart. The reservoir should be made of polyvinyl chloride, polypropylene, or glass. Use a 60-inch microbore non-DEHP extension set with proximal antisyphon valve, low priming volume (0.9 mL), and in-line 0.22-micron filter.
Patients should have access to a backup infusion pump to avoid interruptions in therapy.
Do not administer with any other parenteral medications or solutions.
