Drug Class: Ethambutol is used in the treatment of mycobacterial infections including tuberculosis and atypical mycobacterial infections.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 25 mg/kg/day (Max: 2.5 g/day) PO is the FDA-approved maximum dosage; however, higher intermittent doses have been used off-label.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): blurred vision, optic neuritis, scotomata, visual impairment, confusion, dizziness, hallucinations, headache, leukopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, anaphylactoid reactions, exfoliative dermatitis, lymphadenopathy, myocarditis, pericarditis, pneumonitis, rash (unspecified)

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: The primary route of elimination is via the kidneys, so ethambutol should be used with caution in patients with renal impairment. Dosage adjustments may be necessary (see Dosage).

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  The primary route of elimination is via the kidneys, so ethambutol should be used with caution in patients with renal impairment. Dosage adjustments may be necessary (see Dosage).
 
Ethambutol is contraindicated in patients with known optic neuritis unless clinical judgment determines that it may be used. Ethambutol is also contraindicated in patients who are unable to appreciate and report visual side effects or changes in vision (i.e., young children or unconscious patients). Ethambutol has been associated with optic neuritis characterized by central and peripheral scotomas, decreases in visual acuity, and constriction of visual fields (see Adverse Reactions). Preexisting ocular disease can be potentiated. All patients should be closely monitored for changes in visual acuity. In certain patients with visual defects (e.g., cataracts, recurrent inflammatory conditions of the eye, optic neuritis, and diabetic retinopathy), evaluation of visual acuity changes is more difficult; care should be taken to determine if the variations in vision are not due to the underlying disease conditions. Since the incidence increases with increasing doses, a pretreatment ophthalmologic exam should be performed, in addition to monthly testing in patients receiving doses greater than 15 mg/kg/day. These visual effects are generally reversible when ethambutol is promptly discontinued. However, irreversible blindness has been reported. Ethambutol should not be used in patients who are unable to appreciate and report visual adverse effects or changes in vision (e.g., unconscious patients).
 
Ethambutol should be used cautiously in patients with hepatic disease. Hepatotoxicity including fatalities have been reported. Baseline and periodic hepatic function assessment should be performed.
 
Ethambutol can cause hyperuricemia. Uric acid levels should be measured periodically in patients with a history of gout.
 
Ethambutol should not be used in children whose visual acuity cannot be adequately assessed. Children < 13 years are generally considered too young for accurate assessment and safe and effective use in this age group have not been determined.
 
Ethambutol is classified in FDA pregnancy risk category B. The drug crosses the placenta and results in fetal plasma concentrations that are approximately 30% of maternal plasma concentrations. Although safe and effective use of ethambutol during pregnancy has not been established, it has been used (in combination with isoniazid or isoniazid plus rifampin) in pregnant women to treat tuberculosis. No fetal adverse effects have been reported.
 
Ethambutol should be used cautiously in women who are breast-feeding. Ethambutol is distributed into human breast milk at concentrations similar to maternal serum concentrations. However, according to the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) problems have not been noted in nursing infants.

    Ethambutol dosage adjustment is required in individuals with renal impairment or renal failure, as ethambutol is primarily excreted by the kidneys. Assess renal function at baseline and periodically during ethambutol therapy.
    Headline
    cataracts, children, diabetic retinopathy, infants, loss of consciousness, neonates, ocular inflammation, optic neuritis
    General Information
    Ethambutol is contraindicated in individuals with known optic neuritis, unless clinical judgment supports its use. Ethambutol is also contraindicated in individuals unable to recognize or report visual adverse events or changes in vision, such as those with loss of consciousness and young children, including neonates and infants; however, ethambutol has been used off-label in these pediatric populations. Because ethambutol may have adverse effects on vision, perform ophthalmoscopy, finger perimetry, and testing of color discrimination as part of physical examination in an individual receiving ethambutol. Assess visual acuity prior to initiation and periodically during ethambutol therapy, with monthly testing recommended for individuals receiving doses more than 15 mg/kg/day. In individuals with visual defects, such as cataracts, recurrent ocular inflammation, optic neuritis, or diabetic retinopathy, interpretation of visual changes may be difficult; carefully assess whether observed changes are drug-related or due to underlying disease, and weigh the expected therapeutic benefits against the potential for visual deterioration.
    Headline
    obesity
    General Information
    Optimal ethambutol dosing for individuals with obesity is not established. Ethambutol dosing is based on lean body weight, in general.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Use ethambutol during pregnancy only if the benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of ethambutol use during pregnancy. There have been reports of ophthalmic abnormalities in children born to individuals on antituberculosis therapy that included ethambutol. Ethambutol has been shown to be teratogenic in animal studies when given in high doses. Tuberculosis guidelines support treatment initiation during pregnancy whenever the probability of the pregnant individual's disease is moderate to high due to the risk of untreated tuberculosis to the individual and the fetus.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Ethambutol is considered compatible with breast-feeding. Ethambutol is excreted in human milk. Tuberculosis guidelines encourage breast-feeding for individuals who are noninfectious and being treated with first-line agents, such as ethambutol, as the small concentrations of these drugs measured in the milk have not been reported to produce adverse effects in the breastfed child.

  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information

    General Information
    Optic neuritis, manifested by decreased visual acuity (visual impairment / blurred vision), loss of color discrimination (color blindness), constriction of visual fields, and central and peripheral scotomata, is the most significant adverse reaction to ethambutol therapy. Visual changes may be unilateral or bilateral. These visual changes are dose-related and usually reversible over a period of weeks to months following discontinuation of ethambutol therapy. Optic neuritis is most frequently encountered with doses of 25 mg/kg/day and after 2 months of treatment. Patients with cataracts, inflammatory eye conditions, diabetic retinopathy, and optic neuritis should be closely monitored for changes in visual acuity. Some patients have received ethambutol again after such recovery without recurrence of loss of visual acuity. A pretreatment ophthalmologic exam should be performed on these patients, in addition to monthly testing in patients receiving doses greater than 15 mg/kg/day. Irreversible blindness has been reported.

Description
  Text
  Ethambutol is used in the treatment of mycobacterial infections including tuberculosis and atypical mycobacterial infections. It is considered a first-line antituberculosis agent and is used in combination with isoniazid, pyrazinamide, rifampin, and/or streptomycin for treating isoniazid-susceptible organisms and when resistance to isoniazid or rifampin has been confirmed. Ethambutol appears to be more effective and less toxic than other antitubercular agents. Ethambutol was approved by the FDA in 1967.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Ethambutol is primarily bacteriostatic, although in higher doses it also exhibits bactericidal properties. The exact mechanism is not known; however, it appears to inhibit RNA synthesis, resulting in impaired cellular metabolism and multiplication. Ethambutol is effective only against bacilli that are actively dividing. Cross-resistance between ethambutol and other antitubercular agents has not been demonstrated.
 
In general, the following organisms are susceptible to ethambutol: M. tuberculosis; M. bovis; M. marinum; and some strains of M. kansasii, M. avium, M. fortuitum, and M. intracellulare.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Approximately 75—80% of an ethambutol oral dose is absorbed. Peak serum levels are attained within 2—4 hours.
  Text
  Ethambutol is administered orally. Ethambutol is widely distributed, with high concentrations in the kidneys, lungs, and saliva. It penetrates inflamed meninges (10—50%) to reach therapeutic levels in the CSF. The drug crosses the placenta, resulting in plasma fetal concentrations that are 30% of maternal concentrations, and is distributed into breast milk in amounts similar to maternal plasma levels. Ethambutol is partially metabolized in the liver. The parent drug and its metabolites are excreted primarily in the urine (65%), with the remaining 20—25% excreted unchanged in the feces. The elimination half-life is 3.5 hours and can be up to 15 hours in renal disease.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  Reduce ethambutol dose in patients with renal impairment, since the main path of excretion of this drug is by the kidneys.
