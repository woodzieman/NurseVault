        Dosage Description
        Usual dosage range: Green tea extract, standardized to provide 150 to 690 mg/day of EGCG PO; dosages as high as 856.8 mg/day of EGCG have been reported effective without clinically significant elevations of liver function tests. Limit use to 12 weeks unless supervised by health care practitioner. Average reported weight loss at 12 weeks is small (approximately 0.5 to 1.5 kg). In one clinical trial, the use of decaffeinated green tea extract (GTE) catechin complex supplementation for a duration of 1 year had no effect on overall adiposity in overweight or obese postmenopausal individuals vs. placebo. The mean daily GTE dose was 1,315 +/- 116 mg/day (843 +/- 44 mg as EGCG) PO, which is approximately equivalent to 5 servings/day of brewed green tea. Results a 2012 review of 15 studies indicated that green tea (brewed tea or dietary supplements) produced statistically nonsignificant weight loss, decrease in BMI, and decrease in waist circumference compared to control following 12 weeks of use. Green tea had no effect on weight maintenance. Many of the trials included in the review had methodological deficiencies.

        Heading
        Postmenopausal Adults
        Dosage Description
        1 to 4 cups/day of brewed green tea PO is suggested for maintaining bone health based on epidemiological studies. One meta-analysis examined 18 studies (n = 48,615) and results indicated that postmenopausal individuals who habitually consumed tea had higher BMD at several skeletal sites, including the lumbar spine, greater trochanter, femoral neck, and ward's triangle. Additionally, green tea drinkers had a lower risk of osteoporosis (OR 0.41; 95% CI 0.26 to 0.67; p less than 0.001) and fracture (OR 0.81; 95% CI 0.67 to 0.98; p = 0.031). Green tea extract dietary supplements have not yet been proven to provide similar benefit. In one clinical trial, the use of a decaffeinated green tea extract (GTE) catechin complex supplement for a duration of 1 year had no effect on overall BMD in postmenopausal overweight or obese individuals compared with placebo. The mean daily GTE catechin dose was 1,315 +/- 116 mg/day (843 +/- 44 mg as EGCG) PO, which is approximately equivalent to 5 servings/day of brewed green tea.

        1.5 to 2 cups/day of brewed green tea PO (moderate intake) has been associated with lower all-cause cardiovascular disease mortality versus no consumption in one meta-analysis.   Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  NOTE: The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has not evaluated the safety and efficacy of green tea. Products containing green tea are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Consumers should also be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and purity of these products.Green tea is contraindicated for use in patients with a history of sensitivity to it or any of its components.Because green tea inhibits platelet aggregation and demonstrates fibrinolytic activity in animals , use caution when bleeding is a concern. This may include individuals who are receiving anticoagulant therapy or thrombolytic therapy.Because green tea potentially inhibits platelet function , patients should inform their health care professionals that they take green tea prior to scheduling surgery. While no data exists with green tea, the discontinuation of other platelet inhibitors (i.e., garlic, ginseng) at least 7 days prior to surgery is recommended. It may be prudent to follow similar recommendations with green tea.Green tea has been shown to inhibit the absorption of nonheme iron; a reduction of hemoglobin and ferritin, but not anemia, has been demonstrated in adult patients who consume large amounts of green tea (>= 10 cups daily). While it does not appear that consumption of green tea causes anemia in populations not at risk for iron deficiency, it may be prudent to avoid large volumes of green tea in those patients with preexisting anemia or in those patients at risk for developing iron-deficiency anemia. Microcytic anemia has been observed in infants who consume between 50—750 ml (median 250 ml) of tea daily.Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. Caffeine is a central nervous system stimulant. Caffeine should be used cautiously in patients with anxiety disorders and/or panic disorder because it can aggravate these conditions. Patients suffering from insomnia should not consume caffeine, nor should caffeine be consumed prior to retiring because it can cause insomnia. In overdoses, caffeine has been associated with seizures and it should be prescribed cautiously to those patients with a seizure disorder.Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. Caffeine should be used cautiously in those patients with cardiac disease. Caffeine can stimulate the force of contraction and can increase heart rate. It may increase left ventricular output and stroke volume. Patients who have angina or a history of cardiac arrhythmias should minimize their intake of caffeine. Caffeine should not be taken in the first few days—weeks after a myocardial infarction. Patients with hypertension should minimize their intake of caffeine; however, regular tea consumption (5 cups daily for 7 days) did not have an affect on ambulatory blood pressure monitoring in mildly hypertensive patients.Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. Caffeine should be used cautiously in those with hepatic disease or hepatic impairment. Caffeine clearance may be delayed, leading to toxicity. Also, renal impairment or renal failure may delay caffeine clearance. .Green tea has been shown in vitro to decrease serum glucose concentrations. In addition, some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine and although the effects are mild, caffeine can either raise or decrease blood sugar. Patients with diabetes mellitus should not receive or should minimize their intake of caffeine and monitor their serum glucose concentrations more closely when consuming any green tea product.Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. Patients with thyroid disease, especially hyperthyroidism, should not receive or should minimize their intake of caffeine. The stimulatory effects of caffeine can be augmented in hyperthyroidism.Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. Caffeine can stimulate gastric secretions. Patients with peptic ulcer disease should minimize their intake of caffeine because the condition can be aggravated.Minimal data on the use of green tea in infants and children are available. Green tea demonstrates little toxicity in adults; however, because some green tea products contain caffeine, green tea should be minimized in children 500 mg/day) of caffeine and increased rates of low birth weight, spontaneous abortion, difficulty in getting pregnant or infertility is still controversial, as some studies have not controlled for concomitant cigarette smoking. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of caffeine administration in pregnant women. Neonatal arrhythmias (e.g., tachycardia, premature atrial contractions) and tachypnea have been reported when caffeine was consumed during pregnancy in amounts > 500 mg/day; caffeine withdrawal after birth may account for these symptoms.Clinical evidence for the safety of green tea in lactation is not available.Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. Cytochrome P450 metabolism of caffeine is inhibited in infants who are breast-fed; formula feeding does not appear to affect the pharmacokinetics of caffeine in infants. Peak caffeine milk levels usually occur within 1 hour after the maternal ingestion of a caffeinated beverage; with milk:plasma ratios of 0.5—0.7 reported. Although only small amounts are secreted in breast-milk, caffeine can accumulate in the neonate if maternal ingestion is moderate to high. Higher caffeine intake (> 500 mg/day) by a nursing mother may cause irritability or poor sleeping patterns in the infant who is breast-feeding. Although the American Academy of Pediatrics generally considers the usual use of caffeinated beverages to be compatible with lactation, nursing mothers should limit their intake of beverages containing caffeine if possible. Caffeine containing drug-products should be used cautiously during lactation due to their high caffeine contents.Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. Tobacco smoking (cigarettes) has been shown to increase the clearance of caffeine. Also, passive smoke exposure may cause an increase in caffeine clearance. This may help to explain why tobacco smokers often have concomitantly high caffeine intakes. Tobacco smoke contains hydrocarbons that induce hepatic CYP450 microsomal enzymes. Because the effect on hepatic microsomal enzymes is not related to the nicotine component of tobacco, sudden smoking cessation may result in a reduced clearance of caffeine, despite the initiation of nicotine replacement. Caffeine dosage may need to be reduced at the cessation of smoking.

    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Clinical evidence for the safety of green tea supplements in pregnancy is not available. However, beverages containing green tea are Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) to the general population in usual dietary intakes. Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. It is generally recommended to limit the intake of caffeine-containing beverages during pregnancy, like coffee, teas, and sodas (usually no more than 1 to 2 caffeine-containing beverages/day) or avoid them if possible. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) states that moderate caffeine consumption (less than 200 mg/day) does not appear to be a major contributing factor in miscarriage or preterm birth. The relationship between moderate caffeine intake and intrauterine growth restriction as well as high caffeine consumption and miscarriage is unclear. Studies investigating caffeine use during pregnancy are often limited by small sample size, inconsistency in exposure reporting, and retrospective design subject to recall bias. Low to moderate caffeine intake also does not appear to increase the risk of congenital malformations. Several case-control studies have noted an association between higher caffeine intake (300 mg/day or more) and congenital malformations (craniosynostosis, spina bifida), but these findings have not been consistent across studies, and other studies have not identified an increased risk. Neonatal arrhythmias (e.g., tachycardia, premature atrial contractions) and tachypnea have been reported when caffeine was consumed during pregnancy in amounts more than 500 mg/day; caffeine withdrawal after birth may account for these symptoms.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Green tea supplements should be used with caution during breast-feeding due to a lack of safety data for these products during lactation. However, beverages and food products containing green tea are Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) for consumption by the general population. Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. Peak caffeine milk levels usually occur within 1 hour after the maternal ingestion of a caffeinated beverage with milk to plasma ratios of 0.5 to 0.7 reported. Preterm and younger newborn infants metabolize caffeine very slowly and may have serum levels of caffeine and other active caffeine metabolites similar to maternal levels, so a lower maternal intake level preferable when breast-feeding these infants. Higher maternal caffeine intake (more than 500 mg/day) may cause irritability or poor sleeping patterns in the breastfed child. Although the usual use of caffeinated beverages is considered compatible with lactation, breast-feeding individuals should limit their caffeine intake if possible.

Occult sources of vitamin K include green tea and green tea dietary supplements. Published data are limited in regard to this interaction. A patient with previous INRs of 3.2 and 3.79 on a dose of 7.5mg daily of warfarin (goal INR 2.5 to 3.5) had an INR of 1.37. One month later, the patient's INR was 1.14. The patient admitted that he had started consuming 0.51 gallon of green tea daily approximately one week prior to the INR of 1.37. The patient denied noncompliance and other changes in diet, medications, or health. The patient discontinued green tea and one week later his INR was 2.55. While the amount of vitamin K in a single cup of brewed green tea may not be high (0.03 mcg/100 g), the actual amount may vary from cup to cup depending on the amount of tea leaves used, the length of time the tea bags are allowed to brew, and the volume of tea consumed. Additionally, if a patient drinks multiple cups of tea per day, the amount of vitamin K could reach significance. It is recommended that patients on warfarin maintain a stable intake of green tea.

Bortezomib: (Major) Green tea constituents, in particular (-)-epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) and other polyphenols with 1,2-benzenediol moieties, have been shown to effectively inhibit bortezomib-induced tumor cell death in multiple myeloma and glioblastoma cell lines in vitro and in vivo. EGCG directly reacts with boronic-acid based proteasome inhibitors, like bortezomib, and blocks the proteasome inhibitory function. As a result, when bortezomib and green tea products are coadministered, bortezomib cannot trigger endoplasmic reticulum stress or capsase-7 activation and therefore, cannot induce tumor cell death. This interaction is likely to occur in humans at concentrations of 2-5 mcM EGCG, which are achievable with the use of green tea supplements. The authors of this study suggest that it may be prudent to avoid green tea when patients with multiple myeloma or glioblastoma are receiving bortezomib.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: The majority of adverse reactions that may be experienced after consumption of green tea are related to caffeine. Adverse reactions to caffeine are generally dose-related. A typical cup of green tea contains 50—100 mg of caffeine. Decaffeinated products are available.In a dose-finding, phase 1 study of green tea extract in patients with solid tumors, all adverse events reported were thought to be due to caffeine (7% concentration in the extract.) The most commonly reported adverse events were gastrointestinal (abdominal bloating, nausea/vomiting, constipation, and dyspepsia) or neurologic (insomnia, paresthesias, restlessness, fatigue, headache, pain, tremors, and agitation) and the frequency and severity increased as the dosage of green tea extract increased. Diaphoresis, cough, polydipsia, and polyuria were also reported at higher doses. Palpitations were reported in three patients (dosage of 2.25 g/m2 daily as a single dose, 3.37 g/m2 daily as a single dose, and 4.08 g/m2 daily in three divided doses). At a dosage of 1 g/m2 three times daily (equivalent to 7—8 cups of green tea three times daily), adverse reactions were mild and tolerable. This dosage was tolerated for up to 6 months.Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. Caffeine is a CNS stimulant. Many adverse reactions to caffeine are an extension of caffeine's pharmacologic actions. At therapeutic or nontoxic doses, caffeine can cause tremor, sinus tachycardia, and heightened attentiveness. Other adverse reactions include diarrhea, excitement, irritability, insomnia, headache, muscle twitches and palpitations. Alterations in blood glucose, such as hyperglycemia or hypoglycemia, have been reported. After excessive doses, caffeine can cause considerable nausea/vomiting and anxiety. Cardiac arrhythmias, seizures, and delirium are possible after deliberate overdoses. In humans, a caffeine level of > 50 mg/L may produce toxic symptoms.Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. Caffeine is a mild diuretic. Polyuria can occur. Increased creatinine clearance and increased urinary calcium (hypercalciuria) and sodium excretion are reported in the literature.Some, but not all, green tea products contain caffeine. High caffeine intake has been reported to cause spermatogenesis inhibition in male animals, as noted by spermatogenic cell degeneration within the testes. Although controversial, infertility, as manifested by increased difficulty in getting pregnant, has been reported in females. Couples who are pursuing pregnancy should probably limit excessive intake of caffeine. Description
  Text
  Tea is one of the most commonly consumed beverages in the world, second only to water; worldwide consumption of tea per capita is estimated to be 120 mL/day. Green, black and oolong tea are manufactured from the plant Camellia sinensis. Green tea is usually steamed or pan-fired from freshly harvested leaves to prevent oxidation and preserve tea polyphenols. Oolong tea is partially oxidized and black tea is fully oxidized and therefore, these tea varieties contain less polyphenol than green tea. Historically, black tea accounts for approximately 80% of tea consumed worldwide, oolong tea accounts for approximately 1% of tea consumed, and green tea approximately 20% of tea consumed. Historically, green tea has been consumed primarily in Asia, North Africa, and the Middle East. Green tea has many purported health benefits. Unfortunately, very few large, randomized prospective, clinical trials are available. However, large observational and epidemiologic studies are available and are the basis for many of the proposed health effects of green tea. These studies have found lower incidences of heart disease, osteoporosis, and dental caries in populations who regularly consume green tea beverages. Green tea also has thermogenic effects and is promoted for maintaining healthy body weight, glucose metabolism and body composition, but the effects are thought to be small. Finally, green tea or green tea extract appears to have some cancer protective effects and continues to be investigated for prevention and adjunctive treatment of various cancers.  
NOTE: In the U.S., herbal products and dietary supplements are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for dietary supplements as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since supplements are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for these products and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Green tea composition varies depending on multiple factors including the climate, season, age of the leaf, variety of the leaf, horticulture practices, and manufacture of the tea or green tea extract. Dried green tea leaves can contain 30% to 40% catechins (polyphenols). The chemical composition of green tea is complex: proteins (15% to 20% dry weight); amino acids (1% to 4% dry weight, such as theanine); carbohydrates (5% to 7% dry weight such as cellulose, pectins, glucose, fructose, and sucrose); minerals/trace elements (5% dry weight such as calcium, magnesium, chromium, manganese, iron, copper, zinc, molybdenum, selenium, sodium, phosphorus, cobalt, strontium, nickel, potassium, fluorine, and aluminum); methylxanthines (caffeine, theophylline); and trace amounts of lipids (linoleic and alpha-linolenic acids), sterols (stigmasterol), vitamins (B, C, E), pigments (chlorophyll, carotenoids), and volatile compounds (aldehydes, alcohols, esters, lactones, hydrocarbons). In addition, phenolic acids such as gallic acids are present.
Catechins: The polyphenol of green tea, especially the catechins, are the constituents that are thought to provide health benefits. Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) is the most prevalent catechin found in green tea; others include epicatechin (EC), epicatechin-3-gallate (ECG), and epigallocatechin (EGC). Green tea extract dietary supplement are usually standardized to EGCG content. Green tea catechins appearappears to have antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticarcinogenic effects, may help regulate blood glucose and have beneficial effects on carbohydrate metabolism, and may increase postprandial thermogenesis and fat oxidation.
Caffeine and other methylxanthines: Green tea contains caffeine unless it is decaffeinated. Fresh green tea leaves contain, on average, 3% to 4% of alkaloids known as methylxanthines, such as caffeine, theobromine, and theophylline, with caffeine the predominant ingredient. Caffeine is a mild, direct stimulant at all levels of the CNS and also stimulates the heart and cardiovascular system. The related xanthine, theophylline, shares these properties. Both caffeine and theophylline are CNS stimulants. The xanthines stimulate the medullary respiratory center and relax bronchial smooth muscle. Caffeine stimulates voluntary muscle and gastric acid secretion, increases renal blood flow, and is a mild diuretic.

  Green tea is administered orally as a brewed tea infusion or as green tea extract prepared from dried tea leaves. In addition, green tea can be applied topically and can be found as a fragrance in cosmetics and as an ingredient in topical formulations for the skin.

Caffeine (if present): Many green tea products contain caffeine. Caffeine is distributed rapidly to all body tissues and readily crosses the blood-brain and placental barriers and is distributed into breast milk. In adults, caffeine is partially metabolized in the liver via demethylation reactions dependent on CYP1A2; caffeine biotransformation by CYP1A2 accounts for a mean value of 84% for paraxanthine, 12% for theophylline, and 4% for theobromine. CYP2E1, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 also play a role in metabolism of caffeine. Metabolites are primarily renally excreted and 2% or less of caffeine is excreted unmetabolized in the urine. The half-life of caffeine can vary from 2.3 to 9.9 hours, with noted significant interindividual variability.

In one study, a decaffeinated green tea extract did not alter the activity of CYP2D6 or CYP3A4 pathways of metabolism. Caffeine is a known substrate of CYP1A2.
