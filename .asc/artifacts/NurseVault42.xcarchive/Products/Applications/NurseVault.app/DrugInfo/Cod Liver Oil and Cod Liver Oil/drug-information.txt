Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Fish oils, including cod liver oil, have been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration as pharmaceuticals; only Omacor® (see Fish Oils, Omega-3 Fatty Acids monograph) has received FDA approval for therapeutic clinical use. However, all fish oils are 'Generally Recognized As Safe' and appear on the FDA's GRAS list. Non-prescription dietary supplement fish oil products containing fish oil are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Limited data are available on use of cod liver oil supplements in a variety of health conditions, including asthma (or other pulmonary disease), atherosclerosis, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, inflammatory bowel disease, migraine, myocardial infarction, psychiatric illness (like bipolar disorder or schizophrenia), rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), various types of nephropathy, and many others. Consumers should also be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products. Some products may contain toxic organochlorines including the pesticides DDT and lindane and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) concentrated in the fish liver prior to harvest. Consumers should be encouraged to consult their health care provider prior to the administration of cod liver oil for the adjunctive treatment of these conditions. Other prescribed therapies should be continued as directed by their health care provider.
 
Cod liver oil products should not be used in any person with known hypersensitivity to any active or inactive ingredient, including fish hypersensitivity. Further, supplementation is contraindicated in those with hypercalcemia, hypervitaminosis D, or hypervitaminosis A. Cod liver oil should be used cautiously in patients with hyperphosphatemia due to the risk of metastatic calcification.
 
Vitamin D, as contained in cod liver oil, should be administered with caution to patients with cardiac disease or those receiving digoxin because hypercalcemia with cardiac arrhythmias can result.
 
Using cod liver oil in patients with chronic renal disease may cause vitamin A induced elevations in alkaline phosphatase concentrations and vitamins A and D induced hypercalcemia, as well as vitamin A toxicity. Cod liver oil should be used with caution in patients with renal disease or renal stones (nephrolithiasis).
 
Cod liver oil should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease or hepatic dysfunction, especially alcoholism, cirrhosis, and viral hepatitis; hepatotoxicity can be potentiated by vitamin A supplementation. Further, the fish oils of cod liver oil may increase alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels; aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels appear to be unaffected. ALT levels should be monitored periodically during therapy. Patients with hepatic disease, gallbladder disease, biliary tract disease, or other GI disease including malabsorption syndrome may not absorb the full dose of vitamin D contained in cod liver oil. Supplementation with ergocalciferol, or another active vitamin D analog, separately may be needed to compensate for reductions in hepatic hydroxylation and decreases in intestinal absorption due to a lack of adequate bile production.
 
Because cod liver oil may increase LDL or total serum cholesterol, it should be used cautiously in patients with hyperlipidemia, hypercholesterolemia, or mixed dyslipidemia. As with any lipid-regulating product, LDL-C levels should be monitored periodically. Additionally, clinicians should be aware that some fish oil supplements increase the daily calorie intake and hence may induce weight gain, which may be detrimental to some patients with cardiovascular risk factors, like obesity.
 
Because cod liver oil inhibits platelet aggregation, caution is advised when fish oils are used concurrently with anticoagulant therapy or thrombolytic therapy (see Drug Interactions). Theoretically, the risk of bleeding may be increased, but studies are limited. If bleeding occurs in any patient taking cod liver oil, consider discontinuation of the supplement. Because of the potential effects on platelet aggregation, fish oils should generally be discontinued prior to surgery. It may be wise to discontinue this supplement 2—3 weeks prior to an elective procedure. Use caution in those patients who need extensive dental work.
 
Patients with sarcoidosis, and possibly other granulomatous diseases, may have increased sensitivity to the effects of vitamin D contained in cod liver oil.
 
Cod liver oil contains ingredients which require caution when used during pregnancy. Fish oil supplements and vitamin D are classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. The use of vitamin A in excess of the recommended dietary allowance (RDA) has been reported to cause fetal abnormalities, including growth retardation and early epiphyseal closure. Excessive vitamin D may lead to hypercalcemia which during pregnancy has been associated with suppression of parathyroid hormone in the neonate, resulting in mental retardation and congenital aortic stenosis. Limited data are available to describe the safety of cod liver oil supplementation during pregnancy. Dietary fish intake is considered a part of a healthy diet during pregnancy, however, non-prescription dietary fish oil supplements should be approached with caution.
 
Vitamin A and vitamin D are excreted in human milk. It is not known whether omega-3-acid ethyl esters are excreted in human milk. The omega-3 fatty acid docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) is present in breast milk under normal physiologic circumstances, and is an important nutritional component. During breast-feeding, omega-3 fatty acids do not appear to be harmful to the infant at levels approximating normal maternal dietary intakes (i.e., via fish in the maternal diet). Until more data are available, however, women should seek the advice of a health care professional before taking cod liver oil supplements during lactation.
 
Limited data are available on use of cod liver oil in children. Until more data are available, encourage patients to seek the advice of a health care professional before giving fish oil supplements to children. Supplementation in infants is not recommended.

    General Information
    Cod liver oil is compatible with breast-feeding. Vitamin A and vitamin D are excreted in human milk. Use of vitamin D within the recommended daily dietary intake for lactating patients is generally recognized as safe. Vitamin A, like other fat-soluble vitamins, is transported into breast milk. Because vitamin A is stored in tissues, the impact of excessive vitamin A supplementation on human milk concentrations is not easily measured; milk concentrations do not appear to increase until a significant change occurs in tissue stores. Use of Vitamin A within the recommended daily dietary intake for lactating patients is generally recognized as safe. There are inconclusive data regarding the infant benefits of exogenous marine oil supplementation by the breast-feeding parent. No infant adverse effects have been reported.

  General Information
  NOTE: Some fish oil preparations are a combination of fish oils with several vitamins and each individual component of a product may need to be evaluated for the presence of side effects. Only those adverse reactions pertaining to fish oils, vitamin A, and vitamin D are discussed in this monograph.
 
It has been observed that cod liver oil products may contain high levels of organochlorines including polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and the pesticides DDT and lindane concentrated by the liver prior to fish harvest. This may place the person ingesting these products at an increased risk of toxicity. Consumers should be advised that good manufacturing practices for dietary supplements are not standardized as they are with prescription drug products.
 
Common dose-dependent side effects of cod liver oil include a fishy odor to the breath (halitosis) or taste perversion (dysgeusia) and gastrointestinal upset (e.g., eructation and dyspepsia). Diarrhea or nausea/vomiting (also associated with excessive levels of vitamin A and vitamin D, see below) may occur at higher daily doses. In placebo-controlled, randomized clinical trials in patients receiving omega-3 acid esters for the adjunct treatment of clinical hypertriglyceridemia, the following additional adverse events were reported: back pain (2.2% fish oil vs. 1.3% placebo); flu syndrome (3.5% fish oil vs. 1.3% placebo); infection (4.4% fish oil vs. 2.2% placebo); pain, unspecified (1.8% fish oil vs. 1.3% placebo); and angina pectoris (1.3% fish oil vs. 0.9% placebo).
 
Prolonged bleeding time is expected to be a rare side effect of the use of cod liver oil. The prolongation of bleeding time reported in selected studies has not exceeded normal limits and did not produce clinically significant bleeding episodes; however, patients should report minor bleeding such as nosebleeds (epistaxis) or bruising (ecchymosis) to their health care professional for evaluation. Theoretically, the risk of such bleeding may be increased with the concurrent use of other drugs known to increase bleeding risk (e.g., anticoagulants or platelet inhibitors); however, some studies have not reported clinically significant bleeding events with the concurrent administration of fish oils (see Drug Interactions). If bleeding does occur, consider discontinuation of the fish oil supplement.
 
Some cod liver oil products increase the daily calorie intake and hence may induce weight gain. Fish oils may also increase total cholesterol levels or LDL and aggravate hypercholesterolemia (also associated with excessive levels of vitamin D, see below) in those patients with a combined hyperlipidemia. In some patients, elevated hepatic enzymes (also associated with excessive levels of vitamin D, see below) were observed; alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels increased without a concurrent increase in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels. Hepatic function, including LDL-C and ALT levels, should be monitored periodically during therapy.
 
Various parameters of immune response are decreased by fish oil intake but this is of uncertain clinical significance with respect to side effects. Allergic reactions appear rare, but skin rash (unspecified) has been an observed side effect in some patients.
 
Hypervitaminosis A syndrome can occur with chronic, excessive doses and is characterized by vitamin A plasma levels over 1200 units/100 ml. Anorexia (also associated with excessive levels of vitamin D, see below), abdominal pain/discomfort, lethargy, malaise, nausea/vomiting are general symptoms. Skeletal manifestations include growth inhibition (also associated with excessive levels of vitamin D, see below), hard tender cortical thickening over the radius and tibia, migratory arthralgia, and premature epiphyseal closure. Central Nervous System manifestations include irritability(also associated with excessive levels of vitamin D, see below), headache (also associated with excessive levels of vitamin D, see below), and increased intracranial pressure as manifested by bulging fontanels, papilledema, and exophthalmos. Dermatologic manifestations include fissures of the lips, drying and cracking of the skin, alopecia, scaling, massive desquamation, and skin hyperpigmentation. Systemic effects include hypomenorrhea (dysmenorrhea), splenomegaly, hepatomegaly or hepatotoxicity, jaundice, or leukopenia. The treatment of hypervitaminosis A consists of immediate withdrawal of the vitamin along with symptomatic and supportive treatment. A dosage of 4000 units/kg daily for 6—15 months has caused chronic vitamin A toxicity. Specific dosages that have been implicated in causing chronic toxicity in adults are 1 million IU daily for 3 days, 500,000 IU daily for 2 months, or 50,000 IU daily for longer than 18 months. In infants, doses of 18,500 units/kg of water-miscible vitamin A administered daily for 1—3 months is associated with chronic toxicity. Acute toxicity can occur at single doses of > 2 million IU in adults or > 350, 000 IU in infants. Acute toxicity is characterized by increased intracranial pressure in 8—12 hours following the overdosage, followed by cutaneous desquamation in a few days. Doses of 25,000 IU/kg have also caused acute toxicity.
 
The risk of teratogenesis increases when given in high doses (e.g., 15,000 IU or more per day) during the first seven weeks of pregnancy. Animal studies indicate that retinoids, but not carotenoids such a beta-carotene, are teratogenic. The major defect identified was cranial-neural-crest defects. Musculoskeletal or urogenital defects and neural-tube defects also occurred but to a lesser extent. The adverse reactions of teratogenesis presented here pertain to preformed vitamin A and not to beta-carotene (see separate monograph).
 
Symptomatic hypervitaminosis D can occur with cod liver oil supplementation. Most adverse reactions are associated with excessive doses and manifest initially as hypercalcemia. Early signs of hypercalcemia include fatigue, somnolence, headache, anorexia, xerostomia, metallic taste, nausea/vomiting, abdominal cramps, abdominal pain, constipation, diarrhea, vertigo, tinnitus, ataxia, exanthema, myalgia, arthralgia, and irritability. Dehydration, photophobia, anorexia, rhinorrhea, hyperthermia, cardiac arrhythmias, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertension are all late symptoms of hypercalcemia. Laboratory abnormalities noticed with late symptoms of hypercalcemia include elevated SGOT (AST), SGPT (ALT), and BUN. In conditions of hypercalcemia, discontinuation of vitamin D containing products and reduction in calcium intake is advocated along with an increase in fluid intake until serum calcium levels return to within normal limits. In addition to hypercalcemia, impairment of renal function is possible with hypervitaminosis D. Symptoms include polyuria, nocturia, polydipsia, hypercalciuria, reversible azotemia, hypertension, nephrocalcinosis, generalized vascular calcification, or irreversible renal insufficiency progressing to renal failure (unspecified) that may result in death. Growth inhibition and an increase in the mineralization of bones with accompanying vague musculoskeletal pain, stiffness, and weakness have been reported in infants and children with hypervitaminosis D. Furthermore, extreme cases of long term hypervitaminosis D have lead to widespread calcification of soft tissues, including the heart, blood vessels, renal tubules, and lungs, and, in rare cases, to mental retardation.
 
The vitamin D contained in cod liver oil increases the absorption of phosphorus and can cause hyperphosphatemia, especially in those with renal failure. Vitamin D containing products should be held in patients with hyperphosphatemia until serum concentrations are corrected. In addition, a non-aluminum phosphate-binding compound and a low-phosphate diet should be used to control serum phosphorus levels in patients undergoing dialysis.
 
Metabolic complications associated with vitamin D, as contained in cod liver oil, include mild metabolic acidosis, anemia, and weight loss.
 
Persons receiving vitamin D therapy have developed bone demineralization or osteoporosis in cases of excessive or inadequate dosing.

Description
  Text
  NOTE: In the US, nutraceuticals are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for nutraceuticals as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since nutraceuticals are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products. Monographs on nutraceuticals are included in CP when reliable clinical data are available. The information presented below is condensed from the best clinical data we could find.
 
As the name implies, cod liver oil is derived from the livers of cod fish. The practice of drinking cod liver oil was started several centuries ago by the fisherman of Scandinavia and Greenland for fortification under cold and harsh working conditions. In the late 1800s, prior to the addition of vitamin D to milk, cod liver oil was used successfully to treat and prevent rickets. Currently, fish oil supplements are promoted for a variety of health conditions including hypertriglyceridemia, migraine prophylaxis, psychiatric conditions, secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, various nephropathies, and also for inflammatory conditions such as asthma, gingivitis, inflammatory bowel disease, and rheumatoid arthritis. Although data for most conditions are limited and preliminary, fish oil supplements, including cod liver oil, are 'Generally Recognized As Safe' (GRAS) by the FDA. The data are most compelling for the ingestion of fish in the diet to maintain a healthy heart. The American Heart Association (AHA) recommends eating fish two times per week as a source of lean protein. Until compelling data indicate that fish oil supplements benefit overall cardiovascular health, the AHA does not recommend the use of supplements for this purpose. In November 2004, the FDA approved a prescription only fish oil supplement capsule Omacor™ (see separate monograph: Fish Oils, Omega-3 Fatty Acids) for the treatment of hypertriglyceridemia.
 
Cod liver oil is used as a dietary supplement and is available in both liquid and capsule forms. It is rich in vitamin A, vitamin D, and omega-3 fatty acids; some commercial formulations may contain additional vitamins and nutrients. The omega-3 content includes two essential fatty acids (EFAs), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). EFAs are not synthesized by the human body and therefore must be consumed in the diet to prevent EFA deficiency. However, caution is advised as cod liver oil products may also contain toxic organochlorines, including the pesticides DDT and lindane and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), which are concentrated in the fish liver prior to harvest. Additionally, high vitamin A and vitamin D concentrations in cod liver oil could lead to hypervitaminosis. Patients should consult a health care provider prior to initiating supplementation for appropriate monitoring and therapy.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The actions of cod liver oil can best be described by the individual effects of the active ingredients, vitamin A, vitamin D, and the omega-3 essential fatty acids eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). Information regarding additional vitamins and nutrients contained in some commercial formulations should be obtained separately.
 
Actions related to vitamin A: Vitamin A acts as a cofactor in mucopolysaccharide synthesis, cholesterol synthesis, and the metabolism of hydroxysteroids. Retinal, which is synthesized from retinol, combines with opsin in the retina to form rhodopsin, which is needed for night vision. Vitamin A deficiency is well known to cause xerophthalmia, a condition of extreme dry eyes. Retinol and retinoic acid are also needed for bone development, testicular and ovarian function, embryonic development, and maintenance of mucosal and epithelial surfaces. Dysfunctional storage and transport of vitamin A can occur in some diseases. It is believed that the ability of vitamin A to scavenge free radicals may somehow be responsible for reducing the risk of developing some types of cancers.
 
Actions related to vitamin D: Activated ergocalciferol promotes renal reabsorption of calcium, increases intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphorus, and increases calcium and phosphorus mobilization from bone to plasma. Calcium is absorbed through formation of a calcium-binding protein in the presence of activated vitamin D. Some evidence suggests that the active moiety of ergocalciferol acts at the level of the cell nucleus to increase plasma calcium and phosphorus. Once plasma saturation of these electrolytes occurs, bone mineralization takes place. Additionally, ergocalciferol has been studied as an independent factor in promoting skeletal strength by increasing muscle fiber growth and in balance by reducing body sway ; a meta-analysis found supplementation with vitamin D reduced the risk of falls in an elderly population by 20 percent.
 
Actions related to essential fatty acids, DHA and EPA: Omega-3 fatty acids, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), are eicosanoid precursors; they maintain cell membrane structure and are key determinants of the behavior of cell membrane-bound enzymes and receptors. There is evidence that omega-3 fatty acids support important physiological and developmental processes. Omega-3 fatty acids can form eicosanoids (prostaglandins, leukotrienes, lipoxins, etc.), can be esterified to and hydrolyzed from tissue glycerolipids, and can be metabolically elongated and desaturated to a variety of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). Omega-3 fatty acids form n-3 eicosanoids that often produce different physiologic actions than those eicosanoids formed by n-6 fatty acids. There is evidence that certain actions of fish oils are dose-dependent, and that important physiologic functions may be altered in response to decreased or increased dietary intake. Some of the specific effects of omega-3 fatty acids are noted below.
•Antiarrhythmic effect: Experimental studies have shown the potential antiarrhythmic effects of fish oils in animals and tissue cultures. Omega-3 fatty acids appear to stabilize myocardial membranes and may result in a reduced susceptibility to ventricular arrhythmia. Clinical trials appear to find a greater response in patients with ischemically mediated arrhythmias. Those patients who suffer from arrhythmias secondary to damaged heart tissue may not benefit from fish oil supplementation and further study is needed.
•Blood pressure: Fish oil appears to decrease free radical production which may increase the bioavailability of nitrous oxide and thereby increase vasodilation. Several clinical studies have suggested that fish oil supplements may cause mild, dose-dependent reductions in systolic or diastolic blood pressure in untreated hypertensive patients; little effect is noted to occur in normotensive patients. However caution is advised as the large doses required to produce a blood pressure lowering effect may increase the risk of adverse bleeding.
•Gestation and infant development: DHA, readily found as a component in breast milk, appears to play an important role in fetal and neonatal visual and neural development. The ingestion of adequate amounts of omega-3 fatty acids in the diet during pregnancy may be protective against preterm delivery or low birth weight. Additional studies suggest a lasting effect for the child with an increase in intelligence test scores at age 4 and a decrease in the risk of childhood onset type 1 diabetes mellitus.
•Hyperlipidemia: Essential fatty acids are thought to inhibit very low-density lipoprotein and triglyceride synthesis in the liver. Triglyceride lowering is the most consistent effect observed with fish oil supplementation; average reductions of 25—50% are reported with doses of 3—6 g/day. Similar to the drug gemfibrozil, fish oils can also modestly (i.e., roughly 10%) increase both LDL-C and total cholesterol levels, particularly in patients with combined dyslipidemia. There is no apparent effect on serum HDL.
•Immune function and inflammation: Fish oil exhibits antiinflammatory actions within the endothelium. Various parameters of immune response (i.e., leukotrienes, etc.) are decreased by fish oil intake, but the actions are of uncertain clinical significance at this time; more study is needed. Limited clinical studies suggest fish oils may be useful as adjuncts to the inflammatory condition rheumatoid arthritis.
•Mental health: The exact mechanism of action of fatty acids in psychiatric disorders is unknown; EPA appears to be the more active moiety possibly exerting effect through the inhibition of phospholipase A2 or protein kinase C , or as an anti-inflammatory . More study is needed to determine the ideal dosage and patient population however correction of fatty acid deficiency does appear to improve depressive and psychotic symptoms.
•Platelet aggregation: Ingestion of marine derived oils results in antithrombotic changes in platelet membranes. The essential fatty acids DHA and EPA incorporate into the phospholipids of platelets thus reducing vasoconstriction and platelet aggregation; dose dependent reductions in thromboxane A levels and increases in prostaglandin levels have been observed. Changes in bleeding time might occur in some individuals.
•Reduction in cardiovascular disease: Reductions in cardiovascular disease have been observed epidemiologically in populations with high intakes of fish oils via a marine diet, such as the Greenland Eskimos and in Norwegian populations from dietary changes following the German invasion in World War II. Subsequently, such effects have also been observed in controlled clinical trials , including increased survival rate following myocardial infarction among patients with high dietary intake of omega-3 fatty acids , reduced risk (48%) of thrombotic stroke in females with the ingestion of fish in the diet , and reduced risk of primary cardiac arrest with high dietary intake of omega-3 fatty acids. The effect of fish oils in patients with known atherosclerosis is felt to be modest, at best. A meta-analysis of 10 randomized controlled trials showed a 24% reduction in fatal myocardial infarction (RR of 0.76, 95% CI 0.66—0.88, P<0.001) and a 16% decrease in all causes of mortality (RR of 0.84, 95% CI 0.76—0.94, P<0.001) with daily supplementation of omega-3 fatty acids. Clinically, the ultimate role of fish oil supplements in primary or secondary cardiac prevention remains to be defined by further research.

  Text
  Cod liver oil is administered orally. Serum concentrations of the omega-3 essential fatty acids eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and of vitamin A (retinol) and vitamin D appear to increase in relation to increased dietary consumption. The distribution, metabolism, and elimination of the primary active ingredients of cod liver oil differ and are known as below.
Vitamin A: Retinol esters of vitamin A are hydrolyzed by pancreatic enzymes in the GI lumen, with subsequent absorption and reesterification. Peak plasma concentrations of retinol are attained in 3—5 hours. Retinol is stored primarily in the liver, with small amounts distributed to the kidneys, lungs, adrenals, retina, and intraperitoneal fat. Most plasma retinol is bound to retinol-binding protein (RBP). Toxic effects occur after storage sites are saturated and the binding capacity of RBP is exceeded. Retinol is conjugated, then oxidized, to produce retinoic acid and retinal. The metabolite of retinoic acid is excreted in the bile. Retinal, retinoic acid, and other metabolites are excreted in the urine and feces. Vitamin A may be excreted unchanged in patients with pneumonia.
Vitamin D: Vitamin D, as ergocalciferol, is stored primarily in the liver and fat depots. The parent compound and its metabolites are bound in plasma to alpha-globulins. The intermediate metabolite of ergocalciferol may be distributed into breast milk following high doses. Ergocalciferol is converted in the liver by the enzyme vitamin D-25-hydroxylase (cytochrome P450 27) to 25-hydroxyergocalciferol, an active intermediate, then further converted to its most active form, 1,25-dihydroxyergocalciferol, in the kidneys. Not all of the metabolites of ergocalciferol have been identified. Elimination of vitamin D and its metabolites occurs principally through the bile, with the remainder excreted renally. The extent of vitamin D absorption can be decreased in patients with GI disease.
Essential fatty acids: EFA are distributed widely and found stored within cells, primarily adipose tissue, throughout the body. Serum concentrations of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) appear to increase in relation to increased dietary consumption.  Fatty acids are eliminated primarily by oxidative catabolism to carbon dioxide and water; small quantities are lost when skin and digestive cells are sloughed.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes: none
