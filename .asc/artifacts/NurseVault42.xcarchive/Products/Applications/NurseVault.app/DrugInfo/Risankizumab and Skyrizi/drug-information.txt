Drug Class: Risankizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that selectively binds the p19 subunit of interleukin-23 and inhibits IL-23-mediated inflammatory signaling.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis: 150 mg/dose subcutaneously at Week 0, Week 4, and every 12 weeks after that.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): fever, infection, influenza, nasal congestion, osteomyelitis, pharyngitis, rhinitis, sinusitis, anaphylactoid reactions, atopic dermatitis, folliculitis, rash, urticaria, ecchymosis, hematoma, injection site reaction, pruritus, arthralgia, arthropathy, back pain, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatotoxicity

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Update immunizations in agreement with current immunization guidelines prior to initiating risankizumab therapy.

    Headline
    General Information
    General Information
    Update immunizations in agreement with current immunization guidelines prior to initiating risankizumab therapy. Avoid the use of live vaccines in patients receiving risankizumab.
Before starting risankizumab, test potential drug recipients for hepatitis B (surface antigen and core antibody), hepatitis C (IgG), and HIV (HIV-1 and HIV-2 antibodies and HIV-1 antigen). Consider ongoing screening (e.g., annually) in people who are at increased risk for HIV, hepatitis B, or hepatitis C infection. Retest for HIV in any person who displays symptoms or other conditions that may suggest HIV. In patients found to be HIV positive, consider involving relevant specialists to ensure the HIV viral load is suppressed through antiretroviral therapy. Similarly, retest for viral hepatitis in any person who develops unexplained elevations in hepatic enzymes. It is recommended to consult a hepatologist when using biological therapy in patients with hepatitis B or C, whether acute (newly diagnosed) or chronic.
    Headline
    infection
    General Information
    Avoid starting risankizumab in patients with any clinically significant active infection until the infection resolves or is adequately treated. In people with chronic infection or a history of recurrent infection, consider the risks and benefits of treatment with risankizumab. Instruct patients to seek medical advice if signs or symptoms of a clinically important infection occur. If a severe or clinically important infection develops or if an infection is not responding to standard therapy, discontinue risankizumab until the infection resolves. Evaluate patients for tuberculosis (TB) infection prior to initiating treatment. Do not administer risankizumab to patients with active TB. Consider anti-TB therapy prior to starting risankizumab in patients with a past history of latent or active TB in whom an adequate course of treatment cannot be confirmed. Monitor all patients for signs and symptoms of active TB during and after risankizumab treatment.
    Headline
    hepatic cirrhosis
    General Information
    Consider using an alternative to risankizumab in patients with evidence of hepatic cirrhosis. If liver enzyme elevation is observed during treatment, promptly investigate the cause to identify potential cases of drug-induced liver injury. Interrupt treatment if drug-induced liver injury is suspected, until this diagnosis is excluded.
    Headline
    planned procedure
    General Information
    Patients who undergo surgery while taking biologic therapy, such as risankizumab, may be at greater risk for postoperative infections. For a planned procedure, balance the risk of postoperative infection against the risk of developing a severe or unstable disease by stopping the biologic therapy. Recommendations for the use of immunomodulatory agents in the perioperative setting vary. The American Academy of Dermatology and National Psoriasis Foundation recommend patients with psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis continue to receive IL-23 inhibitors through low-risk surgeries or procedures. They also suggest that IL-23 inhibitors may be safely continued through intermediate- and high-risk surgeries on a case-by-case basis. The British Association of Dermatologists advise stopping biologic therapy 3- to 5-times the half-life or the length of the treatment cycle (whichever is longer) between the last dose and a planned surgery. Restart the biologic therapy postoperatively if there is no evidence of infection and wound healing is satisfactory.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Data regarding the use of risankizumab during pregnancy are insufficient to establish a drug associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. Human immunoglobulin G (IgG) crosses the placental barrier and increases as pregnancy progresses, peaking during the third trimester. Therefore, risankizumab may be transferred to the fetus, which may cause immunosuppression in the in-utero exposed infant. There may be adverse pregnancy outcomes in individuals with inflammatory bowel disease due to increased disease activity including preterm delivery, low birth weight, and small for gestational age at birth. In animal studies involving pregnant cynomolgus monkeys, no adverse effects on neonatal growth or development were observed following maternal doses of 5 and 50 mg/kg administered from gestation day 20 to parturition. However, a dose-dependent increase in fetal loss was noted in the risankizumab-treated groups (32% and 43% for the 5 mg/kg and 50 mg/kg groups, respectively) compared with the vehicle control group (19%). The increased fetal loss in the 50 mg/kg group was considered related to risankizumab treatment. The 50 mg/kg dose in pregnant monkeys resulted in approximately 5 times the exposure (AUC) in humans administered the maximum recommended induction dose (1,200 mg) and 32 times the AUC of the maximum recommended human maintenance dose (360 mg). The clinical significance of these findings for humans is unknown. Following delivery, most of the adult female monkeys and all infants from the risankizumab group had measurable serum concentrations of risankizumab up to 91 days postpartum. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in people who become pregnant while receiving risankizumab; individuals are encouraged to enroll by calling 1-877-302-2161 or visiting http://glowpregnancyregistry.com.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Because risankizumab may cause immunosuppression in neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero, the risks and benefits should be considered prior to administering live vaccines to these neonates or infants. Transport of endogenous IgG antibodies across the placenta increases as pregnancy progresses and peaks during the third trimester. Therefore, risankizumab may be present in infants exposed in utero. The potential clinical impact of risankizumab exposure in infants exposed in utero should be considered. In studies in pregnant cynomolgus monkeys, all infants in the risankizumab treatment group had measurable serum concentrations of risankizumab up to 91 days after birth. Serum concentrations were below detectable levels at 180 days postpartum.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use risankizumab with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of risankizumab in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) and monoclonal antibodies are known to be transferred into human milk. Risankizumab is a large protein and the amount transferred to human milk is likely to be very low. The effects of local gastrointestinal exposure and limited systemic exposure in the breastfed child are unknown; however, absorption is unlikely because it is probably destroyed in the gastrointestinal tract. The Global Consensus Group for Pregnancy and Inflammatory Bowel Disease considers risankizumab use to be acceptable during breast-feeding. Other recommendations state that waiting at least 2 weeks postpartum before resuming therapy may minimize transfer to the breastfed child. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the medication or from the underlying maternal medical condition.

    fever, infection, influenza, nasal congestion, osteomyelitis, pharyngitis, rhinitis, sinusitis
    General Information
    During plaque psoriasis clinical trials, 22.1% of adult risankizumab recipients and 14.7% of patients in the placebo group developed an infection through 16 weeks of treatment. The majority of these infections were mild to moderate in severity, with 0.4% or less being considered serious infections (e.g., pneumonia, cellulitis, osteomyelitis, sepsis, herpes zoster). The most commonly reported infections were tinea fungal infection (1.1%) and upper respiratory tract infection (13%), which consisted of pharyngitis, nasopharyngitis, sinusitis, tonsillitis, rhinitis, and unspecified bacterial and viral infections. In the Crohn's Disease maintenance study through Week 52, the rate of infections was 32.3% (50.2 events per 100 subject-years) in subjects who received risankizumab 180 mg, 36.6% (60.8 events per 100 subject-years) in subjects who received risankizumab 360 mg, and 36.4% (60.3 events per 100 subject-years) in subjects who received placebo after risankizumab induction. The rate of serious infections was 2.6% (2.7 events per 100 subject-years) in subjects who received risankizumab 180 mg, 5.6% (7.4 events per 100 subject-years) in subjects who received risankizumab 360 mg, and 2.1% (2.4 events per 100 subject-years) in subjects who received placebo after risankizumab induction. During Crohn's Disease Induction clinical trials, upper respiratory infections, including influenza like illness, nasopharyngitis, influenza, pharyngitis, upper respiratory tract infection, viral upper respiratory tract infection, COVID-19, nasal congestion, respiratory tract infection viral, viral pharyngitis, tonsillitis, and upper respiratory tract inflammation were reported in 10.6% of patients receiving risankizumab compared to 9.3% receiving placebo. Pyrexia (fever) (2.6% risankizumab 180 mg vs. 4.9% risankizumab 360 mg vs. 2.8% placebo) and urinary tract infection. (0.6% vs. 3.5% vs. 2.8% placebo) were reported during Crohn's Disease maintenance trials with risankizumab. Pyrexia (fever) (4.7% risankizumab 180 mg vs. 4% risankizumab 360 mg vs. 3.5% placebo) was also reported in ulcerative colitis maintenance trails with risankizumab. Instruct patients to seek medical attention if signs or symptoms of an infection occur. If a patient develops a serious infection or is not responding to standard therapy for an infection, discontinue risankizumab until the infection resolves and monitor the patient closely.

    Headline
    anaphylactoid reactions, atopic dermatitis, folliculitis, rash, urticaria
    General Information
    If a serious hypersensitivity reaction occurs, immediately discontinue risankizumab and administer appropriate treatment. Adverse reactions occurring in more than 0.1% but less than 1% of plaque psoriasis patients treated with risankizumab during clinical trials included folliculitis and urticaria. Hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis (anaphylactoid reactions), have been reported with risankizumab administration. In psoriatic arthritis clinical trials, the incidence of hypersensitivity was higher in the risankizumab group (2.3%) compared to the placebo group (1.3%). The reported hypersensitivity reactions were rash (0.7% with risankizumab vs. 0.6% with placebo), allergic rhinitis (0.3% vs. 0.1%), and facial swelling (0.1% vs. 0%). Rash occured in 0.6% to 4.1% of patients treated for ulcerative colitis during maintenance trials. There was one reported case of anaphylaxis involving a patient who received risankizumab. Patients with anti-risankizumab antibodies had a higher incidence of hypersensitivity reactions (6.3%) compared to those without anti-risankizumab antibodies (3.8%). Eczema (atopic dermatitis) and rash have been reported with postmarketing use of risankizumab.

    General Information
    An injection site reaction was observed in 1.5% of adults with plaque psoriasis and 0.7% to 2.5% of psoriatic arthritis patients treated with risankizumab during clinical trials.     Headline
    abdominal pain, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatotoxicity
    General Information
    In psoriatic arthritis clinical trials, adverse hepatic events occurred in 5.4% of adult patients treated with risankizumab (16.7 events per 100 patient years) compared to 3.9% of patients treated with placebo (12.6 events per 100 patient-years). Of the adverse hepatic events, elevated hepatic enzymes were the most common. Elevations in ALT, AST, and GGT were reported in 2.3%, 1.8%, and 1.1% of patients receiving risankizumab, respectively, compared to 1.7%, 1.3%, and 0.7% of placebo patients. No serious hepatic events were reported. Abdominal pain (upper or lower) was reported in 5.8%, 8.5%, and 4.2% of patients receiving risankizumab 180 mg subcutaneously, risankizumab 360 mg subcutaneously, and placebo, respectively, during Crohn Disease maintenance trials. Serious drug-induced hepatic injury or hepatotoxicity [elevated liver function tests (ALT 54x ULN, AST 30x ULN, and total bilirubin 2.2x ULN) in conjunction with a rash that required hospitalization] was reported in a single patient with Crohn disease after 2 doses of risankizumab 600 mg IV infusion. The hepatic test abnormalities resolved after administration of steroids and risankizumab was subsequently discontinued. Prompt investigation of the cause of a hepatic enzyme elevation is recommended to identify potential cases of drug-induced hepatic injury. Interrupt treatment if drug-induced hepatic injury is suspected, until this diagnosis is excluded. Instruct patients to seek immediate medical attention if they experience symptoms suggestive of hepatic dysfunction.

    General Information
    The development of immunogenicity (antibody formation) may occur with the use of risankizumab. During the 52-week plaque psoriasis clinical trial, approximately 24% of adult patients developed antibodies to risankizumab and, of those subjects, about 57% (14% of all risankizumab recipients) were classified as neutralizing (i.e., associated with reduced drug concentration and loss of efficacy). Lower risankizumab concentrations and reduced clinical response were noted in approximately 1% of patients with high antibody titers. In pediatric patients (age: 6 to 17 years), approximately 15% (13/88) of individuals developed antibodies to risankizumab by Week 52. Of those that developed antibodies, approximately 15% had antibodies classified as neutralizing antibodies. In the 28-week psoriatic clinical trial, approximately 12.1% of adult individuals developed antibodies to risankizumab; none of the patients developed neutralizing antibodies. Antibody formation was not associated with changes in clinical response for psoriatic arthritis. However, patients with anti-risankizumab antibodies had a higher incidence of hypersensitivity reactions (6.3%) and injection site reactions (2.5%) compared to those without anti-risankizumab antibodies (3.8% and 0.7%, respectively). None of the hypersensitivity or injection site reactions resulted in discontinuation of therapy. In the Crohn Disease trials, approximately 3.4% (2/58) of subjects treated with risankizumab induction followed by risankizumab 360 mg maintenance therapy developed antibodies to risankizumab-rzaa by week 64. None of the subjects who developed antibodies to risankizumab-rzaa had antibodies that were classified as neutralizing. No Crohn disease subjects treated with induction followed by risankizumab 180 mg maintenance therapy developed antibodies. During ulcerative colitis trials, antibodies developed in 8.9% or 4.4% of subjects treated with risankizumab induction followed by the 180 mg or 360 mg maintenance regimen, respectively. Of those who developed antibodies, 75 (6.7% of all subjects treated with induction followed by the 180 mg maintenance regimen) or 50% (2.2% of all subjects treated with induction followed by the 360 mg maintenance regimen), respectively, had antibodies that were classified as neutralizing. Incidence and detection of antibody formation are highly dependent on the sensitivity and specificity of the assay. Differences in assay methods preclude meaningful comparisons of the incidence of anti-drug antibodies (ADA) in 1 trial with the incidence of ADA in other trials.

Description
  Text
  Risankizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that selectively binds the p19 subunit of interleukin-23 and inhibits IL-23-mediated inflammatory signaling. Unlike ustekinumab, it does not inhibit IL-12. Risankizumab is indicated for the treatment of adults with moderately to severely active Crohn disease (CD) or ulcerative colitis (UC), where it is used to induce remission and, in individuals who respond to induction, to maintain remission. In the CD trials, 45% of risankizumab-treated adults in ADVANCE achieved clinical remission at week 12 compared with 25% receiving placebo, while 42% achieved CD remission in MOTIVATE compared with 20% receiving placebo; endoscopic response also favored risankizumab. In the maintenance CD trial, responders who continued risankizumab were more likely to maintain clinical and endoscopic benefit than those switched to placebo. In the phase 3 UC induction trial, 20.3% of risankizumab-treated adults achieved clinical remission at week 12 compared with 6.2% receiving placebo. Among induction responders who continued into maintenance, UC clinical remission at week 52 occurred in 45% receiving 180 mg and 41% receiving 360 mg of risankizumab, compared with 26% receiving placebo. Adult guidelines support risankizumab for induction and maintenance of remission in moderately to severely active UC or CD. Risankizumab may increase susceptibility to infection or worsen an existing infection. Individuals should be evaluated for tuberculosis infection before initiating treatment. Risankizumab was initially FDA-approved in 2019.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Risankizumab is a humanized immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) monoclonal antibody that selectively binds to the p19 subunit of human interleukin-23 (IL-23), thereby inhibiting its interaction with the IL-23 receptor. Human IL-23 is a naturally occurring cytokine involved in inflammatory and immune responses. By blocking IL-23 from binding to its receptor, risankizumab prevents the release of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines.

  Text
  Risankizumab is administered subcutaneously and intravenously. Once in systemic circulation, the steady-state volume of distribution is 11.2 L in subjects with plaque psoriasis, and 7.68 L in subjects with Crohn's disease. Although a metabolic pathway has not been characterized, risankizumab is expected to be degraded to small peptides and amino acids via catabolic pathways in the same manner as endogenous human immunoglobulin G (IgG). The drug exhibits a systemic clearance of 0.31 L/day and 0.30 L/day and terminal elimination half-life was approximately 28 days and 21 days in subjects with plaque psoriasis and Crohn's disease, respectively.
