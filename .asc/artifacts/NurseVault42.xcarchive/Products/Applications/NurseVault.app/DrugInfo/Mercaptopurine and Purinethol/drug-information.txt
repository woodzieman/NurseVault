Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Mercaptopurine, 6-MP should be used only by clinicians experienced in administering cancer treatments. Mercaptopurine is FDA-approved specifically for acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL) as part of a combination regimen. The manufacturer considers mercaptopurine ineffective for prophylaxis or treatment of CNS leukemia, acute myelogenous leukemia (AML), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), lymphoma, or solid tumors. Dose-related bone marrow suppression, including anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia, is the most common toxicity of mercaptopurine. Mercaptopurine may have a delayed effect on the bone marrow; it is important to withdraw the medication temporarily at the first sign of an abnormally large decrease blood counts, unless bone marrow aplasia is the goal. Patients who have had prior myelosuppressive chemotherapy or radiation therapy are predisposed to myelosuppression, so mercaptopurine should be administered with extreme caution to these patients. Severe bone marrow suppression, including neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, is a relative contraindication to mercaptopurine depending upon the disease. Patients with aggressive leukemia may require treatment with mercaptopurine despite severe bone marrow suppression. Hematological status (leukocyte and platelet counts) should be monitored closely both during and after mercaptopurine therapy; bone marrow function should be allowed to fully recover between treatments. Bone marrow toxicity may be more profound in patients treated with concurrent allopurinol (see Drug Interactions). This problem could be exacerbated by concurrent administration of drugs that inhibit thiopurine methyltransferase such as olsalazine, mesalazine, or sulfasalazine (see Drug Interactions).
 
Mercaptopurine, 6-MP should not be used in patients whose disease has demonstrated prior mercaptopurine, 6-MP resistance. Do not use mercaptopurine, 6-MP in a patient with mercaptopurine, 6-MP hypersensitivity.
 
Individuals with homozygous thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) deficiency are unusually sensitive to the myelosuppressive effects of mercaptopurine and prone to developing rapid bone marrow suppression following initiation of treatment. Laboratory tests are available, genotypic and phenotypic, to determine TPMT status. Substantial mercaptopurine dosage reductions may be required in homozygous-TPMT deficient patients to avoid the development of life-threatening bone marrow suppression in these patients. Although heterozygous patients with intermediate TPMT activity may have increased mercaptopurine toxicity, this is variable, and the majority of patients tolerate normal doses of mercaptopurine. If a patient has clinical or laboratory evidence of severe toxicity, particularly myelosuppression, TPMT testing should be considered. Concurrent administration of drugs that inhibit TPMT such as olsalazine, mesalazine, or sulfasalazine may exacerbate this issue (see Drug Interactions).
 
Increased susceptibility to infection, especially opportunistic viral or fungal infections, may result from mercaptopurine-induced immunosuppression and myelosuppression. Patients with an active infection should be treated prior to receiving mercaptopurine. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with chemotherapy.
 
Myelosuppressive effects of mercaptopurine can increase the risk of infection or bleeding; therefore, dental work should be delayed until blood counts have returned to normal. Patients, especially those with dental disease, should be instructed in proper oral hygiene, including caution in use of regular toothbrushes, dental floss, and toothpicks.
 
Intramuscular injections should not be administered to patients with platelet counts = 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    nucleotide diphosphatase deficiency, thiopurine methyltransferase deficiency
    General Information
    Patients with nucleotide diphosphatase deficiency (NUDT15) and/or thiopurine methyltransferase deficiency (TPMT) may be at a higher risk for severe myelosuppression due to an accumulation of excessive cellular concentrations of active metabolites. Patients who develop severe or repeated episodes of myelosuppression should be evaluated for TPMT or NUDT15 deficiency using TPMT genotyping or phenotyping (red blood cell TPMT activity) and NUDT15 genotyping. A dose reduction is required in patients with homozygous TPMT or NUDT15 deficiency. Patients with heterozygous TPMT or NUDT15 deficiency may require a dose reduction based on CBC monitoring; patients who are heterozygous for both TPMT and NUDT15 may require larger dose reductions. Monitor complete blood counts (CBC) weekly during therapy; evaluate the bone marrow in patients with prolonged or repeated marrow suppression to assess leukemia status and marrow cellularity. Adjust the mercaptopurine dose to maintain desirable absolute neutrophil count (ANC) and platelet count.
    Headline
    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use mercaptopurine with caution in patients with hepatic failure; initiate therapy at the lowest recommended starting dosage in patients with hepatic impairment. Hepatotoxicity has been reported with mercaptopurine therapy; fatal cases of hepatic necrosis have occurred. Monitor liver function tests weekly at the start of therapy and then monthly thereafter; consider more frequent monitoring in patients receiving other hepatotoxic agents or who have pre-existing hepatic disease. An interruption or discontinuation of therapy may be needed in patients who develop hepatoxicity.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Use mercaptopurine with caution in patients with renal impairment, including renal failure. Initiate therapy at the lowest recommended starting dosage or increase the dosing interval to 36 to 48 hours in patients with renal impairment (CrCl less than 50 mL/min).
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Mercaptopurine may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy, based on human and animal data. Advise pregnant people of the potential fetal risk. Patients receiving mercaptopurine in the first trimester have an increased incidence of fetal loss; the risk of malformation in offspring surviving the first trimester is not known. In a report of 28 patients who received mercaptopurine after the first trimester, 3 patients died prior to delivery, 1 patient experienced intrauterine fetal death, and 1 patient aborted; there were no cases of macroscopically abnormal fetuses. There have been rare reports of intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) in pregnant patients who received mercaptopurine, primarily to treat immunologic conditions (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease, systemic lupus erythematosus). Discontinue mercaptopurine in pregnant patients who develop ICP. In animal studies, mercaptopurine was embryo-lethal and teratogenic at doses less than the recommended human dose.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with mercaptopurine. Mercaptopurine can cause fetal harm. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking mercaptopurine and for 6 months after the last dose. For those that can cause pregnancy in others, contraception should be used during mercaptopurine treatment and for 3 months after the last dose. Based on animal data, mercaptopurine may cause male or female infertility. It is not known if these effects are reversible.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking mercaptopurine and for 1 week after the last dose, due to the risk of serious adverse reactions in the breast-fed child. There are no data on the presence of mercaptopurine or its metabolites in human milk, their effects on the breast-fed child, or their effects on milk production.

Allopurinol: (Major) The concomitant use of mercaptopurine and allopurinol may result in increased mercaptopurine toxicity (e.g., bone marrow suppression, nausea, and vomiting). If these drugs are used together, reduce the mercaptopurine dose to one-third to one-quarter of the usual dose to avoid severe toxicity. Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The major dose-limiting adverse effect caused by mercaptopurine is hematologic toxicity, which includes symptoms such as anemia, pancytopenia, leukopenia, neutropenia and thrombocytopenia. Most patients following a 5-day regimen of mercaptopurine experience the neutropenia nadir around day 16 range: 11—23 days) and a thrombocytopenia nadir near day 15 (range 12—21 days). Life-threatening infections and bleeding have been observed as a consequence of mercaptopurine-induced granulocytopenia and thrombocytopenia. Severe hematologic toxicity may require supportive therapy with transfusions, antibiotics, and colony-support factors. Patients with homozygous thiopurine S-methyltransferase (TPMT) deficiency are particularly sensitive to the myelosuppressive effects of mercaptopurine and prone to developing rapid bone marrow suppression following initiation of treatment; some patients with low or intermediate TPMT enzyme activity are more susceptible to hematologic toxicity than patients with normal TPMT activity. Maintenance dosages and schedules should be adjusted to avoid life-threatening myelosuppression.Nausea/vomiting occasionally occur during mercaptopurine therapy. Anorexia, diarrhea, epigastric distress, stomatitis, or mucositis also have been reported. The latter mucosal symptoms are seen with high-dose therapy. Nausea, vomiting, and anorexia are uncommon during initial therapy, but the incidence may increase with continued treatment. The diarrhea sometimes can be severe enough to require parenteral hydration. An increased risk of pancreatitis may be associated with the use of mercaptopurine in inflammatory bowel disease.Hepatotoxicity has occurred rarely during mercaptopurine therapy and can manifest as jaundice, hyperbilirubinemia, cholestasis, ascites, toxic hepatitis, and hepatic encephalopathy. A small number of deaths have been reported that may have been attributed to hepatic necrosis due to mercaptopurine. In reports of studies with mercaptopurine, the incidence of hepatotoxicity has ranged from 0—6%. Hepatotoxicity may occur at any dosage, but seems to occur more frequently with mercaptopurine doses > 2.5 mg/kg/day. The histological pattern of mercaptopurine hepatotoxicity includes features of both intrahepatic cholestasis and parenchymal cell necrosis, either of which may be predominate. It is not clear how much of the hepatic damage is due to direct liver toxicity from the drug and how much may be due to a hypersensitivity reaction. Usually clinically detectable jaundice appears early in the course of mercaptopurine treatment (1—2 months). However, jaundice has been reported as early as 1 week and as late as 8 years after the start of mercaptopurine therapy. In some patients jaundice has cleared following withdrawal of mercaptopurine and recurred when reintroduced. Liver-function tests should be examined regularly during mercaptopurine therapy. The hepatotoxicity may be a dose-limiting side effect of mercaptopurine.Mercaptopurine-induced cellular destruction can result in tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) which includes hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, and hypocalcemia. Uric acid nephropathy, acute renal failure due renal tubular obstruction, metabolic acidosis, and nephrolithiasis can also occur. These problems are more severe when a highly chemosensitive disease is treated (i.e., leukemia or lymphoma). Aggressive alkalinization of the urine and use of allopurinol can prevent urate nephropathy; close monitoring of serum electrolytes and renal function is recommended. However, it should be noted that concurrent administration of mercaptopurine and allopurinol is not generally recommended (see Drug Interactions).Mercaptopurine also has caused fever and eosinophilia. Mercaptopurine-induced drug fever have been reported very rarely with mercaptopurine. Before attributing fever to mercaptopurine, every attempt should be made to exclude more common causes, such as infection. Mercaptopurine has been associated with skin rash (unspecified) and skin hyperpigmentation. Alopecia has been reported. Oligospermia has also been reported in men following treatment with mercaptopurine.Fetal abortion has been associated with the administration of mercaptopurine during the first trimester.

Description
  Text
  Mercaptopurine is a thiopurine antimetabolite. It is indicated for the treatment of acute lymphocytic/lymphoblastic leukemia as part of a combination regimen. Mercaptopurine tablets are not indicated for the prophylaxis or treatment of central nervous system leukemia or for the treatment of acute myelogenous leukemia, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, lymphoma, or solid tumors. The thiopurine agents are also commonly used off-label to treat inflammatory bowel disease, such as Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis, in both adult and pediatric populations. Patients with thiopurine S-methyltransferase (TPMT) or nucleotide diphosphatase (NUDT15) deficiency may be at a higher risk for developing mercaptopurine toxicity, especially severe myelosuppression.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mercaptopurine (6-MP) is activated via hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyl transferase (HGPRTase) to form 6-thioguanine nucleotides (6-TGN) and thioinosinic acid (TIMP). Additionally, 6-methylthioinosinate (MTIMP) is formed by the methylation of TIMP. 6-TGN is incorporated into nucleic acids in place of purine bases resulting in cell-cycle arrest and cell death. TIMP nucleotide inhibits several reactions involving inosinic acid (IMP), including the conversion of IMP to xanthylic acid (XMP) and the conversion of IMP to adenylic acid via adenylosuccinate. TIMP and MTIMP have been reported to inhibit glutamine-5-phosphoribosylpyrophosphate amidotransferase, the first enzyme unique to the de novo pathway for purine ribonucleotide synthesis. Some mercaptopurine is converted to nucleotide derivatives of 6-thioguanine (6-TG) by the sequential actions of IMP dehydrogenase and XMP aminase, converting TIMP to thioguanylic acid.

  Mercaptopurine is administered orally. It has a volume of distribution exceeding that of total body water (approximately 0.9 L/kg) and has minimal cerebrospinal fluid penetration. Following a single oral 50-mg dose, the median elimination half-life of mercaptopurine oral suspension is 1.3 (range, 0.9 to 5.4) hours and the elimination half-life of mercaptopurine tablets is less than 2 hours. The plasma protein binding averaged 19% over a concentration range of 10 to 50 mcg/L following an IV formulation of mercaptopurine administered at doses exceeding 5 to 10 mg/kg. Mercaptopurine is metabolized via 2 major pathways: thiol methylation and oxidation. Thiol methylation is catalyzed by the polymorphic enzyme thiopurine S-methyltransferase (TPMT) to an inactive metabolite, methyl-mercaptopurine. TPMT activity is highly variable in patients due to a genetic polymorphism in the TPMT gene. During oxidation, mercaptopurine is catalyzed via xanthine oxidase to another inactive metabolite, 6-thiouric acid. Following the oral administration of radiolabeled mercaptopurine in 1 subject, 46% of the dose was excreted in the urine as parent drug and metabolites within the first 24 hours.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Initiate mercaptopurine therapy at the lowest recommended starting dosage in patients with hepatic impairment. Adjust subsequent mercaptopurine doses based on efficacy and toxicity.
  Renal Impairment
  Initiate mercaptopurine therapy at the lowest recommended starting dosage in patients with renal impairment. Adjust subsequent mercaptopurine doses based on efficacy and toxicity. Use the lowest recommended starting dosage or increase the dosing interval to 36 to 48 hours in patients with renal impairment (creatinine clearance less than 50 mL/min).
