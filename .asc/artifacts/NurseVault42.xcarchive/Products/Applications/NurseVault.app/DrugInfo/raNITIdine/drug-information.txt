Drug Class: Ranitidine is an oral histamine type 2-receptor antagonist (H2RA).

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 300 mg/day PO for most indications; up to 6 g/day PO for pathologic hypersecretory conditions.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): pancreatitis, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatitis, jaundice, agitation, blurred vision, confusion, depression, dizziness, drowsiness, hallucinations, headache, insomnia, involuntary movements, malaise, vertigo, av block, bradycardia, premature ventricular contractions (pvcs), sinus tachycardia, agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, hemolytic anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, pancytopenia, thrombocytopenia, galactorrhea, gynecomastia, impotence (erectile dysfunction)

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  Contraindications And Precautions
    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Some ranitidine tablets contain FD and C Yellow No. 5 (tartrazine) which may cause allergic-type reactions (including bronchial asthma) in certain susceptible persons. Although the overall incidence of tartrazine sensitivity in the general population is low, it is frequently seen in people who also have aspirin hypersensitivity.
    Headline
    General Information
    General Information
    Symptomatic response to therapy with ranitidine does not preclude the presence of gastric cancer. Individuals who have a suboptimal response or early symptomatic relapse after completing therapy should consider evaluation for gastric malignancy. In the individual who is self-medicating with nonprescription (OTC) formulations, the continuation of heartburn, acid indigestion, or dyspepsia beyond 2 weeks signals the need to consult a health care provider for evaluation.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Ranitidine should be used cautiously in people with renal impairment or renal failure. Accumulation of ranitidine can occur. Ranitidine dosage should be reduced in individuals with creatinine clearances of less than 50 mL/minute.
    Headline
    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Caution should be observed in people with hepatic failure or dysfunction since ranitidine is extensively metabolized in the liver. However, dosage adjustments are not recommended. In patients with compensated cirrhosis, there are minor but clinically insignificant alterations in ranitidine half-life and clearance.
    Headline
    acute hepatic porphyria
    General Information
    Avoid ranitidine use in people with a history of acute hepatic porphyria. Rare reports suggest that ranitidine may precipitate acute porphyric attacks in patients with acute porphyria.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    No special precautions have been advised for ranitidine use in geriatric adults vs. younger adults, but dosage adjustments are necessary in older adults with reduced renal function. According to the Beers Criteria, H2-receptor antagonists are considered potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) in geriatric patients with delirium/high risk of delirium (potential for new-onset or worsening delirium) and should be avoided in this patient population. Dosage reduction of H2-receptor antagonists is recommended in geriatric adults with a creatinine clearance less than 50 mL/minute due to the potential for mental status changes.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate and well-controlled studies with ranitidine during pregnancy; however, reproduction studies in animals have not demonstrated a risk to fertility or harm to the fetus with ranitidine use. Epidemiological evidence based on the use of ranitidine or other H2-blockers during pregnancy does not suggest an increase in fetal malformations compared to the normal population, even with first trimester use. In another study of human data, exposure to H2-blockers, including ranitidine, was not associated with an increased risk for congenital malformations (adjusted OR 1.03, 95% CI: 0.80, 1.32); also, no such association was found when therapeutic pregnancy terminations were included in the analysis (adjusted OR 1.17, 95% CI: 0.93, 1.46). Exposure to H2-blockers was not associated with perinatal mortality, premature delivery, low birth weight, or low Apgar scores. Guidelines recommend a trial of lifestyle modifications as first-line therapy for heartburn and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) during pregnancy, followed by antacids if lifestyle adjustments are ineffective. For ongoing symptoms, an H2-blocker such as famotidine can be used. Other medications should be reserved for pregnant patients who fail H2-blocker therapy. Self-medication with H2-blockers (OTC formulations) during pregnancy is not recommended. Pregnant patients should see their health care professional for a proper diagnosis and for treatment recommendations. Limited use of single dosages of ranitidine for reducing gastric acid during labor and before obstetric delivery, including caesarian section, have not been noted to adversely affect labor or neonatal outcomes. Some guidelines recommend an H2-blocker (PO or IV) for all women presenting for cesarean delivery.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Ranitidine is compatible with breast-feeding. Histamine 2-receptor antagonists, including ranitidine, are excreted into human milk. According to guidelines, if heartburn/gastroesophageal reflux (GERD) symptoms persist after delivery, antacids and sucralfate are safe to use during breast-feeding due to minimal passage into human milk. If symptoms persist, cimetidine or famotidine may be used. Ranitidine itself is not associated with safety concerns in breastfed children and updated formulations have addressed prior concerns regarding N-nitrosodimethylamine (NDMA) exposure.

Description
  Text
  Ranitidine is an oral histamine type 2-receptor antagonist (H2RA). It is used primarily in the treatment of peptic ulcer disease and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). The actions and indications for ranitidine differ little from other H2RAs. However, compared to cimetidine, ranitidine is 5 to 12 times more potent as an H2RA and has less affinity for the cytochrome P450 hepatic enzyme system. Ranitidine is much less likely than cimetidine to interact with other drugs, although drug interactions with ranitidine do exist. Both proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and H2RAs provide symptom control in non-erosive GERD. However, PPIs are preferred over H2RAs for the healing and maintenance treatment of erosive GERD (i.e., erosive esophagitis) due to superior healing rates. H2RAs may be selected when PPIs are not tolerated, are contraindicated, or are not available. H2RAs are often used for stress ulcer prophylaxis in critically ill patients; however, evidence for benefit is lacking, and use may increase the risk of adverse reactions such as pneumonia and Clostridioides difficile infection. Reserve stress ulcer prophylaxis for patients with risk factors for clinically significant gastrointestinal bleeding (e.g., multiple organ dysfunction, prolonged mechanical ventilation, coagulopathy, persistent shock, concomitant corticosteroid therapy). Ranitidine was FDA-approved in 1984.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Ranitidine is a histamine-2 receptor antagonist (H2RA) that competitively and reversibly inhibits at the H2 receptors, including receptors on the gastric parietal cells. Histamine is a natural substance released from enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells that binds to H2 receptors and stimulates the production of gastric acid by parietal cells. By blocking these receptors, H2-blockers inhibit the secretion of acid, leading to a reduction in overall stomach acidity. H2-blockers decrease basal and nocturnal gastric acid secretion and, to a lesser extent, gastric acid secretion in response to food and other stimuli. Both the acid concentration and volume of gastric secretion are suppressed, while changes in pepsin secretion are proportional to volume output. These medications inhibit basal and nocturnal gastric secretion, as well as secretion stimulated by food and pentagastrin, and have little or no effect on fasting or postprandial serum gastrin levels. Gastric emptying and exocrine pancreatic function are not affected. The healing of peptic ulcer disease and gastroesophageal reflux disease correlates with the extent of gastric acid suppression. Ranitidine does not have anticholinergic activity and does not lower serum calcium in hypercalcemic states.

  Text
  Ranitidine is administered orally. Following systemic absorption, serum protein binding averages 15% and the Vd is approximately 1.4 L/kg. There is some metabolism. The primary route of elimination is renal excretion, with approximately 30% of an orally administered dose recovered unchanged in the urine within 24 hours. The principal urinary metabolite is the N-oxide, which accounts for less than 4% of the dose, while the S-oxide and desmethyl ranitidine each account for about 1%. The remainder of the administered dose is found in the stool. Renal clearance is about 410 mL/minute, indicating active tubular secretion. The elimination half-life is about 2.5 to 3 hours.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: OCT1, OCT2, MATE 1/2-K
Rantidine is a substrate for organic cation transporters (OCT1, OCT2) and multidrug and toxin extrusion proteins (MATE1, MATE2-K), which facilitate its renal secretion. Ranitidine acts as an inhibitor of MATE1/2-K, potentially reducing the renal clearance of other drugs. However, whether this action results in clinically significant drug-drug interactions has not been confirmed by clinical studies.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  It appears that no dosage adjustment is needed. In people with compensated cirrhosis, there are minor but clinically insignificant alterations in ranitidine half-life and clearance.
  Renal Impairment
  FDA-approved renal dose adjustments (Adults and Adolescents 17 years of age receiving adult dosage)
CrCl 50 mL/minute or more: No dosage adjustment needed.
CrCl less than 50 mL/minute: Reduce recommended dose by 50% (or extend dosing interval). For example, the manufacturer recommends a dosage of 150 mg PO every 24 hours for most indications. Depending upon the clinical condition, the PO dosage may be cautiously increased to every 12 hours if required.
 
Pediatric dose adjustments (Age 1 month to 16 years)
The following dose adjustments are based on the usual dose of ranitidine in the pediatric population of 2 to 6 mg/kg/day PO divided every 8 to 12 hours:
CrCl 30 to 50 mL/minute/1.73 m2: 2 mg/kg/dose PO every 12 hours.
CrCl 10 to 29 mL/minute/1.73 m2: 1 mg/kg/dose PO every 12 hours.
CrCl less than 10 mL/minute/1.73 m2: 1 mg/kg/dose PO every 24 hours.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
Adults: Ranitidine is removed to some degree by hemodialysis. The patient's normal dosage schedule based on CrCl should be adjusted, when possible, so that the timing of a regularly scheduled dose coincides with the end of a hemodialysis session.
Pediatrics: 1 mg/kg/dose PO every 24 hours is recommended.
 
Peritoneal dialysis
Adults: 75 to 150 mg PO every 24 hours.
Pediatrics: 1 mg/kg/dose PO every 24 hours.
 
Continuous renal replacement therapy
Adults: 150 mg PO every 12 to 24 hours.
Pediatrics: 2 mg/kg/dose PO every 12 hours.
