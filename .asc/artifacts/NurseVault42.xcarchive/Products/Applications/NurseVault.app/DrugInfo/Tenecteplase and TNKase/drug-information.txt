Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Tenecteplase is contraindicated in patients with a prior hypersensitivity to tenecteplase and should be used with caution in patients with a known hypersensitivity to alteplase, reteplase, or other alteplase variants.
 
Tenecteplase should be avoided where possible in patients with atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or other cardiac disease that increases the likelihood for cerebral embolism. In addition, cardiac arrhythmias, including sinus bradycardia, premature ventricular depolarizations, and ventricular tachycardia can develop as a result of reperfusion following coronary thrombolysis. It is recommended that anti-arrhythmic therapy for bradycardia and/or ventricular irritability be available when tenecteplase is administered. Tenecteplase is contraindicated in patients with severe uncontrolled hypertension (systolic blood pressure >= 200 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure >= 110 mmHg). Tenecteplase should be used with caution in patients with moderate uncontrolled hypertension (systolic blood pressure >= 180 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure >= 110 mmHg).
 
Due to an increased risk for bleeding, tenecteplase is contraindicated in patients with the following conditions: aneurysm or arteriovenous malformation; known coagulopathy or bleeding diathesis; active internal bleeding; brain tumor or intracranial mass; spinal anesthesia; intracranial surgery or intraspinal surgery within the last 2 months; history of head trauma, or history of cerebrovascular accident (stroke). In the following relative contraindications, the risk of tenecteplase therapy may be increased and should be weighed against the anticipated benefits. Tenecteplase is relatively contraindicated in patients whom have recently (within the past 10 days) undergone or experienced GI bleeding (e.g., peptic ulcer disease) or genitourinary bleeding, major surgery (e.g., CABG), obstetric delivery, organ biopsy, trauma (including cardiopulmonary resuscitation), or venipuncture of a noncompressible vessel. Tenecteplase is also relatively contraindicated in patients with acute pericarditis, diabetic retinopathy (or other hemorrhagic ophthalmic conditions), infectious endocarditis, mitral stenosis with atrial fibrillation, or other conditions with a high risk of left heart thrombus. Tenecteplase should also be used with extreme caution in other conditions that can be exacerbated as a result of bleeding or in which bleeding could be hazardous or difficult to control due to its location. Use thrombolytics with caution in patients with hemostatic defects due to severe hepatic disease or renal disease associated with renal failure; the bleeding risk of tenecteplase may be increased and should be weighed against the anticipated benefits.
 
In general, the use of thrombolysis in the elderly is controversial due to the uncertain benefits of therapy, relative to the increased risk of bleeding events. Tenecteplase should be used with extreme caution in the very elderly population (> 75 years of age) due to the substantial increase in bleeding risk and potential increase in 30-day mortality with advancing age which was documented in the ASSENT 2 study. The 30-day mortality rates, as well as the incidence of intracranial hemorrhage, total stroke, and major bleeding, increased with advancing age in the ASSENT 2 study.
 
Intramuscular injections and any nonessential handling of the patient should be avoided for the first few hours following treatment with tenecteplase. Intramuscular injections may cause bleeding, bruising, or hematomas due to anticoagulant effects secondary to tenecteplase therapy. Venipunctures should be performed only when required; monitor venipuncture sites carefully.
 
Systemic infection can occur if thrombolytics are administered into an occluded IV or AV cannula that is proximal to an infection site or in the presence of septic thrombophlebitis.
 
Thrombolytics should be used with caution in patients who have recently received glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors or anticoagulant therapy (e.g., warfarin) due to the potential for enhanced risk of bleeding. Although sustained antibody formation in patients who received tenecteplase has not been clearly documented, readministration of tenecteplase should be undertaken with caution. If an anaphylactic reaction occurs, appropriate therapy should be administered.
 
Tenecteplase is classified in FDA pregnancy risk category C. In general, thrombolytics are relatively contraindicated during pregnancy. Tenecteplase should only be used during pregnancy when the potential benefit to the mother justifies the potential fetal risks. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women. Tenecteplase did not elicit maternal and embryo toxicity in rabbits following a single IV administration of 5 mg/kg (approximately 8—10 times the human dose), but maternal and embryo toxicity occurred in rabbits given multiple IV administrations.
 
It is unknown whether tenecteplase is excreted into human breast milk. Tenecteplase should be used during breast-feeding only when the potential benefit to the mother justifies the potential risks to the infant.
 
The safety and effectiveness of tenecteplase in children have not been established.
 
During tenecteplase therapy, results of coagulation tests or measures of fibrinolytic activity may be unreliable, unless specific precautions are taken to prevent in vitro artifacts. Tenecteplase is an enzyme that remains active in a patient's blood sample during in vitro conditions. This can lead to degradation of fibrinogen in blood samples removed for analysis.

    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    In individuals with large ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), choose either thrombolysis or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) as the primary treatment strategy for reperfusion. Rescue PCI or subsequent elective PCI may be performed after administration of thrombolytic therapies if medically appropriate; however, the optimal use of adjunctive antithrombotic and antiplatelet therapies in this setting is unknown. In a clinical trial of subjects with STEMI, there were trends toward worse outcomes in the individual components (i.e., mortality, cardiogenic shock, heart failure) of the primary endpoint between tenecteplase plus PCI vs. PCI alone. In addition, there were trends towards worse outcomes in recurrent myocardial infarction and repeat target vessel revascularization in subjects receiving tenecteplase plus PCI versus PCI alone.

    General Information
    Tenecteplase is contraindicated in individuals with active internal bleeding, known bleeding disorder, current severe uncontrolled hypertension, intracranial surgery or intraspinal surgery or trauma within 2 months, and other intracranial conditions that may increase the risk of bleeding, such as brain tumor, cerebral arteriovenous malformation, or cerebral aneurysm. Additionally, tenecteplase is contraindicated for the treatment of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) in individuals with active intracranial hemorrhage and contraindicated for the treatment of acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction in individuals with a history of intracranial hemorrhage or history of ischemic stroke within 3 months. Because of the increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage, limit tenecteplase treatment for AIS to facilities that can provide timely access to appropriate evaluation and management of intracranial hemorrhage. Weigh the increased risk of bleeding with tenecteplase therapy against the anticipated benefit in individuals with recent major surgery or procedure (e.g., coronary artery bypass graft, obstetrical delivery, organ biopsy, previous arterial puncture or venipuncture of noncompressible vessels), cerebrovascular disease, recent intracranial hemorrhage (if not contraindicated), recent gastrointestinal or genitourinary bleeding, recent trauma, systolic blood pressure more than 175 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure more than 110 mmHg, hemostatic defects including those secondary to severe hepatic or renal disease, significant hepatic dysfunction, pregnancy, diabetic hemorrhagic retinopathy or other hemorrhagic ophthalmic conditions, acute pericarditis, subacute bacterial infective endocarditis, septic thrombophlebitis or occluded AV cannula at seriously infected site, advanced age, individuals receiving anticoagulants, and any other condition where bleeding is a significant hazard or would be particularly difficult to manage due to its location. Concomitant use of other drugs that impair hemostasis increases the risk of bleeding. Carefully monitor for bleeding, especially at arterial puncture sites. Perform arterial and venous punctures carefully and only as required. To minimize bleeding from noncompressible sites, avoid internal jugular and subclavian venous punctures. If an arterial puncture is necessary during tenecteplase administration, use an upper extremity vessel that is accessible to manual compression, apply pressure for at least 30 minutes, and monitor the puncture site closely. Avoid intramuscular injections and trauma to the individual while receiving tenecteplase.

    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Weigh the increased risk of bleeding with tenecteplase therapy against the anticipated benefit in geriatric adults. In the ASSENT-2 study, rates of 30-day mortality, stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, and major bleeds requiring blood transfusion or leading to hemodynamic complications were higher in subjects 65 years and older than in those younger than 65 years treated with tenecteplase for ST-elevation myocardial infarction. No overall differences in safety were observed between subjects 65 years and older and younger subjects treated with tenecteplase for acute ischemic stroke during clinical trials.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no data on the use of tenecteplase during pregnancy to evaluate for a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. Published data consisting of a few case reports with the use of related thrombolytic agents during pregnancy have not identified an increased risk of major birth defects. Weigh the increased risk of bleeding with tenecteplase therapy against the anticipated benefit in pregnant individuals. Myocardial infarction and acute ischemic stroke are medical emergencies that can be fatal if left untreated. Do not withhold life-sustaining therapy during pregnancy because of the potential concerns regarding the effects of tenecteplase on the fetus. Tenecteplase did not elicit maternal and embryo toxicity in rabbits after a single IV administration of tenecteplase 5 mg/kg (approximately 7 times the human exposure based on AUC at the dose for ST-elevation myocardial infarction).

Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breast-fed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition.

  General Information
  NOTE: In the efficacy and safety trials of tenecteplase, patients were treated with adjunctive aspirin and heparin therapy, which contribute to the bleeding risk. To minimize bleeding risks with thrombolytic therapy, standard management of myocardial infarction should be implemented concomitantly with tenecteplase treatment. Similar to other thrombolytic agents, bleeding (internal or external) is the most serious ADR reported with tenecteplase. External bleeding occurs most commonly at access sites such as catheter insertion sites or venipuncture sites. In a comparative trial of accelerated alteplase dosing versus rapid push tenecteplase (ASSENT 2), the 30 day mortality rate was equivalent for both groups (6.2%). In addition, the incidence of intracranial bleeding was similar in patients treated with tenecteplase (0.9%) compared to patients who received front-loaded alteplase (0.9%); the total incidence of stroke was also similar (1.8% for tenecteplase versus 1.7% for alteplase). The incidence of all strokes, including intracranial bleeding, increased with advancing age. The incidence of non-intracranial major bleeding and the need for blood transfusions (4.3% vs. 5.5%) were lower in patients treated with tenecteplase than for alteplase in the ASSENT 2 study. The incidence of major bleeding (bleeding requiring blood transfusion or leading to hemodynamic compromise) was also lower for tenecteplase (4.7% vs. 5.9% for alteplase). The types of major bleeding reported in >= 1% of the patients were hematoma (1.7%) and GI bleeding (1%); while the less frequent types of major bleeding (= 1% of patients included hematoma (12.3%), urinary tract (3.7%), puncture site (including cardiac catheterization site) (3.6%), pharyngeal (3.1%), gastrointestinal tract (1.9%), epistaxis (1.5%), and unspecified (1.3%). Other manifestations of bleeding can include ecchymosis, purpura, hematuria, or hemoptysis. Concurrent therapy with heparin or other anticoagulants may also contribute to bleeding risk. Preventative measures to reduce external bleeding risk include the minimization of arterial and venous punctures, and avoidance of noncompressible arterial punctures (e.g., internal jugular and subclavian venous punctures). Should serious bleeding occur (not controlled by local pressure), concomitant heparin and antiplatelet therapy should be discontinued; heparin effects can be reversed by protamine. Death or permanent disability can occur in patients who experience stroke or serious bleeding episodes.Of the patients in ASSENT 2 study, the age distribution of the tenecteplase-treated group was as follows: 59% aged = 75 years. The 30-day mortality rates, as well as the incidence of intracranial hemorrhage, total stroke, and major bleeding, increased with advancing age. The mortality rates were 2.5% for patients aged = 75. The intracranial hemorrhage rates were 0.4% in patients aged = 75. The total stroke rates were 1.0% in patients aged = 75. Major bleeding rates, defined as bleeding requiring blood transfusion or leading to hemodynamic compromise, were 3.1% in patients aged = 75.Allergic or anaphylactoid reactions including anaphylaxis, angioedema, laryngeal edema, rash (unspecified), and urticaria have rarely (< 1%) been reported in patients receiving tenecteplase. Anaphylaxis was reported in < 0.1% of patients treated with tenecteplase; however, causality was not established. When anaphylactic reactions occur, they generally respond to conventional therapy. The effect of tenecteplase readministration on potential antibody formation is not clearly known. Three of 487 patients tested for antibody formation to tenecteplase had a positive antibody titer via radioimmunoprecipitation assay at 30 days; the clinical significance of these findings are not clear. Antibodies to tenecteplase were not observed in one trial. Although sustained antibody formation in patients who received tenecteplase has not been clearly documented, readministration should be undertaken with caution. If an anaphylatic reaction occurs, appropriate therapy should be administered.Cholesterol microembolization has been reported rarely in patients receiving thrombolytic agents; the true incidence is unknown. This condition, which can be fatal, is also associated with invasive vascular procedures (e.g., cardiac catheterization, angiography, or vascular surgery) and/or anticogulant therapy. Clinical features include livedo reticularis such as 'purple toe syndrome', acute renal failure, gangrenous digitis, hypertension, pancreatitis, myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, spinal cord infarction, retinal artery occlusion, bowel infaction, or rhabdomyolsis.Nausea/vomiting, hypotension, and fever have been reported during administration of tenecteplase, but it is uncertain if these adverse reactions are associated with the myocardial infarction or are attributable to tenecteplase.Rapid coronary lysis can result in the development of cardiac arrhythmias, but these are generally transient. Arrhythmias that have been observed include sinus bradycardia, accelerated idioventricular rhythm, premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), and ventricular tachycardia. Arrhythmias may be managed with standard anti-arrhythmic measures. In general, these adverse reactions are secondary to clot lysis and subsequent reperfusion of ischemic areas and not directly attributable to thrombolytic therapy. Other serious reactions reported specifically with tenecteplase which may be sequelae of myocardial infarction include: cardiogenic shock, arrhythmias, atrioventricular block, pulmonary edema, heart failure, cardiac arrest, recurrent myocardial ischemia, myocardial reinfarction, myocardial rupture, cardiac tamponade, pericarditis, pericardial effusion, mitral regurgitation, thrombosis, embolism, and electromechanical dissociation. The true incidence and causality of these events relative to tenecteplase are unknown.

    General Information
    Tenecteplase can cause significant, sometimes fatal, internal or external bleeding, especially at arterial and venous puncture sites. Carefully monitor for bleeding, especially at arterial puncture sites. Hemorrhage can occur 1 or more days after administration of tenecteplase. If serious bleeding that is not controlled by local pressure occurs, discontinue any concomitant heparin or antiplatelet agents immediately and treat appropriately. Bleeding is the most frequently observed adverse reaction with tenecteplase. Symptomatic intracranial bleeding, defined as intracerebral hemorrhage that, in the opinion of the investigator, was temporally related and directly responsible for worsening of the neurologic condition, was reported with tenecteplase in 3.4% of subjects during clinical trials. Extracranial (peripheral) bleeding requiring blood transfusion was reported with tenecteplase in 1% of subjects.

    Headline
    laboratory test interference
    General Information
    Tenecteplase is associated with laboratory test interference. During tenecteplase therapy, results of coagulation tests or measures of fibrinolytic activity may be unreliable, unless specific precautions are taken to prevent in vitro artifacts. Tenecteplase is an enzyme that remains active in blood under in vitro conditions, which can lead to degradation of fibrinogen in blood samples removed for analysis.

Description
  Text
  Tenecteplase is a tissue plasminogen activator indicated for the treatment of acute ischemic stroke and to reduce the risk of death associated with acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Pharmacologic advantages of tenecteplase, in comparison to alteplase, include higher fibrin affinity, longer half-life, and administration as a single intravenous bolus injection. In the presence of fibrin, in vitro studies demonstrate that tenecteplase-mediated conversion of plasminogen to plasmin is increased relative to its conversion in the absence of fibrin. The clinical significance of fibrin-specificity on safety (e.g., bleeding) or efficacy has not been established. In a large randomized trial of subjects with acute STEMI, tenecteplase and alteplase were equivalent for 30-day mortality rates, but tenecteplase was associated with reduced noncerebral bleeding and need for blood transfusion. Bleeding is the most frequently observed adverse reaction with tenecteplase. Tenecteplase was FDA-approved in June 2000.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Tenecteplase is a modified form of human tissue plasminogen activator that binds to fibrin and converts plasminogen to plasmin. In the presence of fibrin, in vitro studies demonstrate that tenecteplase-mediated conversion of plasminogen to plasmin is increased relative to its conversion in the absence of fibrin. This fibrin specificity decreases systemic activation of plasminogen and the resulting degradation of circulating fibrinogen as compared to a molecule lacking this property.
Pharmacodynamics
After administration of tenecteplase 30, 40, or 50 mg in individuals with ST-elevation myocardial infarction, there are decreases in circulating fibrinogen (4% to 15%) and plasminogen (11% to 24%).

  Tenecteplase is administered intravenously. After IV bolus administration in subjects with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), tenecteplase exhibits a biphasic disposition from the plasma. Vd at central compartment is 4.22 to 5.43 L (approximating plasma volume). Steady-state Vd is approximately 50% higher (6.12 to 8.01 L), suggesting some extravascular distribution. Liver metabolism is the major clearance mechanism for tenecteplase. Tenecteplase has linear pharmacokinetics with mean maximum concentrations increased in a dose-proportional manner, and mean plasma clearance is similar for 30, 40, and 50 mg doses, ranging from 99 to 119 mL/minute. Storage: The tenecteplase vial contains no antibacterial preservatives; therefore, reconstitute immediately before use. If the reconstituted solution is not used immediately, refrigerate the vial at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) and use within 8 hours.
