Drug Class: Certolizumab pegol is a TNF-alpha blocker (TNF-blocker) conjugated to polyethylene glycol for subcutaneous use.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 400 mg/dose subcutaneously.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Certolizumab is only administered subcutaneously. Do not give by intravenous administration.
 
Prescribers should exercise caution when considering the use of certolizumab in patients with preexisting or recent-onset neurological disease such as central nervous system demyelinating disorders. Rare cases of neurological disorders including seizure disorder, optic neuritis, and peripheral neuropathy have been reported in patients treated with certolizumab, but the causal relationship to certolizumab is unclear. Use of TNF blockers including certolizumab has been associated with rare cases of new onset or exacerbation of clinical symptoms and/or radiographic evidence of demyelinating disease. Systemic administration of TNF-alpha antagonists has not provided clinical benefit for those afflicted with multiple sclerosis. In fact, evidence suggests that these therapies exacerbate disease activity. Explanation for the discordance of the effects of TNF-alpha antagonism for different autoimmune diseases is unclear. One proposed possibility is an imbalance between TNF-alpha antagonism and autoimmune exacerbation. Thus, disease worsening may occur with multiple sclerosis, but disease attenuation may occur with rheumatoid arthritis due to prevention of local TNF-alpha-mediated tissue injury.
 
Cases of worsening congestive heart failure (CHF) and new onset CHF have been reported with TNF antagonists. Caution is recommended when using certolizumab in patients with congestive heart failure, as the drug has not been formally studied in this population. In clinical trials, heart failure was reported in some patients who received certolizumab. Infliximab, a similar therapeutic agent to certolizumab, is contraindicated in patients with moderate to severe heart failure (NYHA Class III/IV). Increased mortality and worsening heart failure were noted in a study of infliximab-treated patients. Carefully monitor patients with heart failure who receive certolizumab.
 
Do not start certolizumab in a patient who has an infection including chronic, localized, or systemic infections such as sepsis or influenza. Because certolizumab neutralizes the biological activity of TNF-alpha and TNF mediates inflammation and modulates cellular immune responses, certolizumab may affect host defenses against infection. The impact of certolizumab on the development and course of active and/or chronic infections is not fully understood. Serious infections, invasive fungal infection, sepsis, tuberculosis including disseminated or extrapulmonary tuberculosis, and cases of opportunistic infections, including fatalities, have been reported in patients receiving TNF blockers, including certolizumab. In postmarketing experience with TNF blockers, infections have been observed with various pathogens including viral, bacterial, fungal, and protozoal organisms, and infections have been noted in all organ systems. Infections have been reported in patients receiving certolizumab monotherapy or in conjunction with immunosuppressive agents. Patients who have surgery while taking certolizumab may be at greater risk for postoperative infections. Patients need to be evaluated for tuberculosis risk factors and for latent or active tuberculosis infection with a tuberculin skin test before certolizumab initiation. The possibility of anergy needs to be considered when interpreting the test result. If tuberculin skin testing is performed for latent tuberculosis infection, an induration size of 5 mm or greater should be considered positive even if the patient was vaccinated previously with Bacille Calmette-Guerin. As cases of tuberculosis have occurred in patients who received certolizumab, treatment of latent infection should be started before certolizumab initiation; treatment of patients with latent tuberculosis infection reduces the risk of reactivation in patients receiving treatment with TNF blockers such as certolizumab. It is unknown if certolizumab may be given concurrently with drugs for tuberculosis prophylaxis without the conversion risk of latent to active infection. Consider antituberculosis therapy before certolizumab initiation in patients with a history of latent or active tuberculosis in whom an adequate course of treatment cannot be confirmed and in patients who have several or highly significant risk factors for tuberculosis infection and have a negative test for latent tuberculosis. Monitor all patients, even those with an initial negative latent tuberculosis test, for active tuberculosis during certolizumab receipt. Active tuberculosis has developed in patients receiving certolizumab whose tuberculin test was negative. Instruct patients to seek medical advice if signs/symptoms suggestive of a tuberculosis infection such as persistent cough, wasting, weight loss, and low-grade fever occur. Also, monitor patients for signs and symptoms of infection during and after certolizumab treatment. If a patient develops a new infection while taking certolizumab, closely monitor the infection, and discontinue certolizumab if the infection is serious. Patients with a history of recurrent infections or with underlying conditions that may predispose them to infections (e.g., patients with advanced or uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), or immunosuppression), or those who have lived in tuberculosis or histoplasmosis endemic areas may not be appropriate candidates for certolizumab therapy. Many of the serious infections occurred in patients on immunosuppressive therapy who received certolizumab. The safety and efficacy of certolizumab in patients with immunosuppression has not been formally evaluated. The benefits and risks of certolizumab therapy should be carefully considered before drug initiation.
 
An increased risk of neoplastic disease is associated with the use of TNF blockers such as certolizumab in patients less than 18 years of age. Use of certolizumab by patients with a history of malignancy or current malignancy may be unadvisable (see Adverse Effects).
 
Certolizumab pegol is a FDA pregnancy category B drug; no adequate and well-controlled studies of certolizumab in pregnant women exist. Only use certolizumab during pregnancy if clearly needed.
 
According to the manufacturer, certolizumab or breast-feeding should be discontinued because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in nursing infants. Certolizumab excretion into human milk is unknown. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
No data are available on the response to vaccination or the secondary transmission of infection by live vaccines in patients receiving certolizumab (see Interactions). Live virus vaccines should not be given concurrently with certolizumab.
 
The safety and effectiveness of certolizumab in children have not been established. An increased risk of lymphoma and other cancers is associated with the use of TNF blockers in children and adolescents (see Adverse Reactions).
 
Cautious use of certolizumab in the elderly is advised. Data are insufficient to determine if patients aged 65 and over respond differently than younger subjects, and, in general, a higher incidence of infections in the elderly population exists.
 
Patients treated with certolizumab may develop human anti-chimeric antibodies. Antibody formation against certolizumab occurred in 8% of patients. Fewer patients taking concomitant immunosuppressants had antibody development (3%) as compared with patients not taking immunosuppressants at baseline (11%). In vitro, 80% of the antibodies were neutralizing; however, no apparent correlation of antibody development to adverse events or efficacy was observed. In addition, no apparent correlation of antibody development to adverse events was observed, but the incidence of abdominal pain, arthralgia, edema peripheral, erythema nodosum, injection site erythema, injection site pain, pain in extremity, and upper respiratory tract infection was at least 3% higher in human anti-chimeric antibody (HACA) positive patients as compared with antibody-negative patients.
 
Certolizumab may cause a false coagulopathy because erroneously elevated aPTT assay results may occur in patients without coagulation abnormalities; no effect of certolizumab on in vivo coagulation is known. Certolizumab may interfere with certain coagulation assays such as the PTT-LA test from Diagnostica Stago and the HemosIL APTT-SP liquid and HemosIL lyophilized silica tests from Instrumentation Laboratories; other aPTT assays may be affected. Interference with thrombin time and prothrombin time assays has not been observed.
 
Certolizumab may cause hepatitis B virus (HBV) reactivation in patients who are HBV carriers. In some cases, HBV reactivation occurring in conjunction with TNF blocker therapy has been fatal; most reports have occurred in patients concomitantly receiving other medications that suppress the immune system. Evaluate patients at risk for HBV infection for prior evidence of the infection before certolizumab initiation. Adequate data are not available on the safety or efficacy of certolizumab receipt in HBV carriers who are on antiviral therapy to prevent HBV reactivation. If certolizumab treatment is desired in a HBV carrier, closely monitor the patient for clinical and laboratory signs of active HBV infection during certolizumab receipt and for several months after drug discontinuation. If HBV reactivation occurs, stop certolizumab and begin appropriate HBV therapy (see Adverse Reactions). The safety of resuming TNF blocker therapy after HBV reactivation is controlled is not known. Therefore, exercise caution when considering certolizumab resumption, and monitor patients closely.
 
Cautious use of certolizumab is advised for patients who have current or past significant hematologic abnormalities. Although causality has not been established, medically significant leukopenia, pancytopenia, and thrombocytopenia have been infrequently reported with certolizumab (see Adverse Reactions). Consider certolizumab discontinuation in patients with confirmed significant hematologic abnormalities.

    General Information
    Update immunizations in agreement with current immunization guidelines prior to initiating certolizumab pegol. Avoid the use of live vaccines during and immediately prior to initiation of certolizumab. No data are available on the response to live vaccination or the secondary transmission of infection by live vaccines in patients receiving certolizumab. Before starting certolizumab pegol, test potential drug recipients for hepatitis C (IgG) and HIV (HIV-1 and HIV-2 antibodies and HIV-1 antigen). Consider ongoing screening (e.g., annually) in people who are at increased risk for HIV or hepatitis C infection. Retest for HIV in any person who displays symptoms or other conditions that may suggest HIV. In patients found to be HIV positive, consider involving relevant specialists to ensure the HIV viral load is suppressed through antiretroviral therapy. Similarly, retest for viral hepatitis in any person who develops unexplained elevations in hepatic enzymes. It is recommended to consult a hepatologist when using biological therapy in patients with hepatitis C, whether acute (newly diagnosed) or chronic.
    Headline
    geriatric, infection
    General Information
    Treatment with certolizumab pegol increases the risk of serious infections that may lead to hospitalization or death. Do not initiate certolizumab in people with an active infection, including clinically important localized infections. Use certolizumab with caution in people at increased risk for serious infections, including geriatric adults (age 65 years and older), those with chronic or recurrent infections, a history of opportunistic infections, taking concurrent immunosuppressant medications, or underlying conditions that may predispose to infection. Patients residing in or with travel history to regions where mycoses are endemic (e.g., Ohio and Mississippi River valleys, Southwest) are at increased risk for invasive fungal infections. For patients who develop a serious systemic illness and reside or have traveled to such regions, consider empiric antifungal therapy while a diagnostic workup is being performed. Health care providers are advised that antigen and antibody testing for histoplasmosis may be negative in some patients with active infection. Consider consultation with a physician experienced in fungal infections. Test patients for hepatitis B infection before starting treatment with certolizumab pegol. The use of TNF blockers, including certolizumab, in patients who are chronic hepatitis B carriers has been associated with reactivation of hepatitis B virus, with some cases resulting in death. Patients who are carriers of hepatitis B virus and require treatment with certolizumab should be closely monitored for clinical and laboratory signs of active hepatitis B infection throughout therapy and for several months after treatment discontinuation. Discontinue certolizumab pegol and initiate antiviral therapy if hepatitis B reactivation occurs. The safety of resuming TNF-blocker therapy after hepatitis B reactivation is controlled is not known. Therefore, exercise caution when considering resumption of certolizumab pegol and monitor closely. Certolizumab also increases the risk of reactivation of latent tuberculosis or new tuberculosis infection. Screen (tuberculin skin test and chest x-ray) all patients for active or latent TB infection prior to initiating therapy and periodically during treatment. Consider the possibility of undetected latent TB in patients who have emigrated from or traveled to countries with a high prevalence of TB or had close contact with a person with active TB. The possibility of anergy needs to be considered when interpreting the test results. Consider an induration size of 5 mm or greater as a positive tuberculin skin test, even if the patient was vaccinated previously with Bacille Calmette-Guerin. Initiate treatment for latent TB prior to starting certolizumab. Consider anti-tuberculosis therapy in patients with a history of latent or active TB in whom an adequate course of treatment cannot be confirmed, and in those with risk factors for TB infection. Monitor for signs and symptoms of TB and other infections during and after therapy, even in patients who tested negative for latent TB prior to initiation. For patients who develop an infection during treatment with certolizumab, perform a prompt and complete diagnostic workup appropriate for immunocompromised patients and initiate appropriate antimicrobial therapy. Discontinue certolizumab if a patient develops a serious infection or sepsis. In addition, care should be taken when switching from one biologic product to another as overlapping biological activity may further increase the risk of infection. Health care providers are encouraged to report adverse events to the MedWatch Safety Information and Adverse Event Reporting Program by telephoning 1-800-332-1088 or by completing the form online.
    Headline
    malignancy
    General Information
    Consider the risks and benefits of certolizumab pegol before treatment initiation in people with a known malignancy other than a successfully treated non-melanoma skin cancer. Periodic skin examinations are recommended for all drug recipients, particularly those with risk factors for skin cancer. Incidences of new malignancy, some fatal, have been reported among children, adolescents, and young adults receiving treatment with TNF blockers (therapy initiation prior to 18 years of age). Approximately half of the cases were lymphomas, including both Hodgkin's and non-Hodgkin's lymphoma. Hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma, often fatal, has also occurred, especially in adolescent and young adult males with Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis receiving concomitant azathioprine or 6-mercaptopurine. The other cases represented a variety of malignancies, including rare malignancies that are usually associated with immunosuppression, and malignancies that are not usually observed in pediatric patients. Leukemia, melanoma, and Merkel cell carcinoma have been reported in patients treated with TNF blocking agents, including certolizumab. The malignancies occurred after a median of 30 months (range 1 to 84 months) of TNF blocker therapy. Most of the patients were receiving concomitant immunosuppressants. Consider the risks and benefits of certolizumab continuation in patients who develop a malignancy during treatment.

    demyelinating disorder
    General Information
    Use certolizumab pegol with caution in patients with a history of a demyelinating disorder, including multiple sclerosis and acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy or Guillain-Barre syndrome. Rare cases of new-onset or exacerbation of central and peripheral demyelinating disorders have been reported with the use of TNF blockers.
    Headline
    planned procedure
    General Information
    Patients who undergo surgery while taking biologic therapy, such as certolizumab pegol, may be at greater risk for postoperative infections. For a planned procedure, balance the risk of postoperative infection against the risk of developing a severe or unstable disease by stopping the biologic therapy. Recommendations for the use of immunomodulatory agents in the perioperative setting vary. The American Academy of Dermatology and National Psoriasis Foundation recommend patients with psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis continue to receive TNF blockers through low-risk surgeries or procedures, but hold these medications for one full dosing interval before undergoing total knee or total hip arthroplasties. The British Association of Dermatologists advise stopping biologic therapy 3- to 5-times the half-live or the length of the treatment cycle (whichever is longer) between the last dose and a planned surgery. Restart the biologic therapy postoperatively if there is no evidence of infection and wound healing is satisfactory.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Experts recommend that certolizumab pegol can be used during pregnancy, due to minimal placental transfer. Registry data have not identified an increase in congenital malformations or other adverse outcomes from first trimester exposure. There are risks to the pregnant person and fetus associated with active rheumatoid arthritis or inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) during pregnancy, including fetal loss, preterm delivery, low birth weight, and small for gestational age birth. Due to the small number of certolizumab pegol exposures to the pregnancy registry with known outcomes (n = 217), no meaningful comparisons between the exposed group and control groups may be conducted to determine an association with certolizumab pegol and a drug-associated risk of major birth defects or adverse pregnancy outcomes. Certolizumab pegol plasma concentrations were obtained in 2 studies during the third trimester; placental transfer of certolizumab pegol was negligible in most infants at birth and low in other infants at birth. In 16 individuals treated with certolizumab pegol during the third trimester of pregnancy for rheumatological disease or Crohn disease, certolizumab pegol plasma concentrations were not measurable in 13 out of 15 neonates at birth. The last dose was given on average 11 days prior to delivery (range: 1 to 27 days). The 2 infants with measurable concentrations had an infant/patient plasma ratio of 0.09% and 4.49%. In another study in 10 pregnant individuals with Crohn disease who received certolizumab pegol, plasma concentrations were at least 75% lower in the infants than in the patients. The last dose was administered on average 19 days prior to delivery (range: 5 to 42 days). There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant patients exposed to certolizumab pegol; information about the registry can be obtained at mothertobaby.org/ongoing-study/cimzia or by calling 1-877-311-8972.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Due to its inhibition of TNF-alpha, certolizumab pegol could affect the immune response of neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero. Available data from one exposed infant suggest certolizumab pegol may be eliminated at a slower rate in infants than in adults; in this infant, the drug concentration declined from 1.02 to 0.84 mcg/mL over 4 weeks. Limited data and expert opinion suggest inactive vaccines may be administered without adjustment; biologic exposure in utero does not appear to affect the normal immune response to inactive vaccines in exposed infants. However, the safety of administering live or live-attenuated vaccines in exposed infants is unknown. Consider the benefits and risks of administering live and live-attenutated vaccines in this population. Limited data and expert opinion suggest live rotavirus vaccine may be provided to children with in utero exposure to biologics. However, it is recommended to avoid administration of the live Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) vaccine in the first 6 months of life in those exposed to anti-TNF therapy due to the risk of disseminated infection and associated mortality.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Certolizumab pegol is compatible with breast-feeding. In a study of 17 lactating people, minimal concentrations were observed in human milk, with a calculated average daily infant dose of 0.0035 mg/kg/day (range 0 to 0.01 mg/kg/day). No serious adverse reactions were noted in the 17 infants in the study. In a separate study, certolizumab pegol concentrations were not detected in the plasma of 9 breastfed infants at 4 weeks postpartum. Any medication that is present in human milk is likely partially destroyed in the gastrointestinal tract of the breastfed child. There are no data on the effects of certolizumab pegol on milk production.

Interactions
Abatacept: (Major) Concomitant use of abatacept with other biologic agents, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, is not recommended because of the possibility of additive immunosuppression and increased risk of infection. In controlled clinical trials, patients receiving concomitant abatacept and TNF inhibitor therapy experienced more infections (63%) and serious infections (4.4%) as compared with patients treated with only TNF inhibitors (43% and 0.8%, respectively). These trials failed to demonstrate an important enhancement of efficacy with concomitant administration of abatacept with a TNF inhibitor. Monitor patients for signs of infection during the transition from TNF antagonist therapy to abatacept therapy.
Adalimumab: (Contraindicated) Do not use certolizumab in combination with other tumor necrosis factor (TNF) modifier therapy, including adalimumab, due to the potential for additive effects and drug toxicity. It is unknown if any adverse effects would occur if certolizumab is used concomitantly with adalimumab. A potential exists for increased risk of infection or an impact on the development of malignancies from increased activity toward TNF.
Anakinra: (Major) Avoid the concomitant use of anakinra with biological DMARDs, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) modifiers; coadministration has not been studied and may result in additive immunosuppression and increased risk of infection with no additional clinical benefit. Data suggest a higher rate of serious infections when anakinra and a TNF inhibitor is used in combination with anakinra compared with either drug given alone. Neutropenia (1,000/mcL or less) was observed in 2% of patients receiving the combination. The use of anakinra with a TNF inhibitor in combination did not yield any additional clinical benefit as compared to the use of the TNF inhibitor alone.
Anifrolumab: (Major) Coadministration is not recommended. Anifrolumab has not been studied in combination with other biologic therapies including tumor necrosis factor modifiers (TNF blockers). Potential concerns with use of these drugs together include an increased susceptibility to immunosuppression and serious infections, some of which might be fatal.
Antithymocyte Globulin: (Moderate) The safety and efficacy of certolizumab in patients with immunosuppression have not been evaluated. Patients receiving immunosuppressives along with certolizumab may be at a greater risk of developing an infection. Many of the serious infections occurred in patients on immunosuppressive therapy who received certolizumab.
azaTHIOprine: (Moderate) The safety and efficacy of certolizumab in patients with immunosuppression have not been evaluated. Patients receiving immunosuppressives along with certolizumab may be at a greater risk of developing an infection. Many of the serious infections occurred in patients on immunosuppressive therapy who received certolizumab.
Bacillus Calmette-Guerin Vaccine, BCG: (Contraindicated) Avoid use of live vaccines during or immediately prior to initiation of therapy with certolizumab. Update immunizations in agreement with current immunization guidelines prior to initiating certolizumab therapy. If immunization is necessary, refer to the most recent CDC guidelines regarding vaccination of patients who are receiving drugs that adversely affect the immune system.
Baricitinib: (Major) Concomitant use of baricitinib with biologic DMARDs, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) modifiers, is not recommended because of the possibility of additive immunosuppression and increased infection risk. Baricitinib may be used as monotherapy or concomitantly with methotrexate or other nonbiologic DMARDs.
Basiliximab: (Moderate) The safety and efficacy of certolizumab in patients with immunosuppression have not been evaluated. Patients receiving immunosuppressives along with certolizumab may be at a greater risk of developing an infection. Many of the serious infections occurred in patients on immunosuppressive therapy who received certolizumab.
Belimumab: (Major) Avoid use together. Belimumab has not been studied in combination with other biologic therapies including tumor necrosis factor modifiers (TNF blockers). Therefore, belimumab use is not recommended in combination with TNF blockers. Potential concerns with use of these drugs together include an increased susceptibility to immunosuppression and serious infections, some of which might be fatal.
Canakinumab: (Major) Avoid concomitant administration of canakinumab with tumor necrosis factor (TNF) modifiers; coadministration has not been studied and may result in additive immunosuppression and increased risk of infection. An increased incidence of serious infections and an increased risk of neutropenia have been associated with administration of another IL-1 blocker in combination with TNF inhibitors. Based upon the potential for similar interactions, concomitant administration of canakinumab and TNF inhibitors is not recommended.
Chikungunya Vaccine, Live: (Contraindicated) Avoid use of live vaccines during or immediately prior to initiation of therapy with certolizumab. Update immunizations in agreement with current immunization guidelines prior to initiating certolizumab therapy. If immunization is necessary, refer to the most recent CDC guidelines regarding vaccination of patients who are receiving drugs that adversely affect the immune system.
Cholera Vaccine: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the live cholera vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to cholera bacteria after receiving the vaccine.
cycloSPORINE: (Moderate) The safety and efficacy of certolizumab in patients with immunosuppression have not been evaluated. Patients receiving immunosuppressives along with certolizumab may be at a greater risk of developing an infection. Many of the serious infections occurred in patients on immunosuppressive therapy who received certolizumab.
Dengue Tetravalent Vaccine, Live: (Contraindicated) Avoid administration of the live dengue virus vaccine with immunosuppressive drug therapy and prior to immune recovery following treatment with immunosuppressive drug therapy.   General Information
  The incidence of infection in controlled clinical studies was 38% for certolizumab-treated patients and 30% for placebo-treated patients. The infections consisted primarily of upper respiratory infection (20% certolizumab, 13% placebo). The incidence of serious infections during the controlled clinical studies was 3% for certolizumab-treated patients and 1% for placebo-treated patients. Serious infections included bacterial and viral infections, pneumonia, and pyelonephritis. Cases of opportunistic infection have also been reported in clinical trials. Patients taking TNF blockers are at increased risk of serious infections including invasive fungal infections such as histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, blastomycosis, aspergillosis, candidiasis, cryptococcosis, as well as other opportunistic infections. Postmarketing reports of pulmonary and disseminated histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, blastomycosis, and other opportunistic infections have been received. The FDA reviewed 1 report of histoplasmosis in patients receiving certolizumab. Histoplasmosis and other invasive fungal infections are not consistently recognized in patients taking TNF blockers, which has resulted in delayed appropriate treatment that sometimes led to death. Ascertain if patients live in or have traveled to areas of endemic mycoses, and consider empiric antifungal treatment until the pathogen(s) are identified. A complete diagnostic workup that may include fungal cultures, histopathological or cytological evaluations, antigen detection, and serum antibody titers is advised. Signs and symptoms of possible systemic fungal infection include fever, malaise, weight loss, sweats, cough, trouble breathing, pulmonary infiltrates, or other serious systemic illness with or without concomitant shock. If possible, consultation with an infectious diseases specialist should be sought. As observed with other TNF blockers, tuberculosis associated with certolizumab administration has been reported; some cases have been fatal. In completed and ongoing clinical studies that include over 4650 patients, the overall rate of tuberculosis (pulmonary and disseminated tuberculosis) is approximately 0.5 per 100 patient-years. Many of the serious infections have occurred in patients on concomitant immunosuppressive therapy that, in addition to their Crohn's disease, could predispose them to infections. Infections have been reported in patients receiving certolizumab alone or in conjunction with immunosuppressive agents. Do not initiate certolizumab in patients with active infections including chronic or localized infections. Monitor patients for signs and symptoms of infection during and after certolizumab treatment. Closely monitor patients who develop a new infection while receiving certolizumab. Monitor patients for signs and symptoms of active tuberculosis; tests for latent tuberculosis infection may be falsely negative. Instruct patients to seek medical advice if signs/symptoms suggestive of a tuberculosis infection such as persistent cough, wasting, weight loss, or low grade temperature occur. If a serious infection develops, discontinue certolizumab. The decision to restart the TNF blocker should include a reevaluation of the benefits and risks of TNF blockers, especially in patients who live in regions of endemic mycoses.
 
Angioedema (anaphylactoid reactions), allergic dermatitis, dizziness, dyspnea, hot flush, hypotension, malaise, fever, rash (unspecified), serum sickness, urticaria, and vasovagal syncope have rarely been reported after certolizumab receipt. The symptoms could be compatible with hypersensitivity reactions. If such reactions occur, discontinue certolizumab administration, and institute appropriate therapy.
 
Medically significant cytopenias such as leukopenia, pancytopenia, and thrombocytopenia have been infrequently reported with certolizumab, and the causal relationship of these events to the drug is unknown. Anemia, lymphadenopathy, thrombophilia, retinal hemorrhage, and bleeding have also been reported in clinical trials. Pancytopenia and aplastic anemia have been rarely reported with TNF blockers. Cautious use of certolizumab is advised for patients who have a current or previous history of significant hematologic abnormalities. Instruct patients to seek immediate medical attention if they develop signs and symptoms suggestive of blood dyscrasias or infection such as persistent fever, bruising, bleeding, or pallor. Consider certolizumab discontinuation in patients with confirmed significant hematologic abnormalities.
 
Reactivation of hepatitis B virus (HBV) may occur in patients who are chronic HBV carriers and who take certolizumab. Viral reactivation has been fatal in some patients who took TNF blocker therapy. Most reports of HBV reactivation have been in patients who were taking other medicines that suppress the immune system; immunosuppressant drugs may also contribute to HBV reactivation. Evaluate patients at risk for HBV infection for evidence of HBV infection before certolizumab initiation. Cautious use of certolizumab is advised for carriers of HBV. Adequate data are not available on the safety or efficacy of treating patients who are carriers of HBV with anti-viral therapy in conjunction with TNF blocker therapy to prevent HBV reactivation. Closely monitor HBV carriers who are treated with certolizumab for signs of active HBV infection both during certolizumab receipt and for several months after certolizumab discontinuation. Elevated hepatic enzymes have been reported in clinical trials. Stop certolizumab and start effective antiviral therapy in patients who develop HBV reactivation. The safety of resuming certolizumab after control of HBV reactivation is achieved is unknown. If certolizumab is restarted, carefully monitor patients.
 
The FDA has concluded that there is an increased risk of lymphoma and other cancers (secondary malignancy) associated with the use of TNF blockers in children and adolescents. However, due to the relatively rare occurrence of these cancers, the limited number of pediatric patients treated with TNF blockers, and the possible role of other immunosuppressive therapies used concomitantly with TNF blockers, the FDA is unable to fully characterize the strength of the association. Further, the observed reporting rates offer very limited inference into potential differences in malignancy risk among the TNF blockers because of actual patient exposure uncertainty and possible malignancy case underreporting. The FDA investigated 48 cases of cancer in children and adolescents who took a TNF blocker; most (88%) also took other immunosuppressive medications such as azathioprine and methotrexate. Approximately half of the cancers were lymphomas and included both Hodgkin's and non-Hodgkin's lymphoma. Other cancers reported included leukemia, melanoma, and solid organ cancers; rare cancers in children such as renal cell carcinoma, leiomyosarcoma, and hepatic malignancies were also reported. Certolizumab is not indicated for use in children and was not included in the FDA's review. A possible association also exists between TNF blocker receipt and leukemia development. The FDA reviewed 147 postmarketing reports of leukemia including acute myeloid leukemia (44 cases), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (31 cases), and chronic myeloid leukemia (23 cases). Most cases (n = 143) were in adults, and 61% of the 147 patients were also receiving other immunosuppressive therapies. The average time to leukemia onset was within the first 1 to 2 years of therapy. In clinical studies, the overall incidence rate of malignancies was similar for certolizumab-treated and control patients. Excluding non-melanoma skin cancer, malignancies were observed at a rate of 0.6 (95% CI, 0.4, 0.8) per 100 patient-years among 4650 certolizumab-treated patients versus a rate of 0.6 (95% CI, 0.2, 1.7) per 100 patient-years among 1319 placebo-treated patients. One case of lymphoma among 2657 certolizumab-treated patients and one case of Hodgkin's lymphoma among 1319 placebo-treated patients occurred. The size of the control group and the limited duration of the controlled portions of the studies preclude the ability to draw firm conclusions. More cases of malignancies have been observed among patients receiving other TNF blockers as compared with data from control patients. In the controlled portions of clinical trials of all the TNF blockers, more cases of lymphoma have been observed among patients receiving TNF blockers compared to control patients. Monitor patients for the emergence of cancer during and after certolizumab receipt. Healthcare professionals are encouraged to report side effects to the FDA by calling 800—332—1088.
 
Autoantibody formation and antibodies to certolizumab may occur during certolizumab receipt. A lupus-like syndrome has been reported rarely. Of patients with a negative baseline ANA titer, 4% of certolizumab recipients and 2% of placebo recipients developed positive titers during the studies. One of the 1564 Crohn's disease patients treated with certolizumab developed symptoms of a lupus-like syndrome. If a patient develops lupus-like symptoms, discontinue certolizumab. The impact of long-term certolizumab treatment on the development of autoimmune diseases is unknown. In regard to antibody development to certolizumab, the long-term immunogenicity of the drug is unknown. Antibody formation against certolizumab occurred in 8% of patients. Fewer patients taking concomitant immunosuppressants had antibody development (3%) as compared with patients not taking immunosuppressants at baseline (11%). In vitro, 80% of the antibodies were neutralizing; however, no apparent correlation of antibody development to efficacy was observed. In addition, no apparent correlation of antibody development to adverse events was observed, but the incidence of abdominal pain, arthralgia, edema peripheral, erythema nodosum, injection site erythema, injection site pain, extremity pain, and upper respiratory tract infection was at least 3% higher in human anti-chimeric antibody (HACA) positive patients as compared with antibody-negative patients.
 
Rarely, certolizumab may cause an injection site reaction such as erythema or pain. Certolizumab is only administered subcutaneously.
 
Rare cases of new onset or exacerbation of clinical symptoms or radiographic evidence of demyelinating disease have been associated with certolizumab. Rare cases of seizures, optic neuritis, uveitis, and peripheral neuropathy have been reported in patients treated with certolizumab, but the causal relationship to certolizumab is unclear. Caution is recommended for certolizumab use for patients with preexisting or recent-onset central nervous system demyelinating disorders (see Contraindications).
 
Cases of worsening congestive heart failure (CHF) and new onset CHF have been reported with TNF blockers. Heart failure, angina pectoris, arrhythmias, hypertensive heart disease, myocardial infarction, myocardial ischemia, pericardial effusion, and pericarditis have been reported in clinical trials with certolizumab (see Contraindications).
 
A possible association exists between the development of psoriasis and use of a TNF blocker such as certolizumab. The FDA reviewed 69 cases of new onset psoriasis including 17 cases of pustular psoriasis and 15 cases of palmoplantar psoriasis in patients using TNF blockers for the treatment of autoimmune and rheumatic conditions other than psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis. The development of psoriasis during TNF blocker receipt occurred from weeks to years after drug initiation. Most patients had psoriasis improvement after TNF blocker discontinuation. None of the patients reported pre-existing psoriasis before TNF blocker initiation. Monitor patients for the emergence of or worsening of psoriasis during TNF blocker receipt.

Malignancies, some fatal, have been reported among children, adolescents, and young adults treated with TNF blockers; certolizumab is not indicated for use in pediatric patients. Approximately half of the cancer cases when a TNF blocker was initiated in patients 18 years of age or younger were lymphomas including Hodgkin's and non-Hodgkin's lymphoma. Other cancers included rare malignancies usually associated with immunosuppression and malignancies that are not usually observed in children and adolescents. The malignancies occurred after a median of 30 months of therapy (range, 1 to 84 months), and most patients were receiving concomitant immunosuppressants. Hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma (HSTCL), an often fatal, rare type of T-cell lymphoma, has been reported in patients treated with TNF blockers mostly in adolescent and young adult males with Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis. Almost all of these patients had received treatment with azathioprine and/or 6-mercaptopurine (6-MP) concomitantly with a TNF blocker at or before diagnosis. Although the relationship between HSTCL and use of a TNF blocker or a TNF blocker with another immunosuppressant is unknown, carefully consider the potential risk of using a TNF blocker in combination with azathioprine or 6-MP. Before prescribing certolizumab, especially in adolescents and young adults, carefully weigh the risks and benefits of using certolizumab due to the potential increased risk for cancers including HSTCL. Educate patients and caregivers about the signs and symptoms of malignancies such as HSTCL, so that they are aware of and can seek evaluation and treatment of any signs or symptoms. These may include splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, abdominal pain, persistent fever, night sweats, and weight loss. Monitor for the emergence of malignancies when a patient has been treated with certolizumab including periodic skin examinations for all patients, as melanoma and Merkel cell carcinoma (neuroendocrine carcinoma of the skin) have been with the post-marketing use of certolizumab. Healthcare professionals and patients are encouraged to report adverse events or side effects related to the use of certolizumab to the FDA's MedWatch Safety Information and Adverse Event Reporting Program by calling 1-800-332-1088.

    Headline

    General Information
    Certolizumab may cause laboratory test interference. Interference with certain coagulation assays has been detected in patients treated with the drug. Certolizumab may cause erroneously elevated activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) assay results in patients without coagulation abnormalities. This effect has been observed with the PTT-Lupus Anticoagulant (LA) test and Standard Target Activated Partial Thromboplastin time (STA-PTT) Automate tests from Diagnostica Stago, and the HemosIL APTT-SP liquid and HemosIL lyophilized silica tests from Instrumentation Laboratories. Description
  Text
  Certolizumab pegol is a TNF-alpha blocker (TNF-blocker) conjugated to polyethylene glycol for subcutaneous use. As with other TNF-blockers, it is useful in treating many inflammatory conditions including rheumatoid arthritis (RA), polyarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis (pJIA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), plaque psoriasis, ankylosing spondylitis (AS), non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis, and Crohn disease. For RA, PsA, or AS, certolizumab pegol and other TNF-blockers may be used as monotherapy or may be used with other disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs). The ideal combination of therapy for individual patients with inflammatory arthritis conditions is determined by treat-to-target strategies, as well as factors such as severity of disease and comorbid conditions. For moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis, TNF-blockers can provide rapid clearing of skin plaques, relief of systemic symptoms, and the ability to simultaneously treat associated conditions like PsA. Certolizumab pegol is a guideline-recommended TNF antagonist for induction and maintenance of remission in adults with moderately to severely active Crohn disease; however, the AGA categorizes it as a lower-efficacy advanced therapy relative to several other options, including infliximab and adalimumab, in advanced-therapy-naive patients. As with other TNF blockers, certolizumab pegol labeling carries a boxed warning regarding the risk for serious infections, including tuberculosis, and the potential risk of malignancy. Certolizumab pegol was FDA approved in 2008.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Certolizumab pegol is a recombinant, pegylated, humanized Fab fragment of an anti-TNFalpha monoclonal antibody that neutralizes membrane-associated and soluble human TNFalpha in a dose-dependent manner. Elevated TNFalpha concentrations have been implicated in the pathology of Crohn's disease. TNFalpha is strongly expressed in bowel wall areas affected by Crohn's disease, and fecal concentrations of TNFalpha have been shown to reflect clinical disease severity. In vitro, certolizumab pegol caused a dose-dependent inhibition of lipopolysaccharide-induced TNFalpha and IL-1beta production in human monocytes. Inhibition of bacterially stimulated cytokine production from macrophages may be a necessary function for efficacy of an anti-TNFalpha agent in Crohn's disease. In contrast, induction of apoptosis, antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and complement-dependent cytotoxicity may not be required for clinical efficacy of an anti-TNFalpha agent in Crohn's disease. Unlike etanercept, infliximab, and adalimumab, certolizumab pegol does not contain a fragment crystallizable (Fc) region and thus, does not fix complement or cause antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Subcutaneous Route
    Description
    The pharmacokinetics of certolizumab pegol in patients with rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's disease, and plaque psoriasis are consistent with those observed in healthy subjects. The bioavailability of certolizumab pegol following subcutaneous administration is approximately 80% (range, 76% to 88%). Single subcutaneous doses of certolizumab pegol have predictable dose-related plasma concentrations with a linear relationship between the dose administered and the maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) and systemic exposure (AUC). Peak plasma concentrations of certolizumab pegol are obtained 54 to 171 hours after subcutaneous injection. Using the recommended rheumatoid arthritis regimen of 400 mg in Weeks 0, 2, 4 and 200 mg every other week thereafter, the mean Cmax of approximately 43 to 49 mcg/mL occurs in Week 5.

  Text
  Certolizumab pegol is administered by subcutaneous injection. Once in systemic circulation, the drug has an estimated volume of distribution of 4.7 to 8 L in adult patients with Crohn disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and plaque psoriasis. Covalently linking certolizumab to polyethylene glycol (PEG) delays metabolism and extends the terminal plasma half-life. The metabolic pathway has not been studied in humans; however, data from animals indicate that no additional metabolism occurs after the drug is cleaved from the PEG moiety. The route of elimination of certolizumab pegol has not been studied in humans, but in animals, urinary excretion is the major route of elimination of the PEG component. The terminal elimination half-life is approximately 14 days. The clearance is estimated at 17 mL/hour in Crohn disease, 21 mL/hour in rheumatoid arthritis, and 14 mL/hour in plaque psoriasis. The presence of anti-certolizumab pegol antibodies is associated with a 3- to 4-fold increase in clearance.

      Preparation and Administration of Lyophylized powder vials
Reconstitution of lyophylized powder vial
If the certolizumab powder is prescribed, a health care professional should prepare and administer the injection(s).
If refrigerated, remove the vials from the refrigerator and allow them to sit at room temperature for 30 minutes prior to reconstitution. Do not warm the vials in any other way.
Each single-use vial provides approximately 200 mg certolizumab pegol; therefore, 2 vials will be needed to provide a 400 mg dose.
Reconstitute each vial by adding 1 mL of Sterile Water for Injection to the vial using the provided 20-gauge needle. Direct the diluent to the wall of the vial rather than directly on the lyophilized product.
Gently swirl the vial for approximately 1 minute; do not shake. All the lyophilized product must come into contact with the diluent. Swirl the vial as gently as possible to avoid creating a foaming effect.
Continue to swirl every 5 minutes as long as non-dissolved particles are seen. Full reconstitution may take up to 30 minutes.
The reconstituted product is a clear to opalescent, colorless to yellow liquid with no visible particulates or gels in solution. Discard the solution if particulate matter or discoloration is present.
Storage: The reconstituted solution may be stored at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) for up to 24 hours. Do not freeze. No preservatives are present.
Subcutaneous injection after reconstitution
If the reconstituted product has been refrigerated, allow it to reach room temperature before administering. Do not keep at room temperature for more than 2 hours before administration, as no preservatives are present.
Withdraw the required volume of reconstituted solution from the vial(s) using a separate syringe and 20-gauge needle for each vial. Each reconstituted vial contains 200 mg/mL. If 2 vials (400 mg total) are necessary, use a separate syringe and needle for each vial.
Switch the 20-gauge needle to a 23-gauge needle.
Inject the full contents of the syringe subcutaneously into the abdomen or thigh. Avoid injecting within 2 inches of the navel, and, if 2 injections are needed, at least 1 inch needs to separate the 2 injection sites. Do not inject into skin that is bruised, tender, red, or hard. Also, do not inject into a scar or stretch mark. If a second syringe is needed to achieve a 400 mg total dose, use a new 23-gauge needle and inject into a separate site. Rotate injection sites between the abdomen and thigh to reduce risk of skin reaction.
Do not try to recap the needle and do not rub the injection site.
Disposal: Be sure to properly dispose of needles and syringes in an acceptable sharps container.
 
Prefilled Syringe - General Information
After proper training in subcutaneous injection technique, a patient may self-inject with the prefilled syringe if a physician determines that it is appropriate.
Patients using the prefilled syringe should be instructed to inject the full amount in the syringe (1 mL), according to the directions provided in the Instructions for Use booklet.
The needle shield inside the removable cap of the prefilled syringe contains a derivative of natural rubber latex and should be handled with caution by latex-sensitive individuals.
Administration using prefilled syringe
Remove the prefilled syringe from the refrigerator and keep it at room temperature for 30 minutes before administration.
Each single-use prefilled syringe contains certolizumab 200 mg/mL. The solution should be clear to opalescent and colorless to yellow and essentially free from particles. Do not use the solution if it looks cloudy or if large particulates are present.
Appropriate injection sites are the abdomen or thigh. Avoid injecting within 2 inches of the navel, and, if 2 injections are needed for a 400 mg dose, at least an inch needs to separate the 2 injection sites. Do not inject into skin that is bruised, tender, red, or hard. Do not inject into a scar or stretch mark. Rotate injection sites between the abdomen and thigh to reduce risk of skin reaction.
Remove the needle cover and remove any air by tapping the syringe with the needle pointing upward and pushing the syringe until a drop of liquid appears.
Pinch the skin, and inject the needle at a 45-degree angle using a dart-like motion.
Pull back on the syringe. If blood appears in the syringe, remove the needle and throw away the product. If no blood appears, release the skin and inject the full content of each syringe (1 mL) subcutaneously.
Remove the needle, and do not recap the needle or rub the injection site.
To avoid needle-stick injury, do not attempt to place the needle cap back on the syringe or otherwise recap the needle.
Disposal: Be sure to properly dispose of needles and syringes in an acceptable puncture-proof container. Do not reuse any of the injection materials.
