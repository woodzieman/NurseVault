Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  All dosage forms of mesalamine, 5-ASA are contraindicated in patients with salicylate hypersensitivity. Patients sensitive to sulfasalazine, balsalazide, or olsalazine also may demonstrate cross-sensitivity; therefore, do not use in patients with a history of 5-aminosalicylates hypersensitivity. Mesalamine suppositories are also contraindicated in patients who have demonstrated hypersensitivity to the suppository vehicle (saturated vegetable fatty acid esters; Hard Fat, NF). Mesalamine has been implicated in the production of an acute intolerance syndrome characterized by worsening colitis and symptoms such as abdominal cramping, acute abdominal pain, bloody diarrhea (melena and hematochezia), fever, headache, pruritus, rash (unspecified), and conjunctivitis. In rare cases, pancolitis has occurred. In cases of acute exacerbation of disease, prompt withdrawal of mesalamine therapy is required; symptoms typically resolve after discontinuation. The patient's history of sulfasalazine intolerance, if any, should be re-evaluated.
 
Mesalamine, 5-ASA should be used with caution in patients with renal disease because mesalamine-induced nephrotoxicity, including nephropathy and interstitial nephritis, can occur. Patients with preexisting renal disease, increased BUN or serum creatinine, or proteinuria should be carefully monitored during therapy with mesalamine and particularly closely during the initial phase of treatment. The risk for renal complications can be increased in patients receiving concomitant NSAIDs or other agents containing mesalamine such as olsalazine or sulfasalazine. Mesalamine is known to be substantially excreted by the kidney, and the risk of toxic reactions to this drug may be greater in patients with renal impairment or renal failure. It is recommended that all patients have an evaluation of renal function prior to initiation of mesalamine and periodically while on therapy.
 
Rare incidences of pericarditis have been reported with mesalamine-containing products, including sulfasalazine. Cases of pericarditis have also been reported as manifestations of inflammatory bowel disease. In cases reported with mesalamine, 5-ASA rectal suspension enema (Rowasa), there have been positive rechallanges. Symptoms of pericarditis such as chest pain or dyspnea in patients treated with mesalamine, 5-ASA rectal suspension enema (Rowasa) should be investigated with this information in mind. Discontinuation of mesalamine may be warranted in some cases.
 
Clinical studies of mesalamine, 5-ASA did not include sufficient numbers of elderly subjects to determine whether they respond differently from younger subjects. Other reported clinical experience has not identified differences in responses between the elderly and younger adults. Reports from uncontrolled clinical studies and post-marketing reporting systems suggest a higher incidence of blood dyscrasias (i.e., agranulocytosis, neutropenia, pancytopenia) in elderly subjects receiving mesalamine. Caution should be taken to closely monitor blood cell counts during drug therapy. Because elderly patients are more likely to have decreased renal function, care should be taken when prescribing mesalamine.
 
Mesalamine, 5-ASA is classified by the FDA as a pregnancy category risk B drug. Mesalamine is known to cross the placental barrier. Animal studies have revealed no evidence of teratogenicity or harm to the fetus. Although, there are no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women, several reports describe the successful use of mesalamine during pregnancy. In 2004, a report described the outcomes of 100 pregnancies where a 5-ASA agent (including mesalamine) was used at some time during pregnancy in the treatment of various inflammatory bowel diseases. Authors of this study concluded that there was no evidence that the 5-ASA agents, used either alone or in combination, were associated with poor pregnancy outcomes. Furthermore, only small amounts of mesalamine are systemically circulating in the body after cecum and colon drug absorption, and most of this available drug is rapidly excreted in the urine. Therefore, use of mesalamine during pregnancy appears to pose no significant harm to the developing fetus and the maternal benefits of therapy appear to outweigh any potential for risk to the fetus.
 
According to the manufacturer, caution should be exercised when mesalamine is administered to a nursing woman. Low concentrations of mesalamine and higher concentrations of its N-acetyl metabolite have been detected in human breast milk. The American Academy of Pediatrics has classified mesalamine, 5-ASA as a drug that should be given to nursing mothers with caution; one case of infant diarrhea has been attributed to maternal use of rectal mesalamine during breast-feeding. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
Patients with pyloric stenosis can have prolonged gastric retention of mesalamine, 5-ASA tablets or capsules, which could delay release in the colon.
 
Mesalamine, 5-ASA rectal suspension (Rowasa) can cause an allergic-type reaction in patients with sulfite hypersensitivity because the rectal suspension contains potassium bisulfite. Sensitivity to sulfites appears to be increased in asthmatic compared with non asthmatic patients. Exposure to sulfites has caused anaphylaxis including life-threatening or less severe asthmatic episodes.
 
Hepatic failure has been reported in patients with pre-existing hepatic disease who received mesalamine. Caution is advised if mesalamine is administered to a patient with hepatic disease.
 
Safety and effectiveness of mesalamine extended-release capsules, controlled-release capsules, or delayed-release tablets have not been established in either children or adolescents.
 
The extended-release capsules (Apriso) contain aspartame and may be inappropriate for patients with phenylketonuria.

    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Mesalamine is contraindicated in patients with salicylate hypersensitivity. Patients sensitive to sulfasalazine, balsalazide, or olsalazine also may demonstrate cross-sensitivity; therefore, do not use in patients with a history of 5-aminosalicylates hypersensitivity. As with sulfasalazine, mesalamine-induced hypersensitivity reactions may present as internal organ involvement, including myocarditis, pericarditis, nephritis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, and hematologic abnormalities. Mesalamine suppositories are contraindicated in patients who have demonstrated hypersensitivity to the suppository vehicle (saturated vegetable fatty acid esters; Hard Fat, NF). Some mesalamine rectal suspensions contain potassium metabisulfite and are contraindicated in patients with a sulfite hypersensitivity. Exposure to sulfites may cause allergic-type reactions, including anaphylaxis and life-threatening or less severe asthmatic episodes. Sensitivity to such sulfites appears to be increased in asthmatic or in atopic nonasthmatic patients. Sulfite-free formulas are also available for product selection.
    Headline
    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use mesalamine with caution in people with hepatic failure or impairment. Evaluate the risks and benefits of using mesalamine in these individuals since hepatic failure has been reported in people with pre-existing hepatic impairment who have received mesalamine.
    Headline
    renal disease
    General Information
    Consider the risks and benefits of mesalamine therapy in people with known renal impairment or renal failure, a history of renal disease, or taking concomitant nephrotoxic drugs. Evaluate renal function in all individuals prior to initiating mesalamine and periodically during treatment. Mesalamine is known to be substantially excreted by the kidney. Renal impairment, including minimal change disease, acute and chronic interstitial nephritis, and renal failure, has been reported in mesalamine recipients. Discontinue mesalamine if renal function deteriorates while on therapy.
    Headline
    gastric outlet obstruction
    General Information
    Avoid use of mesalamine delayed-release 1.2 gram tablets (e.g., Lialda) in people with gastric outlet obstruction (e.g., pyloric stenosis or any other functional or organic upper GI obstruction). Individuals with these conditions can have prolonged gastric retention of these tablets, which could delay release in the colon.
    Headline
    skin photosensitivity disorder
    General Information
    Use mesalamine with caution in people with a previous history of a skin photosensitivity disorder. Photosensitivity has been reported in individuals receiving systemic dosage forms of mesalamine. People with pre-existing skin conditions, such as atopic dermatitis and atopic eczema, have reported more severe photosensitivity reactions. Advise treated patients to avoid sun exposure, wear protective clothing, and use a broad-spectrum sunscreen when outdoors.
    Headline
    iron overload disorder
    General Information
    Some mesalamine oral capsule products contain iron oxide (ferric oxide) as a colorant. Before prescribing these mesalamine delayed-release dosage forms to individuals receiving iron supplementation or those at risk of developing iron overload disorder (e.g., hemochromatosis, chronic transfusional iron overload), consider the combined daily amount of iron from all sources, including the mesalamine product.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Closely monitor complete blood cell counts and platelet counts in geriatric adults during treatment with mesalamine. Reports from uncontrolled clinical studies and postmarketing reporting systems suggest a higher incidence of blood dyscrasias (i.e., agranulocytosis, neutropenia, pancytopenia) in geriatric subjects (65 years and older) receiving mesalamine vs. younger adult patients.
    Headline
    phenylketonuria
    General Information
    Mesalamine extended biphasic-release capsules (e.g., Apriso) contain aspartame, which is a source of phenylalanine. These specific products should be used with caution in people with phenylketonuria.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There is no evidence that mesalamine is associated with poor pregnancy outcomes; experts recommend that patients maintained on mesalamine prior to pregnancy continue their treatment regimen. The use of mesalamine during pregnancy appears to pose no significant harm to the developing fetus, and the benefits of therapy may outweigh any potential risk. Mesalamine crosses the placental barrier. However, only small amounts of mesalamine reach systemic circulation and are rapidly excreted in the urine. Published data from meta-analyses, case series, and cohort studies on the use of mesalamine during pregnancy have not reliably informed an association with mesalamine and major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. Animal studies have revealed no evidence of harm to the fetus from mesalamine.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Mesalamine is compatible with breast-feeding. Mesalamine and its metabolite are present in human milk in small amounts. In lactation studies, patients used mesalamine from various oral and rectal formulations and products at doses from 500 mg to 4.8 grams daily. The estimated daily relative infant dose for an exclusively breastfed child is 0.1% or less, with minimal exposure to the metabolite N-acetyl-5-aminosalicylic acid (up to 1.4 to 2.72 mg/kg/day). Diarrhea has been infrequently reported in children exposed to mesalamine through breast milk. Carefully monitor the breastfed child for alterations in bowel function, such as persistent changes in stool frequency. There are no data on the effect of mesalamine on milk production. Among the 5-ASA agents, mesalamine, balsalazide, and olsalazine are preferred over sulfasalazine due to the unknown side effects of sulfasalazine's sulfapyridine metabolite, which is excreted into milk at higher concentrations than the parent drug and has hemolytic and antimicrobial properties.

  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Rectal irritation or proctitis, including burning, pain, and soreness, has occurred with rectal suppositories and suspension in 1.8% (Canasa) and 0.61% (Rowasa) of patients, respectively. Discontinuation of mesalamine may be required; however, most side effects are mild and transient.
 
Dizziness has been reported from both oral and rectal administration of mesalamine. The incidence was not significant compared to placebo, but occurred slightly more frequently from rectal dosage.
 
Mild alopecia has been described as 'more hair in the comb' by patients (incidence < 1%); discontinuation of the medication due to this side effect is rare. Retreatment with mesalamine is not always associated with repeated hair loss.
 
Rash (unspecified) has been reported in 6% of patients treated with oral delayed-release tablets (Asacol) and may be indicative of colitis exacerbation.
 
Abdominal pain and nausea/vomiting frequently are reported with oral administration of mesalamine. In clinical evaluation with oral delayed-release tablets (Asacol), abdominal pain and nausea/vomiting occurred in 18% and 13% of patients, respectively. Headache was the most common adverse effect seen with the oral tablets and was reported in about 35% of patients. Flatulence is most commonly seen with the rectal suspension (Rowasa), occurring in 6.1% of patients. In rare cases, mesalamine can cause an acute intolerance syndrome or colitis exacerbation, with symptoms including abdominal pain or stomach cramps, melena, bloody diarrhea, fever, severe headache, or rash. This occurs in approximately 3% of patients and is reversible upon discontinuation of the drug. Prompt withdrawal of mesalamine is recommended. Some patients have gone on to develop pancolitis. However, in clinical evaluations, the extension of upper disease boundary and/or flare-ups occurred less often in the mesalamine-treated group compared to the placebo-treated group.
 
Asymptomatic, mildly elevated hepatic enzymes have been seen, with resolution to baseline levels after continued use or discontinuation of the drug. Products that are metabolized to (or contain) mesalamine have been reported in post-market use to cause rare cases of hepatitis, hyperbilirubinemia, jaundice, cholestasis with jaundice, cirrhosis, and possible hepatocellular damage including hepatic necrosis and hepatic failure. The incidence of hepatic adverse events is not known. In some cases, the reactions were fatal. One case of Kawasaki-syndrome, which included hepatic changes, was also reported.
 
Allergic or anaphylactoid reactions may occur in patients with drug or preservative sensitivities. Mesalamine, 5-ASA is contraindicated in patients having severe salicylate hypersensitivity. Patients sensitive to sulfasalazine, balsalazide, or olsalazine also may demonstrate cross-sensitivity. In addition, mesalamine, 5-ASA rectal suspension (Rowasa) can cause an allergic-type reaction in patients with sulfite hypersensitivity because the rectal suspension contains potassium bisulfite. Sensitivity to sulfites appears to be increased in asthmatic compared with nonasthmatic patients. Exposure to sulfites has caused anaphylaxis including life-threatening or less severe asthmatic episodes.
 
The following adverse events have been identified during post-approval use of products which contain (or are metabolized to) mesalamine in clinical practice: angioedema, nephrotoxicity [renal failure (unspecified)], pancreatitis, fibrosing alveolitis (pulmonary fibrosis), pericarditis (with chest pain (unspecified) and/or dyspnea), pleuritis, and pneumonitis. Cases of pancreatitis and fibrosing alveolitis have been reported as manifestations of inflammatory bowel disease as well. Published case reports and/or spontaneous post-marketing surveillance have described rare instances of aplastic anemia, agranulocytosis, eosinophilia, granulocytopenia, neutropenia, pancytopenia, or thrombocytopenia. Anemia, leukocytosis, and thrombocytosis can be part of the clinical presentation of inflammatory bowel disease. Oligospermia and infertility in men has also been reported. Cases of lupus-like symptoms have been reported as well. Such complications, if drug-induced, usually require discontinuation of the drug. Guillain-Barre syndrome, which is characterized by muscular weakness of the extremities and can lead to paralysis, has occurred rarely after therapy with oral mesalamine; this complication usually requires discontinuation of the drug.

Description
  Text
  Mesalamine is a 5-aminosalicylate (5-ASA). Mesalamine is used, either orally or rectally, as an anti-inflammatory agent in treating ulcerative colitis (UC). The clinical response to mesalamine is believed to be due to a local effect. Oral mesalamine is considered first-line therapy for induction and maintenance of remission in adults and children with mild-to-moderately active ulcerative colitis (UC). Available evidence does not demonstrate clinically meaningful superiority of one oral mesalamine formulation over another. Rectal mesalamine is recommended for distal UC disease and is commonly combined with oral mesalamine for induction treatment of left-sided colitis because combination therapy is more effective than oral therapy alone. In individuals with suboptimal response to standard-dose therapy, high-dose oral mesalamine (greater than 3 g/day) combined with rectal mesalamine may improve induction of remission. Rectal mesalamine monotherapy remains an appropriate option for ulcerative proctitis and other limited distal UC. For individuals with moderate-to-severe UC or who fail conventional therapy, current guidelines support timely escalation to advanced therapies rather than repeated switching between the various 5-ASA formulations. For adults with inadequate response to optimized 5-ASA therapy, oral prednisone or budesonide MMX 9 mg/day may be used to induce remission rather than switching to another 5-ASA formulation. In mildly active ulcerative proctitis, rectal mesalamine 1 g/day is preferred for maintenance of remission over oral 5-ASA therapy. While mesalamine has been used in the past for Crohn disease and is still sometimes prescribed for CD patients, it has not been shown effective for luminal healing, is not effective for isolated small bowel disease, and it does not effectively help prevent CD relapse. Guidelines recommend against oral 5-ASA use for induction or maintenance treatment of CD. In addition, there is little data to support a role of rectal mesalamine in CD treatment. Mesalamine was FDA-approved in 1987.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The antiinflammatory actions of mesalamine are believed to be secondary to, at least in part, the inhibition of arachidonic acid in the bowel mucosa by the enzyme cyclooxygenase. Inhibition of cyclooxygenase effectively diminishes the production prostaglandins, thereby reducing colonic inflammation. Production of these arachidonic metabolites appears to be increased in patients with inflammatory bowel disease. Mesalamine also interferes with leukotriene synthesis, possibly by inhibiting the lipoxygenase enzyme. In support of prostaglandins as a mediator for inflammatory bowel disease are reports of disease exacerbation after oral administration of misoprostol. However, specific inhibitors of prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis, respectively, are less effective than mesalamine and related compounds. Thus, the exact mechanism of action of mesalamine is as yet uncertain.
 
Inhibition of colonic mucosal sulfidopeptide leukotriene synthesis and chemotactic stimuli for polymorphonuclear leukocytes also may occur. Mesalamine inhibits accumulation of thromboxane A and superoxide formation in the rectal mucosa, which may contribute to its antiinflammatory action. Other possible mechanisms include alterations in colonic fluid balance, immunosuppression, and alteration of the GI bacterial flora. Data also support the mechanism that mesalamine may inhibit the activation of nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-kB), a nuclear protein complex that controls transcription of DNA, cytokine and other pro-inflammatory protein production, and cell survival.

    Description
    Uncoated mesalamine tablets are absorbed extensively in the proximal portion of the GI tract; therefore, delayed-release tablets are used to achieve a local effect in the colon.   Text
  Mesalamine (5-ASA) is administered by rectal suspension or suppository, or by oral delayed-release tablets or capsules. The clinical response of 5-ASA is believed to be due to a local effect. Therefore, mesalamine preparations are designed to deliver drug to the large bowel where its therapeutic action is driven by intestinal lumen absorption. Following administration, 5-ASA absorption is variable and highly dependent on disease activity and colonic pH. Altered mucosal uptake of 5-ASA can occur during periods of active disease when local inflammation causes histological changes to the mucosa as well as epithelial damage and increased blood flow. In addition, as luminal pH drops across the gastric mucosa so does 5-ASA absorption. Mesalamine may be distributed to the kidneys, but the overall distribution is uncertain. Mesalamine is acted upon by N-acetyltransferse in the liver and intestinal mucosa to produce N-acetylsalicylic acid (N-acetyl-5-ASA). The activity of N-acetyl-5-ASA, the major metabolite, is unknown. Any systemically absorbed mesalamine is eliminated from plasma mainly by urinary excretion, predominantly as N-acetyl-5-ASA; less than 8% of the absorbed dose is excreted unchanged in the urine. The mean elimination half-life at steady-state for both mesalamine and N-acetyl-5-ASA is 7-12 hours. Absorbed 5-ASA does not accumulate in plasma with repeated daily dosing.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT)
Aminosalicylates, such as mesalamine, have been shown to inhibit the TPMT enzyme in vitro. The inhibition of TPMT activity could result in a higher risk of bone marrow suppression or other dose-related side effects of drugs that are metabolized by this enzyme.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  Mesalamine is known to be substantially excreted by the kidney, and the risk of toxic reactions may be greater in patients with impaired renal function. Dosage adjustments may be needed; however, specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available. Discontinue mesalamine if renal function deteriorates while on therapy.
