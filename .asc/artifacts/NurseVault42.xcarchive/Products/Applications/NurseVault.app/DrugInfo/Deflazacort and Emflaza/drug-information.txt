    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    infection
    General Information
    Systemic corticosteroids, including deflazacort, may cause dose-dependent immunosuppression and increase the risk of infection with any pathogen, including viral, bacterial, fungal, protozoan, or helminthic pathogens. Corticosteroids may mask symptoms and increase the risk of severe or fatal infectious complications, requiring close monitoring and potential dose adjustment or withdrawal. Systemic deflazacort and other corticosteroids may cause severe or fatal infections by reactivating or worsening TB, hepatitis B, amebiasis, Strongyloides, varicella, measles, herpes simplex, and other viral or herpes infections. Avoid deflazacort in cerebral malaria and systemic fungal infections. Screen for hepatitis B before prolonged immunosuppressive therapy; consult specialists and consider antiviral prophylaxis if infection is present. Rule out latent amebiasis in individuals with tropical exposure or unexplained diarrhea. Use caution in individuals with known or suspected Strongyloides infection; prophylactic therapy may be indicated. Avoid use in systemic fungal infections unless essential; discontinue or reduce corticosteroid if fungal infection develops. Avoid exposing nonimmune corticosteroid-treated individuals to varicella or measles. If exposure occurs, prophylaxis with varicella zoster immune globulin (VZIG) for varicella or immunoglobulin (IG) for measles may be needed. If infection develops, consider antiviral treatment.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus
    General Information
    Corticosteroids, such as deflazacort, may decrease glucose tolerance, produce hyperglycemia, aggravate or precipitate diabetes mellitus, and reduce the effect of anti-diabetic drugs. This may especially occur in individuals predisposed to diabetes mellitus. When deflazacort therapy is necessary in individuals with diabetes mellitus, changes in insulin, oral antidiabetic agent dosage, and/or diet may be required. Monitor blood glucose at regular intervals.
    Headline
    hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism
    General Information
    Metabolic clearance of corticosteroids, such as deflazacort, is decreased in hypothyroidism and increased in hyperthyroidism; changes in the thyroid disease status of the individual may necessitate adjustment in dosage. When concomitant administration of corticosteroids and levothyroxine is required, administration of corticosteroid should precede the initiation of levothyroxine therapy to reduce the risk of adrenal crisis.
    Headline
    pheochromocytoma
    General Information
    Use deflazacort with caution in individuals with pheochromocytoma. Pheochromocytoma crisis, which can be fatal, has been reported after administration of systemic corticosteroids. Consider the risk of pheochromocytoma crisis prior to administering corticosteroids in individuals with suspected or identified pheochromocytoma.
    Headline
    heart failure, hypertension, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Use deflazacort with caution in individuals with congestive heart failure, hypertension, renal impairment, or renal failure. Corticosteroids, including deflazacort, can cause sodium retention with resultant edema, potassium and calcium loss, and hypertension. Exacerbation of congestive heart failure or hypertension may also occur. Monitor blood pressure and serum potassium concentrations in individuals receiving deflazacort. Periodically assess for signs and symptoms of volume overload. Dietary salt restriction and potassium supplementation may be necessary.
    Headline
    myocardial infarction
    General Information
    Use deflazacort with caution in individuals with a history of recent myocardial infarction; corticosteroid therapy has been associated with left ventricular free-wall rupture in these individuals.
    Headline
    diverticulitis, GI abscess, GI perforation, intestinal anastomosis, peptic ulcer disease, ulcerative colitis
    General Information
    Deflazacort increases the risk of gastrointestinal perforation in patients with certain types of GI disease, including diverticulitis, nonspecific ulcerative colitis, active or latent peptic ulcer disease, and intestinal anastomosis. Signs of GI perforation, such as peritoneal irritation, may be minimal or absent in patients receiving corticosteroids. Avoid the use of corticosteroids in patients if there is a probability of impending perforation, GI abscess, or other pyogenic infections; diverticulitis; fresh intestinal anastomoses; or active or latent peptic ulcer disease.
    Headline
    mood disorder, psychotic disorder
    General Information
    Potentially severe psychiatric adverse reactions may occur with systemic corticosteroids, including deflazacort. Symptoms typically emerge within a few days or weeks of starting treatment and may be dose-related. These reactions may improve after either dose reduction or withdrawal, although pharmacologic treatment may be necessary. Psychiatric adverse reactions include hypomanic or manic symptoms (e.g., euphoria, insomnia, mood swings, mania, psychosis) during treatment and depressive episodes after discontinuation of treatment. Also, existing emotional instability or psychotic tendencies may be aggravated by corticosteroids, such as in individuals with a mood disorder or psychotic disorder. Inform individuals or caregivers of the potential for mood and behavioral changes and encourage them to seek medical attention if psychiatric symptoms develop, especially if depressed mood (depression) or suicidal ideation is suspected.
    Headline
    increased risk for osteoporosis
    General Information
    Corticosteroids decrease bone formation and increase bone resorption both through their effect on calcium regulation (e.g., decreasing absorption and increasing excretion) and inhibition of osteoblast function. This, together with a decrease in the protein matrix of the bone secondary to an increase in protein catabolism, and reduced sex hormone production, may lead to the development of osteopenia and osteoporosis at any age. Special consideration should be given to people at increased risk for osteoporosis before initiating corticosteroid therapy.
    Headline
    myasthenia gravis
    General Information
    Use systemic corticosteroids, such as deflazacort, with caution in individuals with myasthenia gravis. A generalized acute myopathy has been reported with the use of high-dose corticosteroids in individuals with disorders of neuromuscular transmission, such as myasthenia gravis. Ocular and respiratory muscles may be involved and may result in quadriparesis; elevations in creatine kinase may also occur. Recovery from the myopathy after discontinuation of corticosteroids may take weeks to years.
    Headline
    infants, neonates, premature neonates
    General Information
    Deflazacort oral suspension contains benzyl alcohol (10.45 mg/mL). Serious and fatal adverse reactions including "gasping syndrome" can occur in neonates, premature neonates, and low birth weight infants treated with benzyl-alcohol preserved drugs. The "gasping syndrome" is characterized by central nervous system depression, metabolic acidosis, and gasping respirations. Consider the combined daily metabolic load of benzyl alcohol from all sources when prescribing deflazacort. The minimum amount of benzyl alcohol at which serious adverse reactions can occur is not known.
    Headline
    thromboembolism, thrombophilia
    General Information
    Use systemic corticosteroids, like deflazacort, with caution in individuals who have thromboembolism or may be predisposed to thromboembolic disease (thrombophilia). Observational studies have shown an increased risk of thromboembolism (including venous thromboembolism) particularly with higher cumulative doses of corticosteroids. It is unclear if risk differs by daily dose or duration of use.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on the use of deflazacort during pregnancy are insufficient to evaluate a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. Use deflazacort only if the potential benefit justifies the risk to the fetus. Corticosteroids cross the placenta. Adverse developmental outcomes, including orofacial clefts, intrauterine growth restriction, and decreased birth weight have been reported with maternal corticosteroid use during pregnancy.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Monitor neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero for signs of hypoadrenalism.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use deflazacort with caution during breast-feeding. Systemically administered corticosteroids such as deflazacort are secreted in human milk and could suppress growth, interfere with endogenous corticosteroid production, or cause other adverse effects in the breast-fed child. There are no data evaluating deflazacort's effect on milk production. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for deflazacort, and any potential adverse effects on the breast-fed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition.

    Headline
    adrenocortical insufficiency, withdrawal
    General Information
    Prolonged administration of pharmacological doses of systemic corticosteroids may result in hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) suppression in some patients. Withdrawal from prolonged systemic corticosteroid therapy should be gradual. Acute adrenal insufficiency and even death may occur following abrupt discontinuation of prolonged systemic therapy. HPA suppression can last for months after cessation of systemic therapy. In addition, a withdrawal syndrome unrelated to adrenocortical insufficiency may occur after sudden discontinuation of corticosteroid therapy. Symptoms of this syndrome include anorexia, nausea, vomiting, lethargy, headache, fever, joint pain, desquamation, myalgia, and/or weight loss. These effects are thought to be due to the sudden change in corticosteroid concentration rather than to low corticosteroid concentrations.

Description
  Text
  Deflazacort is an oral corticosteroid indicated for the treatment of patients 2 years of age and older with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). DMD is the most common type of muscular dystrophy and is caused by an absence of dystrophin, a protein that helps keep muscle cells intact. Affected patients typically develop symptoms in early childhood (usually between 3 and 5 years of age) that quickly progress to a loss of ambulation in adolescence and ultimately, life-threatening heart and respiratory conditions that limit life expectancy to early adulthood. The disease primarily affects boys, but in rare cases it can affect girls. DMD affects approximately 1 in 3,600 male births worldwide. Corticosteroids are commonly used to treat DMD worldwide, but deflazacort is the first corticosteroid approved for DMD treatment in the United States. This approval was based on the effectiveness of deflazacort in a clinical trial involving 196 male patients 5 to 15 years of age at the beginning of the trial with documented mutation of the dystrophin gene and onset of weakness before age 5. At 12 weeks, patients receiving deflazacort had improvements in average strength of 18 muscle groups. The persistence of the treatment effect was also documented in the deflazacort group at week 52. In another trial lasting 104 weeks and involving 29 male patients, deflazacort demonstrated a numerical advantage over placebo on an assessment of average muscle strength. The most common adverse reactions reported during clinical trials were similar to those experienced with other corticosteroids and included Cushingoid appearance, weight again, increased appetite, upper respiratory tract infections, cough, pollakiuria, hirsutism, and central obesity.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Deflazacort is a corticosteroid prodrug, whose active metabolite, 21-desDFZ, acts through the glucocorticoid receptor to exert anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects. The precise mechanism by which deflazacort exerts its therapeutic effects in patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is unknown.
 
The dystrophin protein is responsible for muscle cell integrity. DMD is caused by various mutations, most often internal deletions, in the gene coding for dystrophin. These mutations shift the reading frame so that mRNA does not code correctly for a functional protein, leading to a lack of dystrophin which results in muscle cell membrane destabilization and, consequently, muscle deterioration and loss.

    Description
    After oral administration in the fasted state, peak plasma concentrations occur in about 1 hour (range: 0.25 to 2 hours). A high-fat meal reduces the extent of absorption of the tablets by about 30% and delays the time to peak plasma concentrations by 1 hour; there is no effect on the overall systemic absorption as measured by AUC. The bioavailability of deflazacort tablets is similar to that of the oral suspension. Administration with food or crushed applesauce does not affect the absorption and bioavailability.

  Text
  Deflazacort is administered orally. After administration, deflazacort is rapidly converted to the active metabolite 21-desDFZ by esterases; 21-desDFZ is further metabolized by CYP3A4 to several other inactive metabolites, including 6beta-hydroxy-21-desacetyl deflazacort. 21-desDFZ is about 40% bound to human plasma proteins. Renal clearance is the primary route of deflazacort elimination (about 68% of the dose); elimination is almost completed by 24 hours post dose. 21-desDFZ accounts for 18% of the eliminated drug in the urine.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4, P-gp
The active metabolite of deflazacort, 21-desDFZ, is a substrate of CYP3A4. In vitro data indicate both deflazacort and 21-desDFZ are substrates of P-glycoprotein (P-gp); however, the clinical significance of this is unknown. 6beta-hydroxy-21-desacetyl deflazacort, a secondary and inactive metabolite, is not expected to cause clinically meaningful pharmacokinetic interactions.
