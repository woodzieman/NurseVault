    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    infection
    General Information
    Use isatuximab with caution in patients with an infection. Serious and fatal infections, including opportunistic infections, have occurred. Use isatuximab with caution in patients with a history of shingles (herpes zoster); reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus may occur. Initiate prophylactic antibiotics during treatment if needed; start antiviral prophylaxis per standard guidelines to prevent herpes zoster reactivation.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Based on its mechanism of action, isatuximab can cause fetal harm if administered during pregnancy. There are no human data to evaluate a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. Animal reproduction toxicity studies have not been conducted. Immunoglobulin G1 antibodies are known to cross the placenta. Based on its mechanism of action, isatuximab may cause depletion of fetal CD38-positive immune cells and decreased bone density. Mice that were genetically modified to eliminate all CD38 expression (CD38 knockout mice) had reduced bone density, which recovered 5 months after birth. Data from studies using CD38 knockout animal models also suggest the CD38 is involved in the regulation of humoral immune responses (mice), feto-maternal immune tolerance (mice), and early embryonic development (frogs).
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with isatuximab. Isatuximab can cause fetal harm. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking isatuximab and for 5 months (for intravenous infusion) and for 7 months (for subcutaneous injection) after the last dose.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Defer administration of live vaccinations in neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero until a hematology evaluation is completed. Immunoglobulin G1 monoclonal antibodies are transferred across the placenta. Isatuximab may cause depletion of CD38-positive immune cells and decreased bone density.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Avoid the use of isatuximab during breast-feeding. Isatuximab has the potential to cause serious side effects in the breast-fed child. There are no data on the presence of isatuximab human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effect on milk production. Maternal IgG is present in human milk; the effects of local gastrointestinal exposure and limited systemic exposure in the breast-fed infant to isatuximab are unknown. Advise patients not to breastfeed during treatment with subcutaneous isatuximab and for 7 months after the last dose.

Interactions
Cholera Vaccine: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the live cholera vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to cholera bacteria after receiving the vaccine.
Dengue Tetravalent Vaccine, Live: (Contraindicated) Avoid administration of the live dengue virus vaccine with immunosuppressive drug therapy and prior to immune recovery following treatment with immunosuppressive drug therapy. When feasible, administer indicated live virus vaccines at least 4 weeks before planned immunosuppression or wait until at least 3 months after discontinuation. The time to restoration of immune competence may be longer in some patients. Patients with altered immunocompetence may be at increased risk for severe adverse reactions due to uninhibited growth of the attenuated live virus. Additionally, vaccines may be less effective if administered during a period of altered immunocompetence.
SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) vaccines: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the SARS-CoV-2 virus vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to SARS-CoV-2 virus after receiving the vaccine.

When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications.     General Information

    anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema, bronchospasm, cardiac arrest, chills, cytokine release syndrome, infusion-related reactions, injection site reaction, nasal congestion
    General Information
    Infusion-related reactions have been reported in patients who received IV isatuximab therapy; anaphylactoid reactions occurred in less than 1% of patients. Fatal anaphylactoid reactions have been reported in postmarketing surveillance. Administer with the recommended premedication. Most reactions start during the first infusion or first treatment cycle and resolve on the same day. Monitor vital signs frequently during the isatuximab infusion for signs and symptoms of infusion-related reactions. Common symptoms include dyspnea, cough, chills, nasal congestion, and nausea; serious and life-threatening reactions include cardiac arrest, hypertension, hypotension, bronchospasm, dyspnea, angioedema, and swelling. Stop the infusion and initiate appropriate medical support if a grade 2 or higher infusion-related reaction occurs; an infusion rate reduction or permanent discontinuation may be necessary. Infusion-related reactions (24% to 46%; grade 3 or 4, 0.4 to 2.6%) occurred in multiple myeloma patients who received IV isatuximab with other therapies. The term infusion-related reactions included cytokine release syndrome and hypersensitivity. Severe or life-threatening systemic administration-related reactions (SARs) and local injection site reactions have been reported with subcutaneous isatuximab. In pooled safety data, SARs occurred in 3.2% (grade 3, 0.2%) of patients with 1.9% occurring at the first administration. The median time to onset was 4 hours and the most frequently reported symptoms (all below 1%) were pyrexia and dyspnea. Injection site reaction occurred in 9% of patients and in 0.86% of injections; among these reactions, 82% occurred on the day of administration and 4.2% were delayed by at least 3 days. Symptoms included injection site erythema (3.3%), injection site swelling (2.1%), and injection site pain (1.5%). Higher incidence of injection site reactions occurred in patients who received subcutaneous isatuximab in combination with bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone (27%).

    Headline
    antibody formation
    General Information
    Antibody formation has been reported after isatuximab therapy. Treatment-emergent anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) occurred in less than 2% of relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma patients who received isatuximab as a single agent or in combination with other therapies across clinical studies.     Headline
    laboratory test interference
    General Information
    Laboratory test interference has been reported with isatuximab therapy. Isatuximab binds to CD38 on red blood cells (RBCs) and may interfere with blood bank serologic tests and result in false positive reactions in indirect antiglobulin tests (indirect Coombs test), antibody detection (screening) tests, antibody identification panels, and antihuman globulin crossmatches. A false positive indirect Coombs test was observed in 63% and 68% of tested patients following isatuximab-containing therapy in clinical trials; this interference may persist for approximately 6 months after the last isatuximab administration. Patients should have their blood typed and screened prior to starting treatment and consider phenotyping. In the event of a planned blood transfusion, inform personnel at blood transfusion centers that the patient is receiving isatuximab; interference with blood compatibility testing can be resolved using dithiothreitol-treated RBCs. If a patient requires an emergency transfusion, non-cross-matched ABO/RhD-compatible RBCs can be given as local blood bank practice dictates. Determination of ABO and Rh blood type are not affected by isatuximab. Additionally, isatuximab can be detected on both the serum protein electrophoresis and immunofixation (IFE) assays used for the clinical monitoring of endogenous M-protein which may interfere with the determination of complete response (CR) based on International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) criteria. Consider using an FDA-approved isatuximab-specific IFE assay to determine a CR in patients with persistent very good partial response in which test interference is suspected.

Description
  Text
  Isatuximab-irfc is a CD-38 directed cytolytic antibody. It may be given as an intravenous (IV) infusion or as a subcutaneous injection and is indicated for the treatment of relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma in combination with pomalidomide and dexamethasone in patients who have received at least 2 prior therapies (IV formulation) or 1 prior line of therapy (subcutaneous formulation) including lenalidomide and a proteasome inhibitor and in combination with carfilzomib and dexamethasone in patients who have received 1 to 3 prior lines of therapy. Isatuximab is also indicated for the treatment of newly diagnosed multiple myeloma in combination with bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone in patients who are not eligible for autologous stem cell transplant. Because isatuximab binds to CD38 on red blood cells, it may interfere with the ability to determine a patient's blood type and result in a false positive indirect antiglobulin test (Coombs test). Additionally, isatuximab may interfere with the determination of complete response in some patients. Patients should have their blood typed and screened prior to starting treatment. It was approved by the FDA in March 2020.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Isatuximab is an IgG1-derived monoclonal antibody that binds to CD38 expressed on the surface of normal and multiple myeloma tumor cells. It inhibits the ADP-ribosyl cyclase activity of CD38, activates natural killer cells in the absence of CD38-positive target tumor cells, and suppresses CD38-positive T-regulatory cells. CD38-directed antibodies cause tumor cell death via antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity, complement-dependent cytotoxicity, and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis, and apoptosis. Isatuximab differs from another CD38-directed monoclonal antibody, daratumumab, in its ability to induce direct apoptosis without cross-linking and its dose-dependent inhibition of CD38 enzymatic activity in vitro. Isatuximab has demonstrated synergistic activity with immunomodulatory agents such as lenalidomide and pomalidomide in clinical trials.

Remove the on-body injector from the filling base by holding both side of the injector and pulling it straight up from the base.
Do not touch the white sticky adhesive; the needle in inside the center. Do not remove the red safety tab until the on-body injector is attached to the body. The clear liner will remain attached to the filling base.
Position the on-body injector where you can see the fill window. Do not move the injector once attached to the abdomen.
Once on the skin, press the on-body injector firmly onto the injection site to attach adhesive to the abdomen.
When ready to inject, hold the on-body injector with one hand and use the other hand to pull the red safety tab off to the side.

Do not bump or knock the on-body injector or button during the injection. Leave the injector on the body until the button pops out, signaling that the injection is complete.

Confirm that the button has popped out before removing the on-body injector.
Slowly peel off the on-body injector with the adhesive tab and dispose in an FDA-cleared sharps disposal container.
