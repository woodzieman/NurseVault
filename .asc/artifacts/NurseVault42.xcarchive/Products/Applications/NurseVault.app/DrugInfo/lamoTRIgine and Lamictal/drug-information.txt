Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Medication errors have been reported with lamotrigine starter kits. Mild to severe side effects, including Stevens-Johnson Syndrome have occurred. There are 3 different starter kits with titration schedules dependent on concurrent medications. Individuals must receive the correct kit to avoid over or under dosing.
Orange starter kits: For individuals not receiving carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, primidone, rifampin, or valproate.
Green starter kits: For individuals receiving carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, primidone, or rifampin and not receiving valproate.
Blue starter kits: For individuals receiving valproate.
As with most antiepileptic drugs, do not abruptly discontinue lamotrigine due to seizure risk.
Unless safety concerns require a more rapid withdrawal, taper the lamotrigine dose for any indication by approximately 50% per week over at least 2 weeks.

      Route Age Groups
        Heading
        Children and Adolescents 3 to 13 years
        Dosage Description
        0.25 mg/kg/dose PO twice daily for 2 weeks, then 0.5 mg/kg/day PO twice daily for 2 weeks, and then increase the dose by 1 mg/kg/day every 5 days until an effective dose is reached, side effects prohibit further titration, or to a maximum of 12 mg/kg/day. At 1 month, significantly more children were seizure free with valproic acid (52.6%) than lamotrigine (5.3%). There was no statistical difference by month 3 (63.1% vs. 36.8%). After 12 months, 68.4% and 52.6% of those receiving valproic acid and lamotrigine, respectively, were seizure free. Adverse effects (headache, skin rash, diplopia, nervousness, or increased appetite) were reported in 6 subjects receiving lamotrigine. The delayed response to lamotrigine may in part be due to the slow titration schedule. However, safety issues, such as the risk of a serious skin rash, must be taken into consideration when selecting a dosage titration. Coadministration of certain drugs may need to be avoided or dosage adjustments may be necessary; review drug interactions.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Lamotrigine is contraindicated in any patient who exhibits or is suspected of having a lamotrigine hypersensitivity or hypersensitivity to any components of its formulation. Caution is advised when administering lamotrigine to patients with a history of rash or allergy to other anticonvulsants, since nonserious rashes have occurred 3 times more frequently in these patients during treatment with lamotrigine than in those without this history. Due to life-threatening rashes (including Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis), lamotrigine carries a boxed warning stating the drug should be discontinued if rash appears at any time during treatment. It is important to note that discontinuation of lamotrigine may not prevent progression to a higher level of severity; therefore patients should be closely monitored. Lamotrigine should not be resumed following prior discontinuation due to rash unless the benefits outweigh the risks. The manufacturer recommends that initial dosing recommendations be followed if treatment is reinitiated after 5 half-lives. Age is the only factor currently known to predict the occurrence or severity of a rash, with pediatric patients at increased risk. Other possible but unproven factors include concurrent use of valproate, exceeding the initial recommended dose, or exceeding the recommended dose titration. Almost all cases of life-threatening rash have occurred within the first 2—8 weeks of treatment. However, a prolonged duration of therapy should not preclude the possibility of an association to the drug.
 
In January 2008, the FDA alerted healthcare professionals of an increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior in patients receiving anticonvulsants to treat epilepsy, psychiatric disorders, or other conditions (e.g., migraine, neuropathic pain). This alert followed an initial request by the FDA in March 2005 for manufacturers of marketed anticonvulsants to provide data from existing controlled clinical trials for analysis. Prior to this request, preliminary evidence had suggested a possible link between anticonvulsant use and suicidality. The primary analysis consisted of 199 placebo-controlled clinical studies with a total of 27,863 patients in drug treatment groups and 16,029 patients in placebo groups (>= 5 years of age). There were 4 completed suicides among patients in drug treatment groups versus none in the placebo groups. Patients receiving anticonvulsants had approximately twice the risk of suicidal behavior or ideation (0.43%) as patients receiving placebo (0.24%), corresponding to an estimated 2.1 per 1000 (95% CI: 0.7—4.2) more patients in the drug treatment groups who experienced suicidal behavior or ideation. The relative risk for suicidality was higher in patients with epilepsy compared to those with other conditions; however, the absolute risk differences were similar in trials for epilepsy and psychiatric indications. Age was not a determining factor. The increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior was observed between 1 and 24 weeks after therapy initiation. However, a longer duration of therapy should not preclude the possibility of an association to the drug since most studies included in the analysis did not continue beyond 24 weeks. Data were analyzed from drugs with adequately designed clinical trials including carbamazepine, felbamate, gabapentin, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine, pregabalin, tiagabine, topiramate, valproate, and zonisamide. However, this is considered to be a class effect. All patients beginning treatment with anticonvulsants or currently receiving such treatment should be closely monitored for emerging or worsening depression or suicidal thoughts/behavior. Patients and caregivers should be informed of the increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors and should be advised to immediately report the emergence or worsening of depression, the emergence of suicidal thoughts or behavior, thoughts of self-harm, or other unusual changes in mood or behavior. Anticonvulsants should be prescribed in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management in order to reduce the risk of overdose.
 
Patients receiving lamotrigine for any indication, including bipolar disorder, should not undergo abrupt discontinuation if possible due to the potential for withdrawal seizures. A gradual taper of lamotrigine over 2 weeks is recommended unless safety concerns require a more rapid discontinuation.
 
Lamotrigine commonly causes blurred vision, dizziness, and drowsiness. Patients should be advised to use caution when driving or operating machinery, or performing other tasks that require mental alertness until they are aware of whether lamotrigine adversely affects their mental and/or motor performance.
 
The elderly exhibit a lower clearance and longer lamotrigine half-life when compared to young adults. The manufacturer recommends that the elderly receive dosages at the low end of the normal adult range. Liver and kidney function should be assessed in the elderly prior to lamotrigine initiation.
 
Lamotrigine should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease or impairment because the drug is extensively metabolized by the liver. Animal data reveal that the 2-N-methyl metabolite causes prolongation of the PR interval, widening of the QRS complex, and, at higher doses, complete AV conduction block. This is not expected to occur in humans because only trace amounts of this metabolite is found, however, in patients with hepatic disease, it is possible that concentrations of the 2-N-methyl metabolite might be increased due to decreased glucuronidation. Pharmacokinetic studies have shown that the apparent clearance of the drug is reduced and the half-life is prolonged in those with mild to severe hepatic impairment. The half-life is prolonged as much as 100 +/- 48 hours in those with severe hepatic impairment and ascites compared to 33 +/- 7 hours in healthy controls. Dosing alterations are suggested in patients with moderate to severe hepatic impairment (see Dosage).
 
Lamotrigine should be used with caution in patients with severe renal disease, renal impairment, or renal failure, including patients on dialysis. Initial doses of lamotrigine should be based on the patient's current antiepileptic drug regimen. Reduced maintenance doses of lamotrigine may be effective in maintaining seizure control. In one study of patients with chronic renal failure, the mean plasma half-life of lamotrigine was 42.9 hours, compared to 26.2 hours in healthy volunteers. During hemodialysis sessions the mean plasma half-life was 13 hours, and 57.4 hours between hemodialysis (manufacturer's data).
 
The manufacturer of lamotrigine recommends that caution also be used in patients with cardiac function impairment (e.g. heart failure, cardiac disease) since the metabolism or elimination of the drug could be affected (see details under hepatic disease, renal disease and impairment).
 
In specific patient populations lamotrigine dosage adjustment may be necessary. Oral clearance of lamotrigine in noncaucasians (e.g., black patients) is 25% lower than in Caucasians.
 
Use lamotrigine cautiously in pregnancy and in patients with folate deficiency. Lamotrigine is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of pregnant women receiving lamotrigine, and the effects of lamotrigine during pregnancy, labor, or obstetric delivery are not fully known. Of 564 infants born to women registered in the North American Antiepileptic Drug Pregnancy Registry and who received lamotrigine as monotherapy in the first trimester of pregnancy, 15 had a major malformation (2.6%, 95% CI 1.5—4.3), which was defined as structural abnormalities with surgical, medical, or cosmetic importance. In contrast, the rate in an unexposed comparison group was 1.62% (95% CI 1—2.7). Five of the 15 infants with a major malformation had non-syndromic oral clefts; 3 had isolated cleft palate and 2 had isolated cleft lip. The incidence rate of 8.9 per 1000 is dramatically higher than the rate of 0.37 per 1000 in the unexposed comparison group. As of June 2006, the manufacturer of lamotrigine is aware of 4 additional cases of oral clefts that were identified in 6 other pregnancy registries that include more than 2000 women exposed to lamotrigine monotherapy in the first trimester. Results from a prospective, multi-center, long-term, observational study of fetal death and malformations during in utero exposure to phenytoin, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, or valproate suggest that valproate poses the greatest risk for serious adverse outcomes. Enrollment was limited to pregnant women receiving monotherapy with one of the four agents for epilepsy. The outcomes of 333 infants were analyzed. The total percentages of serious adverse outcomes (fetal death or congenital malformations) were as follows: lamotrigine 1%, carbamazepine 8.2%, phenytoin 10.7%, and valproate 20.3%. Fetal deaths occurred in 3.6% of the carbamazepine and phenytoin groups, 2.9% of the valproate group, and no deaths occurred with lamotrigine. Congenital malformations were reported as follows: lamotrigine 1% (ventricular septal defect), carbamazepine 4.5%, phenytoin 7.1%, and valproate 17.4%. In vitro data reveal that lamotrigine inhibits dihydrofolate reductase. Animal data show that lamotrigine decreased fetal, placental, and maternal folate concentrations. Significant reductions in folate concentrations are associated with teratogenesis. Supplementation with folinic acid returned animal folate concentrations to normal. An increased risk of cleft lip or cleft palate may exist when lamotrigine is used during early pregnancy, although the FDA advises that this remains uncertain. The FDA also cautions that the number of cases of cleft palate reported with newer anticonvulsants is not sufficient to provide a comparison among agents. It should be noted that untreated seizure or bipolar disorders may pose health risks to the expectant mother and fetus. However, specific use of lamotrigine should be considered in pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risks to the fetus, which may include cleft palate. If lamotrigine is used, physiological changes during pregnancy may affect lamotrigine concentrations and therapeutic effect (see Pharmacokinetics). Upward dosage adjustments may be necessary to maintain clinical response during pregnancy, and downward dosage adjustments may be necessary to prevent toxicity after pregnancy. Physicians are advised to recommend that pregnant patients receiving lamotrigine enroll in the North American Antiepileptic Drug (NAAED) Pregnancy Registry to provide information about the effects of in utero exposure to the drug. Patients must call 1-888-233-2334 to enroll in the registry. Physicians may enroll patients in the Lamotrigine Pregnancy Registry prior to any prenatal diagnostic tests and before fetal outcomes are known by calling 1-800-336-2176.
 
Lamotrigine is distributed into breast milk (see Pharmacokinetics); however, the effects on infants exposed while breast-feeding are unknown. Thus, breast-feeding of infants by women who are taking lamotrigine is not recommended.
 
Due to life-threatening rashes (including Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis), lamotrigine carries a boxed warning stating the drug should be discontinued if rash appears at any time during treatment. It is important to note that discontinuation of lamotrigine may not prevent progression to a higher level of severity; therefore patients should be closely monitored. Age is the only factor currently known to predict the occurrence or severity of a rash, with children at increased risk. The incidence of severe, potentially life-threatening rash in children is much higher than that reported in adults receiving lamotrigine. According to the manufacturer, reports from clinical trials suggest as many as 8 in 1000 pediatric patients develop a potentially life-threatening rash compared to 3 in 1000 adult patients (see Adverse Reactions). In a prospective cohort study of 1,983 pediatric patients receiving adjunctive lamotrigine for epilepsy, there was 1 rash-related death. Other possible but unproven factors which may contribute to development of a rash include concurrent use of valproate, exceeding the initial recommended dose, or exceeding the recommended dose titration. Almost all cases of life-threatening rash have occurred within the first 2—8 weeks of treatment. However, a prolonged duration of therapy should not preclude the possibility of an association to the drug. Slow titration of dosage is recommended. Pediatric patients weighing < 6.7 kg (roughly 15 lb) should generally not receive lamotrigine because therapy cannot be initiated using the currently available tablet strengths (see Dosage). The safety and efficacy of lamotrigine have not been established for the treatment of bipolar disorder in children. In children, immediate-release lamotrigine is only indicated as adjunctive therapy for the generalized seizures of Lennox-Gastaut syndrome and as adjunctive therapy in partial seizures, although it has been used for other refractory seizure types. The safety and efficacy of extended-release lamotrigine has not been established in children less than 13 years of age and has not been formally evaluated in children for any indication other than partial onset seizures. Treatment with antidepressants is associated with an increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior in children and adolescents with depression and other psychiatric disorders. It is not known whether lamotrigine is associated with a similar risk in this population. The smallest quantity of tablets should be prescribed to reduce the risk for overdose. The clinician should consider changing or possibly discontinuing lamotrigine in patients whose symptoms worsen, or in patients who present with abrupt onset of suicidal ideation or other new and severe behavioral disturbances. Parents and/or caregivers should closely monitor any child receiving treatment for bipolar disorder.

    Headline
    Asian patients, HLA-B*15:02-positive, serious rash
    General Information
    Cases of life-threatening serious rash, including Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), and/or rash-related death have been caused by lamotrigine. The rate of serious rash is greater in pediatric populations than in adults. Additional factors that may increase the risk of rash include coadministration with valproate, exceeding recommended initial dose of lamotrigine, exceeding recommended dose escalation for lamotrigine, or the presence of the genetic variant Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA)-B*15:02 allele. However, cases have occurred in the absence of these factors. Weigh the risks and benefits of lamotrigine therapy in individuals who are HLA-B*15:02-positive. Retrospective case-control studies in Asian patients of certain ancestry (e.g., Han Chinese and Thai) suggest that the HLA-B*15:02 allele is associated with an increased risk (approximately 2 to 3 times higher) of developing SJS/TEN in individuals receiving lamotrigine. Application of HLA genotyping as a screening tool has important limitations and must never substitute for appropriate clinical vigilance and treatment management. Many HLA-B*15:02-positive individuals treated with lamotrigine will not develop SJS/TEN or other hypersensitivity reactions, and these reactions can still occur in individuals who are HLA-B*15:02-negative of any ethnicity. The risk of nonserious rash may also be increased when the recommended initial dose and/or the rate of dose escalation for lamotrigine is exceeded and in individuals with a history of allergy or rash to other antiepileptic drugs.
    Headline
    mood disorder, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Monitor for worsening of depression, suicidal thoughts or behavior, or any unusual changes in behavior in individuals with mood disorder or suicidal ideation; antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), including lamotrigine, increase the risk of suicidal thoughts or behavior in individuals taking these medications for any indication. Suicidal behavior and ideation may also occur after lamotrigine discontinuation. When considering AED use, balance the risk of suicidal thoughts or behaviors with the risk of untreated illness. The relative risk for suicidal thoughts or behavior was higher in clinical trials in subjects with epilepsy than in clinical trials in subjects with psychiatric or other conditions, but the absolute risk differences were similar for the epilepsy and psychiatric indications. Epilepsy and many other illnesses for which AEDs are prescribed are themselves associated with morbidity and mortality and an increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behavior. If suicidal thoughts and behavior emerge during treatment, consider whether the emergence of these symptoms may be related to the illness being treated.
    Headline
    cardiac disease
    General Information
    Weigh the benefits of treatment with lamotrigine against the risks for serious arrhythmias and/or death in individuals with clinically important structural or functional cardiac disease (i.e., heart failure, valvular heart disease, congenital heart disease, conduction system disease, ventricular arrhythmias, cardiac channelopathies [e.g., Brugada syndrome], clinically important ischemic heart disease, or multiple risk factors for coronary artery disease). Consultation with a cardiologist before lamotrigine initiation may be warranted in these individuals. Consider obtaining a baseline ECG in all individuals over 60 years, as the likelihood of undiagnosed cardiac conduction abnormalities may be increased in this population, and in individuals younger than 60 years with known cardiac disease or significant cardiac risk factors, such as diabetes, hypertension, familial hypercholesterolemia, or smoking. In most cases, the initial ECG can be obtained while titrating lamotrigine (i.e., when the individual is at the first dose of 25 mg/day) because lamotrigine must be titrated slowly and cardiac adverse events are dose-related. If lamotrigine is used in individuals at risk, consider a repeat ECG when the target dose is reached, particularly when the target dose or lamotrigine serum concentrations are near or above the upper limit of the therapeutic range. Always obtain an ECG before and during concomitant use of lamotrigine with other sodium channel blockers known to impair atrioventricular and/or intraventricular cardiac conduction. Consider obtaining an ECG and cardiology consultation in individuals who experience sudden-onset syncope or presyncope with loss of muscular tone without a clear vasovagal or orthostatic cause while taking lamotrigine. Nonspecific ST and T wave abnormalities should not preclude treatment with lamotrigine. In vitro testing showed that lamotrigine exhibits Class IB antiarrhythmic activity at therapeutically relevant concentrations, indicating lamotrigine could slow ventricular conduction (widen QRS) and induce proarrhythmia in individuals with clinically significant cardiac disease.
    Headline
    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Experience with lamotrigine in individuals with hepatic impairment is limited. Lamotrigine dosage adjustment is recommended in individuals with moderate and severe hepatic impairment or hepatic failure.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Use lamotrigine with caution in individuals with renal impairment or renal failure due to limited experience with lamotrigine in this population. Base initial lamotrigine dose on the individual's medication regimen; reduced lamotrigine maintenance doses may be effective for individuals with significant renal impairment. Few individuals with severe renal impairment have been evaluated during chronic treatment with lamotrigine.
    Headline
    autoimmune disease
    General Information
    Lamotrigine therapy increases the risk of developing aseptic meningitis. Some individuals treated with lamotrigine who developed aseptic meningitis had underlying diagnoses of systemic lupus erythematosus or other autoimmune disease. Some individuals also had new onset of signs and symptoms of involvement of other organs (predominantly hepatic and renal involvement), which may suggest that in these cases the aseptic meningitis observed was part of a hypersensitivity reaction.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Advise individuals to avoid driving or operating machinery or participating in activities requiring coordination and concentration until they have gained sufficient experience on lamotrigine to gauge whether it adversely affects their mental and/or motor performance. Lamotrigine may cause dizziness, somnolence, and other signs and symptoms of central nervous system depression.
    Headline
    adolescents, children
    General Information
    Cases of life-threatening serious rash, including Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis, and/or rash-related death have been caused by lamotrigine. The rate of serious rash is greater in pediatric populations than in adults, approximately 0.3% to 0.8% in children and adolescents (age 2 to 17 years) compared to 0.08% to 0.3% in adults. A rash-related death was reported in a prospectively followed cohort of 1,983 pediatric subjects (age 2 to 16 years) with epilepsy taking lamotrigine as adjunctive therapy. The Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) list includes lamotrigine as a potentially inappropriate medication for use in children and adolescents and use should be avoided. If avoidance is not possible, use lamotrigine with caution and appropriate monitoring.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    In general, use cautious dose selection for lamotrigine for geriatric adults, usually starting at the low end of the dosing range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy. According to the Beers Criteria, anticonvulsants are considered potentially inappropriate medications in geriatric adults with a history of falls or fractures; avoid in at-risk individuals, except for treating seizure and mood disorders, since anticonvulsants can produce ataxia, impaired psychomotor function, syncope, and additional falls. If lamotrigine must be used, consider reducing the use of other CNS-active medications that increase the risk of falls and fractures and implement strategies to reduce fall risk.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data have not identified a drug-associated risk of or a consistent pattern of major congenital malformations (MCM) associated with the use of lamotrigine during pregnancy. The majority of this data is from individuals with epilepsy. MCM rates after first-trimester exposure to lamotrigine monotherapy range from 2% to 2.9% across major pregnancy registries (International Lamotrigine Pregnancy Registry: 2.2%, EURAP: 2.9%, NAAED: 2%), consistent with the general population background rate. One registry (NAAED) reported a 3-fold increased risk of isolated oral clefts; however, this has not been confirmed in other large international registries or meta-analyses. Furthermore, an adjusted odds ratio of 1.45 (95% CI: 0.8, 2.63) for oral clefts with lamotrigine exposure was reported from a case-control study based on 21 congenital anomaly registries covering over 10 million births in Europe. Meta-analyses have not demonstrated an increased risk of fetal death, stillbirth, preterm birth, small for gestational age, or neurodevelopmental delays with in utero lamotrigine exposure. Lamotrigine serum concentrations can decrease at any point during pregnancy and return to pre-pregnancy levels after delivery. Serum concentrations should be monitored throughout pregnancy, with dose adjustments made as needed to maintain seizure control. Lamotrigine should not be discontinued abruptly during pregnancy due to the risk of severe or uncontrolled seizures. Untreated or poorly controlled seizures carry significant risks to both the pregnant individual and the fetus, including preeclampsia, preterm labor, antepartum and postpartum hemorrhage, placental abruption, poor fetal growth, prematurity, fetal death, and mortality. Lamotrigine inhibits dihydrofolate reductase and may reduce folate concentrations, which is associated with neural tube defects. Adequate folic acid supplementation is recommended throughout pregnancy. Where possible, treatment should be optimized before conception, using monotherapy at the lowest effective dose appropriate for the individual's seizure type and epilepsy syndrome. Pregnant individuals taking lamotrigine are encouraged to enroll in the North American Antiepileptic Drug (NAAED) Pregnancy Registry at www.aedpregnancyregistry.org or by calling 1-888-233-2334.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use lamotrigine with caution during breast-feeding. Lamotrigine is present in human milk. Neonates and young infants are at risk for high serum concentrations, especially if the maternal dosage was increased during pregnancy but not reduced after delivery to the pre-pregnancy dosage. Additionally, immature glucuronidation capacity in the infant may contribute to an increased risk of lamotrigine exposure. Closely monitor milk-fed children for adverse effects. Rash, apnea, drowsiness, poor sucking, and poor weight gain (sometimes requiring hospitalization) have been reported in breastfed infants exposed to lamotrigine through milk. However, it is not clear whether these events were caused by lamotrigine. If concerns arise, monitor infant serum concentrations. Discontinue breast-feeding if toxicity occurs. Data from multiple small studies indicate that lamotrigine plasma concentrations in breastfed infants have been reported to reach 50% of maternal plasma concentrations. There is no data on the effect of lamotrigine on milk production.

Atazanavir: (Major) Adjustments in lamotrigine maintenance dose regimens may be necessary with concomitant use of atazanavir boosted with ritonavir. No dose adjustments during dose escalation are necessary. Monitoring lamotrigine plasma concentrations may be indicated, particularly during dosage adjustments. Lamotrigine is metabolized predominantly by glucuronic acid conjugation, and atazanavir with ritonavir induces glucuronidation. Daily doses of atazanavir/ritonavir (300 mg/100 mg) in healthy volunteers reduced the AUC and Cmax of a single lamotrigine dose (100 mg) by approximately 32% and 6%, respectively. The lamotrigine half-life decreased by 27%. Concurrent use of lamotrigine and unboosted atazanavir is not expected to alter the plasma concentration of lamotrigine, and no dose adjustment of lamotrigine is necessary when administered without ritonavir.

carBAMazepine: (Major) Adjustments in lamotrigine escalation and maintenance dose regimens are necessary with concomitant carbamazepine use. Monitoring lamotrigine plasma concentrations may be indicated, particularly during dosage adjustments. During concurrent use of lamotrigine with carbamazepine, lamotrigine steady-state concentration decreased by approximately 40%. Lamotrigine may increase the concentration of the 10, 11-epoxide metabolite of carbamazepine; small studies have demonstrated mixed results when evaluating carbamazepine-epoxide concentrations in the presence of lamotrigine. Limited data suggest that there is a higher incidence of dizziness, diplopia, ataxia, and blurred vision in patients receiving lamotrigine with carbamazepine than in patients receiving lamotrigine with other AEDs; the mechanism of the interaction is not known.

cloZAPine: (Major) One report has described an interaction between lamotrigine and clozapine. A 3-fold increase in clozapine concentrations occurred in a patient after lamotrigine was added to the drug regimen. The patient experienced drowsiness and dizziness that were clinically significant. Measurement of clozapine plasma concentrations during the time of the interaction and after lamotrigine was discontinued indicated a probable interaction, although the mechanism of the interaction is not certain. Clozapine may interact with anticonvulsants in several ways; therefore concurrent use of clozapine in patients on antiepileptic medications is not recommended if seizures are not controlled. Clozapine lowers the seizure threshold in a dose-dependent manner and thus may induce seizures. Dosage adjustments of clozapine should be cautious.

Conjugated Estrogens: (Major) A lamotrigine maintenance dose increase of up to 2-fold may be required during concomitant use of estrogen hormones. Increase the dose no more rapidly than 50 to 100 mg/day every week based on clinical response. Coadministration of an oral contraceptive containing 30 mcg of ethinyl estradiol has been observed to decrease the AUC and Cmax of lamotrigine by 52% and 39%, respectively. During the oral contraceptive pill-free week, trough lamotrigine concentrations have been observed to increase an average of 2-fold which may transiently increase the risk for lamotrigine-related adverse effects. If lamotrigine-related adverse effects consistently occur during the pill-free week, the overall lamotrigine maintenance dose may need to be reduced.

Coadministration of an oral contraceptive containing 30 mcg of ethinyl estradiol has been observed to decrease the AUC and Cmax of lamotrigine by 52% and 39%, respectively. During the oral contraceptive pill-free week, trough lamotrigine concentrations have been observed to increase an average of 2-fold which may transiently increase the risk for lamotrigine-related adverse effects. If lamotrigine-related adverse effects consistently occur during the pill-free week, the overall lamotrigine maintenance dose may need to be reduced. (Moderate) Patients taking progestin hormones for contraception may consider an alternate or additional form of contraception, such as nonhormonal and/or barrier methods, during and for at least 1 month after discontinuation of lamotrigine. Higher-dose hormonal regimens may also be considered. Patients taking these hormones for other indications may need to be monitored for reduced clinical effect while on lamotrigine with dose adjustments made based on clinical efficacy. The AUC and Cmax of levonorgestrel decreased by 19% and 12%, respectively, among 16 volunteers during concurrent use with lamotrigine 300 mg/day. Serum progesterone concentrations did not suggest ovulation, however, serum FSH, LH, and estradiol concentrations suggested some loss of suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis.
Tetracaine: (Moderate) Consider ECG monitoring before and during concomitant use of lamotrigine with other sodium channel blockers known to impair atrioventricular and/or intraventricular cardiac conduction, such as tetracaine. Concomitant use of tetracaine with lamotrigine may increase the risk of proarrhythmia, especially in patients with clinically important structural or functional heart disease. In vitro testing showed that lamotrigine exhibits class IB antiarrhythmic activity at therapeutically relevant concentrations.
Topiramate: (Moderate) Monitor for loss of topiramate efficacy and/or an increase in topiramate-related adverse events during coadministration. Coadministration has resulted in both a 13% decrease and 15% increase in topiramate concentrations.
Tramadol; Acetaminophen: (Moderate) Monitor patients for possible loss of lamotrigine efficacy and seizure activity during coadministration with acetaminophen. Acetaminophen may induce glucuronidation pathways involved in lamotrigine metabolism. During a study among 12 healthy volunteers, concomitant administration of acetaminophen 4 g/day with lamotrigine at steady-state increased the formation clearance of lamotrigine glucuronide conjugates by 45%, decreased lamotrigine AUC by 20%, and reduced lamotrigine trough concentrations by 25%.

Concomitant use of tricyclic antidepressants with lamotrigine may increase the risk of proarrhythmia, especially in patients with clinically important structural or functional heart disease. Valproic Acid, Divalproex Sodium: (Major) Coadministration of valproic acid with lamotrigine can decrease the elimination of lamotrigine. Valproic acid more than doubles the elimination half-life of lamotrigine in both pediatric and adult patients. In a steady-state study involving 10 healthy volunteers, the elimination half-life of lamotrigine increased from 26 to 70 hours with valproate coadministration (a 165% increase). The decrease in apparent clearance of lamotrigine may occur via inhibition of lamotrigine metabolism through competition for liver glucuronidation sites. Serious skin reactions (such as Stevens-Johnson Syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis) have been reported with concomitant lamotrigine and valproate administration. In any patient receiving valproic acid, lamotrigine must be initiated at a reduced dosage that is less than half the dose used in patients not receiving valproic acid. In controlled clinical trials, lamotrigine had no appreciable effect on plasma valproic acid concentrations when added to existing valproic acid therapies. If valproic acid therapy is discontinued, lamotrigine doses may need to be adjusted upward. The inhibitory effects of valproic acid on lamotrigine elimination may offset the actions of other anticonvulsants with known hepatic enzyme-inducing properties on lamotrigine clearance.

  General Information
  Anticonvulsants are thought to carry an increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior. An analysis by the FDA of previously gathered drug data showed that patients receiving anticonvulsants had approximately twice the risk of suicidal behavior or ideation (0.43%) as patients receiving placebo (0.24%). The relative risk for suicidality was higher in patients with epilepsy compared to those with other conditions. Age was not a determining factor. The increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior occurred between 1 and 24 weeks after therapy initiation. However, a longer duration of therapy should not preclude the possibility of an association to the drug since most studies included in the analysis did not continue beyond 24 weeks. Depression has been reported during lamotrigine use. All patients beginning treatment with anticonvulsants or currently receiving such treatment should be closely monitored for emerging or worsening suicidal thoughts/behavior or depression. Patients and caregivers should be informed of the increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors and should be advised to immediately report the emergence or worsening of depression, the emergence of suicidal thoughts or behavior, thoughts of self-harm, or other unusual changes in mood or behavior.
 
Adverse reactions to lamotrigine are difficult to interpret since clinical trials of lamotrigine often were conducted in patients receiving other anticonvulsants concomitantly. Nevertheless, centrally-mediated effects were among the most frequent adverse reactions associated with immediate-release lamotrigine in combination with other AEDs and included dizziness, ataxia, drowsiness, headache, and asthenia. Dose-related side effects included dizziness (females report a higher incidence) and ataxia. Other CNS-related reactions have included tremor, anxiety, and insomnia. Other less frequently reported adverse effects include sweating (diaphoresis), confusion, dysarthria, and emotional lability. In a randomized, comparison study of pediatric patients receiving valproic acid or lamotrigine as monotherapy for absence seizures, nervousness occurred in 1 out of 19 patients in the lamotrigine group. Headache was reported by 2 patients receiving lamotrigine. In a clinical trial of extended-release lamotrigine as an adjunct to other AEDs in the treatment of partial seizures, the following CNS-related effects occurred more frequently in the active treatment group than the placebo group: vertigo (4% vs 0%), asthenia/asthenic conditions (9% vs 5%), gait disturbance (2% vs 0%), dizziness (19% vs 5%), drowsiness (7% vs 5%), tremor (7% vs 2%), cerebellar balance disorder (5% vs 0%), nystagmus (3% vs 1%), depression (4% vs 1%), and anxiety (3% vs 0%).
 
In a clinical trial of extended-release lamotrigine as an adjunct to other AEDs in the treatment of partial seizures, chest pain (unspecified) occurred more frequently in the active treatment group than the placebo group (3% vs 1%).
 
In a clinical trial of extended-release lamotrigine as an adjunct to other AEDs in the treatment of partial seizures, hot flashes occurred more frequently in the active treatment group than the placebo group (3% vs 0%).
 
In a clinical trial of extended-release lamotrigine as an adjunct to other AEDs in the treatment of partial seizures, alopecia occurred more frequently in the active treatment group than the placebo group (2% vs 1%).
 
In a clinical trial of extended-release lamotrigine as an adjunct to other AEDs in the treatment of partial seizures, the following effects occurred more frequently in the active treatment group than the placebo group: pain (unspecified) (2% vs 1%) and myalgia (3% vs 0%).
 
In a clinical trial of extended-release lamotrigine as adjunct treatment to other AEDs in the treatment of partial seizures, the following types of infection occurred more frequently in the active treatment group than the placebo group: influenza/influenza-like illness (3% vs 2%) and sinusitis (3% vs 1%). Related effects included pharyngolaryngeal pain (3% vs 2%), epistaxis (2% vs 1%), and sinus congestion (2% vs 0%). In a small controlled trial of pediatric patients 1—24 months of age, the immediate-release formulation of lamotrigine was associated with an increased risk of infectious and respiratory adverse reactions including bronchiolitis, bronchitis, ear infection, eye infection, otitis externa, pharyngitis, urinary tract infection, viral infection, nasal congestion, cough, and apnea. During clinical trials with immediate-release lamotrigine in combination with other AEDs, rhinitis was reported.
 
During clinical trials, nausea/vomiting was among the most frequent adverse reactions associated with immediate-release lamotrigine in combination with other AEDs and was considered to be a dose-related side effect. Less frequently reported gastrointestinal (GI) effects included dyspepsia. In a randomized, comparison study of pediatric patients receiving valproic acid or lamotrigine as monotherapy for absence seizures, increased appetite occurred in 1 out of 19 patients in the lamotrigine group. In a clinical trial of extended-release lamotrigine as an adjunct to other AEDs in the treatment of partial seizures, the following GI effects occurred more frequently in the active treatment group than the placebo group: diarrhea (8% vs 5%), nausea (7% vs 2%), abdominal pain (6% vs 4%), vomiting (4% vs 2%), constipation (3% vs 1%), anorexia/decreased appetite (3% vs 2%), weight gain (2% vs 1%), and xerostomia (3% vs 2%).
 
During clinical trials, diplopia and blurred vision were among the most frequent adverse reactions associated with immediate-release lamotrigine in combination with other AEDs. Both diplopia and blurred vision were considered to be dose-related effects. In a randomized, comparison study of pediatric patients receiving valproic acid or lamotrigine as monotherapy for absence seizures, diplopia occurred in 1 out of 19 patients in the lamotrigine group. In a clinical trial of extended-release lamotrigine as an adjunct to other AEDs in the treatment of partial seizures, the following effects occurred more frequently in the active treatment group than the placebo group: blurred vision (4% vs 2%) and diplopia (4% vs 0%).
 
Rash (unspecified) is the most common cause for discontinuation of lamotrigine therapy. Approximately 10% of patients develop erythema and a maculopapular rash; however, rashes associated with lamotrigine do not have specific identifying characteristics. The rash usually develops during the first 2—8 weeks of therapy and may resolve despite continuation of therapy. Isolated cases occurring after prolonged treatment have been reported. Nonserious rashes associated with lamotrigine administration have occurred 3 times more frequently in patients with a history of rash or allergy to other anticonvulsants than in those without this history. Serious dermatologic reactions have been reported in approximately 1% of patients and include Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, angioedema, and pruritus. Serious rashes, some of which have been life-threatening or rarely fatal, are observed more often in children than adults. Post-marketing surveillance and controlled clinical studies have revealed that as many as 8 in 1000 pediatric patients develop a potentially life-threatening rash compared with 3 in 1000 adults. In clinical trials evaluating bipolar disorder, the rate of serious rash for adult patients receiving lamotrigine as initial monotherapy was 0.08% (0.8/1000) and 0.13% (1.3/1000) in adult patients receiving lamotrigine as adjunctive therapy. (NOTE: Lamotrigine is not indicated for children < 16 years of age except for those who have seizures associated with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome or as adjunctive therapy for partial seizures or tonic-clonic seizures.) Rash also appears to be more common in patients receiving valproic acid concomitantly or when the recommended dose escalation schedule is exceeded. Rash has occurred, however, when these factors were not present. In a randomized, comparison study of pediatric patients receiving valproic acid or lamotrigine as monotherapy for absence seizures, rash occurred in 1 out of 19 patients in the lamotrigine group. In a clinical trial of extended-release lamotrigine as an adjunct to other AEDs in the treatment of partial seizures, rash occurred more frequently in the active treatment group than the placebo group (2% vs 1%). Initiating therapy at the lowest dosage possible and escalating slowly appears to minimize the occurrence of skin rash. Bullous rash or vesicular rash should also result in prompt discontinuation of lamotrigine therapy and appropriate evaluation. Some reports suggest that when strict adherence of lamotrigine dose titrations and dose reduction in patients receiving concomitant valproate are followed, the incidence of serious and life-threatening rash is rare.
 
Hypersensitivity reactions, some fatal or life threatening, have also occurred with lamotrigine. These reactions may include clinical features of multiorgan dysfunction such as hepatic abnormalities and evidence of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Early manifestations of hypersensitivity (e.g., fever, lymphadenopathy) may be present even though a rash is not evident. Fatalities associated with hepatic failure or multiorgan failure occurred in 2 of 3796 adults and 4 of 2435 pediatric patients during clinical trials. Fatalities have also been reported rarely during post-marketing use. Most cases have been in association with serious medical conditions (e.g. sepsis, status epilepticus). Three cases of multiorgan dysfunction and DIC occurred within 14 days of adding lamotrigine to an existing antiepileptic drug regimen, with subsequent resolution of symptoms following discontinuation of the drug.
 
Hematological reactions observed during immediate-release lamotrigine therapy have included anemia, eosinophilia, leukopenia, or thrombocytopenia. These adverse reactions are rare. Although, lamotrigine has weak antifolate activity, no clinically significant alterations in serum or erythrocyte folate concentrations have been identified. Abnormal laboratory values that have been associated with administration of extended-release lamotrigine include decreased total white blood cells and monocytes.
 
A report of attempted suicide with lamotrigine 3000 mg resulting in serum concentrations of 18 mcg/ml produced clinical symptoms of ataxia, nausea/vomiting, and nystagmus. These symptoms resolved within 2 days. In clinical trials, 4 cases of overdose occurred, all with complete recovery. Two of the overdoses occurred in a 14 year old female and a 4 year old male. They had ingested 3000 mg and 1000 mg of lamotrigine, respectively. The 14 year old lost consciousness and the 4 year old progressed to coma. With supportive therapy, the 4 year old recovered fully in 3 days. The 14 year old child also recovered fully.
 
During add-on placebo controlled trials, female patients taking immediate-release lamotrigine reported vaginitis (4.1%) and amenorrhea (1.9%) compared to 0.5% occurrence in patients on placebo.
 
Lamotrigine may cause hyponatremia. Lamotrigine decreases calcium conductance and may act at voltage-sensitive sodium channels to stabilize neuronal membranes. Both actions are also provided by carbamazepine, which has been associated with the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (SIADH). Decreasing synthetic ADH requirements have been documented in 2 patients who received lamotrigine (see Drug Interactions).

    Lamotrigine can cause serious rash requiring hospitalization and discontinuation of treatment. Nearly all cases of life-threatening rashes caused by lamotrigine have occurred within 2 to 8 weeks of treatment initiation. However, isolated cases have occurred after prolonged treatment (e.g., 6 months). Accordingly, duration of therapy cannot be relied upon as a means to predict the potential risk heralded by the first appearance of a rash. Although benign rashes are also caused by lamotrigine, it is not possible to predict reliably which rashes will prove to be serious or life-threatening. Eosinophilia is often present. This disorder is variable in its expression, and other organ systems not noted here may be involved. Rare fatalities from multiorgan failure have also been reported with lamotrigine in postmarketing. Pruritus was reported with lamotrigine in 2% of pediatric subjects and 5% or more of adults. Photosensitivity was reported with lamotrigine in 2% of pediatric subjects and in postmarketing. Maculopapular rash and urticaria were reported infrequently (0.1% to 1%) with lamotrigine. Angioedema, erythema, and eosinophilia were reported rarely (less than 0.1%) with lamotrigine. Early manifestations of hypersensitivity (e.g., fever, lymphadenopathy) may be present even though a rash is not evident. If such signs or symptoms are present, evaluate immediately. Discontinue lamotrigine if an alternative etiology for the signs or symptoms cannot be established.

    hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis
    General Information
    Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) has been reported with lamotrigine. HLH is a life-threatening syndrome of pathologic immune activation characterized by clinical signs and symptoms of extreme systemic inflammation. It is associated with high mortality rates if not recognized early and treated. Common findings include fever, hepatosplenomegaly, rash, lymphadenopathy, neurologic symptoms, cytopenias, high serum ferritin, hypertriglyceridemia, and liver function and coagulation abnormalities. In cases of HLH reported with lamotrigine, individuals have presented with signs of systemic inflammation (fever, rash, hepatosplenomegaly, and organ system dysfunction) and blood dyscrasias. Symptoms have been reported to occur within 8 to 24 days after the initiation of lamotrigine. Evaluate individuals who develop early manifestations of pathologic immune activation immediately, and consider a diagnosis of HLH. Discontinue lamotrigine if an alternative etiology for the signs or symptoms cannot be established.

    General Information
    Lamotrigine has been reported to cause laboratory test interference with the assay used in some rapid urine drug screens, which can result in false-positive readings, particularly for phencyclidine (PCP). Use a more specific analytical method to confirm a positive result.

Description
  Text
  Lamotrigine is an oral antiepileptic drug (AED) indicated for adjunctive therapy for partial-onset seizures, primary generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and generalized seizures of Lennox-Gastaut syndrome and conversion to monotherapy in individuals with partial-onset seizures who are receiving treatment with carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, primidone, or valproate as the single AED. Lamotrigine is also indicated for the maintenance treatment of bipolar I disorder to delay the time to occurrence of mood episodes in individuals treated for acute mood episodes with standard therapy. Treatment of acute manic or mixed episodes with lamotrigine is not recommended; the effectiveness of lamotrigine in the acute treatment of mood episodes has not been established. Cases of life-threatening serious rashes, including Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), and/or rash-related death have been caused by lamotrigine. The incidence of serious rash is greater in pediatric individuals compared to adults. Retrospective case-control studies in people of certain Asian ancestry (e.g., Han Chinese and Thai) suggest that the HLA-B*1502 allele is associated with an approximately 2 to 3 times higher risk of developing SJS/TEN in individuals using lamotrigine. Benign rashes are also caused by lamotrigine; however, it is not possible to predict which rashes will prove to be serious or life-threatening. Therefore, lamotrigine should be discontinued at the first sign of rash, unless the rash is clearly not medication-related. Lamotrigine was FDA-approved in December 1994.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The exact mechanism of anticonvulsant activity is not known. One proposed mechanism of action of lamotrigine, the relevance of which remains to be established in humans, involves an effect on sodium channels. In vitro pharmacological studies suggest that lamotrigine inhibits voltage-sensitive sodium channels, thereby stabilizing neuronal membranes and consequently modulating presynaptic transmitter release of excitatory amino acids (e.g., glutamate and aspartate). The mechanisms by which lamotrigine exerts its therapeutic action in bipolar disorder have not been established.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Immediate-release tablets
Lamotrigine is rapidly and completely absorbed after oral administration with negligible first-pass metabolism (absolute bioavailability is 98%). The bioavailability is not affected by food. All of the immediate-release oral formulations have a similar rate and extent of absorption. The Cmax occurs 1.4 to 2.3 hours after an oral dose of immediate-release lamotrigine in normal adult volunteers or in adults receiving enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants and up to 4.8 hours after an oral dose in subjects on concomitant valproic acid. Lamotrigine tablets for oral suspension were found to be equivalent, whether administered as dispersed in water, chewed and swallowed, or swallowed whole, to the lamotrigine compressed tablets in terms of rate and extent of absorption. In terms of rate and extent of absorption, lamotrigine orally disintegrating tablets, whether disintegrated in the mouth or swallowed whole with water, were equivalent to the lamotrigine compressed tablets swallowed with water.
 
Extended-release tablets (e.g., Lamictal XR)
The relative bioavailability of the extended-release tablets is about 21% lower than immediate-release lamotrigine in subjects receiving enzyme-inducing antiepileptic drugs (AEDs). The bioavailability of lamotrigine is not affected by food. The steady state bioavailability of extended-release lamotrigine relative to immediate-release lamotrigine at equivalent daily doses was 0.79 (AUC0-24) and 0.71 (Cmax) in subjects taking carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, or primidone, 0.94 (AUC0-24) and 0.88 (Cmax) in subjects taking valproic acid, and 1 (AUC0-24) and 0.89 (Cmax) in subjects taking other AEDs (the Tmax was 4 to 6 hours, 9 to 11 hours, and 6 to 10 hours, respectively). Plasma concentrations after the administration of extended-release lamotrigine are characterized by lower peaks, longer time to peaks, and lower peak-to-trough fluctuations compared to immediate-release lamotrigine; however, there are not significant changes in trough plasma concentrations between the two formulations. In some people, a reduction in exposure of up to 70% has been observed when switching from the immediate-release to the extended-release formulation.
 
Suspension (e.g., Subvenite)
A pharmacokinetic study in healthy adult subjects under fasting conditions at a single 100 mg dose concentration demonstrated similar bioavailability for lamotrigine oral suspension and oral tablet. The bioavailability is not affected by food, but the Tmax was delayed by 3 hours in presence of food.

    Description
    The pharmacokinetics of lamotrigine following a single 2 mg/kg dose were evaluated in 2 studies in pediatric subjects (n = 29 for subjects 10 months to 5.9 years and n = 26 for subjects 5 to 11 years). Forty-three subjects received concomitant therapy with other antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) and 12 subjects received lamotrigine as monotherapy. Lamotrigine pharmacokinetic parameters Tmax, elimination half-life, and clearance were 1.6 to 3 hours, approximately 7 hours, and 2.54 to 3.62 mL/minute/kg after the administration of lamotrigine in subjects taking carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, or primidone, 2.9 to 4.5 hours, 44.9 to 65.8 hours, and 0.24 to 0.47 mL/minute/kg after administration in subjects taking valproate, and 3.3 to 5.2 hours, approximately 19 hours, and 0.89 to 1.2 mL/minute/kg after administration in subjects taking other AEDs, respectively. Population pharmacokinetic analyses involving subjects 2 to 18 years demonstrated that lamotrigine clearance was influenced predominantly by total body weight and concurrent AED therapy. The oral clearance of lamotrigine was higher, on a body weight basis, in pediatric subjects than in adults. Weight-normalized lamotrigine clearance was higher in those subjects weighing less than 30 kg compared with those weighing greater than 30 kg. Accordingly, subjects weighing less than 30 kg may need an increase of as much as 50% in maintenance doses, based on clinical response, as compared with subjects weighing greater than 30 kg being administered the same AEDs. These analyses also revealed that, after accounting for body weight, lamotrigine clearance was not significantly influenced by age. Thus, the same weight-adjusted doses should be administered to children irrespective of differences in age. Concomitant AEDs which influence lamotrigine clearance in adults were found to have similar effects in children.

    Pregnancy
Increased clearance of lamotrigine occurs during pregnancy. Of 14 women who were taking lamotrigine monotherapy and not on drugs known to affect lamotrigine metabolism, the mean relative clearance was 0.71 +/- 0.24 (daily lamotrigine dose in mg /patient weight in kg)/(serum lamotrigine concentration in mg/L) before conception, 1.55 +/- 1.02 (mg/kg)/(mg/L) during the first trimester, 1.93 +/- 1.22 (mg/kg)/(mg/L) during the second trimester, 2.19 +/- 1.09 (mg/kg)/(mg/L) during the third trimester, and 0.74 +/- 0.11 (mg/kg)/(mg/L) after delivery.   Text
  Lamotrigine is administered orally. Estimates of the mean apparent Vd following oral administration ranged from 0.9 to 1.3 L/kg independent of dose and is similar following single and multiple doses in both subjects with epilepsy and in healthy volunteers. Protein binding is 55% at plasma concentrations up to 10 mcg/mL. Lamotrigine is metabolized predominantly by glucuronic acid conjugation; the major metabolite 2-N-glucuronide is an inactive conjugate. After multiple dosing in normal adult volunteers receiving no other medications, lamotrigine may induce its own metabolism which may decrease the half-life by 25% and increase plasma clearance by 37%. The mean elimination half-life of lamotrigine following multiple oral doses in adult subjects with epilepsy is approximately 32 hours (range 8.5 to 59 hours), but the half-life and apparent oral clearance vary depending on concomitant antiepileptic drugs. Following oral administration of 250 mg of radiolabeled lamotrigine to 6 healthy volunteers, 95% was recovered in urine and 2% in feces. Contents consisted of unchanged lamotrigine (10%), 2-N-glucoronide (76%), 5-N-glucuronide (10%), 2-N-methyl metabolite (0.14%), and other minor metabolites (4%).
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: UGT1A4, UGT2B7, OCT2
Lamotrigine is predominately metabolized by glucuronic acid conjugation; hence, drugs that are known to inhibit or induce glucuronidation may alter its clearance. In addition, lamotrigine is an inhibitor of renal tubular secretion via organic cationic transporter 2 (OCT2) proteins at potentially clinically relevant concentrations, and may increase plasma concentrations of agents substantially excreted by this route.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Initial, escalation, and maintenance doses should generally be reduced by 25% in patients with moderate hepatic impairment or severe impairment without ascites, and by 50% in patients with severe hepatic impairment with ascites. Adjust doses as needed according to clinical response.
  Renal Impairment
  Dosage should be modified depending on clinical response and degree of renal impairment. A reduced maintenance dosage may be effective, but due to limited experience, no quantitative recommendations are available.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
Hemodialysis lowers the apparent elimination half-life of lamotrigine by 70% to 80%. The half-life decreases from 43 to 57 hours in anuric subjects between dialysis treatments to 13 hours during dialysis treatments. Approximately 20% of lamotrigine present in the body is removed after a standard 4-hour dialysis session. No quantitative recommendations are available for adjustments.
