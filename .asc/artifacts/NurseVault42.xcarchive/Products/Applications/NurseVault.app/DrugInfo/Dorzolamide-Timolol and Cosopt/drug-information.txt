  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  NOTE: This monograph discusses the contraindications/precautions of the dorzolamide-timolol combination product. Clinicians may wish to consult the individual monographs for more information about each agent.
 
Patients with low endothelial cell counts are at increased risk for the development of corneal edema. Dorzolamide; timolol should be used cautiously in these patients.
 
Since ophthalmic timolol is absorbed systemically, all of the precautions and contraindications pertaining to systemically-administered beta-blockers should be carefully considered prior to prescribing timolol. Pre-existing cardiac disease increases the risk of adverse reactions to systemic beta-blockers. Due to the potential for systemic effects from the timolol component, dorzolamide; timolol (Cosopt) is contraindicated in patients with sinus bradycardia, sick sinus syndrome, second or third degree AV block, overt heart failure, or cardiogenic shock. Treatment with dorzolamide; timolol should be discontinued at the first signs of cardiac failure. Rare deaths have been reported in patients at high risk for cardiac complications secondary to topical treatment with beta-blockers, particularly in patients with severe left ventricular dysfunction. Timolol should be used with caution in patients with other circulatory disorders including hypotension, coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease, vasospastic angina, and Raynaud's disease.
 
Timolol is a non-selective beta-blocker and should be avoided in patients with pulmonary disease; alternative selective ophthalmic beta1-blockers (e.g., betaxolol, levobetaxolol) are preferred in the treatment of glaucoma in patients at risk for bronchospasm. Timolol is contraindicated in patients with a history of bronchial asthma or other pulmonary disease [e.g., severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), emphysema, bronchitis] in which acute bronchospasm would put them at risk. Timolol should be used with extreme caution (use alternative non-selective beta-blocking agents where possible) in patients with mild to moderate COPD, emphysema, or bronchitis, or history of bronchospastic disease other than bronchial asthma. Severe respiratory reactions, including death due to bronchospasm in patients with asthma, have been reported with topical application of beta-adrenergic blocking agents.
 
Because of potential effects of beta-blockade on blood pressure and pulse, β-blockers should be used with caution in patients with cerebrovascular insufficiency (cerebrovascular disease) or stroke. If signs or symptoms suggesting reduced cerebral blood flow develop following initiation of beta-blocker, alternative therapy should be considered.
 
Dorzolamide; timolol (Cosopt) has not been studied in patients with renal disease (e.g., renal impairment or renal failure). Because dorzolamide and its metabolites are excreted primarily by the kidney, dorzolamide; timolol is not recommended in patients with severe renal impairment (CrCl < 30 ml).
 
Dorzolamide; timolol (Cosopt) contains dorzolamide and should not be administered to patients with carbonic anhydrase inhibitor hypersensitivity. In addition, since dorzolamide is a sulfonamide derivative, it should not be used in patients with sulfonamide hypersensitivity. Topical administration of dorzolamide; timolol can result in systemic absorption of dorzolamide. Dorzolamide is a chemical sulfonamide possessing a structure and pharmacologic activity distinct from the bacteriostatic sulfonamides. Although not reported with dorzolamide, administration of sulfonamides by any route can precipitate a possibly severe reaction in sulfonamide-sensitive patients.
 
Dorzolamide; timolol (Cosopt) is formulated with the preservative benzalkonium chloride, which may be absorbed by soft contact lenses. Users of soft contact lenses should not administer dorzolamide; timolol while wearing the lenses. Lenses may be reinserted 15 minutes following administration of dorzolamide; timolol.
 
The use of multiple dose containers of ophthalmic products has been associated with bacterial keratitis. Inadvertent contamination of the product may occur if the patient has an ocular infection, or corneal abrasion. If there is any damage to the ocular epithelial surface, dorzolamide; timolol should be used with caution.
 
Dorzolamide; timolol (Cosopt)should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease. There have been no studies on safe use of the dorzolamide component in patients with hepatic impairment.
 
Dorzolamide; timolol (Cosopt) is classified as pregnancy category C. Animal studies have revealed that dorzolamide causes some abnormal effects on fetal development at high dosage levels. Adequate studies in pregnant women have not been undertaken and the use of dorzolamide; timolol in pregnant women is only justified if the benefits outweigh the possible risks to the fetus. It is not known if the dorzolamide component is excreted into breast milk, but the timolol component has been detected in human milk following oral and ophthalmic administration of the drug as a single agent. If it is essential for breast-feeding women to use dorzolamide; timolol, an alternative method of feeding is recommended.
 
Safe and effective use of dorzolamide; timolol (Cosopt) in children or infants < 2 years of age has not been established.
 
Dorzolamide; timolol (Cosopt) should be used with caution in patients with hyperthyroidism or thyrotoxicosis. Dorzolamide; timolol contains timolol which is systemically absorbed and can mask the tachycardia that can otherwise be a useful monitoring parameter in thyroid disease. Patients suspected of developing thyrotoxicosis should be managed carefully to avoid abrupt withdrawal of beta-blocker therapy that might precipitate a thyroid storm.
 
Dorzolamide; timolol (Cosopt) contains timolol. Beta-blocking agents should be administered with caution in patients subject to spontaneous hypoglycemia or to patients with diabetes mellitus (especially those with labile diabetes) who are receiving insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents. Beta-blockers may mask the signs and symptoms of acute hypoglycemia.
 
Beta-blockers should be used with caution in patients with a pheochromocytoma if effective blockade of alpha and beta receptors is not achieved, because of the risk of hypertension.
 
Dorzolamide; timolol (Cosopt) contains the beta-blocker timolol which is absorbed systemically. General anesthetics can potentiate the antihypertensive effects of beta-blockers and can produce prolonged hypotension. Patients receiving beta-blockers before or during surgery involving general anesthetics that possess negative inotropic effects (e.g., ether or cyclopropane) should be monitored closely for signs of heart failure. The necessity or desirability of withdrawal of beta-adrenergic blocking agents prior to major surgery is controversial. Beta-adrenergic receptor blockade impairs the ability of the heart to respond to beta-adrenergically mediated reflex stimuli. Severe, protracted hypotension and difficulty in restarting the heart have been reported after surgery in patients receiving beta-blockers. For these reasons, in patients undergoing elective surgery, gradual withdrawal of ophthalmic beta-adrenergic receptor blocking agents may be appropriate, depending an evaluation of the potential risks and benefits.
 
In general, beta-blockers can be used safely in elderly patients, however these patients may have unpredictable responses to systemic beta-blockade. Elderly patients appear to be more likely to experience adverse systemic effects following ophthalmic application of beta-blockers. The manufacturer states that no overall differences in the safety or effectiveness of dorzolamide; timolol (Cosopt) have been observed between elderly and younger patients.

    Headline
    acute heart failure, bradycardia, cardiogenic shock, heart failure, second or third-degree AV block
    General Information
    Dorzolamide; timolol is contraindicated in sinus bradycardia, second or third-degree AV block, cardiogenic shock, or acute heart failure. Sympathetic stimulation is necessary in supporting circulatory function in congestive heart failure, and beta blockade carries the potential hazard of further depressing myocardial contractility and precipitating more severe failure. In patients without a history of heart failure, continued depression of the myocardium with beta-blocking agents over a period of time can, in some cases, lead to cardiac failure. At the first sign or symptom of cardiac failure, dorzolamide; timolol should be discontinued.
    Headline
    asthma, bronchospasm, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
    General Information
    The timolol component of dorzolamide; timolol is a non-selective beta-blocker and should be avoided in patients with pulmonary disease; alternative selective ophthalmic beta1-blockers (e.g., betaxolol, levobetaxolol) are preferred in the treatment of glaucoma in patients at risk for bronchospasm. Dorzolamide; timolol is contraindicated in patients with bronchial asthma, a history of bronchial asthma, severe chronic pulmonary disease (COPD), and other diseases or disorders associated with bronchospasm. Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (e.g., chronic bronchitis, emphysema) of mild or moderate severity, bronchospastic disease, or a history of bronchospastic disease (other than bronchial asthma or a history of bronchial asthma, in which dorzolamide; timolol is contraindicated) should, in general, not receive beta-blocking agents, including dorzolamide; timolol. Severe respiratory reactions, including death due to bronchospasm in patients with asthma, have been reported with topical application of beta-adrenergic blocking agents.

    Headline
    atopy
    General Information
    While taking beta-blockers, patients with a history of sensitivity to multiple allergens (atopy) may be more reactive to repeated challenge, whether accidental, diagnostic, or therapeutic. Such patients may be unresponsive to the usual doses of epinephrine used to treat allergic reactions.
    Headline
    myasthenia gravis
    General Information
    Beta-blockers have been reported to potentiate muscle weakness consistent with certain myasthenic symptoms (e.g., diplopia, ptosis, and generalized weakness). Timolol has been reported rarely to increase muscle weakness in some patients with myasthenia gravis or myasthenic symptoms. Therefore, use dorzolamide; timolol cautiously in patients with myasthenia gravis and monitor for exacerbation or deterioration of myasthenia gravis.
    Headline
    hepatic failure, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Dorzolamide; timolol is not recommended for use in patients who have severe renal impairment or renal failure because dorzolamide and its metabolite are excreted predominantly by the kidney. Dorzolamide; timolol should be used with caution in patients who have hepatic failure due to lack of studies of dorzolamide use in such patients.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate, well-controlled studies of ophthalmic dorzolamide; timolol use during human pregnancy. No evidence of teratogenicity has been observed in mice, rats, and rabbits with dorzolamide or timolol at clinically relevant doses. In animal studies, oral dorzolamide was not teratogenic in rabbits at 13 times the recommended human ophthalmic dose (RHOD), but caused fetal vertebral malformations at 31 times the RHOD. There was no evidence of fetal malformations when oral timolol doses up 7,000 times the RHOD were administered to mice, rats, and rabbits.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Opthalmic dorzolamide; timolol is considered compatible with breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of dorzolamide in human milk, but based on its limited systemic absorption, milk levels are expected to be low to undetectable. While timolol has been detected in small amounts in human milk after ophthalmic administration, the levels do not appear to be clinically relevant. To further minimize systemic absorption, apply nasolacrimal occlusion by placing pressure over the tear duct at the inner corner of the eye for at least 1 minute after administration, then blot excess solution with an absorbent tissue.

Interactions
Acarbose: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

It is thought that beta-blockers may worsen anaphylaxis severity by exacerbating bronchospasm or by increasing the release of anaphylaxis mediators; alternately, beta-blocker therapy may make the patient more pharmacodynamically resistance to epinephrine rescue treatment.

Fluticasone; Salmeterol: (Moderate) Beta-blockers will block the pulmonary effects of inhaled beta-agonists, and in some cases may exacerbate bronchospasm in patients with reactive airways. Beta-agonists can sometimes increase heart rate or have other cardiovascular effects, particularly when used in high doses or if hypokalemia is present. Use of a beta-1-selective (cardioselective) beta blocker is recommended whenever possible when this combination of drugs must be used together. Glimepiride: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Miglitol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Description
  Text
  NOTE: This monograph discusses the use of the dorzolamide; timolol combination product for management of increased intraocular pressure. Clinicians may wish to consult the individual monographs for more information about the specific mechanism of action and pharmacokinetics of each agent.
 
Dorzolamide and timolol are combined in an ophthalmic solution for the reduction of elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) in patients with open-angle glaucoma or ocular hypertension who have not adequately responded to topical beta-blockers alone. Dorzolamide is a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor and timolol is a beta-adrenergic blocking agent. Dorzolamide; timolol given twice daily, reduces IOP to a greater extent than ophthalmic administration of either dorzolamide or timolol alone. However, the IOP reduction observed with twice daily dorzolamide; timolol is slightly less than that seen with concomitant administration of 0.5% timolol given twice daily and 2% dorzolamide given three times per day. Cosopt ophthalmic solution (2% dorzolamide; 0.5% timolol) was approved by the FDA in April 1998 and a preservative-free formulation (Cosopt PF) was approved in February 2012.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Dorzolamide and timolol decrease elevated intraocular pressure, whether or not associated with glaucoma, by reducing aqueous humor secretion. Elevated intraocular pressure is a major risk factor in the pathogenesis of optic nerve damage and glaucomatous visual field loss. The higher the level of intraocular pressure, the greater the likelihood of glaucomatous field loss and optic nerve damage.
 
Dorzolamide: Dorzolamide inhibits carbonic anhydrase II. Inhibition of carbonic anhydrase in the ciliary processes of the eye slows the formation of bicarbonate ions. Decreased formation of bicarbonate ions leads to a reduction in sodium and fluid transport which decreases aqueous humor secretion and ultimately intraocular pressure.
Timolol: Timolol maleate is a beta1 and beta2 (non-selective) adrenergic receptor blocking agent that does not have significant intrinsic sympathomimetic, direct myocardial depressant, or local anesthetic (membrane-stabilizing) activity. The exact mechanism by which beta-blockers lower intraocular pressure has not been clearly defined. However, studies suggest that they may block catecholamine-stimulated increases in cyclic AMP within ciliary processes, with subsequent reduction in aqueous humor formation. Visual acuity, pupil size, and accommodation do not appear to be affected by timolol.

  Text
  Dorzolamide; timolol is administered via the ophthalmic route.
Dorzolamide: In the systemic circulation, dorzolamide accumulates in RBCs, where it binds strongly to CA-II, and weakly to CA-I. It is metabolized to a N-desethyl metabolite which has less affinity for CA-II and is less selective than dorzolamide, but also binds to CA-I. Steady state concentrations in RBCs are usually reached after four weeks. Dorzolamide is about 33% bound to plasma protein. Dorzolamide and the metabolite are primarily excreted unchanged in the urine. Systemically-absorbed dorzolamide washes out of RBCs nonlinearly. An initial rapid decline is followed by a slow elimination phase, (i.e., elimination half-life range of about 4 months).
Timolol: Once in the systemic circulation, binding of timolol to plasma proteins is minimal (10%). The drug is relatively lipophilic and is distributed into breast milk. Timolol is extensively metabolized to inactive metabolites. Both the parent drug and metabolites are excreted in the urine. Plasma half-life is about 4 hours.

Administration

    Ophthalmic Administration
    Description
    For ophthalmic use only.

To prevent contamination, do not touch the tip of the dropper to the eye, fingertips, or other surface. To further avoid contamination and the spread of infection, do not use dropper for more than 1 person.

Use the solution from 1 individual unit of Cosopt PF immediately after opening for administration to 1 or both eyes. Discard the remaining contents after administration; sterility cannot be maintained after the individual unit is opened.
