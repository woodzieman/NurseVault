Drug Class: Valerian is obtained from the dried rhizome and roots of the perennial herb Valeriana officinalis (family Valerianaceae).

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 1,200 mg/day PO has been suggested as a maximum dose.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): agitation, blurred vision, confusion, contact dermatitis, dizziness, drowsiness, elevated hepatic enzymes, headache, hepatitis, hypertension, insomnia, lethargy, pruritus, sinus tachycardia, delirium, physiological dependence, withdrawal

In an Overdose: Rare cases of valerian overdose have been published, but no permanent sequelae have been noted to date.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Valerian has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. Products containing valerian are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Consumers should also be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.The American Sleep Disorder Association considers valerian an alternative treatment and has not recommended its use due to a lack of clinical evidence of safety and efficacy. Due to a lack of scientific data and an unknown potential for side effects, valerian is not recommended for use in infants or children under 12 years old without the approval and observation of a health care professional. Caution is also warranted in the elderly due to limited study of valerian in this patient population at this time. If an adult patient decides to self-treat insomnia with valerian, it is recommended that use not exceed 2 weeks without consultation of a health care professional.Although the German Commission E does not list pregnancy or lactation as a contraindication to valerian use, it is unknown what effect valerian would have on a developing human fetus or a breast-feeding infant. Valerian is not recommended for use in pregnancy and breast-feeding at this time. The risks versus benefits to the mother should be considered before using valerian, and use in pregnancy should occur with the advice and supervision of a health care professional.Valerian may cause drowsiness. Driving or operating machinery, or performing other tasks that require mental alertness requires caution when taking valerian. Patients should determine how valerian affects them before participating in these activities. Excessive daytime sedation occurring after valerian use may indicate excessive dosage.Valerian acts on the central nervous system and has sedative effects. It is unknown if valerian could potentially worsen depressive conditions. Caution should be used when patients are being treated for other psychiatric diseases (e.g., depression, dementia) by a health care professional, particularly if they are on prescription medication for such problems. Valerian could interfere with prescribed therapies. The decision to self-treat insomnia with valerian should be weighed against the possibility that the problem could coexist with another medical condition (e.g., depression, thyroid disease, neurologic disorders). Valerian should be used with caution by patients with a seizure disorder or who have any preexisting neurological disease. Patients with psychiatric or neurological problems are encouraged to consult their health care professionals prior to using valerian.There is no experience with valerian use in the presence of renal disease or hepatic disease. Valerian should be used with caution in these populations, particularly in the presence of pre-existing liver dysfunction. Limited case reports allude to a possible connection between valerian use and the development of hepatotoxicity. There is also some evidence, based on preliminary studies, that valerian has cardiovascular effects, and valerian should be utilized with caution in patients with cardiac disease.Prior to undergoing surgery, the surgeon and anesthesiologist should be notified of the use of valerian; the herb has the potential to increase the sedative effects of drugs commonly used during surgical procedures. It may be wise to discontinue this herb 2—3 weeks prior to an elective procedure, when possible. However, due to the potential for benzodiazepine-like withdrawal reactions with valerian , abrupt discontinuation of the herb is not recommended; it is recommended that the herb be gradually tapered when possible.

The valerian derivative, dihydrovaltrate, binds at barbiturate binding sites; valerenic acid has been shown to inhibit enzyme-induced breakdown of GABA in the brain; the non-volatile monoterpenes (valepotriates) have sedative activity. These interactions are probably pharmacodynamic in nature. There is a possibility of interaction with valerian at normal prescription dosages of anxiolytics, sedatives, and hypnotics (including barbiturates and benzodiazepines). Patients should not abruptly stop taking their prescribed psychoactive medications.

Description
  Text
  Valerian is obtained from the dried rhizome and roots of the perennial herb Valeriana officinalis (family Valerianaceae). Valerian has a long history of use in Europe, including ancient Greece, for the treatment of various internal and external ailments. In the U.S., valerian was used as a tranquilizer through the time of World War 2, and was once listed in the United States Pharmacopeia (USP) prior to 1945, but the USP later deemed the evidence for valerian's use too limited. Germany's Commission E has approved valerian as an effective minor sedative and hypnotic. Valerian's purported efficacy for nervous anxiety and insomnia is supported primarily by subjective clinical data; most of the available randomized controlled trials have not shown a significant effect of the herb on sleep latency or anxiety symptoms. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Veteran's Affairs/Department of Defense (VA/DoD) recommends against the use of valerian for treatment for sleep onset or sleep maintenance insomnia in adults due to a lack of data. Valerian is "generally recognized as safe (GRAS)" by the FDA. Rare cases of hepatotoxicity have been reported with valerian supplementation.
 
NOTE: In the U.S., nutraceuticals are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for nutraceuticals as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since nutraceuticals are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: The actual active principle of valerian is uncertain, and sedative activity may be the result of cumulative actions of all the components identified in the rhizome. Valerian appears to have activity similar to benzodiazepines. Valerian exhibits some binding to GABA-A receptors and may increase GABA release from brain synaptosomes. The valerian derivative, dihydrovaltrate, binds at barbiturate binding sites. Also, valerenic acid has been shown to inhibit enzyme-induced breakdown of GABA in the brain, and the non-volatile monoterpenes (valepotriates) have sedative activity. There is also some evidence that valerian possesses smooth muscle relaxant and vasodilatory properties. While anticonvulsant action may be attributable to valerian extract, the activity is probably too limited to be of clinical significance. Valerian root contains an odorous volatile oil that consists of bornyl isovalerenate, and the sesquiterpenes valerenic acid and its derivatives. Additional components include at least six valepotriates which have been identified and chemically classified. The valepotriates, including valtrate, isovaltrate, dihydrovaltrate, and acevaltrate, possess an epoxide structure and are thought to be the major sedative compounds. Under suitable circumstances, the valerian rhizome (root) is cultivated to yield approximately 0.9% of the essential oils and 0.5% of valerenic acid and derivatives once processed and dried. The valepotriates have demonstrated alkylating properties in cell cultures, leading to concern over possible mutagenic and cytotoxic effects with long term use of the herb. No mutagenic toxicity has been observed in animals or humans in clinical use of valerian to date. Many other components of valerian have been identified, including pyridine alkaloids and aromatic acids, and it is uncertain what role these compounds play in the pharmacology of the herb.
