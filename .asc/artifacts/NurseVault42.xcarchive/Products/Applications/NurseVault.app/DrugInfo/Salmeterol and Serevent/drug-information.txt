Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Salmeterol should not be used in patients who have demonstrated a hypersensitivity reaction to salmeterol or to any of the components in the commercial product. Immediate hypersensitivity reactions may occur after administration of salmeterol, as demonstrated by rare cases of urticaria, angioedema, rash, and bronchospasm.
 
Like other inhaled beta-agonists, salmeterol can produce paradoxical bronchospasm, which may be life threatening. If paradoxical bronchospasm occurs, it should be treated immediately with a short-acting, inhaled bronchodilator, and salmeterol should be discontinued immediately and alternative therapy instituted. In addition, upper airway symptoms such as laryngeal spasm, irritation, or swelling, such as stridor and choking, have been reported with salmeterol use.
 
Due to its relatively prolonged onset of action, salmeterol should not be used in patients with acute bronchospasm, acute asthma attack, status asthmaticus, or acute respiratory insufficiency; salmeterol is only indicated for asthma prophylaxis. It is crucial to inform patients of this and prescribe an inhaled, short-acting beta-agonist, such as albuterol, for rescue treatment of an acute attack as well as to warn them that increasing inhaled short-acting beta-agonist use is a signal of deteriorating asthma. Additionally, long acting beta-agonists, such as salmeterol, have been associated with an increased risk of severe asthma exacerbations and asthma-related death. It is unknown if the rate of death increases with the use of long-acting beta agonists in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). If wheezing worsens and cannot be relieved during an acute asthma attack, patients should be instructed to seek immediate medical attention. In December 2008, an FDA Advisory Panel recommended against using single-ingredient long-acting beta agonists (LABAs), (i.e., salmeterol , formoterol), for asthma in all age groups in response to a meta-analysis finding that monotherapy with LABAs increases the risk of asthma-related events (i.e., asthma-related hospitalization, asthma-related intubation, and asthma-related death). However, the Advisory Panel recommended combination products containing a LABA with an inhaled steroid (i.e., Advair, Symbicort) can retain current indications including asthma, as an increased risk of asthma-related complications was not demonstrated with this combination. The meta-analysis did not examine COPD-related risk; single ingredient LABAs can continue to be marketed for COPD, if already marketed as such. The FDA has not yet taken action on the panel's recommendation. Salmeterol should not be the first drug used to treat asthma and should only be added to the asthma treatment regimen if other drugs (including low- or medium-dose corticosteroids) do not control asthma. Data from a large placebo-controlled trial (the Salmeterol Multi-center Asthma Research Trial, a.k.a. SMART) that compared the safety of salmeterol to placebo, in combination with usual asthma therapy, showed a small but significant increase in asthma-related deaths in patients receiving salmeterol (13 deaths out of 13,176 patients treated for 28 weeks) versus those receiving placebo (3 of 13,179); subgroup analyses suggest the risk may be greater in African-American patients (Black patients) compared to Caucasians (relative risk of asthma-related deaths were 7.26, 95% CI 0.89—58.94 in African Americans and 5.82, 95% CI 0.7—48.37 in Caucasians). Salmeterol should not be initiated in patients with significantly worsening or acutely deteriorating asthma, which may be a life-threatening condition. Serious acute respiratory events, including fatalities, have been reported when salmeterol has been initiated in this situation. This information comes from post-marketing reports and although it is not possible from these reports to determine whether salmeterol contributed to these adverse events, or simply failed to relieve the deteriorating asthma, the use of salmeterol in this setting is inappropriate. Salmeterol is not a substitute for inhaled or oral corticosteroid therapy; corticosteroids should not be stopped or reduced when salmeterol is initiated. Subsequent changes to any pre-existing corticosteroid regimen, such as a dose reduction, should only be made following a thorough clinical evaluation.
 
Salmeterol should be used with caution in patients with cardiovascular disorders including ischemic cardiac disease (coronary artery disease), hypertension, cardiac arrhythmias (including tachycardia), or QT prolongation. In addition, beta-agonists should be avoided in patients with congenital long QT syndrome due to the risk of torsade de pointes. Significant changes in systolic and diastolic blood pressures could be expected to occur in some patients after use of any beta-adrenergic bronchodilator. Elderly patients may be more sensitive to the side effects of beta-agonists, especially tremor and tachycardia; this risk is higher in patients with preexisting coronary artery disease. Although not clearly established, airway responsiveness to beta-agonist medications may also change with age.
 
Salmeterol also should be used cautiously in patients with hyperthyroidism (thyrotoxicosis, thyroid disease), diabetes mellitus, pheochromocytoma, unusual responsiveness to other sympathomimetic amines, or history of seizures (seizure disorder).
 
Salmeterol is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. It should be used during pregnancy with caution and only if the benefits of therapy outweigh the risk to the fetus.
 
Plasma concentrations of salmeterol after inhaled therapeutic doses are very low. In rats, salmeterol is excreted in milk. However, since there is no experience with use of inhaled salmeterol by breast-feeding mothers, a decision should be made whether to discontinue nursing or to discontinue salmeterol, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother. Caution should be exercised when salmeterol is administered to a breast-feeding woman.
 
Safety and efficacy for salmeterol have not been established in children under 4 years of age.
 
Beta2-agonists should be administered with extreme caution to patients being treated with MAOI therapy (see Drug Interactions).

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. The salmeterol dry powder discus inhaler contains lactose and milk protein; use of this formulation is contraindicated in patients with a severe milk protein hypersensitivity. There have been reports of anaphylaxis in patients with severe milk protein allergy.
    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    Salmeterol is contraindicated for monotherapy treatment of asthma; it should not be used without an asthma controller medication (i.e. inhaled corticosteroid). All long-acting beta agonists (LABAs) are contraindicated for monotherapy treatment of asthma and, in patients with asthma, must be used with an asthma controller medication (i.e., inhaled corticosteroid).
Do not use salmeterol as a reliever therapy for acute bronchospasm. Acute symptoms should be treated with an inhaled short-acting beta agonist (SABA). Increasing use of inhaled SABAs is one marker of deteriorating asthma or COPD. In this situation, the patient requires immediate reevaluation with reassessment of the treatment regimen.
    Headline
    General Information
    General Information
    Do not use salmeterol in combination with another long-acting beta agonist (LABA) due to the risk for overdose. Do not exceed the maximum recommended salmeterol daily dosage. Clinically significant cardiovascular effects and fatalities have been reported in association with excessive use of inhaled sympathomimetic drugs.
    Headline
    asthma-related death
    General Information
    Long-acting beta-2 agonist (LABA) monotherapy for asthma increases the risk of asthma-related death in pediatric and adolescent patients. When LABAs are used in fixed-dose combination with an inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) to control lung inflammation, data from large clinical trials do not show a significant increase in the risk of serious asthma-related events (hospitalizations, intubations, death) compared with ICS alone. The Salmeterol Multicenter Asthma Research Trial (SMART) was a large 28-week placebo-controlled U.S. trial that found a higher rate of asthma-related death with salmeterol added to usual therapy, supporting a LABA class effect. The 16-week UK SNS trial similarly showed a numerically higher, though not statistically significant, rate of asthma-related death with salmeterol (42 mcg twice daily) compared to albuterol (180 mcg 4 times daily). Both trials included only individuals with asthma. There are no data to suggest an increased risk of death with use of LABAs in patients with COPD. In December 2017, the FDA announced that there is no increase in risk of serious asthma outcomes with LABAs when used in combination with an ICS; the results of all trials showed that the use of LABAs in combination with ICS does not significantly increase the risk of serious asthma outcomes compared to ICS alone. The trials showed that ICS/LABA combination medicines were more effective in decreasing asthma attacks (e.g., the need to use oral corticosteroids) compared to ICS alone. Three trials were conducted in patients 12 years and older and the fourth trial included pediatric patients 4 to 11 years.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperthyroidism, seizure disorder
    General Information
    Use salmeterol cautiously in individuals with hyperthyroidism, seizure disorder, or other unusual responsiveness to other sympathomimetic amines. Large doses of intravenous racemic albuterol have been reported to aggravate preexisting diabetes mellitus and diabetic ketoacidosis.
    Headline
    cardiac arrhythmias, coronary artery disease, hypertension
    General Information
    Use salmeterol with caution in patients with cardiovascular disease, especially coronary insufficiency (coronary artery disease), cardiac arrhythmias, and hypertension. Salmeterol can produce a clinically significant cardiovascular effect in some patients as measured by increases in pulse rate, systolic or diastolic blood pressure, and cardiac arrhythmias, such as supraventricular tachycardia and extrasystoles. If such effects occur, salmeterol may need to be discontinued. In addition, beta-agonists have been reported to produce electrocardiographic (ECG) changes, such as flattening of the T wave, QT prolongation, and ST segment depression, although the clinical significance of these findings is unknown. Fatalities have been reported in association with excessive use of inhaled sympathomimetic drugs. Beta-adrenergic agonist therapies like salmeterol may produce significant hypokalemia in some patients, possibly through intracellular shunting, which has the potential to produce adverse cardiovascular effects. The decrease is usually transient, not requiring supplementation.
    Headline
    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Formal pharmacokinetic studies using salmeterol have not been conducted in individuals with hepatic impairment. However, since salmeterol is predominantly cleared by hepatic metabolism, hepatic failure may lead to salmeterol plasma accumulation. Closely monitor individuals with hepatic disease.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Salmeterol is compatible with breast-feeding due to low oral bioavailability and low maternal serum concentrations after inhaled use. These criteria significantly limit the amount of systemic salmeterol exposure in the breast-fed child. According to the National Asthma Education and Prevention Program (NAEPP) Asthma and Pregnancy Working Group guidelines, long-acting beta-2 agonists (LABAs) in combination with inhaled corticosteroids are one of the preferred treatment options for the long-term management of moderate persistent asthma during lactation.
    Headline
    labor, pregnancy
    General Information
    Salmeterol may be used during pregnancy. Large studies of pregnant individuals with asthma have not identified a relationship between the use of inhaled beta-2 agonists and adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. It is known that improved maternal and perinatal outcomes are achieved with optimal control of asthma during pregnancy. Asthma guidelines recommend long-acting beta agonists (LABAs), in combination with inhaled corticosteroids, as one of the preferred treatment options for the long-term control of moderate persistent asthma during pregnancy. The advantages of actively treating asthma in pregnancy outweigh any potential risks of usual asthma medications, such as LABAs. While less data are available for LABAs vs. short-acting beta agonists (SABAs), most inhaled beta-2 agonists are considered acceptable for use during pregnancy because of low bioavailability and low maternal serum concentrations after use. There are no adequate and well-controlled human studies assessing the effects of salmeterol during labor and delivery. Because beta-2 agonists may interfere with uterine contractility, salmeterol use during labor should be restricted to those patients in whom the benefits clearly outweigh the risks.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Post-marketing experience has revealed serious exacerbations of asthma, including fatal respiratory events, associated with the use of salmeterol. In most cases, these have occurred in patients with severe asthma and/or in some patients in whom asthma has been acutely deteriorating, but they have also occurred in patients with less severe asthma. It is not possible from these reports to determine whether salmeterol contributed to these events or simply failed to relieve the deteriorating asthma. If a patient using salmeterol experiences an asthma exacerbation with worsening wheezing, immediate medical attention should be sought.The most common adverse effects of salmeterol reported from clinical studies are cough (7%), headache (28%), pharyngitis (14%), disease of nasal cavity/sinus (6%), and upper respiratory tract infection (14%). Other adverse reactions were reported during salmeterol therapy at a frequency of 1—4% but were not directly attributable to the drug. Those adverse effects include sinus headache, stomach ache, tremor, lower respiratory infection, influenza, allergic rhinitis, dizziness/giddiness, tachycardia, palpitations, dysmenorrhea, rash, tracheitis, bronchitis, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, urticaria, dental pain, joint and back pain, muscle cramps, myalgia/myositis, nervousness, and malaise or fatigue. During clinical practice, adverse reactions have included upper airway symptoms of laryngospasm, irritation, or swelling such as stridor or choking; hypertension; sinus tachycardia; arrhythmia exacerbation; and cardiac arrhythmias including atrial fibrillation, supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), and extrasystoles. High doses or overdosage of salmeterol can lead to clinically significant QT prolongation, which can produce ventricular arrhythmias. Beta-agonist-induced hypokalemia may increase risk of cardiac arrhythmias in predisposed patients. As with other beta2-agonists, a number of metabolic disturbances can develop including hyperglycemia and hypokalemia; these events rarely were clinically significant in patients receiving inhaled salmeterol for long-term therapy at recommended dosages.Several studies were done to determine if salmeterol administration would promote tachyphylaxis to the pulmonary effects of albuterol. Although long-term salmeterol therapy does not appear to produce tachyphylaxis to the bronchodilating effects of albuterol, tachyphylaxis to extrapulmonary effects, such as hyperglycemia, decreased diastolic blood pressure, and tremor, was observed.

    Headline
    bronchospasm, cough, infection, influenza, laryngospasm, nasal congestion, paradoxical bronchospasm, pharyngitis, rhinitis, sinusitis, tracheitis
    General Information
    Paradoxical bronchospasm can occur after treatment with salmeterol and can be life threatening. If this occurs, discontinue salmeterol immediately. Respiratory and infectious adverse reactions reported in adult patients receiving salmeterol in clinical trials include nasal congestion (4% to 9%), rhinitis (4% to 5%), tracheitis (3% to 7%), influenza (5%), asthma (3%), throat irritation (7%), sinusitis (4%), cough (5%), and viral respiratory infection (5%). Adverse reactions occurring in 3% or more of patients receiving salmeterol in clinical trials but occurring in a greater percentage of patients receiving placebo included pharyngitis, sinusitis, upper respiratory tract infection, and cough. Ear signs and symptoms (4%), pharyngitis (6%), and asthma (4%) were reported in pediatric patients 4 to 11 years receiving salmeterol in clinical trials. Upper airway symptoms of laryngospasm, irritation, or swelling, including stridor and choking, and oropharyngeal irritation have been reported with postmarketing use of salmeterol. Fatalities have been reported in association with excessive use of inhaled sympathomimetic drugs in patients with asthma. The exact cause of death is unknown, but cardiac arrest after an unexpected development of a severe acute asthmatic crisis and subsequent hypoxia is suspected.

    Headline
    asthma-related death, wheezing
    General Information
    An increase in the need for rescue inhaler use and/or worsening wheezing may occur during therapy with salmeterol and are symptoms of a deterioration of the underlying respiratory condition, a potentially life-threatening situation. Advise patients not to exceed recommended doses of this medicine, but instead, seek immediate medical attention if such symptoms occur. When long acting beta-agonists (LABAs) are used in fixed-dose combination with inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), data from large clinical trials do not show a significant increase in the risk of serious asthma-related events (hospitalizations, intubations, death) compared with ICS alone. The use of a LABA as asthma monotherapy, without an inhaled corticosteroid (ICS), is associated with an increased risk of asthma-related death. Available data from controlled clinical trials also suggest that use of LABA as monotherapy increases the risk of asthma-related hospitalization in pediatric and adolescent patients. These findings are considered a class effect of LABA monotherapy. In most cases, serious acute respiratory events have occurred in patients with severe asthma and/or in patients in whom asthma has been acutely deteriorating, but such events have also occurred in patients with less severe asthma. These findings pertain only to patients who have asthma.

Description
  Text
  Salmeterol is an inhaled long-acting inhaled beta-2 adrenergic agonist (LABA) indicated for the treatment of asthma in combination with an asthma controller medication [i.e., inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)], for the prevention of exercise-induced bronchospasm (EIB), and for the maintenance treatment of bronchospasm associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). The use of LABAs alone to treat asthma without an ICS to treat lung inflammation is associated with an increased risk of asthma-related death; therefore, LABAs are contraindicated for the treatment of asthma without the use of an asthma controller medication (i.e., an ICS). Combination ICS-LABA therapy improves lung function and reduces symptoms and exacerbations, more than doubling the ICS dose, while lowering steroid burden. Due to its slower onset of action versus formoterol, ICS-salmeterol cannot be used as a reliever or in maintenance-and-reliever therapy (MART), requiring a separate short-acting beta2-agonists (SABA) or ICS-SABA reliever. It is an alternative to the preferred ICS-formoterol regimen in adults and adolescents, and one of several comparable options in children 6 to 11 years. Although indicated for the prevention of exercise-induced bronchospasm (EIB), the addition of salmeterol alone to an inhaled SABA for EIB is not recommended; salmeterol may be added to ICS controller medication for concomitant treatment in select populations. COPD guidelines recommend a bronchodilator as initial therapy for individuals with COPD in Group A; a long-acting bronchodilator, such as a LABA or long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA), is preferred except in individuals with very occasional breathlessness. In individuals with stable COPD, LAMAs have a greater effect on exacerbation reduction than LABAs and decrease hospitalizations. In individuals in Group B and Group E, guidelines recommend initial therapy with a LABA plus LAMA combination. Salmeterol was FDA-approved in February 1994.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: Salmeterol is an agonist at beta-2 receptors. These receptors are present in large numbers in the lungs and are located on bronchiolar smooth muscle. Stimulation of beta-2 receptors in the lung causes relaxation of bronchial smooth muscle, which produces bronchodilation and a resultant increase in bronchial airflow. These effects are believed to be mediated, in part, by increased activity of adenyl cyclase, an intracellular enzyme responsible for the formation of cyclic-3',5'-adenosine monophosphate (cAMP).Salmeterol can inhibit the bronchoconstriction produced by histamine, methacholine, and exercise. In addition, salmeterol appears to inhibit the late phase of allergen-induced bronchoconstriction, which usually appears after the bronchodilating effects of the shorter-acting agents have resolved.The extended action of salmeterol is due to its long, lipophilic side chain. This side chain appears to bind to an exosite present only near beta-2 receptors. Binding to this exosite allows the active portion of the molecule to remain at the receptor site and continually engage and disengage with the receptor, therefore providing a long duration of action. Also, exosite binding contributes to salmeterol's inhibition of late-phase responses to allergens.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Inhalation Route
    Description
    Following inhalation, a minimal amount of salmeterol is systemically absorbed to produce plasma concentrations of 0.1—0.2 mcg/L in healthy subjects. The onset of therapeutic effects, as measured by a 15% improvement in forced expiratory flow in one second (FEV1), occurs in approximately 14 minutes with salmeterol aerosol compared to 7 minutes with albuterol. Peak effects of salmeterol aerosol are observed 3—4 hours following oral inhalation. The median time to therapeutic onset with salmeterol inhalational powder is 30—50 minutes, with maximal increases in FEV1 occurring within 2 hours. Compared to albuterol, salmeterol produces similar mean peak increases in peak expiratory flow rates (PEFR) and FEV1. With salmeterol, however, the bronchodilator effects persisted at greater than half of the maximum effect for 12 hours, whereas with albuterol, PEFR and FEV1 returned to baseline within 6 hours.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    Salmeterol use has not been studied in patients with hepatic impairment. However, salmeterol is predominately eliminated by hepatic metabolism, via hydroxylation and aliphatic oxidation. In vitro study has identified a hepatic isoenzyme CYP3A4-dependent pathway. Use of this medication in patients with hepatic disease or other hepatic impairment may result in drug accumulation. The manufacturer advocates close monitoring in such patients.
  Text
  Salmeterol is administered by oral inhalation. Protein binding of salmeterol is approximately 94—98% of the serum concentration. Extensive metabolism occurs via hydroxylation. In addition, in vitro data indicate that salmeterol also undergoes aliphatic oxidation through hepatic CYP3A4, which results in the formation of alpha-hydroxysalmeterol. Use of a strong inhibitor of CYP3A4, ketoconazole, in in vitro study has been shown to inhibit metabolism and result in significantly increased salmeterol systemic exposure. The elimination half-life of orally administered salmeterol was determined as approximately 5 hours. Excretion is primarily in the feces. No significant amount of unchanged salmeterol has been detected in either urine or feces.

    Administration Details
      Name
      Oral Inhalation Administration
      Description
      NOTE: Never use salmeterol to treat acute bronchospasm. If any patient who uses salmeterol experiences wheezing that worsens and cannot be relieved during an acute asthma attack, they should be instructed to seek immediate medical attention.
 
Powder for oral inhalation (Serevent Diskus)
One Diskus blister (one inhalation) contains 50 mcg of salmeterol as the xinafoate salt.
Instruct the patient to open and prepare mouthpiece of Diskus device and slide device lever to activate the first dose (see package instructions). Do not advance the lever more than once at any one time as this will release further doses that will be wasted. Holding the Diskus mouthpiece level to, but away from, the mouth, exhale; be careful never to breathe into the Diskus inhaler. Then, put the mouthpiece to the lips and breathe in the dose quickly and deeply through the mouth. Remove the Diskus from the mouth, hold breath for at least 10 seconds, and then exhale slowly. 
Instruct patient to close the Diskus, which will also reset the dose lever for the next scheduled dose.
Most patients can either feel or taste the powder medication during administration; however, do not repeat the dose if it is not felt.
Do NOT use a spacer with the Diskus inhaler.
After administration, instruct patient to rinse mouth with water to minimize dry mouth.
To avoid the spread of infection, the device should not be used by more than one person. 
Storage: The Diskus device and mouthpiece should be kept dry; do not wash. Discard the Diskus device 6 weeks after opening the foil pouch or when the counter reads “0” (after all blisters have been used), whichever comes first.
