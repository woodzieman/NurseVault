Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Transient increases in hepatic enzymes and severe, sometimes fatal, liver toxicity may occur during pyrazinamide treatment. Pyrazinamide (PZA) should be used with caution in patients with alcoholism and is contraindicated in patients with chronic or severe hepatic disease or hepatic damage (e.g., hepatitis, jaundice) due to the increased risk of hepatotoxicity in these patients. Liver-function tests (LFTs) should be conducted prior to and every 2—4 weeks during treatment. Pyrazinamide should be discontinued if signs or symptoms of hepatic damage become evident. In August 2003 the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the American Thoracic Society (ATS) treatment recommendations for latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) were changed to indicate that the 2-month regimen of rifampin plus pyrazinamide should generally not be offered, especially to patients with underlying hepatic disease (e.g., hepatitis, jaundice) or those with a history of isoniazid-associated liver injury. This recommendation is based on a total of 48 cases (between October 2000 and June 2003) of confirmed pyrazinamide-associated severe liver injury reported to CDC, with 11 cases being fatal. A subsequent survey found high rates of hospitalization and death from liver injury associated with the use of pyrazinamide. After a review of surveillance data, survey results, and recent published studies of pyrazinamide-associated liver injury, the ATS and CDC (with Infectious Diseases Society of America endorsement) revised their original recommendation to advise that the rifampin-PZA regimen should generally not be offered. Other regimens for the treatment of LTBI, which are known to be safe and effective, such as rifampin alone or isoniazid (INH), should be used. It is important to note that the recommendation against the use of pyrazinamide for treatment of LTBI does not apply to the appropriate use of rifampin and pyrazinamide in multidrug regimens for the treatment of persons with active TB disease. For persons taking the rifampin-PZA regimen, LFTs and bilirubin should be measured at baseline and at 2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks of treatment. Because the majority of patients reported to have liver injury had onset of symptoms after the 4th week of therapy, monitor the patient throughout the entire course of treatment. Discontinute treatment immediately and not resume for any of the following findings: 1) LFTs > than 5-times the upper limit of normal (ULN) in an asymptomatic person, 2) LFTs > ULN when accompanied by symptoms of hepatitis, or 3) a serum bilirubin concentration > ULN, whether or not symptoms are present. The CDC continues to collect reports of severe liver injury leading (those requiring hospitalization or leading to death) in persons receiving any treatment for LTBI. Health-care providers are encouraged to report such events to CDC's Division of Tuberculosis Elimination, telephone (404)—639—8442.
 
Pyrazinamide can interfere with urine ketone determination of the Acetest and Ketostix test strips. Alternative methods of ketone determination should be considered when administering pyrazinamide to patients with diabetes mellitus.
 
Pyrazinamide is contraindicated in patients with acute gout and should be discontinued if acute gouty arthritis develops. Pyrazinamide has caused hyperuricemia through inhibition of urate excretion. Approximately 40% of patients have developed nongouty polyarthralgia. Serum uric acid concentrations should be evaluated prior to treatment in all patients and monitored throughout treatment in patients with a history of gout.
 
Pyrazinamide is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. No published safety data exist concerning the effects of pyrazinamide on the fetus. In general it is recommended to avoid pyrazinamide during pregnancy, but it may be given after the first trimester in women with HIV infection.
 
Pyrazinamide distributes into breast milk, but adverse effects to nursing infants have not been documented. Infants who are breast-feeding should be monitored for any adverse reactions.
 
Patients with nicotinic acid hypersensitivity or sensitivity to chemically related drugs, such as isoniazid or ethionamide, can experience a similar reaction to pyrazinamide.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Hepatotoxicity is the most severe adverse reaction of pyrazinamide. Jaundice, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatitis, and a syndrome of fever, anorexia, malaise, hepatomegaly, and splenomegaly have been reported. In rare instances, hepatic necrosis and fatalities have occurred. This is a dose-related adverse reaction and is typically seen with large pyrazinamide dosages, such as 40—50 mg/kg/day, for prolonged periods of treatment. The combination of pyrazinamide and rifampin has been associated with severe liver injury, including hepatitis, jaundice (hyperbilirubinemia), and deaths due to hepatic failure. This combination treatment regimen should be used with caution (see Precautions). The CDC is collecting reports of severe liver injuries (those requiring hospitalization or leading to death) in patients on any regimen for latent TB infection; call (404) 639—8125 to report possible cases.Mild arthralgia and myalgia have been reported frequently during pyrazinamide treatment. Nongouty arthralgia has been reported in as many as 40% of patients and appears to occur secondary to hyperuricemia caused by inhibition of urate excretion. This reaction is often asymptomatic; however, if acute gout or acute gouty arthritis develops, pyrazinamide should be promptly discontinued.In rare instances, hematologic complications, such as thrombocytopenia, porphyria, and sideroblastic anemia, have occurred during pyrazinamide therapy.Hypersensitivity reactions including rash (unspecified), urticaria, and pruritus have been reported with pyrazindamide therapy. Angioedema has been reported rarely. Maculopapular rash, acne or acneiform rash, and photosensitivity with reddish-brown skin discoloration have also been reported rarely.Dysuria and interstitial nephritis have been reported rarely during pyrazinamide therapy.

Description
  Text
  Description: Pyrazinamide (PZA) is used in the treatment of tuberculosis. Pyrazinamide is considered a first-line agent when used in addition to isoniazid, ethambutol, rifampin, and/or streptomycin for treatment of isoniazid-susceptible organisms and when resistance to isoniazid or rifampin has been confirmed. Pyrazinamide appears to be less toxic and more effective than other antitubercular agents including aminosalicylic acid, capreomycin, cycloserine, ethionamide, and kanamycin; however, liver function must be carefully monitored during PZA use. While the drug has been used in combination with a rifamycin as an alternative for latent tuberculosis, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the American Thoracic Society (ATS) no longer recommend the 2-month regimen of rifampin plus PZA for this indication (see Contraindications/Precautions). Pyrazinamide was approved by the FDA in 1955.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: Pyrazinamide's exact mechanism of action is not known. Susceptible strains of M. tuberculosis release pyrazinamidase, which converts PZA to pyrazinoic acid (POA). Conversion of PZA to this active metabolite may be partially responsible for its activity. In vitro studies have demonstrated that POA decreases the pH below that which promotes the growth of M. tuberculosis. The mechanism of the parent compound has not been elucidated. Pyrazinamide exhibits bacteriocidal or bacteriostatic action, depending on the drug concentrations within the infected site and the susceptibility of the organism. Pyrazinamide exerts its most significant effect at intracelluar sites where M. tuberculosis replicates slowly, such as within macrophages. Studies indicate that PZA is most effective in the initial stages of treatment, which may be the result of diminished organism populations in macrophages early in therapy. Pyrazinamide also inhibits the tubular secretion of uric acid.M. tuberculosis is the only organism susceptible to pyrazinamide.

  Text
  Pyrazinamide (PZA) is administered orally. PZA is widely distributed. It penetrates inflamed meninges to reach therapeutic levels in the CSF. It is unknown whether PZA crosses the placenta, but distribution into breast milk has been documented (see Contraindications). The plasma half-life is 9—10 hours. Pyrazinamide is hydrolyzed in the liver to pyrazinoic acid, its major active metabolite. Subsequently, pyrazinoic acid is hydroxylated to the main excretory compound. Pyrazinamide and its metabolites are excreted in the urine (70%), primarily via glomerular filtration.

Extemporaneous preparation of 100 mg/mL pyrazinamide oral suspension
Using a mortar and pestle, grind twenty-four 500-mg pyrazinamide tablets to a fine powder.
Levigate with a small amount of vehicle (simple syrup or 1:1 mixture of simple syrup and 1% methylcellulose) to form a paste.
Add the vehicle in geometric portions and mix thoroughly after each addition.
Transfer contents to a graduated cylinder.
Rinse the mortar and pestle with base solution and pour into graduated cylinder.
Add enough vehicle to bring the final volume to 120 mL.
Transfer the mixture into an amber plastic bottle.
Label the bottle with 'Shake well before use'
Storage: The suspension is stable for 60 days when stored at room temperature or refrigerated.

Add the vehicle in geometric portions and mix thoroughly after each addition.
Add enough vehicle to bring the final volume to 150 mL.
Transfer the mixture into an amber plastic bottle.
Label the bottle with 'Shake well before use'.
Storage: The suspension is stable for 60 days when stored at room temperature or refrigerated.
