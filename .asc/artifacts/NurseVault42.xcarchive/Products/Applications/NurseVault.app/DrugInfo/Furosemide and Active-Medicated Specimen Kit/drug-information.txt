Drug Class: Furosemide is a sulfonamide-derived oral and parenteral loop diuretic indicated for the management of edema associated with congestive heart failure, cirrhosis, and renal disease, including the nephrotic syndrome, as well as pulmonary edema.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC), oral; Common adult doses: 600 mg/day PO or 6 g/day IV infusion. Up to 4 g/day PO has been given to treat chronic renal failure. 160 mg/day subcutaneously (Lasix Onyu on-body infusor). 80 mg/dose subcutaneously (Furoscix on-body infusor or Furoscix ReadyFlow autoinjector).

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): cholestasis, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatic encephalopathy, jaundice, pancreatitis, blurred vision, dizziness, headache, paresthesias, restlessness, vertigo, xanthopsia, azotemia, dehydration, hypocalcemia, hypochloremia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hyponatremia, hypovolemia, interstitial nephritis, metabolic alkalosis, oliguria, orthostatic hypotension, polyuria, syncope, phlebitis, thromboembolism, thrombosis, diabetes mellitus

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Furosemide is contraindicated in patients with known hypersensitivity to this drug. Because cross-sensitivity with furosemide has rarely been observed, bumetanide can be substituted for furosemide in patients allergic to furosemide.

          Dosage Description
          Although use in clinical practice is not uncommon, limited published data are available evaluating furosemide prior to or after the administration of blood products. One prospective, unblinded trial evaluated furosemide 1 mg/kg IV after transfusions of 10 mL/kg of packed red blood cells (PRBC) in 24 cases; 3 of the 21 infants were studied twice. The PRBCs were administered at a rate of 5 mL/kg/hour, and furosemide was given in 16 of the 24 cases. The infants that received furosemide demonstrated an improvement in lung compliance, tidal volume, and minute ventilation compared to baseline. There was no observed improvement in these values in the untreated group. The authors noted no difference in the clinical respiratory status between the 2 groups. Some authors caution against the routine use of furosemide following PRBC transfusions due to a lack of efficacy data and the potential for electrolyte imbalance.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Furosemide is contraindicated in patients with known hypersensitivity to this drug. Because cross-sensitivity with furosemide has rarely been observed, bumetanide can be substituted for furosemide in patients allergic to furosemide. The risk of an allergic reaction after administration of a loop diuretic in a patient with sulfonamide hypersensitivity or thiazide diuretic hypersensitivity appears to be very low. Although furosemide is a sulfonamide derivative, sulfonamide cross-sensitivity has been rarely documented. A case report, published in 1987, documents an anaphylactic reaction to IV furosemide in a patient who was subsequently skin-tested with furosemide, bumetanide, ethacrynic acid, chlorothiazide, and sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim. A positive reaction was elicited to all except ethacrynic acid. This case documents hypersensitivity to both furosemide and bumetanide in a patient with sulfonamide hypersensitivity. Prior to this, neither the FDA nor the manufacturer of furosemide (Lasix) had received any reports of cross-sensitivity between furosemide and sulfonamide antibiotics. Furosemide does not contain the N4-aromatic amine or the N1-substituent which are present in sulfonamide antibiotics. Non-arylamine sulfonamide derivatives, such as loop diuretics, have been proposed to have a lower risk of allergic reactions in patients with sulfonamide allergy, presumably due to lack of an arylamine group at the N4 position (a proposed structural site of action for sulfonamide allergy). One large retrospective cohort study has reported that in patients with the presence of an allergic reaction after exposure to a sulfonamide antibiotic, 9.9% had an allergic reaction after receiving a non-antibiotic sulfonamide derivative, while in patients who lacked an allergic reaction after sulfonamide antibiotic exposure, 1.6% had an allergic reaction after administration of a non-antibiotic sulfonamide derivative (adjusted odds ratio 2.8; 95% CI, 2.1—3.7). A causal relationship between sulfonamide hypersensitivity and allergic reactions with non-arylamine sulfonamide derivatives has not been definitively established and remains controversial. In general, patients with a documented sulfonamide allergy are considered to be predisposed for development of allergic drug reactions.
 
Preexisting electrolyte imbalance such as severe hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, hypochloremia, or hypomagnesemia should be corrected before initiating furosemide therapy. Loop diuretics may induce metabolic alkalosis associated with hypokalemia and hypochloremia; this acid/base imbalance is effectively treated with potassium chloride replacement.  Furosemide-induced fluctuations in serum electrolyte concentrations can occur rapidly and precipitate hepatic encephalopathy and coma in susceptible patients. Therefore, furosemide should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease such as cirrhosis. Furosemide should be used with caution in patients with hepatic coma until this condition is corrected.
 
Blood and urine glucose levels should be assessed in patients with diabetes mellitus or hyperglycemia during treatment with furosemide; loop diuretics can impair glucose tolerance.
 
Patients with ventricular arrhythmias, heart failure, potassium-losing nephropathy, aldosterone excess, or diarrhea should be monitored closely since furosemide-induced hypokalemia can exacerbate these conditions.
 
Excessive diuresis with furosemide should be avoided in patients with acute myocardial infarction due to the risk of precipitating shock.
 
Furosemide is contraindicated in patients with anuria. It should be used cautiously in any patient with renal disease such as severe renal impairment or renal failure. Drug-induced hypovolemia can precipitate azotemia in these patients. Furosemide is an effective diuretic for many patients with renal impairment. Renal impairment may reduce clearance and warrant the use of higher doses with extended dosing intervals. Furosemide may be less effective in these patients and delayed excretion of drug may increase the risk of toxicity.
 
Patients with pre-existing hypovolemia or hypotension should have their condition corrected before furosemide is initiated. Orthostatic hypotension may occur during treatment with loop diuretics. Excessive hypotension can result in syncope. The antihypertensive effects of diuretics may be enhanced in patients predisposed for orthostatic hypotension, including the post-sympathectomy patient. Greater sensitivity to the hypotensive and diuretic effects of furosemide is possible in elderly patients. Controlled clinical studies of furosemide did not include sufficient numbers of subjects aged 65 or older to determine whether they respond differently from younger subjects. Other reported clinical experience has not identified differences in responses between the elderly and younger patients. In general, dose selection for elderly patients should be cautious, usually starting at the low end of the dosing range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function, and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy. Furosemide is known to be substantially excreted by the kidney, and the risk of toxic reactions to this drug may be greater in patients with impaired renal function. Because elderly patients are more likely to have decreased renal function, care should be taken in dose selection and it may be useful to monitor renal function.
 
Furosemide has been reported to activate or exacerbate systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), although the association is less certain than with procainamide or other drugs.
 
Since loop diuretics can reduce the clearance of uric acid, patients with gout or hyperuricemia can have exacerbations of their disease.
 
High doses and accumulation of furosemide may cause ototoxicity (see Adverse Reactions). Furosemide should be used with caution in patients with hearing impairment. The recommended rate of infusion should not be exceeded when IV doses are administered (see Dosage).
 
Furosemide has been reported to cause pancreatitis. It should be used with caution in patients with a history of pancreatitis.
 
Because no well-controlled studies have been performed with furosemide in pregnant women, it is classified as pregnancy category C. After the first trimester, it has been used for edema and hypertension. However, it is important to note that diuretics are generally not recommended for the treatment of pregnancy-induced hypertension (preeclampsia, eclampsia) since they do not alter the course of toxemia and may exacerbate maternal hypovolemia associated with this condition.
 
Because furosemide appears in breast milk, caution should be exercised when furosemide is administered to a breast-feeding mother. In addition, diuretics such as furosemide may suppress lactation.
 
Nephrocalcinosis (calcium nephrolithiasis) has occurred in some premature neonates treated with furosemide for edema. The concurrent use of chlorothiazide has been reported to decrease hypercalciuria and dissolve some calculi. In addition, patients with neonatal prematurity who receive furosemide in the first few weeks of life may have an increased risk of persistent patent ductus arteriosus.

Because cross-sensitivity with furosemide has rarely been observed, bumetanide can be substituted for furosemide in individuals allergic to furosemide. According to the manufacturer, individuals with a sulfonamide hypersensitivity may show a hypersensitivity to furosemide. However, while furosemide is a sulfonamide derivative, sulfonamide cross-sensitivity has rarely been documented. Sulfonamide antibiotics contain the N4-aromatic amine (arylamine) and the N1-aromatic heterocyclic ring which are believed to be involved in the development of IgE-hypersensitivity reactions; bumetanide does not contain either. A relationship between sulfonamide hypersensitivity and allergic reactions with non-arylamine sulfonamide derivatives has not been definitively established and remains controversial. It has been theorized that individuals with a documented sulfonamide allergy may have a predisposition for the development of allergic drug reactions.

    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    Subcutaneous furosemide infusion is not indicated for treatment of acute pulmonary edema or for use in emergency situations. The total dose of furosemide delivered by the subcutaneous on-body infusor is only 80 mg.
    Headline
    electrolyte imbalance, hepatic encephalopathy, vomiting
    General Information
    Furosemide should not be initiated in individuals with hepatic encephalopathy or electrolyte imbalance until the condition is improved. In individuals with hepatic cirrhosis and ascites, consider initiating furosemide in a hospital setting since furosemide-induced fluid and electrolyte fluctuations can occur rapidly and may precipitate hepatic encephalopathy in these individuals. Correct any significant electrolyte imbalances, such as hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, hypochloremia, and/or hypomagnesemia, before administration. Individuals receiving higher furosemide doses, with inadequate oral electrolyte intake, vomiting excessively, receiving parented fluids, and who are elderly, have a higher risk of electrolyte imbalance. Excessive doses or too frequent administration may lead to profound water loss, electrolyte depletion, reduction in blood volume, and circulatory collapse with the possibility of vascular thrombosis and embolism, particularly in elderly patients. Loop diuretics may induce metabolic alkalosis associated with hypokalemia and hypochloremia; this acid/base imbalance is effectively treated with potassium chloride replacement. Monitor individuals taking furosemide for signs of electrolyte or fluid imbalance and periodically monitor serum electrolytes.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus
    General Information
    Monitor blood and/or urine glucose periodically in individuals with diabetes mellitus who are receiving furosemide. Elevations in blood and urine glucose concentrations may occur with furosemide therapy.
    Headline
    anuria, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Furosemide is contraindicated in individuals with anuria. Use furosemide with caution in patients with severe renal disease, including severe renal impairment or renal failure, as furosemide can precipitate oliguria and azotemia in these patients. If increasing azotemia and oliguria occur during treatment of severe progressive renal disease, furosemide should be discontinued. Furosemide is an effective diuretic for many individuals with renal impairment. Renal impairment may reduce clearance and warrant the use of higher doses with extended dosing intervals. Furosemide may be less effective in these individuals and delayed excretion of drug may increase the risk of toxicity. Additionally, subcutaneous infusion via the On-body Infusor only delivers an 80 mg dose of furosemide, thus individuals with significantly reduced renal function may require additional diuretic therapy when utilizing this modality. Monitor renal function frequently during the first few months of furosemide therapy and then periodically thereafter.
    Headline
    dehydration, hypovolemia, syncope
    General Information
    Individuals with pre-existing hypovolemia or dehydration should have their condition corrected before furosemide initiation. Excessive doses or too frequent administration may lead to profound water loss, electrolyte depletion, dehydration, reduction in blood volume, and circulatory collapse. Carefully monitor patients and adjust the dose as necessary. The antihypertensive effects of furosemide may be enhanced in individuals predisposed to orthostatic hypotension, including older adults and those with hypovolemia, dehydration, or a history of syncope. Excessive hypotension may result in syncope or falls.
    Headline
    systemic lupus erythematosus
    General Information
    Furosemide has been reported to activate or exacerbate systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE); use with caution in individuals with SLE.
    Headline
    gout, hyperuricemia
    General Information
    Use furosemide with caution in individuals with gout or hyperuricemia. Loop diuretics, such as furosemide, have been reported to increase serum uric acid concentrations, which may cause or exacerbate hyperuricemia and precipitate or exacerbate gout in susceptible patients. Monitor uric acid frequently during furosemide therapy.
    Headline
    hearing impairment, hypoproteinemia
    General Information
    Tinnitus and hearing loss have been reported with loop diuretics, including furosemide. The risk of ototoxicity may be higher with rapid injection, severe renal impairment, use of higher than recommended furosemide doses, hypoproteinemia, pre-existing hearing impairment, or coadministration with other ototoxic medications (e.g., aminoglycosides). Do not exceed the recommended rate of infusion when IV doses are administered. Hearing loss has been reported in newborn infants, including premature infants, receiving furosemide therapy. Premature infants with post conceptual age less than 31 weeks receiving doses exceeding 1 mg/kg/24 hours may develop plasma levels associated with potential toxic effects, including ototoxicity. Hypoproteinemia (e.g., nephrotic syndrome) may weaken the effect of furosemide.
    Headline
    prostatic hypertrophy, urethral stricture, urinary retention
    General Information
    Furosemide therapy may precipitate acute urinary retention in individuals with severe symptoms of urinary retention due to bladder emptying disorder, prostatic hypertrophy, or urethral stricture or narrowing. This is related to increased production and retention of urine. Monitor these individuals carefully, especially during the initiation of furosemide therapy.
    Headline
    magnetic resonance imaging
    General Information
    The use of subcutaneous furosemide infusion via on-body infusor is not compatible for use in or around a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) machine.
    Headline
    premature neonates
    General Information
    Although diuretics are often a part of the medical management of patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), the manufacturer states that premature neonates who receive furosemide in the first few weeks of life may have an increased risk of persistent PDA.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Greater sensitivity to the usual doses of furosemide may occur in geriatric patients and care should be taken in initial dose selection. In general, initial doses should be at the low end of the dosage range to reflect the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy. Geriatric adults may have a higher risk of orthostatic hypotension with furosemide therapy. Monitor renal function and serum electrolytes along with signs and symptoms of fluid and electrolyte imbalances. According to the Beers Criteria, diuretics are considered potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) in geriatric adults and should be used with caution due to the potential for causing or exacerbating SIADH or hyponatremia. Sodium levels should be closely monitored when starting or changing dosages of diuretics in older adults.
    Headline
    preeclampsia, pregnancy
    General Information
    Published data from observational studies, case reports, and postmarketing reports have not demonstrated a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes with furosemide use during pregnancy. Furosemide therapy during pregnancy requires monitoring of fetal growth because of the potential for higher birth weights. In animal reproduction studies, furosemide has been shown to cause unexplained maternal deaths and abortions in rabbits at 2, 4, and 8 times the human dose of 600 mg/day, presumably due to volume depletion. An increased incidence and severity of hydronephrosis (distension of the renal pelvis and, in some cases, ureters) has been observed in mice and rabbit fetuses exposed to furosemide during pregnancy. Diuretics are generally considered second-line agents for the treatment of hypertension during pregnancy. Theoretically, there is concern about the potential for diuretics to cause intravascular volume depletion, which could cause fetal growth restriction or oligohydramnios. However, this concern has not been supported by the literature. Closely monitor the pregnant person's blood pressure, heart rate, and volume status and adjust their pharmacologic treatment as necessary to avoid hypotension and placental hypoperfusion. Diuretics should be generally avoided in preeclampsia, as plasma volume is reduced, unless in the context of oliguria, when low dose furosemide may be considered. Subcutaneous furosemide injection (Furoscix ReadyFlow autoinjector) contains benzyl alcohol as a preservative. The FDA-approved labeling for this product states exposure in the fetus is unlikely, as benzyl alcohol undergoes rapid metabolism during pregnancy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use furosemide with caution during breast-feeding. Furosemide is excreted in human milk. In addition, high dose furosemide may suppress lactation as a result of intense diuresis. Low dose furosemide (20 mg daily) does not suppress lactation. Alternatives such as chlorothiazide and hydrochlorothiazide may be preferred. Subcutaneous furosemide injection (Furoscix ReadyFlow autoinjector) contains benzyl alcohol as a preservative. The FDA-approved labeling for this product states exposure in the breastfed neonate is unlikely as benzyl alcohol undergoes rapid metabolism during pregnancy.

Description
  Text
  Furosemide is a sulfonamide-derived oral and parenteral loop diuretic indicated for the management of edema associated with congestive heart failure, cirrhosis, and renal disease, including the nephrotic syndrome, as well as pulmonary edema. Furosemide is also available as a pre-programmed on-body infusor for subcutaneous infusion and autoinjector for the treatment of congestion due to fluid overload in chronic heart failure. Furosemide works primarily by inhibiting the reabsorption of sodium and chloride in the proximal and distal tubules in the loop of Henle. Furosemide was FDA-approved in 1968.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Furosemide is a loop diuretic that inhibits sodium and chloride resorption by competing with chloride for the Na+/K+/2Cl- co-transporter in the ascending limb of the loop of Henle. The manufacturer also states that furosemide inhibits the absorption of sodium and chloride in the proximal and distal tubules.The action of furosemide on the distal tubule is independent of any inhibitory effect on carbonic anhydrase and aldosterone. A profound diuresis results from the increased urinary excretion of sodium, chloride, potassium, and hydrogen ions. In addition, furosemide increases the excretion of calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate, ammonium, and phosphate. The diuresis caused by furosemide can lead to increased aldosterone production, resulting in increased sodium resorption, and increased potassium and hydrogen excretion. Excessive loss of these electrolytes can lead to metabolic alkalosis.

  Text
  Furosemide is administered orally, intravenously, and subcutaneously. It is 91% to 99% plasma protein-bound, mainly to albumin. Furosemide undergoes minimal metabolism in the liver, with 50% to 80% of a dose excreted in the urine within 24 hours; significantly more furosemide is excreted in the urine after IV administration as compared to oral. The half-life of furosemide is approximately 2 hours.

Storage: Store the subcutaneous autoinjector at controlled room temperature between 20 and 25 degrees C (68 and 77 degrees F). Do not refrigerate or freeze. Keep the autoinjector in the original carton until ready to be used. Protect from light.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed. Use diuretics with caution in individuals with hepatic disease since minor alterations of fluid and electrolyte balance may precipitate hepatic coma.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available. However, significantly reduced renal function can affect the response to furosemide; individuals with impaired renal function may require a higher dose. Individuals with significantly reduced renal function using the on-body infusor may require additional diuretic therapy as the device can only deliver an 80 mg dose of furosemide.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
The clearance of furosemide does not appear to be affected by hemodialysis; no dosage adjustment is needed.
