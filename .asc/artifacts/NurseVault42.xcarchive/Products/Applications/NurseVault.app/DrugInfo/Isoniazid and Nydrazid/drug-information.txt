Drug Class: Isoniazid (INH) is used in the treatment of mycobacterial disease.

Routes: oral, injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 5 mg/kg/dose (Max: 300 mg/dose) PO/IV/IM once daily or 5 days/week or 15 mg/kg/dose (Max: 900 mg/dose) PO/IV/IM 3 days/week or twice weekly. 15 mg/kg/dose (Max: 900 mg) PO/IV/IM once daily or once weekly has been used off-label.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): gynecomastia, hyperglycemia, lupus-like symptoms, metabolic acidosis, pancreatitis, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatitis, hyperbilirubinemia, jaundice, cerebellar syndrome, encephalopathy, memory impairment, optic atrophy, optic neuritis, paresthesias, peripheral neuropathy, psychosis, seizures, agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, eosinophilia, hemolytic anemia, sideroblastic anemia, thrombocytopenia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Isoniazid is contraindicated in patients who develop severe hypersensitivity reactions, including drug-induced hepatitis; severe adverse reactions to isoniazid such as drug fever, chills, arthritis; acute hepatic disease; and patients with a history of isoniazid-induced hepatic disease or injury. Transient increases in liver-function tests (transaminases) have occurred during isoniazid treatment (see Adverse Reactions). In rare instances, hyperbilirubinuria, jaundice, and fatal hepatitis have been reported. Isoniazid should be given with caution in patient with chronic hepatic disease (e.g., alcoholism, cirrhosis, or chronic hepatitis). Increased age, injection substance abuse, and alcohol consumption have been associated a higher risk of developing isoniazid-induced hepatoxicity. Elderly or older patients (aged >= 50 years) are at increased risk of developing hepatic toxicity as compared to patients less than 20 years of age. Rates of isoniazid-induced hepatitis are four-fold higher in persons who drink alcohol daily compared to those who do not drink alcohol. The risk of fatal isoniazid-associated hepatitis appears to be increased among females, particularly black and Hispanic women. An increased risk may also be associated with the post partum period. Baseline liver-function tests (LFTs) should be performed in all patients, followed by periodic monitoring of LFTs during isoniazid treatment in persons whose pretreatment LFTs were abnormal (i.e., > 3-times the upper limit of normal), persons who are 35 years or older, and in women, including those who are pregnant or within 3 months of delivery. Discontinuation of isoniazid, INH should be considered if serum transaminases rise more than three times the upper limit of normal. Isoniazid should be used with caution in patients with severe renal impairment because elimination of the drug can be prolonged, increasing the likelihood of adverse reactions. Alternative tuberculosis treatment should be initiated in patients who develop hepatitis. If isoniazid therapy must be restarted, do so only after symptoms and lab abnormalities have cleared. Start therapy in very small and gradually increasing doses; stop therapy immediately if there is any indication of recurrent hepatotoxicity. In patients with acute hepatic disease, preventative treatment should be delayed.
 
Isoniazid can cause peripheral neuropathy due to pyridoxine antagonism or increased excretion of pyridoxine. Patients with diabetes mellitus, malnutrition, or alcoholism may be predisposed to developing this complication. Isoniazid therapy may worsen pre-existing peripheral neuropathy, especially in patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. Concurrent administration of pyridoxine may be considered for prophylaxis against neurotoxic side effects.
 
Isoniazid should be used with caution in patients with a seizure disorder. Isoniazid may cause neurotoxicity and result in seizures. Patients with a seizure disorder who are being treated with isoniazid should receive pyridoxine supplementation.
 
Patients with nicotinic acid hypersensitivity or sensitivity to chemically related drugs, such as pyrazinamide or ethionamide, can experience a similar reaction to isoniazid.
 
Isoniazid is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. Extensive use of isoniazid during pregnancy has indicated that although the drug readily crosses the placental barrier, isoniazid is not teratogenic even when given during the first 4 months of gestation. Reports have suggested that isoniazid-induced hepatitis may be increased by the administration of isoniazid to a pregnant woman in the third trimester or the immediate postpartum period. For women at high-risk of progression to active tuberculosis, especially those who are infected with HIV or who have been infected recently, initiation of tuberculosis prophylaxis should not be delayed on the basis of pregnancy alone, even during the first trimester. Careful clinical and/or laboratory monitoring for hepatitis is warranted. Pregnant women taking isoniazid should receive pyridoxine supplementation.
 
Although isoniazid is excreted into breast milk, the American Academy of Pediatrics considers isoniazid compatible with breast-feeding, although pyridoxine supplementation of the infant is recommended. Periodic monitoring of the nursing infant for signs and symptoms of peripheral neuritis or hepatitis is recommended.
 
Isoniazid may produce an acneiform rash or exacerbate preexisting acne. This potential should be considered in patients with acne vulgaris.
 
Isoniazid, INH should not be used concurrently with MAOI therapy (see Drug Interactions).

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    Black patients, ethanol ingestion, females, hepatic disease, hepatitis, hepatotoxicity, Hispanic patients, history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use, people who inject drugs
    General Information
    Isoniazid is associated with a risk of serious hepatotoxicity, which can manifest as severe and sometimes fatal hepatitis. Isoniazid is contraindicated in individuals with history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use, including prior isoniazid-related hepatic injury, drug-induced hepatitis, or severe adverse reactions to isoniazid (i.e., drug fever, chills, arthritis), as well as in those with any form of acute hepatic disease. Older adults, especially those over 35 years and particularly those 50 years or older, are at higher risk of hepatic toxicity compared to younger adults. Carefully monitor older adults during therapy. People who inject drugs, have daily ethanol ingestion, or have chronic liver disease are also at increased risk of hepatitis. The rate of isoniazid-induced hepatitis is 4 times higher in daily alcohol consumers compared to nondrinkers. The risk of fatal isoniazid-associated hepatitis also appears increased among females, especially Black patients and Hispanic patients, and may also be elevated during the postpartum period. Obtain baseline liver function tests (LFTs) and conduct monthly symptom assessments and examinations in all individuals. Monitor LFTs monthly and more frequently as needed in individuals 35 years or older or in any other high-risk individuals. If any of the values exceed 3 to 5 times the upper limit of normal, isoniazid should be temporarily discontinued and consideration given to restarting therapy.
    Headline
    alcohol use disorder, diabetes mellitus, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, malnutrition, peripheral neuropathy, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Carefully monitor the use of isoniazid in individuals with peripheral neuropathy or conditions that predispose to neuropathy, such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, diabetes mellitus, alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, severe renal impairment or renal failure, and advanced age. Isoniazid can cause peripheral neuropathy by antagonizing pyridoxine or increasing its excretion. Therefore, concomitant pyridoxine supplementation is recommended for anyone receiving isoniazid and at risk for peripheral neuropathy. Cerebellar syndrome was reported most often in patients with chronic kidney disease; however, cases were also reported in patients without chronic kidney disease.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Carefully monitor isoniazid use in geriatric adults, who may be predisposed to developing peripheral neuropathy with isoniazid. Concomitant pyridoxine is recommended in any patient receiving isoniazid and at risk for peripheral neuropathy.  Also, the frequency of isoniazid-induced hepatitis increases with age. It is rare in individuals younger than 20 years, but occurs in up to 2.3% of those older than 50 years.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Use isoniazid during pregnancy only if the benefit outweighs the potential fetal risk. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of isoniazid use during human pregnancy. However, extensive use of isoniazid during pregnancy has indicated that although the drug readily crosses the placental barrier, it is not teratogenic. All pregnant individuals taking isoniazid should receive pyridoxine supplementation; pregnancy may increase the risk for drug-induced neuropathy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Isoniazid is compatible with breast-feeding. Small concentrations of isoniazid are excreted into human milk. It is unlikely that these low concentrations will cause side effects in the breastfed child. Encourage breast-feeding in those who are noninfectious and taking first-line tuberculosis agents, such as isoniazid. Pyridoxine supplementation is recommended for both the lactating individual and the breastfed child.
    Headline
    postpartum
    General Information
    Reports have suggested that the risk of isoniazid-induced hepatitis may be increased during the postpartum period. Careful clinical and/or laboratory monitoring for hepatitis is warranted.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The major toxic adverse reaction to isoniazid is hepatitis. Hepatitis occurs in about 2.1% of recipients. Patients aged 35 years or older are three times more likely to develop drug-induced hepatitis than those under 35. In one study, hepatitis was fatal in 4.6% of cases. Progressive liver damage may be seen in up to 2.3% of patients over age 50. Patients should be advised to report any of the prodromal symptoms of hepatitis including fatigue, loss of appetite, and nausea/vomiting. The drug should be discontinued if clinical symptoms of hepatitis occur. It has been suggested by some clinicians that the drug be discontinued if transaminase levels exceed three times normal, even without clinical evidence of hepatitis. Other adverse effects include epigastric distress, xerostomia, pellagra, hyperglycemia, metabolic acidosis, and urinary retention and gynecomastia in males. Systemic lupus-like symptoms and a rheumatic syndrome with arthralgia have also occurred.
 
Elevated hepatic enzymes (SGOT, SGPT (10—20%)), bilirubinemia, and jaundice also have been reported. Baseline liver-function tests should be performed, followed by periodic monitoring of LFTs during treatment.
 
Peripheral neuropathy characterized by paresthesias in the hands and feet is a frequent dose-related adverse effect. Up to 44% of patients can develop this complication on a dosing regimen of 16—24 mg/kg/day. This reaction occurs more frequently in malnourished patients, diabetics, and alcoholics. Concomitant administration of pyridoxine is recommended in these patients to reduce the risk of neurotoxicity. Much less frequently reported neurotoxic effects include toxic encephalopathy and optic neuritis.
 
Gastrointestinal side effects, such as diarrhea, abdominal pain, and nausea/vomiting, have been reported with oral use of isoniazid.
 
Hematologic reactions that occur infrequently with isoniazid include agranulocytosis, hemolysis with anemia, sideroblastic anemia, aplastic anemia, pancytopenia, and thrombocytopenia.
 
Dermatologic reactions that occur infrequently with isoniazid include maculopapular rash, acneiform rash, or exfoliative dermatitis. Injection site reaction can be seen with intramuscular administration of isoniazid. Isoniazid has also been associated with acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP). The nonfollicular, pustular, erythematous rash starts suddenly, is associated with fever above 38 degrees C, and is distinct from pustular psoriasis, although biopsy results in each reveal spongiform subcorneal pustules. Drugs are the main cause of AGEP. A period of 2—3 weeks after an inciting drug exposure appears necessary for a first episode of AGEP. Unintentional reexposure may cause a second episode within 2 days. Clinical presentation is diverse with cutaneous lesions beyond erythema and pustules present in half of the cases. For example, bullous lesions, edema, purpura, pruritus, and mucosal erosions are possible. The mean duration of the pustules is 9.7 days followed by an annular desquamation, as long as the causative drug or factor is discontinued. The physiopathological mechanisms of AGEP have not been determined but the pathological criteria of edema, leukocytoclastic vasculitis, eosinophil exocytosis, and keratinocyte focal necrosis are distinctive. Pustule confluence or very small pustules may lead a clinician to make an incorrect diagnosis of TEN, of drug-induced erythroderma, or of staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome.
 
Although rare, interstitial nephritis has been reported with isoniazid therapy.

Description
  Text
  Isoniazid (INH) is used in the treatment of mycobacterial disease. Isoniazid is chemically related to iproniazid, a drug that was used in the early 1950s for the treatment of tuberculosis but also possessed some monoamine oxidase inhibiting properties. Due to its efficacy and low cost, INH is considered a first-line agent in the treatment of susceptible M. tuberculosis organisms. INH appears to be more effective and less toxic than other antitubercular agents including aminosalicylic acid, capreomycin, cycloserine, ethionamide, and kanamycin. Isoniazid was approved by the FDA in 1952.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Molecular Pharmacology: Isoniazid, INH inhibits a variety of enzymes. In mycobacterial cells, isoniazid appears to interfere with mycolic acid synthesis, disrupting the synthesis of the bacterial cell wall. The exact antimycobacterial action, however, is uncertain. Isoniazid inhibits plasma monoamine oxidase, but, unlike the chemically related iproniazid, does not inhibit mitochondrial MAO. It also inhibits mycobacterial MAO and diamine oxidase and mammalian diamine oxidase. Inhibition of these oxidases interferes with the metabolism of both tyramine and histamine. The mechanism by which isoniazid causes toxicity in humans has also been identified. In vitro studies show that isoniazid induces cytotoxicity via apoptosis in both hepatoma and lymphoma cells. Apoptosis was accompanied by a breakdown in mitochondrial membrane potential and the presence of DNA strand breaks. This resulted in a significant disruption of the cell cycle in mammalian cells.
 
Clinical Pharmacology: INH is bactericidal or bacteriostatic, depending on the drug concentration within an infected site and the susceptibility of the organism. INH is bactericidal against rapidly dividing intracellular and extracellular organisms such as those found in extracellular cavitary lesions but is bacteriostatic against organisms found within closed caseous lesions and macrophages that divide slowly and intermittently. Rifampin and pyrazinamide may be more effective against these slowly dividing organisms than INH. Since mycolic acids are unique to mycobacteria, this explains isoniazid's remarkable selectivity for mycobacteria. Cross-resistance between INH and other antitubercular agents has not been demonstrated except for ethionamide, which is structurally related to INH.  Resistance in tuberculosis infections develops rapidly with monotherapy. Resistance to INH occurs due to mutations in the KatG, inhA, kasA, and ahpC genes.
 
Although isoniazid does not appear to inhibit mitochondrial monoamine oxidase, some patients taking isoniazid have experienced flushing, headache, and palpitations after ingesting tyramine-containing foods. This reaction suggests it may inhibit plasma monoamine oxidase. INH is not known to possess clinically-significant antidepressant activity, however.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Following oral administration, isoniazid is absorbed rapidly from the GI tract. Peak serum levels are attained within 1—2 hours after an oral dose. Food decreases the rate and extent of absorption.
  Text
  Isoniazid is most often administered orally but can also be administered intramuscularly. It is distributed into all body tissues and fluids, penetrates inflamed meninges and achieves therapeutic levels in the CSF. Isoniazid crosses the placenta and is distributed into breast milk. It is not significantly bound to plasma proteins. Metabolism occurs in the liver to inactive metabolites, primarily via acetylation. The half-life is 1—4 hours, depending on the patient's acetylation rate; however, the effectiveness of the drug does not appear to be altered if dosed only once daily. Approximately 75% of the drug and its metabolites are excreted in the urine. The remainder is excreted in the feces, saliva, and sputum.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes: CYP2C19, CYP3A4, CYP2A6, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, CYP2E1
Isoniazid has been associated with many clinically significant interactions involving reduced drug metabolism. It is a potent inhibitor of CYP2C19 and an inhibitor of CYP3A4 in vitro. It only weakly inhibits CYP2A6, CYP2C9, and CYP2D6. Results are conflicting for CYP1A2, but it appears that only negligible inhibition occurs. Also, isoniazid has demonstrated both inhibitory and inducing actions on the hepatic isoenzyme CYP2E1. This dual affect is theorized to result from isoniazid competitively blocking the active site on the CYP2E1 isoenzyme resulting in decreased metabolism of the substrate as well as preserving CYP2E1. Upon disassociation from the active site, more CYP2E1 isoenzyme is available resulting in a temporary increased enzymatic activity.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Use isoniazid with caution in patients with chronic hepatic disease. Delay preventive therapy in persons with acute hepatic disease.
  Renal Impairment
  Carefully monitor isoniazid use in patients with severe renal dysfunction. Guidelines recommend no dosage adjustment with any degree of renal dysfunction.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
Isoniazid is 50% to 100% dialyzable. Dose after hemodialysis on dialysis days.
 
Peritoneal dialysis
No dosage adjustment needed.
 
Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT)†NOTE: Various CRRT modalities include continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH), continuous venovenous hemodialysis (CVVHD), continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF), continuous venovenous high-flux hemodialysis (CVVHFD), continuous arteriovenous hemofiltration (CAVH), continuous arteriovenous hemodialysis (CAVHD), and continuous arteriovenous hemodiafiltration (CAVHDF). Dosing should take into consideration patient-specific factors (e.g., intrinsic renal function), type of infection, the duration of renal replacement therapy, the effluent flow rate, and the replacement solution administered.
