Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Prior to administration, health care personnel should inform the patient, parent, guardian, or responsible adult of the vaccine's benefits and risks. This should include the provision of the vaccine information statement from the manufacturer. The responsible adult should report any adverse reaction following vaccine administration to the health care provider. The US Department of Health and Human Services has established a Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) to accept all reports of suspected adverse events after the administration of any vaccine. This includes, but is not limited to, the reporting of events required by the National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act of 1986. The toll-free number for VAERS is (800) 822—7967.
 
Hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant is contraindicated in patients with a previous or known hypersensitivity to any component of the combination or individual vaccines. Anaphylaxis has been reported with both the hepatitis A and B component of the combination vaccine. The combination vaccine should also be used cautiously in individuals who are sensitive to plasma-derived hepatitis B vaccine because cross-sensitivity can occur. Additionally, the hepatitis B vaccine, inactivated is produced using yeast cells and is contraindicated in patients with yeast hypersensitivity.
 
As with any biologic product, the prescriber or health care professional should take precautions to prevent allergic reactions. The health care professional should have immediate availability of epinephrine (1:1000) injection and other agents used in the treatment of severe anaphylaxis in the event of a serious allergic reaction to the vaccine.
 
For known exposures to HAV or HBV, immune globulin for intramuscular administration (IGIM) or hepatitis B immune globulin (HBIG), respectively should be given in combination with the appropriate vaccine in accordance with the ACIP recommendations. Hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant may not prevent HAV or HBV in those with an unrecognized illness at the time of vaccination. Additionally, it may not prevent HAV or HBV if adequate antibody titers are not achieved.
 
Do not give hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant via intravenous administration or intradermal administration. Patients with thrombocytopenia, vitamin K deficiency, a coagulopathy (e.g., hemophilia) or receiving anticoagulant therapy should be monitored closely when given hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine because bleeding can occur at the IM injection site.
 
Any serious active infection including a fever is reason for delaying use of the hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant except when in the opinion of the prescriber, withholding the vaccination entails a greater risk. Caution and appropriate care should be exercised in administering the vaccine to individuals with severe cardiac disease or to others in whom a febrile or systemic reaction could pose a significant risk.
 
Patients with immunosuppression may respond to hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant with lower antibody titers than non-immunosuppressed patients. This is particularly a concern in patients receiving chemotherapy or in patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. Since a second infection in HIV-positive patients further weakens their immune system, it is still preferable to administer the vaccine. The seroconversion (SC) rate to hepatitis A vaccine is higher than the seroprotection (SP) rate to hepatitis B in these patients. Following vaccination with hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated the SC rates ranged between 77—88% for HIV-positive individuals and was 100% for HIV-negative individuals. Following vaccination with hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant the seroprotection rates ranged between 24—46% in HIV-positive individuals and was 93% in HIV-negative individuals. If a primary immunization course fails to confer immunity, repeat doses may be required.
 
The safety and effectiveness of hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant in infants and children have not been established. The hepatitis B component of the combination vaccine contains trace amounts of thimerosal ( 65 years of age) were not included in clinical trials to determine if geriatric populations will respond differently to hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant. The response to hepatitis B immunization is known to decline in vaccinees over age 40.
 
Hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant is classified in FDA pregnancy risk category C. The combination vaccine has not been evaluated in animal reproduction studies. Theoretically the vaccine should pose minimal risk to the fetus because it contains no infectious particles. The ACIP states that pregnancy should not be considered a contraindication to hepatitis B vaccination. The CDC states that the risk versus benefit of the hepatitis A vaccine must be considered, but the theoretical risk to the developing fetus from hepatitis A vaccine containing inactivated HAV is low.
 
The hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant should be used with caution in women who are breast-feeding; it is not known if it is excreted in human milk. However, hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant is characterized as an inactivated vaccine and should be harmless. The ACIP states that breast-feeding should not be considered a contraindication for immunization with inactivated or killed vaccines.

    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    No adequate and well controlled studies have been conducted with hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant during pregnancy. Available data from a pregnancy exposure registry do not suggest an increased risk of major birth defects or miscarriage in individuals who received the vaccine within 28 days prior to conception or during pregnancy. Administration of inactivated or recombinant virus vaccines during pregnancy has not resulted in adverse effects on the fetus. It is recommended to administer a vaccine during pregnancy when the likelihood of disease exposure is high, when the potential infection would cause harm to the pregnant person or fetus, and when the vaccine is unlikely to cause harm.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information

Inactivated and recombinant vaccines pose no risk to breast-feeding individuals or their children. Additionally, breast-feeding does not adversely affect immunization; limited data suggest breast-feeding may enhance the immune response to certain vaccine antigens.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Adverse events seen after the administration of hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant are similar to those that occur after administration of the monovalent components. Most adverse events reported have been mild, self-limiting and did not last for more than 48 hours. Adverse reactions that have been reported to occur with an incidence of 1% to 10% include injection site reaction (such as induration), and upper respiratory tract infection (not considered related to vaccination). In a comparative trial, adverse events following the administration of hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant were compared to separate injections of the monovalent vaccines. Injection-site soreness (38% vs. 46%, respectively), headache (17% vs. 15%, respectively) and fatigue (12% vs. 11%, respectively) were the most common reported adverse events. Other reported, but less frequent reactions included local redness and swelling, diarrhea, nausea/vomiting, and fever. Reactions occurring in less than 1% of clinical trial injections with hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant included, but were not limited to pruritus, sweating, weakness, flushing, flu-like symptoms, syncope, abdominal pain, anorexia, myalgia, back pain, migraine, rash, urticaria, and dizziness. No serious adverse events related to vaccination were reported in either trial group.There have been rare reports of anaphylactic shock or anaphylactoid reactions following administration of both the hepatitis A and B vaccines. Serum sickness has occurred days to weeks after hepatitis B vaccine administration and is characterized by arthralgia, arthritis, fever, ecchymosis, erythema multiforme or erythema nodosum. Any patient who has previously had a hypersensitivity reaction to either the hepatitis A or B vaccine should not receive the combination product.There have been additional post-marketing adverse drug reactions (ADRs) with the individual hepatitis A and B monovalent vaccines. As with most post-marketing ADRs, it is difficult to assign causality to the drug. Post-marketing ADRs included, but were not limited to erythema multiforme (Stevens-Johnson syndrome), angioedema, sinus tachycardia, palpitations, dyspepsia, thrombocytopenia, seizures, dyspnea, bronchospasm, and tinnitus.Approximately 60 cases of alopecia occurring after vaccination (primarily with the monovalent vaccine for hepatitis B) have been reported. Although some people lost all of their hair, including eyelashes, in most instances, some or all of the hair grew back. A positive rechallenge was reported in sixteen cases. This side effect is very rare and has been estimated to occur in roughly 5 people a year out of about 1 billion vaccinations.Guillain-Barre syndrome has been reported rarely with both the hepatitis A vaccine and hepatitis B vaccine. Several well-designed epidemiologic studies have shown that there is no association of hepatitis B vaccine administration with the development of multiple sclerosis (MS). Additionally, hepatitis B vaccination does not appear to increase the risk of relapse in MS. Similarly, one would not expect an increased risk of MS with the combined hepatitis A and B vaccine.

    erythema, injection site reaction
    General Information
    Adverse events seen after the administration of hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant are similar to those that occur after administration of the monovalent components. Most adverse events reported have been mild, self-limiting and did not last for more than 48 hours. Description
  Text
  Hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant (Twinrix) is a bivalent combination vaccine containing the antigenic components of the hepatitis A vaccine and hepatitis B vaccine. It contains inactivated hepatitis A virus and noninfectious recombinant hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), so it is an inactivated/non-live vaccine. It is indicated for active immunization against disease caused by hepatitis A virus and infection by all known subtypes of the hepatitis B virus. In clinical studies, immune responses to the bivalent vaccine were non-inferior to the corresponding monovalent vaccines. One month after the 3-dose series, hepatitis A seroconversion was 99.9% and hepatitis B seroprotection was 98.5%.Twinrix was FDA approved in 2001.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant induces active immunity by stimulating production of antibodies against hepatitis A virus and hepatitis B surface antigen. The presence of anti-HAV antibodies confers protection against hepatitis A disease, although the lowest protective titer has not been determined. Antibody concentrations of at least 10 milli-International Units/mL against HBsAg are recognized as conferring protection against hepatitis B virus infection. Because hepatitis D virus infection requires hepatitis B virus infection, protection against hepatitis B also provides indirect protection against hepatitis D in persons who are not already infected with HBV.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Intramuscular Route
    Description
    In clinical trials in healthy adults vaccinated with hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant on a 0, 1, and 6-month schedule, hepatitis A seroconversion and hepatitis B seroprotection rates after Dose 2 were 98.8% and 78.2%, respectively. One month after completion of the 3-dose series, hepatitis A seroconversion was 99.9% and hepatitis B seroprotection was 98.5%. In clinical trials involving 129 subjects, antibodies to both HAV and HBV surface antigen persisted for at least 4 years after the first vaccine dose in a 3-dose series. In an accelerated-schedule study, hepatitis A vaccine, inactivated; hepatitis B vaccine, recombinant given on Days 0, 7, and 21 to 30 with a booster at Month 12 produced hepatitis B seroprotection of 96.4% at Month 13, compared with 93.4% after separate HAVRIX and ENGERIX-B vaccination; hepatitis A seroconversion at Month 13 was 100% in both groups.

Administration
  General Information
  For storage information, see the specific product information within the How Supplied section.
Verify immunization status in the state or local immunization information system in addition to the health care electronic record. If this vaccine has been previously given, question the patient, parent, or guardian about any symptoms or signs of an adverse reaction.
Advise individuals on the benefits and risks of the vaccine. Provide a copy of the Vaccine Information Statement (VIS).
Record manufacturer, lot number, administration date, and the name and address of the person administering the vaccine.
Ensure supportive care, including epinephrine 1 mg/mL injection, is readily accessible in the event of a clinically significant hypersensitivity reaction.
Complete a Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) report if adverse events have been identified. The VAERS toll-free information line is 1-800-822-7967 and email address is info@vaers.org.
