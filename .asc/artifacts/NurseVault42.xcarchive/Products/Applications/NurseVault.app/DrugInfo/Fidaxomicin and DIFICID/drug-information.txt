Drug Class: Fidaxomicin is an oral macrolide antibiotic indicated for the treatment of Clostridioides difficile-associated diarrhea (CDAD).

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 400 mg/day PO.

    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Patients with a documented macrolide hypersensitivity may be at increased risk of developing hypersensitivity reactions to fidaxomicin.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on the use of fidaxomicin during pregnancy are insufficient to evaluate a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. Fidaxomicin and OP-1118 (its main metabolite) have minimal systemic absorption after oral administration. In animal studies, no evidence of fetal harm was observed with fidaxomicin and OP-1118 exposures 65 times or more than the clinical exposure at the recommended human dose.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use fidaxomicin with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of fidaxomicin or its main metabolite (OP-1118) in human milk, the effects on the breast-fed child, or the effect on milk production. Fidaxomicin and OP-1118 have minimal systemic absorption after oral administration. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breast-fed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition.

Interactions
cycloSPORINE: (Minor) Cyclosporine, a P-glycoprotein (PGP) inhibitor, significantly increased the serum concentrations of fidaxomicin and its microbiologically active metabolite, OP-1118 (both PGP substrates); however, the serum concentrations of both fidaxomicin and OP-1118 were still in the ng/ml range. The manufacturer states a dosage adjustment is not necessary. The mean serum Cmax for fidaxomicin increased from 4.67 ng/ml when fidaxomicin was used alone to 19.4 ng/ml when administered with cyclosporine, while the mean AUC increased from 59.5 ng-h/ml for fidaxomicin alone to 114 ng-h/ml when administered with cyclosporine. The mean serum Cmax for OP-1118 increased from 10.6 ng/ml when fidaxomicin was used alone to 100 ng/ml when administered with cyclosporine, while the mean AUC increased from 106 ng-h/ml for fidaxomicin alone to 438 ng-h/ml when administered with cyclosporine. These concentrations are still well below the fecal concentrations demonstrated with a 10 day course of fidaxomicin.
Desogestrel: Ethinyl Estradiol; Ferrous Bisglycinate: (Moderate) It would be prudent to recommend alternative or additional contraception when oral contraceptives (OCs) are used with antibiotics. It was previously thought that antibiotics may decrease the effectiveness of oral contraceptives containing estrogens due to stimulation of estrogen metabolism or a reduction in estrogen enterohepatic circulation via changes in GI flora. One retrospective study reviewed the literature to determine the effects of oral antibiotics on the pharmacokinetics of contraceptive estrogens and progestins, and also examined clinical studies in which the incidence of pregnancy with oral contraceptives (OCs) and antibiotics was reported. It was concluded that the antibiotics ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin, doxycycline, metronidazole, ofloxacin, roxithromycin, temafloxacin, and tetracycline did not alter plasma levels of oral contraceptives. Antituberculous drugs (e.g., rifampin) were the only agents associated with OC failure and pregnancy. Based on the study results, these authors recommended that back-up contraception may not be necessary if OCs are used reliably during oral antibiotic use. Another review of the subject concurred with these data, but noted that individual patients have been identified who experienced significant decreases in plasma concentrations of combined OC components and who appeared to ovulate; the agents most often associated with these changes were rifampin, tetracyclines and penicillin derivatives. These authors concluded that because females most at risk for OC failure or noncompliance may not be easily identified and the true incidence of such events may be under-reported, and given the serious consequence of unwanted pregnancy, that recommending an additional method of contraception during short-term antibiotic use may be justified. During long-term antibiotic administration, the risk for drug interaction with OCs is less clear, but alternative or additional contraception may be advisable in selected circumstances. Data regarding progestin-only contraceptives or for newer combined contraceptive deliveries (e.g., patches, rings) are not available.

Dichlorphenamide: (Moderate) Use dichlorphenamide and fidaxomicin together with caution as both drugs can cause metabolic acidosis. Concurrent use may increase the severity of metabolic acidosis. Measure sodium bicarbonate concentrations at baseline and periodically during dichlorphenamide treatment. If metabolic acidosis occurs or persists, consider reducing the dose or discontinuing dichlorphenamide therapy.

During long-term antibiotic administration, the risk for drug interaction with OCs is less clear, but alternative or additional contraception may be advisable in selected circumstances. Description
  Text
  Fidaxomicin is an oral macrolide antibiotic indicated for the treatment of Clostridioides difficile-associated diarrhea (CDAD). It is bactericidal against C. difficile, including hypervirulent strains. Fidaxomicin may have an increased sustained response as compared to oral vancomycin. Systemic absorption of fidaxomicin is minimal.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Fidaxomicin, a macrolide antibiotic, is a fermentation product obtained from Dactylosporangium aurantiacum. In vitro, fidaxomicin is bactericidal against Clostridioides difficile, including hypervirulent restriction endonuclease analysis (REA) group B1 strains and ATCC 43255. Fidaxomicin inhibits RNA synthesis by inhibiting sigma-dependent transcription of bacterial RNA polymerases and may act at the early stages of transcription. Fidaxomicin has a microbiologically active metabolite, OP-1118. With minimal to no activity against gram-negative anaerobes, facultative aerobes, and Enterobacteriaceae, fidaxomicin has a limited effect on normal fecal flora.
 
Fidaxomicin and OP-1118 demonstrate time-dependent killing of C. difficile. Fidaxomicin demonstrates a post-antibiotic effect against C. difficile of 6 to 10 hours. High inoculum or high cation concentrations do not appear to affect fidaxomicin activity.
 
A specific mutation (Val-ll43-Gly) in the beta subunit of RNA polymerase is associated with reduced susceptibility to fidaxomicin. However, since fecal concentrations are significantly higher than required bactericidal concentrations, it is possible that elimination of these mutants may occur. These mutant strains were rapidly killed in vitro at concentrations 4 times the MIC. These concentrations are likely achievable at routine oral doses.
 
There is no cross-resistance with other classes of antibacterial agents, including the rifamycin class, as the site of action for fidaxomicin is thought to be distinct from that of the rifamycins which also act on RNA polymerases. Fidaxomicin and OP-1118 do not exhibit any antagonistic interaction with other classes of antibacterial agents and synergy has been observed in vitro with rifampin and rifaximin against C. difficile.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Fidaxomicin has minimal systemic absorption after oral administration. In pharmacokinetic single-dose studies (n = 14), the serum Cmax for fidaxomicin and its microbiologically active metabolite, OP-1118, were 5.2 +/- 2.81 ng/mL and 12 +/- 6.06 ng/mL. Fecal concentrations of fidaxomicin and OP-1118 (n = 8) were 639 to 2,710 mcg/g and 213 to 1,210 mcg/g, respectively, obtained within 24 hours after the last dose when fidaxomicin was dosed twice daily for 10 days as compared to the serum concentrations of these patients during the Tmax window (1 to 5 hours) of 2 to 179 ng/mL and 10 to 829 ng/mL, respectively. During clinical trials, plasma concentrations of fidaxomicin and OP-1118 were 2 to 6 fold higher when compared to healthy adults. During a 10-day course, plasma concentrations of OP-1118 were approximately 50% to 80% higher after 10 days of treatment than on day 1, while concentrations of fidaxomicin were similar on days 1 and 10.
 
In a food-effect study (n = 29) with healthy adults, the Cmax of fidaxomicin and OP-1118 decreased by 21.5% and 33.4%, respectively, when fidaxomicin was administered with a high-fat meal versus under fasting conditions. This change in Cmax is not considered to be significant.

  Text
  Fidaxomicin is administered orally and is mainly confined to the gastrointestinal tract after administration. It is primarily transformed by hydrolysis at the isobutyryl ester to form its main metabolite, OP-1118, which is microbiologically active. Metabolism is not dependent on the cytochrome P450 enzymes; however, both fidaxomicin and OP-1118 are substrates of the P-glycoprotein (PGP) efflux transporter, which is expressed in the gastrointestinal tract. The half-life of fidaxomicin is 11.7 +/- 4.8 hours, and the half-life of OP-1118 is 11.2 hours +/- 3.01 hours. Fidaxomicin Tmax is 2 hours (range 1 to 5 hours); OP-1118 Tmax occurs at 1.02 hours (range 1 to 5 hours). Fidaxomicin is primarily excreted in the feces, with more than 92% of the dose recovered in the stool as parent drug and OP-1118 and 0.59% of the dose recovered in the urine as OP-1118 after single-dose studies.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: none
Because fidaxomicin and OP-1118 are substrates of the P-glycoprotein (PGP) efflux transporter, concentrations of both may be decreased at the site of action (gastrointestinal tract) via PGP inhibition, but concomitant PGP inhibitor use had no attributable effect on safety or treatment outcomes in controlled clinical trials. FDA-approved labeling states that fidaxomicin may be coadministered with PGP inhibitors, and no dosage adjustments are recommended.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Fidaxomicin and its microbiologically active metabolite, OP-1118, do not appear to undergo significant hepatic metabolism; therefore, hepatic impairment is not expected to significantly impact fidaxomicin or OP-1118 elimination. Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  In controlled trials, plasma concentrations of fidaxomicin and its microbiologically active metabolite, OP-1118, did not vary based on severity of renal impairment. No dosage adjustment is recommended in patients with renal dysfunction.
