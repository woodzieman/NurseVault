        Dosage Description
        Safety and efficacy have not been established. Trials have used 120 to 160 mg/day PO, given in divided doses 2 to 3 times daily; at least 4 to 6 weeks of treatment are needed to assess response. The German Commission E recommends GBE 120 to 240 mg/day PO divided into 2 or 3 doses for at least 4 to 6 weeks. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) effectiveness review states that the evidence for whether GBE improves cognition or function in Alzheimer's disease is inconsistent and unreliable; the data are insufficient to draw conclusions about relative differences between GBE and approved drug treatments such as cholinesterase inhibitors (ChEIs) regarding cognition, function, and safety. Indirect evidence from placebo-controlled trials suggests herbal supplements such as GBE are less effective than ChEIs for Alzheimer's disease. Studies evaluating GBE for VaD have produced inconsistent results.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Ginkgo has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. Products containing ginkgo are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Consumers should also be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.Avoid accidental exposure of the skin with ginkgo fruit pulp as contact may produce erythema, edema, contact dermatitis, severe itching and vesicle formation lasting 7—10 days. The fruit pulp and seed cover contain compounds structurally similar to the urushiol allergens in poison ivy, mango fruit rind, and cashew nut shell. Ginkgo pollen is also highly allergenic. GBE should not be given to patients with documented hypersensitivity to ginkgo preparations. Use caution in patients with a history of plant-related atopy.Ginkgo should be used with caution in patients with bleeding disorders or a coagulopathy (e.g., vitamin K deficiency) or who are taking anticoagulant therapy. Ginkgo should not be used at all in patients with active bleeding, including GI bleeding, intracranial bleeding, retinal bleeding, or retroperitoneal bleeding. In addition, ginkgo should not be given in combination with other medications or herbs that have anticoagulant or antiplatelet effects. Ginkgo inhibits the binding of platelet-activation factor (PAF) to platelets; this action may increase bleeding time. Ginkgo should also be avoided in patients with risk factors for intracranial bleeding such as diabetes mellitus, amyloid senile plaques, and severe hypertension.Until more is known, ginkgo should be used cautiously or avoided in patients who have a history of a seizure disorder or who have a predisposition to seizure activity (e.g., head trauma, increased intracranial pressure, or unstable metabolic conditions). Seizure activity may also be a manifestation of Alzheimer's disease. Seizures have been reported voluntarily in association with the use of ginkgo-containing products. While it is impossible to determine causality with the limited data available, clinicians should consider ginkgo discontinuation in any patient experiencing seizures while taking this herb.The risks of using the herb would outweigh any potential benefits of use during gestation. Use of ginkgo in pregnancy is not recommended due to effects on the arachadonic acid pathway. Also, limited in vitro data suggests that ginkgo may inhibit fertility in couples trying to conceive; use with caution in patients experiencing infertility (see Adverse Reactions).The administration of ginkgo in women who are breast-feeding or children has not been studied and is not recommended. Avoid excessive ingestion of ginkgo biloba by infants and children. Excessive ingestion of the seeds is associated with seizures and loss of consciousness and can be fatal.Patients taking Ginkgo biloba should inform their health care practitioners that they are taking this herb prior to scheduling surgery. Because ginkgo can interfere with the proper functioning of platelets and interferes with blood clotting, it may be wise to discontinue this herb 2—3 weeks prior to an elective procedure, when possible. Based on limited pharmacokinetic data available for the ginkgosolides, discontinue ginkgo at least 36 hours prior to the surgical procedure.

    Headline
    planned procedure
    General Information
    Advise patients to discontinue ginkgo supplements 2 weeks prior to a planned procedure (e.g., surgery). Ginkgo may increase the risk of bleeding due to its antiplatelet effects.
    Headline
    labor, obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    Ginkgo should be avoided during pregnancy since it contains flavonoids and other compounds that have not undergone reproductive testing. In addition, in vitro data and animal data suggest that gingko has antiplatelet activity and may prolong bleeding during labor and obstetric delivery.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise individuals to avoid breast-feeding while taking ginkgo biloba, due to the potential for serious adverse effects in the breast-fed child. It contains flavonoids and other compounds that can be toxic if ingested in excessive amounts in an infant or young child. There are no data on the safety of ginkgo biloba in human milk.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Advise people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with ginkgo. Based on limited in vitro data, ginkgo may cause infertility by inhibiting sperm motility or the ability to fertilize oocytes.

Description
  Text
  Ginkgo biloba extract (GBE) is a dietary supplement derived from the leaves of Ginkgo biloba Linne, an ornamental, deciduous tree. It is considered the world's oldest living tree species. Ginkgo biloba is also known as gingko, gingkco, hill apricot, maiden hair tree, kew tree, oriental plum tree, silver apricot, silver fruit, and silver plume. Active ingredients of GBE include flavonone glycosides and ginkgolides. The concentration of the active compounds in the leaves is very low, which is the reason for the extraction process. The use of crude leaf powder or tea from the leaves is probably ineffective. Many pharmacological and clinical effects of ginkgo have been investigated, and the herb has been used for many centuries. The German Commission E monograph states that GBE, as studied with a few proprietary ginkgo extracts, is effective for the treatment of vertigo and tinnitus of vascular and involutional origin, primary progressive dementia (PPD) or vascular dementia (VaD), mixed PPD and VaD, and the treatment of Fontaine Stage II intermittent claudication to increase pain-free walking as part of a total treatment plan including physical therapy. In the U.S., GBE supplements are sometimes promoted as agents to improve circulation and are also often promoted to provide memory support and improve cognitive function. According to an effectiveness review from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), the evidence for whether ginkgo improves cognition or function in Alzheimer's disease is inconsistent and unreliable and the evidence is considered insufficient to draw conclusions about relative differences between ginkgo biloba and approved drug treatments such as cholinesterase inhibitors (ChEIs) regarding cognition, function, and safety. Indirect evidence from placebo-controlled trials suggests herbal supplements such as GBE are less effective than ChEIs for Alzheimer's disease, and safety of these supplements in these patients has not been established. Studies evaluating GBE for VaD have produced inconsistent results.
 
NOTE: In the U.S., herbal products and dietary supplements are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for dietary supplements as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since supplements are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for these products and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Ginkgo has many pharmacologic effects due to the various compounds present in the extract. These compounds may act synergistically as the whole extract seems to be more active than the individual compounds. GBE acts as a scavenger for free radicals by preventing lipid peroxidation, directly enhancing glutathione levels and glutathione disulfide reductase and possessing superoxide dismutase activity. The flavonoids act primarily on hydroxyl free radicals and rutin-type flavones to inhibit lipid peroxidation of cell membranes. GBE protects tissues against damage due to hypoxia and reperfusion injury due to free radical formation. Ginkgolides and bilobalide decrease the effects of hypoxia on the brain by increasing cerebral blood flow by stimulating prostaglandin biosynthesis or indirectly stimulating norepinephrine release resulting in arterial vasodilatation. These components of EGb 761 have been shown to have cardioprotective effects against free radical formation. The flavones also can prevent hypoxic tissue injury by reducing capillary fragility, blood viscosity and erythrocyte aggregation and increasing erythrocyte flexibility. The ginkgolides A, B, and C (ginkgo derivatives BN 52020, 52021, 52022) competitively inhibit platelet activating factor (PAF) binding to platelets, neutrophils and eosinophils resulting in anti-inflammatory effects. The anti-stress and anxiolytic effects of ginkgo are thought to be due to the inhibition of monoamine oxidase (MAO-A and MAO-B). Ginkgolic acid and related alkylphenols are found in the leaves, covering of the seeds and in the pulp of the fruit of ginkgo biloba L. These compounds are thought to be responsible for the allergic reactions and GI and skin irritation seen with contact and ingestion of the fruit and leaves.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    After oral administration of 80 mg EGb 761, the following pharmacokinetic parameters for several of the components have been characterized. The bioavailability of the ginkgo-flavone glycosides is > 60%, with a time to peak concentration (Tmax) of 1.5—3 hours. The bioavailability of ginkgolides A and B is > 80% with a Tmax of 1—2 hours. Bilobalide has a bioavailability of 70% and Tmax is 1—2 hours.
  Text
  Ginkgo is administered orally. The different active components and potential interactions of these components make it difficult to quantify the pharmacokinetics of ginkgo. The elimination half-life is 3 hours. The only metabolites identified in urine samples following oral administration of EGb to healthy volunteers were substituted benzoic acids. This is different from animal models indicating more extensive metabolism in humans. There were no metabolites detected in blood samples during this study. There is no evidence that ginkgo affects hepatic microsomal metabolism.
