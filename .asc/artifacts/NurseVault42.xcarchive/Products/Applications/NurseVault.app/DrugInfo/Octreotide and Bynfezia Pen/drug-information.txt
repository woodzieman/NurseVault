Drug Class: Octreotide is a synthetic analog of the naturally occurring hormone somatostatin.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC), oral; Common adult doses: 80 mg/day PO for acromegaly. Injectable doses are dependent on indication for therapy, route of administration, and patient response.

Transitioning from octreotide solution for injection to octreotide depot suspension for injection
Individuals should be maintained on octreotide solution for injection for at least 2 weeks to determine tolerance and clinical response.         Uncontrolled studies indicate responsiveness with an initial dose of 50 mcg subcutaneously every 8 hours as needed, and then titrated to effectiveness, especially if no infectious etiology is present. Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Octreotide should be used with caution in patients with gallbladder disease because octreotide may increase the risk of acute cholecystitis, ascending cholangitis, biliary obstruction or cholestatic hepatitis. Single doses of octreotide have been shown to inhibit gallbladder contractility and decrease bile secretion.Although octreotide has been studied in the adjunctive treatment of acute pancreatitis, results of these studies have been controversial. Octreotide is known to increase the frequency of sphincter of Oddi contractions which could impair the flow of bile and pancreatic juices into the duodenum, possibly leading to pancreatic complications. Several cases of new onset pancreatitis have been reported in patients receiving octreotide therapy. The half-life and clearance of octreotide have been shown to be altered in patients with hepatic disease; however, recommendations regarding dosage adjustments are unavailable from the manufacturer.The half-life of octreotide is possibly increased in patients with severe renal failure requiring dialysis. These patients may require dosage adjustments. However, recommendations regarding dosage adjustments are unavailable from the manufacturer.The use of octreotide is often associated with mild hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia. However, overt diabetes mellitus or the necessity for dose adjustments in hypoglycemic agents may occur. Acromegalic patients, due to their underlying disease and/or subsequent treatment, are at an increased risk of developing diabetes. In acromegalic patients treated with octreotide in clinical trials, hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia occurred in 3% and 16% of patients, respectively. Severe hyperglycemia, subsequent pneumonia, and death following treatment with octreotide was reported in one patient with no history of hyperglycemia. Octreotide is likely to affect glucose regulation in patients with Type I diabetes, requiring adjustment in insulin dose. Symptomatic hypoglycemia, which may be severe, has been reported in patients with Type I diabetes receiving octreotide. In non-diabetic patients and patients with Type II diabetes with partially intact insulin reserves, the administration of octreotide may result in decreases in plasma insulin concentrations and hyperglycemia. It is therefore recommended that glucose tolerance and antidiabetic treatment be periodically monitored during therapy with these drugs. Also, octreotide should be used cautiously in patients with diabetic gastroparesis because octreotide reduces gut motility.Octreotide suppresses the secretion of thyroid stimulating hormone which may result in hypothyroidism or goiter (see Adverse Reactions). Baseline and periodic assessment of thyroid function is recommended during chronic therapy.Vitamin B12 deficiency may occur in patients receiving octreotide; abnormal Schilling's test have been observed in some patients receiving octreotide therapy. Monitoring of vitamin B12 levels is recommended during chronic therapy.Octreotide has been shown to alter the absorption of dietary fats in some patients.Octreotide is classified as FDA pregnancy riskcategory B. It is known to cross the human placenta and has been measured in the newborn. Available data of the use of octreotide during pregnancy are limited. Most women who have taken octreotide during pregnancy have taken it during the first trimester; doses ranged from 200—300 mcg/day (regular acting formulation) or 20—30 mg/month (long-acting formulation). In those women who continued it throughout pregnancy and had a known outcome, congenital abnormalities were not reported. The few cases describing octreotide use during some portion of pregnancy resulted in normal outcomes to the fetus. Also, animal studies have not revealed any teratogenic effects. Nevertheless, the data are too limited to asses the true human embryo/fetal risk.It is not known if octreotide is excreted in breast milk, but it should be expected because of the documented placental passage. No reports have been located describing octreotide use during lactation; however, because the drug is digested following oral administration, the risk to the nursing infant appears to be nonexistent. Nevertheless, octreotide should be used with caution in women who are breast-feeding.Patients with cardiac disease should be closely monitored while receiving octreotide. Octreotide has been associated with sinus bradycardia, cardiac arrhythmias, and conduction abnormalities (see Adverse Reactions), although an exact causative mechanism has not been determined. In one patient with acromegaly and severe congestive heart failure, initiation of octreotide therapy resulted in worsening of the patients CHF with improvement when the drug was stopped.Experience with octreotide in children is limited. Several reports in the medical literature describe its use for the treatment of congenital hyperinsulinism (also called nesidioblastosis) in neonates and infants; the youngest patient to receive the drug has been 1 month old. Most of the use of octreotide in this manner is short-term, but there are reports of longer-term use (i.e., 2.2—5.5 years). At doses of 1— 40 mcg/kg/day, the most common side effects are steatorrhea, diarrhea, vomiting, and abdominal distention. Additionally, poor growth has been reported in several patients treated with octreotide for > 1 year, although catch-up growth does occur after drug discontinuation. Additionally, the safety and efficacy of the depot formulation (Sandostatin® LAR) have been examined in a randomized, placebo-controlled trial in children aged 6—17 years with hypothalamic obesity from a cranial insult; patients received 40 mg IM once monthly for 6 months. Data to support the use of the depot formulation for this indication are limited as the mean BMI increased by 0.1 kg/m2 in the octreotide group compared to an increase of 0 kg/m2 in the placebo group, demonstrating no difference in weight loss between the 2 groups. Additionally, diarrhea occurred in 37% of the patients, and new cholelithiasis occurred in 33% of the patients. In contrast, preliminary data supporting the use of SC octreotide in the treatment of hypothalamic obesity in children are promising (See Dosage). Clinical studies of octreotide did not include sufficient numbers of subjects aged 65 and over to determine whether they respond differently from younger subjects. Reported clinical experience has not identified differences in responses between the elderly and younger patients. In general, dose selection for an elderly patient should be cautious, usually starting at the lower end of the dosing range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function, and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    gallbladder disease
    General Information
    Use octreotide with caution in individuals with preexisting gallbladder disease, as it may reduce gallbladder motility and cause or exacerbate gallstone formation. Monitor individuals receiving octreotide periodically. If complications of cholelithiasis are suspected, discontinue octreotide and treat appropriately.
    Headline
    hepatic cirrhosis
    General Information
    Individuals with hepatic cirrhosis and fatty liver disease have a prolonged half-life and decreased clearance of octreotide when compared to healthy individuals. Adjustment of the maintenance dose may be necessary in individuals with cirrhosis.
    Headline
    renal failure
    General Information
    Use octreotide with caution in patients with severe renal failure requiring dialysis, including patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Studies indicate that drug clearance is reduced in adult patients with severe renal failure requiring dialysis. Initial dosage adjustments are recommended for oral dosing, and dosage adjustments for injectable therapy may be necessary; dosage should be guided by the indication for use and patient response and tolerance.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus
    General Information
    Use octreotide with caution in individuals with pre-existing diabetes mellitus. Octreotide may cause hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia by altering the balance between the counter-regulatory hormones insulin, glucagon, and growth hormone in the body. Individuals receiving antidiabetic medications may need to have their dosages adjusted. Monitor blood glucose concentrations in all individuals.
    Headline
    bradycardia
    General Information
    Use caution in individuals with bradycardia. Bradycardia has been reported during treatment with octreotide. Dosage adjustments of concomitantly used medications with bradycardic effects (e.g., beta-blockers) may be necessary.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on octreotide use during pregnancy are limited and insufficient to determine a drug-associated risk of major congenital anomalies, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. Postmarketing reports include a small number of pregnancies in individuals with acromegaly exposed to octreotide at subcutaneous doses of 100 to 300 mcg daily or intramuscular doses of 20 to 30 mg monthly. Most exposures occurred during the first trimester, although some individuals continued treatment throughout pregnancy. No congenital anomalies were reported among cases with known outcomes. In animal studies, intravenous administration of octreotide at doses approximately 7 and 13 times the clinical dose (based on body surface area) did not result in adverse developmental effects. In a pre- and postnatal rat study, transient growth delays were observed at intravenous doses below the clinical dose (based on body surface area), with no effects on postnatal development.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with octreotide. Treatment with octreotide may improve fertility by reducing growth hormone levels and normalizing insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) concentrations in individuals with acromegaly, which may increase the risk of unintended pregnancy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use octreotide with caution during breast-feeding. There are limited data on the presence of octreotide in human milk. Given its high molecular weight, transfer into milk is expected to be low. In one case, the concentration in colostrum was 4.6 mcg/L in an individual receiving 1,200 to 2,400 mcg/day throughout pregnancy. Octreotide is poorly absorbed orally and has been administered directly to infants by injection; therefore, clinically significant adverse effects in a breastfed child are unlikely. Limited case reports (at least 3 infants) describe breast-feeding during maternal treatment with no reported adverse effects. Consider the benefits of breastfeeding, the individual's clinical need for octreotide, and any potential risks to the child from the medication or the underlying condition.

  General Information
  The most common adverse effects of octreotide therapy reported by the manufacturer are GI effects including include abdominal pain, diarrhea, and nausea/vomiting. In rare instances, gastrointestinal side effects may resemble acute intestinal obstruction (e.g., ileus), with progressive abdominal distention, severe epigastric pain, abdominal tenderness, and guarding. Additionally, FDA is investigating these reports in patients taking the depot injection to determine if the adverse events are drug-related; FDA will determine if additional study or action is warranted. Patients receiving the depot injection reported less diarrhea, abdominal pain and nausea and more flatulence and constipation as compared to those receiving the SC injections. The incidence of GI symptoms in patients receiving the SC injection is not dose-related; however, the symptoms resolved more quickly in patients receiving 300 mcg/day than those receiving 750 mcg/day. In patients receiving the depot injection, the diarrhea was dose-related. Diarrhea, abdominal pain and nausea developed primarily during the first month of treatment with the depot injection and were uncommon thereafter. Gastrointestinal effects, such as diarrhea, steatorrhea, vomiting and abdominal distention have also occurred in 22—35% of pediatric patients receiving octreotide in doses of 3—40 mcg/kg/day SC. Resolution of vomiting and distention typically occurred in 2—4 days, and diarrhea/steatorrhea in 2—4 weeks.Injection site reaction manifested as pain, tingling, burning, or stinging with redness or swelling at the injection site has been reported with both routes of administration of octreotide, although is more common with the depot injection. Pain associated with the depot injection is usually mild-moderate, of short duration and is dose-related. Up to 50% of patients reported pain with the 30 mg injection.The use of octreotide is often associated with mild hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia, although overt diabetes mellitus or the necessity for dose adjustments in hypoglycemic agents may occur. Severe hyperglycemia, subsequent pneumonia, and death following treatment with octreotide was reported in one patient with no history of hyperglycemia. Octreotide is likely to affect glucose regulation in patients with Type I diabetes, requiring adjustment in insulin dose; symptomatic hypoglycemia, which may be severe, has been reported patients with Type I diabetes. The administration of octreotide may result in decreased plasma insulin concentrations and hyperglycemia in non-diabetic patients and patients with Type II diabetes with partially intact insulin reserves. Symptoms of hyperglycemia include: drowsiness; flushed, dry skin; stomach ache or nausea/vomiting; unusual tiredness; polyuria and polydipsia; and dehydration and weight loss. Symptoms of hypoglycemia include: an anxious feeling; chills; cool, pale skin; difficulty concentrating; headache; hunger; nausea; nervousness; tremor or shakiness; diaphoresis; unconsciousness; unusual tiredness; and weakness. Although octreotide is sometimes used to increase blood glucose concentrations in patients with hyperinsulinism and insulinoma, octreotide can occasionally aggravate hypoglycemia in these patients. In the presence of an unresponsive insulinoma, octreotide can suppress the counterregulatory hormones glucagon and growth hormone, thus leading to more severe hypoglycemia.Cholelithiasis and/or gallbladder sludge have been reported in patients treated chronically with octreotide. In a group of patients receiving chronic octreotide for metastatic carcinoid and malignant islet cell tumors, cholelithiasis and/or gallbladder sludge were observed in 52.3% (23/44) of patients; however, only 3 patients (6.8%) had symptomatic disease requiring emergency cholecystectomy. Five other patients underwent elective or incidental gallbladder surgery. In a study of patients with acromegaly who had normal baseline ultrasound examinations of the gallbladder, 23.5% (24/102) developed gallstones, usually during the first year of treatment, and 21 patients developed sludge alone. Most patients with cholelithiasis were asymptomatic, and none developed cholecystitis. Gallstone formation was not related to the dose of octreotide. In children with hypothalamic obesity secondary to cranial insult, the use of 40 mg IM of depot octreotide for 6 months was associated with cholelithiasis in 33% of the patients. Furthermore, asymptomatic gallstones with sludge were reported in one infant after one year of treatment with octreotide.Several cases of pancreatitis have been reported in patients receiving octreotide.Bradycardia has occurred during octreotide administration. In patients with acromegaly, 25% developed sinus bradycardia, 10% had conduction abnormalities and arrhythmias developed in 9% of patients treated with octreotide. In patients with carcinoid treated with octreotide, sinus bradycardia developed in 19%, conduction abnormalities in 9% and arrhythmias in 3%. Other EKG changes observed during octreotide therapy include QT prolongation , axis shifts, early repolarization, low voltage, R/S transition, early R wave progression and non-specific ST-T wave changes. The relationship of these events to octreotide is not established as many of these patients had underlying cardiac disease.Vitamin B12 deficiency may occur in patients receiving octreotide. In addition, octreotide alters the absorption of dietary fats. In patients receiving total parenteral nutrition (TPN) for excessive fluid loss from the GI tract, serum zinc levels may rise excessively when the fluid loss is reversed.Octreotide suppresses the secretion of thyroid stimulating hormone which may result in hypothyroidism. In acromegaly patients receiving octreotide, 12% developed hypothyroidism, 8% developed goiter and 4% required thyroid replacement therapy while receiving octreotide. In acromegalics treated with the depot injection hypothyroidism was reported in only 2% of patients and goiter in 2%; only 2 patients required thyroid replacement therapy. In patients with carcinoid, hypothyroidism has been reported rarely.Other adverse effects associated with octreotide therapy include arthropathy, chest pain (unspecified), dizziness, dyspnea, fatigue, fever, flushing, malaise, tolerance or tachyphylaxis, and upper respiratory infection.. Rarely, anaphylactoid reactions, including anaphylactic shock have been reported. A seizure occurring in an infant with nesidioblastosis has been reported; however, a causal association with octreotide has not been made. One death has been reported in a 16-month old male with enterocutaneous fistula who received a single 100 mcg SC dose of octreotide.Antibodies to octreotide develop in roughly 25% of patients. These antibodies do not affect the efficacy of octreotide; however, in two acromegalic patients who received octreotide injection, the duration of growth hormone suppression following each injection was approximately twice as long as those patients without antibodies. It has not been determined whether octreotide antibodies will also prolong growth hormone suppression following depot injections.Growth inhibition has been reported in 37% of pediatric patients (n=7) who received octreotide for 1—4.3 years. Growth inhibition was associated with low serum growth hormone and/or IGF-1 levels in 4/6 patients. Catch up growth occurred in 3/3 patients who were followed after discontinuation of octreotide. Poor weight gain has also been reported.

Description
  Text
  Octreotide is a synthetic analog of the naturally occurring hormone somatostatin. The activity of octreotide is similar to that of somatostatin. Octreotide, however, has a longer half-life, greater selectivity for inhibiting glucagon, growth hormone, and insulin release, and a lower incidence of rebound hypersecretion after discontinuation. Based on this activity, octreotide injectable products have been approved for various indications, including the treatment of acromegaly and for use in controlling the symptoms associated with vasoactive intestinal peptide tumors (VIPomas; watery diarrhea) and metastatic carcinoid tumors (flushing and diarrhea). An oral formulation is approved for acromegaly treatment in adults previously responding to octreotide or lanreotide injections. Non-FDA-approved uses of octreotide include AIDS-associated diarrhea, esophageal varices, pituitary tumors (i.e., thyrotropinomas), and hepatorenal syndrome. Preliminary data also indicate that octreotide may be useful in suppressing excessive insulin secretion in pediatric patients with hypothalamic obesity from a cranial insult due to a tumor, surgery, or after cranial irradiation. The suppression of insulin secretion by octreotide leads to decreases in weight and BMI while improving quality of life.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The pharmacologic effects of octreotide are similar to those of somatostatin, a hypothalamic peptide. Although the exact mechanism of action is not known, octreotide is believed to act at somatostatin receptors. Octreotide inhibits the secretion of both pituitary and gastrointestinal hormones including serotonin, gastrin, vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), insulin, glucagon, secretin, motilin, pancreatic polypeptide, growth hormone, and thyrotropin. Due to the number of hormones affected by octreotide, the actions of octreotide are diverse. Inhibiting the secretion of serotonin and other gastroenteropancreatic peptides results in increased intestinal absorption of water and electrolytes, decreased pancreatic and gastric acid secretions, and increased intestinal transit time. In contrast, cisapride, a serotonin receptor agonist, stimulates peristalsis. Thus, with regard to serotonin, octreotide and cisapride exert opposite actions in the GI tract.
 
Octreotide can inhibit the secretion of hormones involved in vasodilation. This property makes octreotide useful in treating variceal bleeding and orthostatic hypotension. Octreotide increases splanchnic arteriolar resistance and decreases gastrointestinal blood flow, hepatic-vein wedge pressure, hepatic blood flow, portal vein pressure, and variceal pressure. Decreased blood flow to the portal vein reduces portal venous pressure in patients with cirrhosis or portal hypertension. A majority of patients with portal hypertension have a reduction in variceal bleeding when given octreotide. In patients with orthostatic or postprandial hypotension due to autonomic neuropathy, it is believed that their condition occurs as a result of inadequate sympathetic reflexes in conjunction with splanchnic vasodilation and VIP secretion. Administration of octreotide results in increases in both semirecumbent and standing blood pressures. Octreotide's effects were seen in patients with progressive autonomic failure, multiple-organ-system atrophy, and diabetic autonomic neuropathy but not in patients with sympathotonic orthostatic hypotension.
 
Because octreotide affects many GI hormones, octreotide is useful in controlling many types of secretory diarrhea including patients with VIPomas and AIDS-associated diarrhea. In patients with VIPomas, octreotide reduces serum levels of VIP. As a result, stool volume decreases and hypokalemia and achlorhydria improve. Tachyphylaxis to octreotide, however, has been observed in treating this condition. In patients with AIDS, diarrhea is caused by an infectious agent such as Cryptosporidium or is secondary to HIV infection. Human immunodeficiency virus contains an amino acid sequence similar to VIP which is believed to cause manifestations similar to VIP. Because of its inhibitory effects on VIP, octreotide is effective in patients with diarrhea due to HIV.
 
In patients with carcinoid syndrome, octreotide's ability to inhibit serotonin secretion results in decreased flushing, diarrhea, and wheezing, as well as reduced urinary excretion of 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA), the primary metabolite of serotonin. Improvements in the musculoskeletal symptoms of carcinoid have been reported with octreotide. In combination with other therapies, octreotide has also been effective in improving symptoms and decreasing tumor size in patients with metastatic carcinoid tumors.
 
Studies supporting the role of octreotide in the treatment of other gastroenteropancreatic tumors (i.e., gastrinomas, glucagonomas, insulinomas) are limited. In the treatment of gastrinomas (e.g., Zollinger-Ellison syndrome), octreotide may be more costly and no more effective than conventional therapies (H2-antagonists, proton-pump inhibitors or PPIs), however, a benefit may be derived from combination therapy because octreotide inhibits gastrin release whereas H2-antagonists and PPIs cause significant increases in gastrin serum concentrations. Octreotide may provide benefits in patients with unresectable insulinomas by decreasing insulin secretion and improving hypoglycemia in patients unresponsive to other therapies. In the treatment of the very rare glucagonomas, octreotide has only demonstrated improvement in the dermatosis (necrolytic migratory dermatitis) associated with this condition and has minimal effects on serum glucagon levels.
 
Octreotide also inhibits the secretion of some anterior pituitary hormones. Octreotide has been studied in the treatment of acromegaly and thyrotropinomas. Octreotide's effects in treating acromegaly results from its inhibition of growth hormone (GH). In acromegalic patients, octreotide reduces serum levels of GH resulting in a decrease in associated symptoms such as headache, hyperhidrosis, arthralgia, and finger circumference. In the treatment of thyrotropinomas, Chanson et al reported that thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels were decreased in the majority of patients treated with octreotide. Because these tumors are rare, data on the use of octreotide in this condition are limited to case reports. Further investigation is needed to determine octreotide's role as an alternative for patients with thyrotropinomas that are refractory to surgery and radiation.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    The exposure of octreotide (AUC) is similar between a single oral dose of octreotide 20 mg and a single subcutaneous dose of octreotide 0.1 mg in healthy adult patients, however, the maximum concentration (Cmax) was 33% lower after oral administration compared to subcutaneous administration. Absorption time was longer after oral administration compared to subcutaneous administration; Cmax was reached at a median of 1.67 to 2.5 hours after 20 mg of oral octreotide compared to 0.5 hours for subcutaneous injection administration. In healthy adult patients, the Cmax and AUC of octreotide increased dose-proportionally at doses ranging from 3 to 40 mg. In adult patients with acromegaly, there was a dose-related increase in the mean Cmax after chronic oral administration of octreotide. The Cmax after chronic dosing was lower in patients with acromegaly compared to single-dose peak concentrations observed in healthy adult patients at the same dose. In healthy patients, the administration of an octreotide 20 mg capsule with food led to an approximate 90% decrease in the Cmax and AUC; thus, the delayed-release capsules should be administered on an empty stomach and not with food.
    Name
    Intravenous Route
    Description
    Intravenous and subcutaneous octreotide are bioequivalent; peak concentrations and AUC are dose-proportional.
    Name
    Intramuscular Route
    Description
    After intramuscular (IM) administration of the long-acting depot formulation, octreotide is slowly released as the glucose star polymer (D,L-lactic and glycolic acids copolymer) biodegrades in the muscle, primarily through hydrolysis. Once octreotide is released from the polymer, it has the same pharmacokinetic and mechanistic characteristics as the immediate-release dosage form. The relative bioavailability of the depot injection as compared to the immediate release injection given subcutaneously is 60% to 63%. After a single IM injection in healthy adult patients, peak serum concentrations reach a transient initial peak of 0.03 ng/mL/mg within 1 hour. Subsequently, concentrations decline over 3 to 5 days (nadir less than 0.01 ng/mL/mg) before increasing and reaching a plateau 2 to 3 weeks after injection. Plateau concentrations are maintained for 2 to 3 weeks, with dose-proportional peak concentrations of 0.07 ng/mL/mg. Octreotide concentrations start to decrease slowly 6 weeks after the IM injection. By week 12 to 13, serum concentrations are less than 0.01 ng/mL/mg. After multiple injections of the depot formulation given every 4 weeks, steady state serum concentrations are reached after the third dose. After administration of octreotide depot injection 20 mg and 30 mg every 4 weeks in adult patients, respective steady-state concentrations are 1.6 ng/mL and 2.6 ng/mL at peak and 1.2 ng/mL and 2.1 ng/mL at trough. The depot formulation has smaller peak-to-trough concentration variations than the 3 times daily subcutaneous injections (44% to 68% versus 163% to 209%, respectively).
    Name
    Subcutaneous Route
    Description
    Octreotide is absorbed rapidly and completely after subcutaneous injection. Distribution of octreotide from plasma occurs rapidly, with an apparent distribution half-life of 12 minutes. In adult patients, peak concentrations of 5.2 ng/mL occur within 25 minutes after a 100 mcg dose. The effects of subcutaneous octreotide are variable but can last for up to 12 hours, depending on the indication for use.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    The effect of hepatic impairment on the disposition of octreotide is unknown. After administration of octreotide immediate-release injection solution subcutaneously or IV, adult patients with cirrhosis have demonstrated a prolonged octreotide half-life (3.7 hours) and decreased clearance (5.9 L/hour), while adult patients with fatty liver disease have shown a similar half-life (3.4 L/hour) to normal adults, but a larger reduction in clearance (8.2 L/hour). Initial dosage adjustments are recommended for adult patients with cirrhosis.
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    Patients with renal impairment have a reduced total body clearance and prolonged elimination half-life of octreotide (injection solution, given IV or subcutaneous). In adult patients, those with mild renal impairment (CrCl 40 to 60 mL/minute) have a clearance of 8.8 L/hour and a half-life of 2.4 hours; patients with moderate impairment (10 to 39 mL/minute) have a clearance 7.3 L/hour and a half-life of 3 hours. Adult patients with severe renal impairment (CrCl less than 10 mL/minute) have a clearance of 7.6 L/hour and a half-life of 3.1 hours. Patients with severe renal failure requiring dialysis have a clearance of 4.5 L/hour, half of that found in healthy subjects (10 L/hour). The depot injection suspension formulation has not been studied in patients with renal dysfunction; however, initial dose adjustment is recommended for adult patients with severe renal failure on dialysis. Altered pharmacokinetics were also noted in patients with renal impairment receiving oral dosing. In adult patients with severe renal impairment (eGFR 15 to 29 mL/minute/1.73m2) and patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) requiring dialysis who received a single oral dose of octreotide 20 mg, a 46% decrease in clearance, an 87% increase in AUC, and an 85% increase in half-life were observed in patients with ESRD on dialysis compared to matched healthy controls. ESRD patients had higher mean plasma concentrations compared to those with severe renal impairments with higher mean values for Cmax, AUC, and half-life, consistent with the known effects of renal impairment on octreotide exposure.
    Name
    Pediatrics
    Description
    Limited pharmacokinetic data exist in children; no specific pharmacokinetic data are available for octreotide injection solution given IV or subcutaneously. In a clinical study of 60 pediatric patients age 6 to 17 years with hypothalamic obesity resulting from cranial insult, the mean octreotide concentration after 6 doses of the Sandostatin LAR Depot injection suspension (40 mg IM every four weeks) was approximately 3 ng/mL. Steady-state was achieved after 3 injections of the 40 mg IM dose. However, efficacy was not demonstrated.
    Name
    Geriatric
    Description
    In geriatric patients, the half-life of octreotide is increased by 46% and clearance is decreased by 26%.
    Name
    Other
    Description
    Patients with Gigantism/Acromegaly
Pharmacokinetics in adult patients with acromegaly differ from those in healthy volunteers. Protein binding is less, 41% compared to 65% in healthy volunteers. Volume of distribution is larger (21.6 +/- 8.5 L vs. 13.6 L) and total body clearance is increased (18 L/hour vs. 7-10 L/hour). Elimination half-life is similar in both populations. After a 100 mcg subcutaneous dose in adults with acromegaly, peak concentrations (2.8 ng/mL) are lower and occur after a prolonged period (42 minutes), when compared to healthy adult patients (Cmax 5.2 ng/mL; Tmax 25 minutes). After a single IM injection of the depot formulation, peak concentrations are 0.3 ng/mL, 0.8 ng/mL, and 1.3 ng/mL for 10 mg, 20 mg, and 30 mg doses, respectively. Plateau concentrations of 0.5 ng/mL, 1.3 ng/mL, and 2 ng/mL are achieved 3 weeks (vs. 2 to 3 weeks) after injection and maintained for nearly 2 weeks (vs. 2 to 3 weeks).
  Text
  Octreotide acetate is administered orally and parenterally; the immediate-release injection solution should be given intravenously or subcutaneously, while the depot injection suspension formulation should only be given intramuscularly. Approximately 65% of a parenteral dose is bound to lipoprotein and albumin in a concentration-dependent manner. The volume of distribution is estimated to be 13.6 L in adult patients. Octreotide undergoes extensive hepatic metabolism. Total body clearance ranges from 7 to 10 L/hour in adult patients. The apparent elimination half-life of immediate-release octreotide injection is 1.7 to 1.9 hours, which is significantly greater than somatostatin's half-life of 1 to 3 minutes. In patients with acromegaly, the half-life is 3.2 to 4.5 hours for all oral doses (20 to 80 mg per day) and elimination is complete approximately 48 hours after the last dose in patients who have achieved steady-state plasma levels. Minimal accumulation (approximately 10%) was observed in patients after repeat oral octreotide administration. Approximately 32% of a dose is excreted in the urine as unchanged drug.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP450) isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4
Octreotide suppresses growth hormone secretion, which may decrease the metabolic clearance of drugs metabolized by CYP3A4. A potential for drug-drug interactions exists with medications metabolized by CYP3A4 with a narrow therapeutic index.

For intramuscular (IM) administration as a once monthly (every 4 weeks) injection. Do NOT give by any other route.

Preparation:
Drug product kit should be removed from the refrigerator and should stand at room temperature for a minimum of 30 minutes (not to exceed 24 hours) prior to preparation of the suspension. If necessary, the kit may be re-refrigerated.
Remove plastic cap from vial containing Sandostatin LAR powder and clean the vial stopper with an alcohol swab.
Remove the lid film of the vial adapter packaging and position the vial adapter on top of the vial and push it down until it snaps in place, confirmed by an audible "click". Lift the packaging off the vial adapter.
Remove cap from syringe prefilled with diluent solution and screw syringe onto vial adapter.
Slowly push plunger all the way down to transfer all of the diluent solution into the vial. Do not disturb the vial while the diluent saturates the powder. After 2 to 5 minutes, without inverting the vial, check the sides and bottom of vial for dry spots. The powder must be fully saturated before withdrawing from the vial.
Once the powder is completely saturated, press plunger all the way back into the syringe. Keep plunger pressed and shake vial moderately in a horizontal direction for approximately 30 seconds to form a uniform milky suspension.
Repeat shaking for another 30 seconds if powder is not completely suspended.
Prepare Syringe to Administer Depot Injection:
Turn the syringe and vial upside down and slowly pull the plunger out to draw entire contents from vial into the syringe.
Unscrew syringe from the vial adapter.
Peel off outer syringe label.
Just prior to injection, screw safety injection needle onto syringe, pull protective cover straight off needle, and gently shake syringe to maintain a uniform suspension.
Gently tap syringe to remove any visible bubbles and eliminate air from syringe. Ensure that the powder is completely suspended at the time of injection.
Administer immediately after preparation. Failure to inject immediately after suspension preparation may cause the product to become solid. If the product 'clumps' or flocculates, it is not usable. The preparation process will need to begin again with a new kit.
Injection Administration:
Must be given only by deep intragluteal IM injection. NEVER give intravenously.
Inject immediately after product preparation.
Prepare the injection site with an alcohol wipe.
Insert the needle fully into the left or right gluteus at a 90 degree angle to the skin.
At the next monthly injection, rotate the gluteal IM injection site.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Oral delayed-release capsules
Adult patients with liver cirrhosis and patients with fatty liver disease showed prolonged elimination of octreotide injection in studies. Titrate the adult maintenance dosage according to IGF-1 levels and clinical symptoms.
 
IV, IM, or subcutaneous formations
Patients with cirrhosis and fatty liver disease have prolonged elimination of octreotide. Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available for the immediate-release injection solution. The initial dose of the depot IM suspension should be decreased by 50% in adult patients (e.g., 10 mg IM) with cirrhosis; titrate dose based on clinical response. Once at a higher dose, the patient should be maintained or dose-adjusted as for any noncirrhotic patient. Pediatric data are not available.
  Renal Impairment
  Oral delayed-release capsules
Adult patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD): Initiate at a dosage of 20 mg PO once daily. Titrate the maintenance dosage according to IGF-1 levels and clinical symptoms.
 
IV, IM, or subcutaneous formations
In adult patients with mild, moderate, or severe renal impairment (non-dialysis patients) there is no need to adjust the initial dose of octreotide; the maintenance dose should be adjusted based on clinical response and tolerability as in nonrenal patients. Data for pediatric patients with renal impairment are not specifically available.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
See oral dosage adjustment in renal impairment for ESRD. Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in severe renal impairment or dialysis are not available for the immediate-release injection solution. The initial dose of the depot IM injection suspension should be decreased by 50% in adult patients (e.g., 10 mg IM); titrate and dose-adjust based on clinical response and tolerability as in nonrenal patients. Pediatric data are not available.
