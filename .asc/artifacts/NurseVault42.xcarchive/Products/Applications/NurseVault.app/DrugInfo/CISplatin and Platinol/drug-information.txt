Drug Class: Cisplatin is a non-cell-cycle specific alkylating agent with activity in many cancers.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 100 mg/m2 IV.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): dehydration, gi perforation, hiccups, azotemia, fanconi syndrome, nephrotoxicity, renal failure (unspecified), renal tubular acidosis, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatemia, hearing loss, ototoxicity, tinnitus, dysgeusia, paresthesias, peripheral neuropathy, tremor, weakness, encephalopathy, leukoencephalopathy, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, muscle cramps, seizures, tetany, blurred vision, optic neuritis, papilledema, retinal pigment changes

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Cisplatin is contraindicated in patients who exhibit platinum compound hypersensitivity. For example, patients who have worked with platinum in the electronics industry can develop sensitivity to platinum. Emetic risk: Pediatric patients: Doses 12 mg/m2 or higher: High Extravasation risk: Irritant Hazardous drug: NIOSH 2024 List: Table 1

          70 mg/m2 on day 2 plus gemcitabine 1,000 mg/m2 IV over 30 to 60 minutes on days 1, 8, and 15 repeated every 28 days (GC regimen) for up to 6 cycles was compared with methotrexate 30 mg/m2 on days 1, 15, and 22; vinblastine 3 mg/m2 IV on days 2, 15, and 22; doxorubicin 30 mg/m2 on day 2; and cisplatin 70 mg/m2 on day 2 (MVAC regimen) as first-line therapy for advanced or metastatic transitional cell carcinoma of the bladder in a multicenter, randomized, phase 3 trial. Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Cisplatin is contraindicated in patients who exhibit platinum compound hypersensitivity. For example, patients who have worked with platinum in the electronics industry can develop sensitivity to platinum. There is an increased incidence of hypersensitivity reactions in patients previously exposed to platinum therapy. Anaphylactic reactions may occur within minutes of cisplatin administration. Epinephrine, corticosteroids, and antihistamines have been utilized to treat these reactions.
 
Cisplatin is contraindicated in patients with severe bone marrow suppression, including neutropenia and thrombocytopenia. Patients should have normal WBC and platelet counts prior to cisplatin administration. While severe bone marrow suppression is uncommon with cisplatin therapy, patients who have had previous myelosuppressive therapy such as chemotherapy or radiation therapy may be at increased risk. Cisplatin is a known radiation sensitizer and may increase the toxicity as well as efficacy of radiation therapy. Cranial radiation has been associated with an increased risk of ototoxicity. Cisplatin should be administered under the supervision of a physician experienced in the management of cancer chemotherapy. Adequate diagnostic and treatment facilities are needed for appropriate management of cisplatin therapy and complications. Exercise caution to prevent cisplatin overdose. Doses greater than 100 mg/m2/cycle once every 3 to 4 weeks are rarely used. Avoid inadvertent cisplatin overdose due to confusion with Paraplatin (carboplatin) or prescribing practices that fail to differentiate daily doses from total dose per cycle.
 
Severe cisplatin neurotoxicity (see Adverse Reactions) has been associated with higher doses of cisplatin or greater dose frequencies than those recommended. Cisplatin should be used cautiously in patients with preexisting peripheral neuropathy. A thorough neurological exam prior to each dose of cisplatin is important.
 
Ototoxicity due cisplatin, which can be more severe in children, is significant and may result by tinnitus, and/or loss of high frequency hearing and occasionally deafness (see Adverse Reactions). Cisplatin is contraindicated in patients with pre-existing hearing impairment. Patients should have a baseline audiology exam before starting cisplatin therapy and prior to each dose due to cumulative ototoxicity. Audiology should be repeated if complaints of a hearing deficit occur. A hearing loss measured in the high decibel range outside normal tones is common and is not a reason to stop therapy.
 
Patients with an active infection should be treated prior to receiving cisplatin. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with chemotherapy.
 
Myelosuppressive effects of cisplatin can increase the risk of infection or bleeding; therefore, dental work should be delayed until blood counts have returned to normal. Patients, especially those with dental disease, should be instructed in proper oral hygiene, including caution in use of regular toothbrushes, dental floss, and toothpicks.
 
Cisplatin is contraindicated in patients with pre-existing renal impairment or renal failure due to cumulative nephrotoxicity. Patients with any underlying renal disease should receive cisplatin with caution. Serum creatinine, BUN, creatinine clearance, and serum electrolytes should be measured prior to initiating therapy and prior to each dose of cisplatin. Appropriate measures should be taken to keep serum fluid and electrolyte levels balanced. Hydration prior to and following cisplatin is critical in lessening the renal toxic effects; avoid dehydration. Generally, hydration for cisplatin doses = 100 mg/m2 are given, hydration with Dextrose 5%/0.9% Sodium Chloride at 150—250 ml/hr is recommended. Hypertonic saline solutions have been used as the vehicle for cisplatin doses >= 100 mg/m2. Patients who receive cisplatin without hydration have an increased incidence and severity of nephrotoxicity. It is also critical to maintain urine output of at least 100 ml/min for 24 hours following cisplatin therapy. Mannitol may be of benefit in maintaining urine output. Furosemide may be used in cardiac patients predisposed to heart failure due to the hydration regimen. Cisplatin therapy may worsen hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, or other electrolyte imbalances; these abnormalities should be corrected prior to the administration of cisplatin. Other agents that may cause nephrotoxicity should be avoided during or immediately following cisplatin chemotherapy (see Drug Interactions).
 
Hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, hypocalcemia, and decreased urine output may be indicative of cisplatin-induced tumor lysis syndrome (TLS). Appropriate measures (e.g. aggressive hydration and allopurinol) must be taken to prevent severe electrolyte imbalances and renal toxicity during and following chemotherapy administration in patients with large chemosensitive tumors (e.g., non-Hodgkin's lymphoma or small cell lung cancer).
 
Cisplatin is classified in FDA pregnancy risk category D. Cisplatin is mutagenic in bacteria and causes chromosomal aberrations in animal cells in tissue culture. Cisplatin is also embryotoxic and teratogenic in mice. Women of childbearing potential should be advised to avoid becoming pregnant during treatment with cisplatin. Use in pregnancy should be avoided except in cases where the potential benefits of cisplatin therapy outweigh the possible risks to the fetus.
 
Cisplatin has been detected in breast milk. Breast-feeding is not recommended during cisplatin therapy because of the potential risks to the infant.
 
Patients should be monitored carefully for extravasation of IV infusions. Cisplatin is considered an irritant; however, extravasation may cause necrosis if large amounts of highly concentrated cisplatin solutions are extravasated. If more than 20 ml of concentrated solution (> 0.5 mg/ml) is extravasated, therapy is required. Sodium thiosulfate 1/6 Molar has been reported to be effective in treating large cisplatin extravasations. Intramuscular administration and subcutaneous administration of cisplatin should be avoided due to the potential for severe local reactions.
 
Clinical experience suggests that elderly patients may be more susceptible to myelosuppression, infectious complications, and nephrotoxicity than younger patients. In trials of cisplatin in combination with cyclophosphamide or paclitaxel in the treatment of ovarian cancer, higher incidences of severe neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and leukopenia were seen in elderly compared with younger patients, although not in all cisplatin-containing treatment arms. In the two ovarian cancer trials where nonhematologic toxicity was evaluated according to age, elderly patients had a numerically higher incidence of peripheral neuropathy than younger patients. Other reported clinical experience suggests that elderly patients may be more susceptible to myelosuppression, infectious complications, peripheral neuropathy, and nephrotoxicity than younger patients. Because elderly patients are more likely to have decreased renal function, care should be taken in dose selection, and renal function should be monitored.
 
Use care to avoid accidental exposure to cisplatin during preparation, handling, and administration. The use of protective gowns, gloves and goggles is recommended. Following skin or ocular exposure, skin and eyes should be thoroughly rinsed.
 
Vaccination during chemotherapy or radiation therapy should be avoided because the antibody response is suboptimal. When chemotherapy is being planned, vaccination should precede the initiation of chemotherapy by >= 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Cross-reactivity between platinum-based antineoplastic agents has been reported; cases of severe hypersensitivity reactions have recurred after rechallenge with a different platinum agent.
    Headline
    bone marrow suppression
    General Information
    Cisplatin can cause severe bone marrow suppression with fatalities due to infection. Monitor complete blood counts before initiating therapy, prior to each new cycle, and as clinically indicated. An interruption of therapy, dose reduction, or discontinuation of therapy may be necessary for myelosuppression. Closely monitor patients for signs and symptoms of infection during and after treatment.
    Headline
    peripheral neuropathy
    General Information
    Use cisplatin with caution in patients with pre-existing peripheral neuropathy. Cisplatin can cause a dose-related peripheral neuropathy that becomes more severe with repeated courses of therapy; geriatric patients may be more susceptible. Neuropathy may progress after stopping treatment and may be irreversible in some patients. Perform a neurological examination prior to starting cisplatin therapy, at appropriate intervals during therapy, and after completion of therapy. Consider discontinuation of therapy in patients who develop symptomatic peripheral neuropathy.
    Headline
    dehydration, nephrotoxicity, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Cisplatin can cause severe nephrotoxicity including acute renal failure that becomes more prolonged and severe with repeated courses of treatment. Patients with baseline renal impairment, geriatric patients, patients with dehydration, and patients receiving concomitant therapy with other nephrotoxic medications may be more susceptible to cisplatin-related nephrotoxicity; patients with renal impairment may also be at increased risk of cisplatin-related ototoxicity. Consider alternative treatments or reduce the dose of cisplatin for patients with baseline renal impairment or who develop significant reductions in creatinine clearance during treatment. Ensure adequate hydration before, during, and after cisplatin administration. Monitor renal function and serum electrolytes including magnesium prior to initiating therapy and as clinically indicated; consider magnesium supplementation if appropriate.
    Headline
    chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting
    General Information
    Administer highly effective antiemetic therapy prior to treatment with cisplatin as it is highly emetogenic chemotherapy. Chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting occurs in almost all patients treated with cisplatin who do not receive antiemetic therapy and may require discontinuation of therapy.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Cisplatin can cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy, based on published human data. Advise pregnant people of the potential fetal risk. Cisplatin crosses the placenta. In utero exposure to cisplatin-containing chemotherapy has been associated with oligohydramnios, intrauterine growth restriction, and preterm birth. Reported neonatal outcomes include acute respiratory distress syndrome, cytopenias, and hearing loss. In animal studies, cisplatin caused teratogenic effects when administered during and after organogenesis. In murine models, placental transfer of cisplatin increased with placental maturation. The American Society of Clinical Oncology provides additional information on the use of certain medications for the management of cancer during pregnancy.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with cisplatin. Cisplatin can cause fetal harm. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking cisplatin and for 14 months after the last dose. For people who can cause pregnancy in others, contraception should be used during cisplatin treatment and for 11 months after the last dose. Both male and female infertility has been reported with cisplatin treatment, including cumulative dose-dependent ovarian failure and premature menopause and cumulative dose-dependent impairment of spermatogenesis (possibly irreversible oligospermia and azoospermia).
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking cisplatin, due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in the breastfed child. Limited data from published literature report the presence of cisplatin in human milk in low amounts.

Acyclovir: (Moderate) Closely monitor renal function if concomitant use with cisplatin and acyclovir is necessary. Cisplatin can cause nephrotoxicity. Acyclovir can cause renal impairment or renal failure, which may be additive when used with cisplatin.

Amphotericin B liposomal (LAmB): (Moderate) Closely monitor renal function if concomitant use with cisplatin and amphotericin B is necessary. Both drugs can cause nephrotoxicity, which may be additive when used together.
Amphotericin B: (Moderate) Closely monitor renal function if concomitant use with cisplatin and amphotericin B is necessary. Both drugs can cause nephrotoxicity, which may be additive when used together.
Arsenic Trioxide: (Major) Because electrolyte abnormalities increase the risk of QT interval prolongation and serious arrhythmias, avoid the concomitant use of arsenic trioxide with drugs that may cause electrolyte abnormalities, particularly hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia such as cisplatin. If concomitant drug use is unavoidable, frequently monitor serum electrolytes (and replace as necessary) and electrocardiograms. Additionally, closely monitor renal function if concomitant use is necessary. Both drugs can cause nephrotoxicity, which may be exacerbated with the use of additional nephrotoxins.
Bacillus Calmette-Guerin Vaccine, BCG: (Contraindicated) Do not administer live vaccines to cisplatin recipients; no data are available regarding the risk of secondary transmission of infection by live vaccines in patients receiving cisplatin. Cisplatin recipients may receive inactivated vaccines, but the immune response to vaccines or toxoids may be decreased.
Bacitracin: (Moderate) Closely monitor renal function if concomitant use with cisplatin and bacitracin is necessary. Both drugs can cause nephrotoxicity, which may be exacerbated with the use of additional nephrotoxins.
Bictegravir; Emtricitabine; Tenofovir Alafenamide: (Moderate) Concomitant use of cisplatin and tenofovir alafenamide may result in additive nephrotoxicity. Monitor for renal toxicity if concomitant use is required.
Bleomycin: (Moderate) Monitor for an increase in bleomycin-related adverse reactions if coadministration with cisplatin is necessary. Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The dose-limiting toxicity of cisplatin is nephrotoxicity, which may be acute or chronic. Acute renal failure, including azotemia, increased serum creatinine, and electrolyte abnormalities, may occur within 24 hours of administration in 5—10% of patients, especially in those patients with inadequate hydration. In patients receiving cisplatin in doses 400 mg/m2 have not consistently been associated with renal failure. The schedule (i.e., more frequently than every 3—4 weeks) and rate of cisplatin infusion (> 1 mg/min) may be associated with increased renal toxicity. Aggressive normal saline hydration pre- and post-cisplatin and adequate urine output of 100—150 ml/hr for 24 hours following cisplatin decrease the severity of nephrotoxicity.Hyperuricemia, which usually occurs 3—5 days following cisplatin, may indicate cisplatin induced renal toxicity. Cisplatin-induced hyperuricemia is associated with cisplatin doses > 50 mg/m2. Uric acid nephropathy and nephrolithiasis can occur secondary to hyperuricemia. Allopurinol therapy is effective in reducing serum uric acid levels in these patients. Other electrolyte disturbances, including hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hyponatremia, and hypophosphatemia, may occur 48—72 hours following cisplatin administration. Cases of tetany have been occasionally reported in patients with hypocalcemia and hypomagnesemia. Hypomagnesemia may persist for months to years after completing cisplatin therapy. Hyponatremia secondary to syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) has been reported. There is no evidence that electrolyte supplementation during cisplatin treatment decreases cisplatin-induced electrolyte imbalances. Serum electrolytes should be monitored and replaced intravenously as needed prior to and during cisplatin treatment.Cisplatin-induced cellular destruction can result in tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) which includes hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, hypocalcemia, and metabolic acidosis. These problems are more severe when a highly chemosensitive disease is treated (e.g., non-Hodgkin's lymphoma or small cell lung cancer). Aggressive alkalinization of the urine and use of allopurinol can prevent urate nephropathy; close monitoring of serum electrolytes and renal function is recommended.Cumulative ototoxicity consisting of abnormal audiograms occurs in up to 31% of patients treated with cisplatin and 10—15% of patients develop clinical hearing loss. Cisplatin-induced ototoxicity is manifested as tinnitus and/or hearing loss in the high frequency range (4,000—8,000 Hz). Deafness after the initial dose of cisplatin has been reported rarely. Decreased ability to hear normal conversation tones may occur occasionally. Hearing loss can be unilateral or bilateral. Ototoxicity appears to be associated with high peak cisplatin levels. Signs of ototoxicity may be more pronounced in children than in adults and in patients who receive cranial radiation. Vestibular dysfunction such as dizziness or nystagmus is rare, but has been reported.Neurotoxicity, usually manifested as a sensory peripheral neuropathy, can occur during cisplatin therapy. The neuropathies usually occur after 4—7 months of cisplatin treatment but neuropathies have been reported following the first dose of cisplatin or 3—8 weeks after the last dose of cisplatin. Neuropathy is, for the most part, reversible but occasionally can be irreversible. Usually the neuropathy appears distally on the fingers and toes, and then proceeds proximally toward the trunk. This is referred to as a "stocking-and-glove" pattern. Long-term survivors of testicular cancer treated with cisplatin, bleomycin and vinblastine report paresthesias, numbness, and cold sensitivity in fingers and toes up to 8 years after completing therapy. Other neurological symptoms include loss of motor function, areflexia, loss of proprioception, and aphasia. Cisplatin should be discontinued when these symptoms are first observed; however, symptoms may progress even after stopping therapy. Concomitant administration of other neurotoxic agents such as paclitaxel and vinca alkaloids may increase the severity of the neuropathy. A myasthenia-like syndrome, headache, seizures, dysgeusia consisting of a metallic taste, and loss of taste also can also occur. Muscle cramps defined as painful, localized, involuntary skeletal muscle contractions of sudden onset and short duration have been associated with high cummulative doses of cisplatin and advanced peripheral neuropathy.High doses of cisplatin are associated with blurred vision, which gradually resolves after discontinuation of therapy. Visual impairment consisting of altered color perception is also frequently reported and may be persistent even after therapy is discontinued. Altered color perception is manifested as the inability to discriminate colors in the blue-yellow axis. The only finding on fundoscopic exam is irregular retinal pigmentation of the macular area. Optic neuritis, papilledema, and cerebral blindness have been reported infrequently at standard cisplatin doses. Improvement and/or total recovery of visual impairment usually occurs after discontinuing cisplatin therapy.Cisplatin causes relatively less severe bone marrow suppression than do other antineoplastic drugs. Anemia, neutropenia and thrombocytopenia may occur between days 18—23 following cisplatin treatment and are associated with doses > 50 mg/m2. Fever and infection have been reported in patients with neutropenia. Coombs' positive hemolytic anemia has been reported. Patients with hemolytic anemia may experience increased hemolysis with subsequent treatment with cisplatin.Cisplatin is one of the most potent emetogenic antineoplastic agents used. Severe nausea/vomiting will occur in all patients not pre-treated with antiemetics. Despite premedication, discontinuation of the drug may occasionally be required. Acute nausea and vomiting may begin within 1—4 hours of treatment with cisplatin and last up to 24 hours. Delayed nausea/vomiting (beginning more than 24 hours following the administration of cisplatin) and anorexia occur frequently in patients in whom the acute symptoms are controlled. The mechanisms of acute and delayed nausea and vomiting differ. The acute effects are thought to be related to the stimulation of the vomiting center via the release of serotonin from the enterochromaffin cells in the GI tract. The cause of delayed nausea and vomiting is not known and appears to be unrelated to serotonin release. Patients should receive an aggressive antiemetic regimen including a serotonin antagonist and dexamethasone prior to treatment with cisplatin. Treatment with dexamethasone and a dopamine antagonist (e.g., metoclopramide) for 3—7 days after cisplatin will help prevent persistent nausea/vomiting or anorexia. Patients receiving continuous infusion cisplatin over several days may experience both acute and delayed nausea/vomiting.Anaphylactoid reactions have occurred during cisplatin therapy, and may manifest as facial edema, flushing, wheezing, tachycardia, and hypotension. These reactions usually happen in patients with prior exposure to platinum. There is an increasing incidence of hypersensitivity reactions in patients receiving repeated courses of cisplatin. Hypersensitivity usually develops after >= 6 courses of cisplatin. Reactions may occur up to 3 days after therapy and desensitization is not usually successful.Extravasation of cisplatin infusions may cause an injection site reaction including pain, erythema, phlebitis, cellulitis, and fibrosis of local tissue. Rarely, cisplatin has been associated with tissue necrosis after extravasation of >20 ml of concentrated (>0.5 mg/ml) solutions. Sodium thiosulfate 1/6 Molar has been effective in treating large extravastions.In the treatment of ovarian cancer, platinum-based therapy has been associated with an increased risk of secondary malignancy, primarily acute myelogenous leukemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, acute lymphocytic leukemia and chronic myelogenous leukemia. The risk of secondary malignancy increases with cumulative cisplatin doses >500 mg. However, in evaluating the risks versus benefits of platinum-based chemotherapy in the treatment of ovarian cancer, the survival benefits exceed the risk of secondary malignancy.Other adverse reactions reported with cisplatin include hiccups, elevated hepatic enzymes including hyperbilirubinemia, and alopecia. Alopecia is not as severe as that seen with other alkylating agents. Cardiac abnormalities including ST-T wave changes, bundle-branch block, atrial fibrillation, angina, myocardial infarction, and supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) have been reported infrequently with cisplatin.

Description
  Text
  Cisplatin is a non-cell-cycle specific alkylating agent with activity in many cancers. It is indicated for the treatment of advanced testicular cancer, advanced ovarian cancer, and advanced bladder cancer, but also has several off-label uses including head and neck cancer, lymphoma, lung cancers, and gynecological cancers. Cisplatin can cause severe nephrotoxicity including acute renal failure that becomes more prolonged and severe with repeated courses of treatment. Ensure adequate hydration before, during, and after administration; closely monitor renal function and serum electrolytes.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Cisplatin is an alkylating agent. In the blood, cisplatin is present in an inactive, uncharged state due to the high concentration of chloride ions. Intracellularly, cisplatin loses its two chloride groups and becomes a positively charged electrophilic compound. Cisplatin becomes activated once it enters the cell. It binds to the N7 reactive center on guanine and adenine in the cell nucleus to form interstrand and intrastrand crosslinks. This interferes with normal transcription and/or DNA replication mechanisms, causing DNA damage, blocking cell division, and resulting in apoptosis. While considered cell-cycle non-specific, cells are most sensitive to cisplatin in the G1-phase, just prior to the onset of DNA synthesis. Cisplatin exposure increases the duration of S-phase and causes a dose-dependent inhibition of cells in the G2-phase, resulting in cell cycle arrest and programmed cell death or apoptosis. Other mechanisms of cisplatin cytotoxicity include mitochondrial damage, decreased ATPase activity, and altered cellular transport mechanisms.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Intravenous Route
    Description
    Maximum red blood cell platinum concentrations are reached within 90 to 150 minutes after a 100 mg/m2 dose of cisplatin.
  Text
  Cisplatin is administered intravenously. Platinum from cisplatin, but not cisplatin itself, is bound to several plasma proteins including albumin, transferrin, and gamma globulin. Three hours after a bolus injection and 2 hours after the end of a 3-hour infusion, 90% of plasma platinum is protein bound. It exhibits reversible binding to plasma protein that is characteristic of normal drug-protein binding. The albumin-platinum complexes do not significantly dissociate and are slowly eliminated with a minimum half-life of at least 5 days. Platinum is present in tissues for up to 180 days after the last administration of cisplatin doses ranging from 20 mg/m2 to 120 mg/m2. Platinum tumor concentrations are somewhat lower than the concentrations in the organ where the tumor is located, except for intracerebral tumors. Hepatic metastases have the highest platinum concentrations, which are similar to the platinum concentrations in normal liver. The chlorine atoms of cisplatin are more subject to chemical displacement reactions by nucleophiles, such as water or sulfhydryl groups, than to enzyme-catalyzed metabolism. Cisplatin and monohydroxymonochloro cis-diamine platinum (II) are present in almost equal concentrations at physiological pH. Monohydroxymonochloro cis-diamine platinum (II), combined with possible direct displacement of chlorine atoms by sulfhydryl groups of amino acids or proteins, accounts for the instability of cisplatin in biological matrices. The plasma ratio of cisplatin to total free (ultrafilterable) platinum has considerable interpatient variability, ranging from 0.5 to 1.1 after a single dose of 100 mg/m2. After a 7 hour infusion, the cisplatin volume of distribution at steady-state was about 11 to 12 liters/m2.
 
After a 7 hour infusion, the total body clearance of cisplatin at steady-state was approximately 15 to 16 liters/hour/m2. The mean renal clearance of cisplatin was 50 to 62 mL/min/m2, exceeding creatinine clearance. The renal clearance of free (ultrafilterable) platinum also exceeds the glomerular filtration rate, indicating that cisplatin or other platinum-containing molecules are actively secreted by the kidneys. The renal clearance of free platinum is nonlinear and variable and is dependent on dose, urine flow rate, and individual variability in the extent of active secretion and possible tubular reabsorption. No significant relationships exist between the renal clearance of either free platinum or cisplatin and creatinine clearance. Cisplatin concentrations decrease monoexponentially with a half-life of approximately 20 to 30 minutes. Red blood cell platinum concentrations decline in a biphasic manner with a terminal half-life of 36 to 47 days. Cisplatin is excreted in the urine, with 13% to 17% of a 50 mg/m2 dose in the urine within 1 hour of administration. When a single dose of cisplatin (40 mg/m2 to 140 mg/m2) is given as an IV bolus or IV infusion (range, 1 hour to 24 hours), between 10% to 50% of the administered platinum is excreted in the urine within 24 hours, mostly within the first few hours; only a small percentage of the administered platinum is excreted beyond 24 hours. After 5 daily administrations of cisplatin (20 mg/m2 to 50 mg/m2), the mean urinary platinum recovery ranged from 14% to 30% of the dose. The mean urinary platinum recovery was 35% to 51% after 5 daily administrations of cisplatin 40 mg/m2 to 100 mg/m2.

    Description
    Cisplatin is administered intravenously but also may be administered intra-arterially for the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma.
Visually inspect parenteral products for particulate matter and discoloration prior to administration whenever solution and container permit.
Adequate renal and hematologic function should be established prior to administration.
Avoid the use of needles or IV sets with aluminum parts during cisplatin preparation and administration. Aluminum reacts with cisplatin to form a precipitate, causing loss of potency.
Due to the possibility of extravasation, closely monitor the infusion site during drug administration. The severity of local tissue toxicity appears to be related to the concentration of the solution; concentrations greater than 0.5 mg/mL may result in tissue cellulitis, fibrosis, necrosis, pain, edema, and erythema.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  No dosage adjustments are needed in patients with hepatic impairment.
  Renal Impairment
  No specific dosage guidelines have been established for cisplatin in patients with renal impairment, although the manufacturer recommends considering alternative treatments or reducing the dose of cisplatin for patients with baseline renal impairment or who develop significant treatment-related reactions in creatinine clearance (CrCL). The following recommendations have been made in the literature:
 
Kintzel, et al.:
CrCL 46 to 60 mL/min: Reduce cisplatin dose by 25%.
CrCL 31 to 45 mL/min: Reduce cisplatin dose by 50%.
CrCL 30 mL/min or less: Not recommended.
 
Pedrazzoli, et al.:
Hemodialysis: Reduce cisplatin dose by 50% to 75%; administer after hemodialysis.
