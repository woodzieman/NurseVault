Drug Class: Dexmedetomidine is an intravenous and sublingual relatively selective alpha2-adrenergic agonist sedative.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC), oral; Common adult doses: 1 mcg/kg IV loading dose and 1.5 mcg/kg/hour continuous IV infusion; 180 mcg as initial single dose maximum and do not exceed 360 mcg/day total for sublingual film.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): bradycardia, hypertension, hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, arrhythmia exacerbation, atrial fibrillation, av block, cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction, palpitations, qt prolongation, sinus tachycardia, supraventricular tachycardia (svt), ventricular tachycardia, agitation, anxiety, confusion, delirium, dizziness, drowsiness, dysarthria, hallucinations, headache, neuritis, seizures, gastroesophageal reflux, polydipsia, diabetes insipidus, oliguria, polyuria

In an Overdose: Five patients received an overdose of dexmedetomidine in ICU sedation clinical trials.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Caution should be exercised when administering dexmedetomidine to patients with bradycardia, advanced AV block, sick sinus syndrome, or other AV conduction abnormalities. Hypotension and bradycardia are frequently associated with dexmedetomidine infusion.

  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Intravenous Dexmedetomidine
Hemodynamic instability is more likely with bolus dosing; for this reason, loading doses of dexmedetomidine are often bypassed in critically ill individuals. If a loading dose is used, it must be administered over a minimum of 10 minutes.
Dexmedetomidine does not provide deep sedation and individuals may be arousable and alert when stimulated; this should not be considered lack of efficacy in the absence of other clinical signs and symptoms. Dexmedetomidine does not produce amnesia and is not appropriate in clinical situations where amnesia is required (e.g., during neuromuscular blockade).
Dexmedetomidine is not indicated for infusions lasting longer than 24 hours; however, longer infusions are commonly used in clinical practice.
Abrupt discontinuation of intravenous dexmedetomidine may produce a clonidine-like withdrawal syndrome. Monitor for withdrawal symptoms after the infusion is discontinued. If dexmedetomidine has been infused for more than 6 hours, monitor for at least 48 hours after infusion discontinuation.
Gradual tapering of the infusion or transitioning to oral or transdermal clonidine may aid in the prevention of withdrawal after prolonged infusion.
Sublingual Dexmedetomidine
The effectiveness of sublingual dexmedetomidine beyond 24 hours after the first dose has not been established. There may be a risk of tolerance and tachyphylaxis if sublingual dexmedetomidine is administered at higher doses or for a longer duration than were evaluated, or in a manner other than indicated.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Caution should be exercised when administering dexmedetomidine to patients with bradycardia, advanced AV block, sick sinus syndrome, or other AV conduction abnormalities. Hypotension and bradycardia are frequently associated with dexmedetomidine infusion. Because dexmedetomidine may augment bradycardia induced by vagal stimuli, the prophylactic use of IV administration of anticholinergic agents (e.g., atropine) should be considered to modify vagal tone. In clinical trials, atropine or glycopyrrolate were effective in the treatment of most episodes of dexmedetomidine-induced bradycardia. However, some patients required more advanced resuscitative measures. Clinically significant episodes of bradycardia and sinus arrest have been associated with dexmedetomidine administration in young, healthy volunteers with high vagal tone or with different routes of administration, including rapid intravenous or bolus administration. Caution should be used in administering dexmedetomidine to patients with hypotension, cerebrovascular disease, or cardiac disease (including advanced heart block, heart failure, or severe ventricular dysfunction) due to the potential for hypotensive effects, which can worsen these conditions. Hypotension may aggravate ischemia in patients with coronary artery disease or cerebrovascular disease precipitating a myocardial infarction, angina, or cerebrovascular accident. Dexmedetomidine should be used with caution in patients with aortic stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. As with other hypotensive agents, dexmedetomidine should be given with caution to patients with obstruction in the outflow tract of the left ventricle.Because dexmedetomidine decreases sympathetic nervous system activity, hypotension and/or bradycardia may be expected to be more pronounced in patients with hypovolemia, diabetes mellitus, or chronic hypertension. Caution should also be used in patients with uncontrolled hypertension; transient hypertension has been observed primarily during the loading dose in association with the initial peripheral vasoconstrictive effects of dexmedetomidine and may be associated with the rate of infusion.Although not specifically studied, abrupt discontinuation of dexmedetomidine could potentially lead to withdrawal symptoms similar to those reported for another alpha-2-adrenergic agent, clonidine. These symptoms may include nervousness, agitation, and headaches, accompanied or followed by a rapid rise in blood pressure and elevated catecholamine concentrations in the plasma. The use of dexmedetomidine is limited to durations < 24 hours.Dexmedetomidine should be used with caution in adrenal insufficiency. Dexmedetomidine had no effect on ACTH-stimulated cortisol release in dogs after a single dose; however, after the subcutaneous infusion of dexmedetomidine for one week, the cortisol response to ACTH was diminished by approximately 40%.Dexmedetomidine clearance decreases with the severity of hepatic impairment. Although dexmedetomidine is generally dosed to effect, dose reduction should be considered in patients with impaired hepatic function due to hepatic disease (see Dosage and Pharmacokinetics).Metabolites of dexmedetomidine are known to be substantially excreted by the kidney, and the risk of adverse reactions may be greater in patients with impaired renal function. Dexmedetomidine pharmacokinetic values are not significantly different in subjects with severe renal impairment (creatinine clearance < 30 ml/min) compared to healthy subjects. However, the pharmacokinetic values of dexmedetomidine metabolites have not been evaluated in renal dysfunction, and about 28% of dexmedetomidine's metabolites and their potential activity have not been identified. Increased sedation was found in a small study of 5 patients with a mean CrCl of 20.9 +/- 9.3 ml/minute. After receipt of dexmedetomidine 0.6 mcg/kg given over 10 minutes, the mean sedation score on a 0—100 visual analog scale (0 is very alert, and 100 is very sedated) was 50 +/-29.4. In contrast, the mean sedation score was 17.8 +/-21.7 for 5 patients with a mean CrCl of 122.1 +/- 30.8 ml/minute. Dosage reduction may be considered due to the potential for metabolite accumulation during dexmedetomidine infusions in patients with significant renal disease or renal failure (See Dosage and Pharmacokinetics). Of note, a higher dexmedetomidine exposure and higher rescue sedative and analgesic doses were required to maintain an adequate Ramsay sedation score in a limited number of adults on continuous venovenous dialytic therapy. Of 20 adults, 5 had acute renal failure and required continuous venovenous dialytic therapy. The median total dexmedetomidine infusion dose over the first 24 hours was 13.6 mcg/kg (range, 11.8—15.3) in the patients who required dialysis. In contrast, the median total infusion dose over the first 24 hours was 9.9 mcg/kg (range, 5.3—16) in the other 15 patients of whom 4 had acute renal failure.The pharmacokinetic profile of dexmedetomidine is not altered by age. However, in elderly patients, the bradycardic and hypotensive effects of dexmedetomidine are more pronounced. Also, elderly patients are more likely to have decreased renal function. Therefore, a dose reduction and renal function assessment may be considered in elderly patients.Dexmedetomidine is classified as a pregnancy category C drug by the FDA. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women. Dexmedetomidine should be used during pregnancy only if the potential benefits justify the potential risk to the fetus. Placental transfer of dexmedetomidine has been observed when dexmedetomidine was administered subcutaneously to pregnant rats. The safety of dexmedetomidine during labor and obstetric delivery has not been studied. Therefore, dexmedetomidine is not recommended during labor and obstetric delivery, including cesarean section deliveries.It is not known whether dexmedetomidine is excreted in human milk. Dexmedetomidine administered subcutaneously to lactating female rats was excreted in milk. Because many drugs are excreted in human milk, caution should be exercised when dexmedetomidine is administered to a woman who is breast-feeding.The safety and efficacy of dexmedetomidine have not been established in children < 18 years old; however, data are available (see Dosage). Although dexmedetomidine appears to be effective for sedation in children based on limited data, 4 of 10 infants and children who started with a dexmedetomidine infusion of 0.25 mcg/kg/hour and 2 of 10 infants and children who started with a dexmedetomidine infusion of 0.5 mcg/kg/hour had a Ramsay score of 1 (inadequate sedation) at various assessment points over a 24-hour period. Of note, 5 of the 6 children with a Ramsay score of 1 were less than 12 months of age.

    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hypotension, hypovolemia, second or third-degree AV block, syncope, vagotonia, ventricular dysfunction
    General Information
    Because dexmedetomidine decreases sympathetic nervous system activity, hypotension or bradycardia may be expected to be more pronounced in individuals with hypovolemia, diabetes mellitus, or chronic hypertension. Use intravenous dexmedetomidine with caution in individuals with advanced heart block (i.e., second or third-degree AV block) or severe ventricular dysfunction. Additionally, bradycardia and sinus arrest have occurred in young healthy volunteers with high vagal tone (vagotonia) or with different routes of administration (e.g., rapid intravenous or bolus administration). Consider an anticholinergic (e.g., glycopyrrolate, atropine) to modify vagal tone. Because dexmedetomidine has the potential to augment bradycardia induced by vagal stimuli, clinicians should be prepared to intervene. More advanced resuscitative measures may be required in individuals with significant cardiovascular dysfunction. Avoid sublingual dexmedetomidine use in individuals with hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, advanced heart block, severe ventricular dysfunction, or a history of syncope. Due to risk of hypotension, additional half-doses are not recommended in individuals with systolic blood pressure (SBP) less than 90 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) less than 60 mmHg, heart rate less than 60 beats per minute, or postural decrease in SBP of 20 mmHg or more or DBP of 10 mmHg or more.
    Headline
    bradycardia, cardiac arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy, congenital long QT syndrome, coronary artery disease, females, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, QT prolongation
    General Information
    Avoid use of sublingual dexmedetomidine in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk. Use intravenous dexmedetomidine with caution in these populations. Additional risk factors for torsades de pointes or sudden death with dexmedetomidine may include a history of other cardiac arrhythmias.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Use dexmedetomidine during human pregnancy only if the potential benefits justify the potential risk to the fetus.There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of IV dexmedetomidine use in pregnant women. There are no available data on the use of dexmedetomidine sublingual film in pregnant women to evaluate for a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage or other adverse maternal or fetal effects. In an in vitro human placenta study, placental transfer of dexmedetomidine occurred. Available data from published randomized controlled trials and case reports over several decades of IV dexmedetomidine use during pregnancy have not identified a drug-associated risk of major birth defects or miscarriage; however, the reported exposures occurred after the first trimester. Most of the available data are based on use at the time of cesarean-section delivery, and these studies have not identified an adverse effect on maternal outcomes or infant Apgar scores. In animal reproduction studies, fetal toxicity occurred in the presence of maternal toxicity with subcutaneous administration of dexmedetomidine to pregnant rats during organogenesis at doses 5 times the maximum recommended human dose [MRHD] of 360 mcg/day based on mg/m2 body surface area (BSA). Increased post-implantation losses and reduced live fetuses occurred with subcutaneous administration of dexmedetomidine to pregnant rats during organogenesis at doses 1.8 times the MRHD of 17.8 mcg/kg/day based on BSA. Adverse developmental effects, including early implantation loss and decreased viability of second generation offspring, occurred when pregnant rats were subcutaneously administered doses less than or equal to the MRHD based on mg/m2 from late pregnancy through lactation and weaning. The safety of dexmedetomidine during labor and obstetric delivery has not been studied.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class A, Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Consider dose reduction of intravenous dexmedetomidine in individuals with impaired hepatic function as dexmedetomidine clearance decreases with severity of hepatic failure. Sublingual dexmedetomidine dosage reduction is recommended in individuals with hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A, Child-Pugh class B, and Child-Pugh class C).
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    There is no information regarding the effects of dexmedetomidine on the breast-fed child or the effects on milk production; caution is advised when dexmedetomidine is used during breast-feeding. Available published literature report the presence of dexmedetomidine in human milk after IV administration. Consider the developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding along with the mother's clinical need for dexmedetomidine and any potential adverse effects on the breast-fed child from dexmedetomidine or the underlying maternal condition. Advise persons who resume breast-feeding after recent exposure to dexmedetomidine sublingual film to monitor the infant for irritability. To minimize potential drug exposure to the breast-fed infant, a lactating person may consider pumping and discarding breast milk for 10 hours (approximately 5 half-lives) after receiving dexmedetomidine. The relative infant dose of dexmedetomidine was estimated to range from 0.02% to 0.098% after 6 mcg/kg/hour IV for 10 minutes after delivery followed by 0.2 to 0.7 mcg/kg/hour continuous IV infusion. The milk-to-plasma ratio from single paired maternal milk and plasma concentrations at each measurement time point (0, 6, 12, and 24 hours after dexmedetomidine discontinuation) ranged from 0.53 to 0.95. After discontinuation, plasma and milk concentrations were detectable up to 6 hours in most subjects, up to 12 hours in 1 subject, and undetectable in all subjects at 24 hours.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Advise individuals to avoid driving or operating machinery or participating in activities requiring coordination and concentration for at least 8 hours after receiving sublingual dexmedetomidine. Dexmedetomidine can cause somnolence and may impair the ability to perform tasks that require complex motor and mental skills.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Consider dose reduction of intravenous dexmedetomidine in geriatric adults. A higher incidence of bradycardia and hypotension was observed with intravenous dexmedetomidine in subjects older than 65 years in clinical trials. Lower sublingual dexmedetomidine doses are recommended in geriatric adults. Because dexmedetomidine decreases sympathetic nervous system activity, hypotension and/or bradycardia may be more pronounced in geriatric adults. Additionally, geriatric age may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes with dexmedetomidine.

  General Information
  Adverse event information is reported from the placebo-controlled, continuous infusion trials of dexmedetomidine for sedation in the ICU setting (387 adults received dexmedetomidine). Overall, the most frequently observed treatment-emergent adverse events for dexmedetomidine versus placebo included hypotension (28% vs 13%), hypertension (16% vs 18%), nausea/vomiting (11%/4% vs 9%/6%), sinus bradycardia (7% vs 3%), fever (5% vs 4%), hypoxia (4% vs 4%), sinus tachycardia (3% vs 5%), and anemia (3% vs 2%). Aggravated hypertension occurred in <=1% of patients receiving dexmedetomidine. Transient hypertension was observed primarily during the loading dose in association with the initial peripheral vasoconstrictive effects of dexmedetomidine. Transient hypertension was generally treated by reducing the infusion rate of dexmedetomidine. The mean heart rate among children 1—7 years of age who received dexmedetomidine 1 mcg/kg over 10 minutes followed by a continuous infusion of 0.5 mcg/kg/hour fell from 110 +/- 12.09 beats/minute to 96 +/- 11.42 beats/minute at 50 minutes after drug initiation. Bradycardia did not occur in any of the 30 children. If medical intervention is required for hypotension or bradycardia induced by alpha-2 agonism, treatment may include decreasing or stopping the infusion of dexmedetomidine, IV fluid resuscitation, elevation of the lower extremities, and the use of pressor agents. Clinically significant episodes of bradycardia and sinus arrest have been associated with dexmedetomidine administration in young, healthy volunteers with high vagal tone or with different routes of administration, including rapid intravenous or bolus administration. Dexmedetomidine may augment bradycardia induced by vagal stimuli; and therefore, IV administration of anticholinergic agents (e.g., atropine) should be considered to modify vagal tone. In clinical trials, atropine or glycopyrrolate were effective in the treatment of most episodes of dexmedetomidine-induced bradycardia. However, in some patients with significant cardiovascular dysfunction, more advanced resuscitative measures were required.Other less frequent (< 5%) adverse effects of dexmedetomidine that occurred more often than with placebo included pleural effusion (2% vs 1%), oliguria (2% vs < 1%), xerostomia (3% vs 1%), atrial fibrillation (4% vs 3%), and thirst (2% vs < 1%).In addition to the more frequent occurrence of hypotension, bradycardia, and tachycardia in patients receiving dexmedetomidine, other cardiovascular disorders occurring in <= 1% of patients receiving dexmedetomidine include AV block, cardiac arrest, extrasystoles, atrial fibrillation, T wave inversion, supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), atrial tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, and ventricular arrhythmia (unspecified).Infrequent, but clinically relevant systemic adverse events reported in 1% dexmedetomidine patients included diaphoresis, hypovolemia, light anesthesia, and rigors.Central nervous system effects occurring in <= 1% of patients receiving dexmedetomidine in ICU sedation studies included agitation, confusion, delirium, dizziness, hallucinations or illusions, headache, neuralgia, neuritis, lethargy, and a speech disorder (unspecified).Infrequent (<=1%) gastrointestinal adverse effects included abdominal pain and diarrhea. The maximum blood concentration documented was approximately 13 times the upper limit of the therapeutic range. Notable adverse effects observed in two subjects who achieved the highest doses were first degree AV block and second degree heart block; no hemodynamic compromise was noted with the AV block and the heart block resolved spontaneously within one minute. Five patients received an overdose of dexmedetomidine in ICU sedation clinical trials. Two of these patients had no symptoms reported; one patient received a 2 mcg/kg loading dose over 10 minutes (twice the recommended loading dose) and one patient received a maintenance infusion of 0.8 mcg/kg/hr. Maximal reductions in BP and HR were observed 2 hours post-sublingual or buccal dosing, and most patients experienced some effect on BP or HR within 24 hours of dosing.

    General Information

    adrenocortical insufficiency
    General Information
    Dexmedetomidine IV infusion may cause adrenocortical insufficiency because it is structurally similar to etomidate. While in vivo and in vitro animal studies have demonstrated decreased cortisol concentrations and blunted cortisol response to adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH) stimulation after dexmedetomidine administration, human studies have shown mixed results. Transient adrenocortical insufficiency has been described in a single case report. A 1-year-old, 10 kg, previously healthy boy presenting to the hospital with 24% total body surface area second degree burns was emergently intubated. Initially, the patient required a combination of sedative and analgesic medications including fentanyl, morphine, and ketamine infusions, as well as scheduled lorazepam and methadone. In order to wean his sedation regimen, dexmedetomidine was initiated at a rate of 0.5 mcg/kg/hour on hospital day 8; the infusion was eventually titrated up to 2.7 mcg/kg/hour on day 10, and clonidine was added. The dexmedetomidine infusion was discontinued on hospital day 15 after 6.5 days of continuous infusion. On hospital day 18, the patient was diagnosed with adrenocortical insufficiency, presenting with lethargy, hypotension, tachycardia, low cortisol (0.4 mcg/dL), and blunted cortisol response. A septic workup was unremarkable. Hydrocortisone was initiated and the patient was discharged after 23 days of hospitalization. Repeat cortisol and ACTH stimulation test 2 months after discharge showed a complete resolution of adrenal insufficiency.

    Headline
    withdrawal
    General Information
    Withdrawal symptoms including agitation, nervousness, sleeplessness, headache, tremor, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, tachycardia, and hypertension can occur with discontinuation of dexmedetomidine IV infusion. The primary manifestations of dexmedetomidine withdrawal after IV infusion involve the central nervous system (e.g., agitation, hypertonicity, tonic-clonic movements). During adult clinical trials, IV administration of dexmedetomidine for up to 7 days, regardless of dose, resulted in 5% and 3% of subjects experiencing at least 1 withdrawal event in the first 24 hours and 24 to 48 hours after drug discontinuation, respectively. Mild transient withdrawal symptoms of emergence delirium or agitation occurred in 3 pediatric patients after discontinuation of short-term (less than 2 hours) dexmedetomidine IV infusions (0.5 to 1.5 mcg/kg/hour) in procedural sedation clinical studies (n = 122). Monitor for withdrawal symptoms after the infusion is discontinued. If dexmedetomidine has been infused IV for more than 6 hours, monitor for at least 48 hours after infusion discontinuation. Gradual tapering of the infusion or transitioning to oral or transdermal clonidine may aid in the prevention of withdrawal after prolonged IV administration. Initiate supportive care if withdrawal symptoms occur. Withdrawal symptoms reported with dexmedetomidine sublingual film in published literature include cardiovascular symptoms, such as hypertension, tachycardia, arrhythmias, and in severe cases, hypertensive crisis; neurological symptoms, such as agitation, anxiety, tremors, seizures, and altered mental status; and autonomic symptoms such as diaphoresis, nausea, vomiting, hyperthermia, and mydriasis. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multiple ascending dose study assessing dexmedetomidine sublingual film at doses up to 120 mcg per day administered over 7 days in healthy volunteers, elevated heart rate and systolic blood pressure and vomiting occurred at a higher frequency upon abrupt discontinuation of 120 mcg doses of dexmedetomidine sublingual film as compared to placebo. There may be a risk of physical dependence and a withdrawal syndrome if dexmedetomidine sublingual film is used in a manner other than indicated. Withdrawal symptoms have been reported in pediatric patients who received another dexmedetomidine product intravenously for another indication. Dexmedetomidine sublingual film is not approved for pediatric use.

Description
  Text
  Dexmedetomidine is an intravenous and sublingual relatively selective alpha2-adrenergic agonist sedative. The intravenous formulation is indicated in adults for intensive care sedation and for procedural sedation prior to and/or during surgical and other procedures. The intravenous formulation is indicated in pediatric patients for procedural sedation prior to and/or during non-invasive procedures. Dexmedetomidine has centrally mediated sedative, analgesic/opioid-sparing, and sympatholytic properties. It has similar pharmacological effects to clonidine, with a greater affinity for the alpha2-receptor. Sedation from dexmedetomidine often results in a patient who is tranquil yet easily aroused, making it an ideal sedative agent when neurologic examination is required. Short half-life, limited effects on respiratory drive, and reduced analgesic and anesthetic requirements make it an attractive agent in many clinical situations; however, dexmedetomidine does not have amnestic properties and should not be used as the sole sedative in paralyzed patients. Nonbenzodiazepine sedatives like dexmedetomidine are recommended over benzodiazepines in non-cardiac surgery, mechanically ventilated patients. Dexmedetomidine is a first line sedative in the intubated burn patient, and is recommended for adjunct pain management, particularly if the patient is showing signs of withdrawal or prominent anxiety. When compared to dexmedetomidine, benzodiazepines are associated with a longer time to light sedation, longer duration of mechanical ventilation, and higher incidence of delirium in critical care patients. Dexmedetomidine is associated with dose-related hypotension and bradycardia that is more likely to occur during the intravenous loading phase. Abrupt discontinuation can result in withdrawal symptoms. The sublingual formulation of dexmedetomidine is indicated for the acute treatment of agitation in adult patients with schizophrenia or bipolar I or II disorders. Dexmedetomidine film may cause sedation, dizziness, or hypotension; patients should be under close supervision of a health care provider, with vitals and alertness assessed prior to ambulation to ensure minimal risk of falls or syncope.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Dexmedetomidine is a relatively selective, centrally-acting, alpha2-adrenergic agonist with sedative, analgesic, and sympatholytic properties but without significant ventilatory effects. Selectivity for the alpha2-receptors is observed in animals after slow intravenous (IV) infusion of dosages ranging from 10 to 300 mcg/kg. Both alpha1 and alpha2 activity is observed after slow IV infusion of very high doses (1,000 mcg/kg or more) or with rapid IV administration. Dexmedetomidine is 8 times more specific for alpha2-receptors than clonidine; activity ratios of alpha2:alpha1 activity are 1,620:1 and 220:1 for dexmedetomidine and clonidine, respectively.
 
In general, presynaptic activation of alpha2-receptors inhibits the release of norepinephrine and terminates the propagation of pain signals. Postsynaptic activation of alpha2-receptors in the central nervous system (CNS) inhibits sympathetic activity and therefore decreases blood pressure and heart rate. Together, the presynaptic and postsynaptic actions produce sedation, analgesia, and anxiolysis. The analgesic effect of dexmedetomidine is not clearly understood. It is believed that the drug may somehow regulate the transmission of nociceptive signals at both CNS and peripheral sites. In addition, it is known that alpha2-agonism activates G1-protein-gated potassium channels, preventing neuronal firing, and reduces G0-protein-mediated calcium conductance into the cells, inhibiting neurotransmitter release. High densities of alpha2-receptors are found in the locus coeruleus, a predominate noradrenergic center in the brain, which not only modulates alertness but is the site of origin for the descending medullospinal noradrenergic pathway, an important modulator of nociceptive transmission. Dexmedetomidine also directly stimulates alpha2-receptors in the spinal cord, inhibiting nociceptive neuronal firing and the release of substance P.
 
Alpha2-receptor agonists reduce blood pressure, heart rate, and plasma catecholamine concentrations; hypotension and bradycardia occur in a dose-dependent manner. Rapid injection of dexmedetomidine activates alpha2-receptors in peripheral blood vessels, resulting in transient hypertension and significant reflex bradycardia. Dexmedetomidine does not alter respiratory rate or oxygen saturation at the recommended dosage. The sympathetic effects of dexmedetomidine may attenuate myocardial oxygen requirements and myocardial oxygen consumption, as well as increase endocardial/epicardial blood flow ratios. Other physiologic responses to alpha2-receptor activation outside the CNS include decreased salivation, secretion, and bowel motility in the gastrointestinal tract; contraction of vascular and smooth muscle; inhibition of renin release, increased glomerular filtration, and increased sodium and water secretion in the kidney; decreased intraocular pressure; and decreased release of insulin from the pancreas.

    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    Protein binding and clearance values are significantly lower in patients with hepatic impairment.     Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    Dexmedetomidine pharmacokinetics (Cmax, Tmax, AUC, half-life, clearance, Vd) were not significantly different in patients with severe renal dysfunction (CrCl less than 30 mL/minute) compared to healthy subjects in clinical trials. However, in a small study of 12 adults, those with renal failure (mean CrCl 20.9 +/- 9.3 mL/minute) had a significantly shorter half-life and increased sedation scores compared to the control group (mean CrCl 122.1 +/- 30.8 mL/minute). After receipt of dexmedetomidine 0.6 mcg/kg given intravenously over 10 minutes, half-life in the renal failure group was 113.4 minutes, compared to 136.5 minutes in the control group. Though protein binding was not specifically measured, protein binding of the drug (as often seen in renal dysfunction) may have resulted in more profound central nervous system effects and increased availability of the drug for elimination.

    Description
    Children and Adolescents
A pooled analysis of 4 pediatric pharmacokinetic studies revealed clearance estimates of 10.76, 15.89, and 24.45 L/hour for children 12 to 23 months, 2 to 5 years, and 6 to 16 years, respectively. Corresponding Vd estimates for these populations were 7.35, 13.78, and 24.47 L, respectively.
 
Infants
The clearance of dexmedetomidine in infants is considerably reduced compared to older populations. This is attributable to immature hepatic microsomal enzyme pathways. Clearance at birth is approximately 42% of adult values, reaching 72% by 6 months, and 85% by 12 months of age. In a phase I pharmacokinetic study (n = 20; postmenstrual age [PMA]: 33 to 61 weeks; weight: 2 to 6 kg) where the median maximum dexmedetomidine IV infusion rate was 1.8 mcg/kg/hour, the estimated clearance and Vd were 0.87 to 2.65 L/kg/hour and 1.5 L/kg, respectively. In another pooled analysis of 4 pediatric pharmacokinetic studies, clearance estimates were 6.94 and 8.15 L/hour for infants 1 to 5 months and 6 to 11 months, respectively. Corresponding Vd estimates for these populations were 4.34 and 7.29 L, respectively.
 
Premature and Term Neonates
Premature neonates and, to a lesser extent, term neonates have decreased protein binding, a larger Vd, decreased clearance, increased half-life, and increased AUC values when compared to older children and adults. Decreased protein binding capacity and an immature hepatic system allow for increased unbound dexmedetomidine in premature neonates; this may contribute significantly to increased half-life and AUC values. Overall, premature neonates (n = 18; gestational age: 28 to 35 weeks) appeared to have lower clearance (0.3 vs. 0.9 L/kg/hour), increased elimination half-life (7.6 vs. 3.2 hours), and increased AUC (2,049 vs. 357 pg x hour/mL per mcg) compared to term neonates (n = 24; gestational age: 36 to 44 weeks) receiving dexmedetomidine for at least 6 hours. Vd was also significantly different, with mean values of 2.7 and 3.8 L/kg for premature and term neonates, respectively. Clearance in term neonates is approximately 42% of adult values. In a phase I pharmacokinetic study (n = 20; postmenstrual age [PMA]: 33 to 61 weeks; weight: 2 to 6 kg) where the median maximum dexmedetomidine IV infusion rate was 1.8 mcg/kg/hour, the estimated clearance and Vd were 0.87 to 2.65 L/kg/hour and 1.5 L/kg, respectively. Younger PMA was a significant predictor of lower clearance, which increased by 4.5% per 1-week increase in PMA at median PMA (44 weeks). Dexmedetomidine is highly lipid-soluble. The immature blood-brain barrier of the neonate may potentially facilitate higher cerebrospinal fluid drug concentrations, resulting in increased sedative effects. Thus, lower doses than those recommended in other populations should be considered initially.

In a pharmacokinetic study of 36 open-heart surgery patients (aged 1 to 24 months), clearance of dexmedetomidine was greater (mean increase 22%, range 1% to 49%) in patients with single-ventricle physiology and inversely related to the duration of cardiopulmonary bypass.   Text
  Dexmedetomidine is administered by intravenous infusion or as an orally dissolving film that is administered sublingually or buccally. It is rapidly distributed with a distribution half-life of 6 minutes and a large volume of distribution (118 L in adults at steady-state). Plasma protein binding is 94%. Dexmedetomidine undergoes almost complete biotransformation with minimal unchanged drug excreted in urine and feces. Biotransformation involves direct N-glucuronidation, aliphatic hydroxylation (mediated primarily by CYP2A6), and N-methylation. About 95% of the dose is recovered in the urine (85% within 24 hours) and 4% in the feces. Pharmacokinetic parameters (Vd, half-life, and clearance) are similar between IV continuous infusion dosage ranges of 0.2 to 0.7 mcg/kg/hour for 24 hours or less and 0.2 to 1.4 mcg/kg/hour for more than 24 hours in adults. Terminal elimination half-life of the intravenous injection is approximately 2 hours, and clearance is estimated to be approximately 39 L/hour in adults. The mean terminal elimination half-life of dexmedetomidine is approximately 2.8 hours following sublingual or buccal administration.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2A6
Biotransformation of dexmedetomidine involves both direct glucuronidation and cytochrome P450 mediated metabolism. Dexmedetomidine undergoes aliphatic hydroxylation mediated primarily by CYP2A6 with a minor role of CYP1A2, CYP2E1, CYP2D6, and CYP2C19; however, clinically significant cytochrome P450-mediated drug interactions are not expected.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  IV dexmedetomidine: Consider initial dosage reduction in patients with hepatic impairment and titrate to effect.
 
Sublingual film (Igalmi):
Mild to moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh Class A or B): Reduce initial dose to 90 mcg for mild to moderate agitation or 120 mcg for severe agitation. Repeat doses should be limited to 60 mcg regardless of severity of hepatic impairment or agitation.
Severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh Class C): Reduce initial dose to 60 mcg for mild to moderate agitation or 90 mcg for severe agitation. Repeat doses should be limited to 60 mcg regardless of severity of hepatic impairment or agitation.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
