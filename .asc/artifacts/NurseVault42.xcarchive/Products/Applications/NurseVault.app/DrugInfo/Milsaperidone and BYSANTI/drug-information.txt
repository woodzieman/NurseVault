Drug Class: Milsaperidone is an oral atypical antipsychotic that is the major active metabolite of iloperidone.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 24 mg/day PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): amnesia, dizziness, drowsiness, fatigue, headache, lethargy, nystagmus, paresthesias, restless legs syndrome (rls), restlessness, confusion, delirium, depression, emotional lability, hostility, impulse control symptoms, libido decrease, mania, orgasm dysfunction, paranoia, polydipsia, fecal incontinence, hypersalivation, oral ulceration, peptic ulcer, hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, syncope, arrhythmia exacerbation, av block

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Milsaperidone is an active metabolite of iloperidone.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, obesity
    General Information
    Milsaperidone, like other atypical antipsychotic agents, may cause metabolic changes. Metabolic changes are of particular concern in patients with pre-existing risk factors, such as diabetes mellitus and/or obesity. Increased blood sugar, worsening or new-onset diabetes and weight gain may occur during therapy. While all medications in the atypical antipsychotic class have been shown to produce some metabolic changes, each drug has its own specific risk profile. Metabolic changes may increase cardiovascular or cerebrovascular risk over time. Hyperglycemia, sometimes associated with diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar coma, or death, has been reported in people treated with atypical antipsychotics. Monitor weight and for symptoms of hyperglycemia. Regularly monitor patients with established diabetes mellitus for changes in weight and worsening of glucose control during atypical antipsychotic treatment. In those with risk factors for diabetes mellitus (e.g., obesity, family history of diabetes), perform fasting blood glucose testing at the beginning of treatment. In patients who develop symptoms of hyperglycemia during treatment, order fasting blood glucose testing. In some cases, hyperglycemia has resolved when the antipsychotic is discontinued. However, some patients require continuation of antidiabetic agents despite discontinuation of the suspect drug.
    Headline
    hyperlipidemia
    General Information
    Milsaperidone like other atypical antipsychotic agents, may cause metabolic changes. Metabolic changes are of particular concern in those with pre-existing risk factors, such as hyperlipidemia (e.g., hypercholesterolemia or hypertriglyceridemia). While all medications in the atypical antipsychotic class have been shown to produce some metabolic changes, each drug has its own specific risk profile. Dyslipidemia (increased cholesterol and/or triglycerides) may occur during therapy. Metabolic changes may increase cardiovascular or cerebrovascular risk over time. Clinical monitoring of serum fasting lipids is recommended at baseline and frequently throughout treatment with milsaperidone. Inform all patients of the importance of physical activity and maintaining a nutritionally balanced diet during treatment.
    Headline
    cardiovascular disease, hypovolemia
    General Information
    Milsaperidone should be used with caution in patients with known cardiovascular disease (e.g., history of myocardial infarction or ischemic heart disease, heart failure or conduction abnormalities), cerebrovascular disease, or conditions which would predispose patients to hypotension (advanced age, dehydration, hypovolemia, and treatment with antihypertensive medications). Milsaperidone can cause orthostatic hypotension and syncope; risks are increased with rapid dose escalations and in patients with underlying risk factors. Orthostatic hypotension from antipsychotic use may lead to falls with the potential for fractures and other injuries; a fall risk assessment should be completed and reassessed as needed in patients with conditions, diseases, or concurrent medication use that could exacerbate orthostasis.
    Headline
    bradycardia, cardiomyopathy, congenital long QT syndrome, coronary artery disease, females, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, QT prolongation
    General Information
    Avoid use of milsaperidone in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk.   Additionally, avoid milsaperidone use in people with a genetic predisposition for QTc interval prolongation.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Milsaperidone may cause somnolence, postural hypotension, motor and sensory instability, which may lead to falls and, consequently, fractures or other injuries. These effects may be most evident in the initial days of treatment or during rapid dose titration. For people with diseases, conditions, or medications that could exacerbate these effects, complete fall risk assessments when initiating antipsychotic treatment and periodically during long-term antipsychotic therapy. Milsaperidone may have the potential to impair judgment, thinking, or motor skills; advise patients about driving or operating machinery or other activities requiring coordination and concentration until they are reasonably certain that milsaperidone does not affect them adversely. Given the primary CNS effects of milsaperidone, use caution during coadministration with other CNS depressants and alcohol. Ethanol ingestion may further impair cognitive and motor skills and patients should be advised to avoid the use of alcoholic beverages when taking milsaperidone.
    Headline
    Parkinson disease
    General Information
    Avoid use of milsaperidone in people with Parkinson disease unless the benefit clearly outweighs the risk of worsening motor symptoms. Atypical antipsychotics can worsen the motor symptoms of Parkinson disease and can also lead to the development of extrapyramidal symptoms.
    Headline
    leukopenia
    General Information
    Use milsaperidone with caution in those with a history of leukopenia. Hematologic effects including leukopenia, neutropenia, and agranulocytosis have been associated with antipsychotic use. A history of drug-induced leukopenia or neutropenia or pre-existing low white blood cell (WBC) count may increase the likelihood of developing hematologic effects during treatment with an antipsychotic medication. Patients with a history of clinically significant low WBC count or drug-induced leukopenia/neutropenia should have frequent complete blood count (CBC) assessments during the first few months of treatment. Consider discontinuation of the antipsychotic if a clinically significant decline in WBC occurs in the absence of an identifiable cause. Monitor patients with clinically significant neutropenia closely for fever and infection; institute appropriate medical intervention if necessary. Milsaperidone should be discontinued in patients with severe neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count less than 1,000/mm3), with ongoing medical care until the symptoms resolve.
    Headline
    seizures
    General Information
    As with other antipsychotic drugs, milsaperidone should be used with caution in patients with a history of seizures, a seizure disorder, or with conditions that may lower the seizure threshold. Conditions that lower the seizure threshold may be more prevalent in a population 65 years or older.
    Headline
    conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature
    General Information
    Disruption of the body's ability to reduce core body temperature has been attributed to antipsychotic agents. Patients receiving milsaperidone should be advised of conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature (e.g., exercising strenuously, exposure to extreme heat, receiving concomitant medication with anticholinergic activity, or being subject to dehydration).
    Headline
    dysphagia, increased risk for aspiration
    General Information
    People with dysphagia or who are at increased risk for aspiration should be closely monitored while receiving milsaperidone. Antipsychotic use, including milsaperidone, has been associated with esophageal dysmotility and aspiration of gastric contents. Aspiration pneumonia is a common cause of morbidity and mortality in older adults, in particular those with advanced Alzheimer disease.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, CYP2D6 poor metabolizer, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use milsaperidone with caution in people with hepatic impairment or CYP2D6 poor metabolizer status, as increased milsaperidone exposure is expected. Milsaperidone dosage should be reduced for poor metabolizers of CYP2D6. While no dosage adjustment is needed in people with mild hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A), patients with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B) may require dose reduction. The use of milsaperidone is not recommended for individuals with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure.
    Headline
    planned procedure
    General Information
    The initiation of milsaperidone is not recommended for people who have a planned procedure for cataract or glaucoma surgery scheduled. Use caution in patients currently taking milsaperidone who will have these procedures. Milsaperidone has some alpha-1 adrenergic receptor blocking activity. Intraoperative floppy iris syndrome (IFIS) has been observed during ocular surgery for cataracts in patients who are taking or were previously treated with alpha-1 adrenergic blockers. This variant of small pupil syndrome is characterized by the combination of a flaccid iris that billows in response to intraoperative irrigation current, progressive intraoperative miosis despite preoperative dilation with standard mydriatic medications, and potential prolapse of the iris toward the phacoemulsification incisions. The patient's surgeon should be prepared for possible modifications to their surgical technique, such as the utilization of iris hooks, iris dilator rings, or viscoelastic substances. There does not appear to be a benefit in stopping alpha-1 blocker therapy such as milsaperidone prior to cataract surgery.
    Headline
    dementia with psychotic disturbance, geriatric
    General Information
    Antipsychotics, including milsaperidone, pose a risk of increased mortality when used for dementia with psychotic disturbance in geriatric adults. Most of the deaths are cardiovascular (e.g., heart failure, sudden death) or infectious (e.g., pneumonia) in nature. A higher incidence of stroke and transient ischemic attack, including fatal stroke, has also been reported. Initiate milsaperidone at the lower end of the dosage range and titrate carefully in the older adult. The Beers Criteria label antipsychotics as potentially inappropriate medications for geriatric adults, except for specific indications like schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder adjunct therapy, Parkinson disease psychosis, or short-term use as part of an antiemetic regimen during chemotherapy. Avoid use in delirium, dementia, or non-psychotic Parkinson disease. Reserve antipsychotic use for when non-pharmacologic strategies have failed, are not possible, or the patient poses a danger to self or others. Due to the potential for hyponatremia and SIADH, monitor sodium levels. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates antipsychotic use in long-term care facilities and requires documented clinical justification. Taper antipsychotics unless clinically contraindicated, following guidelines for indications, dosages, and duration. OBRA limits "as needed" use to 14 days in long-term care facilities, with ongoing use requiring evaluation by the prescriber.
    Headline
    breast cancer, increased risk for breast cancer
    General Information
    Milsaperidone can cause hyperprolactinemia due to central dopamine D2 receptor antagonism; the elevation persists during chronic administration. In animal studies, mammary gland proliferative changes and increases in serum prolactin were observed during administration of milsaperidone. Although the clinical significance of these findings is unknown, some human breast cancers may be prolactin-dependent; therefore, milsaperidone should be used cautiously in those who have breast cancer or an increased risk for breast cancer, such as those with genetic markers or a personal or family history. Published epidemiologic studies have shown inconsistent results when exploring the potential association between hyperprolactinemia and breast cancer.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    The safe use of milsaperidone during pregnancy has not been established. When considering the use of milsaperidone during pregnancy, the possibility of milsaperidone-related adverse outcomes should be weighed against the risks of an untreated maternal psychiatric condition, which may include hospitalization, relapse, suicide, and increased adverse perinatal outcomes (e.g., pre-term birth). During animal studies, milsaperidone caused developmental toxicity (e.g., decreased fetal weight and length, decreased fetal skeletal ossification, minor fetal skeletal anomalies and variations, and increased early intrauterine deaths). There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant individuals exposed to milsaperidone and other psychiatric medications; information about the registry can be obtained at womensmentalhealth.org/research/pregnancyregistry or by calling 1-866-961-2388. It is not known if antipsychotics, through their effect on prolactin, would affect labor or delivery.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Consider pregnancy testing in people who may become pregnant prior to initiation of an antipsychotic, as there are limited controlled data regarding use of these agents during gestation. Ideally, health care providers should use pre-conceptual planning to help such individuals taking atypical antipsychotics balance the risk for illness relapse with the potential risk of continuing medication while pregnant. Counsel patients about the reproductive risk associated with milsaperidone. Milsaperidone can cause hyperprolactinemia as a result of central dopamine D2 receptor antagonism, which may suppress hypothalamic GnRH and can result in reduced pituitary gonadotropin secretion. This, in turn, may inhibit reproductive function by impairing gonadal steroidogenesis in both females and males. Elevations in prolactin may lead to reversible infertility in people who may become pregnant and impairment of gonadal steroidogenesis may also be of concern in people who can cause pregnancy in others. Hyperprolactinemia can also cause other endocrine abnormalities in both adult and pediatric populations. Close monitoring for adverse endocrine effects is advisable during milsaperidone use.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero during the third trimester of pregnancy are at risk for extrapyramidal and/or withdrawal symptoms following delivery. There have been reports of agitation, hypertonia, hypotonia, tremor, somnolence, respiratory distress and feeding disorders in these neonates. These complications have varied in severity and have ranged from self-limited to those requiring intensive care unit support and prolonged hospitalization. Neonates exhibiting signs or symptoms of extrapyramidal effects or withdrawal should be carefully monitored. The knowledge about long-term neurobehavioral effects in offspring is limited for all antipsychotic agents and requires further investigation.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Breast-feeding should be avoided during milsaperidone administration per the manufacturer label. There is no information regarding the presence of milsaperidone or its metabolites in human milk, the effects of milsaperidone on a breastfed child, nor the effects of milsaperidone on human milk production. Iloperidone (iloperidone and milsaperidone rapidly interconvert in vivo) is present in rat milk. Due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in the breastfed infant, patients should be advised to avoid breastfeeding during milsaperidone treatment and for 6 days after the last dose in CYP2D6 normal metabolizers or 8 days after the last dose in CYP2D6 poor metabolizers. Other agents may be preferred for patients who want to breast-feed, especially while breast-feeding a newborn or preterm infant. Alternate medications for consideration include atypical agents such as olanzapine or quetiapine. Data related to the safety of antipsychotics during breast-feeding are limited and chronic administration of any antipsychotic during breast-feeding should be avoided if possible. Regardless of the antipsychotic used, the breastfed infant should be closely monitored for excessive drowsiness, lethargy, and developmental delays. Combination treatment with antipsychotics may increase the risk of these adverse events.

    Headline
    hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, syncope
    General Information
    Milsaperidone has alpha-1 adrenergic antagonist properties and can produce hypotension or orthostatic hypotension, which appear to be dose-related. Careful titration of dosage helps limit hypotension risk. More rapid titration would be expected to increase rates of orthostatic hypotension. Symptoms of orthostatic hypotension may include lightheadedness, dizziness, tachycardia, or syncope. During clinical trials of iloperidone for the treatment of schizophrenia in which the manufacturer-recommended dosing titration was followed, the incidence of syncope was 0.4% (5 in 1,344) for iloperidone-treated subjects and 0.2% with placebo. In other pre-marketing trials, orthostatic hypotension occurred in 3% of subjects receiving iloperidone 10 to 16 mg/day and 5% of subjects receiving iloperidone 20 to 24 mg/day vs. 1% with placebo. Hypotension was reported in less than 1% of subjects receiving iloperidone 10 to 16 mg/day, 3% of subjects receiving iloperidone 20 to 24 mg/day, and less than 1% with placebo. In placebo controlled trials of people with bipolar mania, syncope was reported in 0.5% of subjects treated with iloperidone vs. 0% in the placebo group. Orthostatic hypotension was reported in 4% of subjects with bipolar disorder treated with iloperidone vs. 2% with placebo. Hypotension was also reported in 6% of subjects taking iloperidone versus 3% with placebo. Geriatric or debilitated people and those who are vulnerable to hypotension or with known cardiovascular disease may be more at risk for these side effects; careful monitoring of orthostatic vital signs is recommended in people for whom orthostasis is a concern.

    Headline
    arrhythmia exacerbation, AV block, edema, heart failure, palpitations, QT prolongation, sinus tachycardia
    General Information
    During pre-marketing clinical trials for the treatment of schizophrenia, sinus tachycardia occurred in 3% of patients receiving iloperidone 10 to 16 mg/day (n = 483), 12% of patients receiving iloperidone 20 to 24 mg/day (n = 391), and 1% of those receiving placebo (n = 587). Tachycardia was considered a dose-related adverse reaction in clinical trials. Palpitations were reported in at least 1% of patients in other pre-marketing evaluation. Edema (general, pitting, due to cardiac disease) was reported in 0.1% to 1% of patients receiving iloperidone. Arrhythmia exacerbation, first degree AV block, and acute and congestive heart failure were reported rarely (less than 0.1%). In clinical trials of iloperidone for the treatment of bipolar mania, tachycardia (including sinus tachycardia, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, and heart rate increased) was reported in 23% of patients on iloperidone versus 5% of patients on placebo. Iloperidone has been associated with QT prolongation in some patients. During a phase III trial, the effect of iloperidone on the QT interval was evaluated.     General Information
    Tardive dyskinesia (TD) is a syndrome of potentially irreversible, involuntary, dyskinetic movements that may develop during treatment with antipsychotic drugs. Involuntary orofacial movements (tongue, mouth, jaw, eyelids, or face) or choreoathetoid movements in the extremities may occur. The effect that symptomatic suppression has upon the long-term course of TD is unknown. In people who require chronic treatment with iloperidone, the lowest possible dose and the shortest duration of treatment should be used. Monitor milsaperidone recipients routinely (at 3 to 6-month intervals) for movement disorders using standardized assessments (e.g., AIMS, DISCUS). If signs or symptoms of TD develop, consider discontinuation of milsaperidone therapy.

    hyperthermia, hypothermia
    General Information
    Antipsychotic agents can disrupt the hypothalamic mechanism for temperature regulation and increase the risk for hyperthermia. People receiving milsaperidone should be advised of conditions that contribute to an elevation in core body temperature (e.g., strenuous exercise, ambient temperature increase, dehydration). Hyperthermia was reported in less than 0.1% of treated subjects during premarketing evaluation of iloperidone. A less frequently described alteration in thermoregulatory processes reported with both conventional and atypical antipsychotics is hypothermia. Thermoregulation is multi-factorial; however, the dopaminergic system appears to have a primary role, and serotonin may also have modulatory activity (5-HT2a receptors). Most cases of hypothermia associated with antipsychotics have occurred in conjunction with other potential precipitating factors such as hypothyroidism, sepsis, organic brain injury, or environmental temperature. Hypothermia appears to occur more frequently during initiation of antipsychotic therapy or after dose increases.

    Headline
    neuroleptic malignant syndrome
    General Information
    A potentially fatal symptom complex referred to as neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a potential side effect of all antipsychotics. NMS is characterized by hyperpyrexia, muscle rigidity, alterations in consciousness, altered mental status, and autonomic instability (e.g., irregular pulse or blood pressure, tachycardia, diaphoresis, arrhythmia). Elevated creatine kinase (CK), rhabdomyolysis, and acute renal failure may occur. The cause of NMS is not completely understood; however, dopamine receptor blockade is one of the mechanisms by which NMS is thought to occur. A primary risk factor for developing NMS appears to be the initiation or increase in dose of an antipsychotic. High potency and depot antipsychotics carry the greatest risk. Environmental risk factors include conditions that inhibit heat dissipation such as an elevated ambient room temperature, prolonged heat exposure, the use of individual restraints, or dehydration. NMS occurs more frequently in young adults, which is most likely the result of age of first exposure rather than an age-related risk. NMS occurs more frequently in men, which is thought to be related to the higher likelihood of male vs. female exposure to the causative agent. Risk factors for recurrent NMS include a personal history of NMS, increasing age, and certain medical comorbities (e.g., electrolyte imbalances, dehydration). Milsaperidone should be immediately discontinued, and appropriate supportive therapy initiated as soon as symptoms of NMS are discovered.

Description
  Text
  Milsaperidone is an oral atypical antipsychotic that is the major active metabolite of iloperidone. Pharmacokinetic studies have found milsaperidone and iloperidone to be bioequivalent in both single and steady state dosing models, and binding receptor properties are similar between the two compounds. Milsaperidone is used for the treatment of schizophrenia and for the acute treatment of manic or mixed episodes associated with bipolar I disorder in adults. Data supporting the clinical efficacy of milsaperidone was derived from clinical studies conducted with iloperidone; for schizophrenia, effectiveness was studied in two placebo- and active-controlled short-term trials (a 6-week trial and a 4-week trial) and one long-term placebo-controlled randomized withdrawal trial in adult patients who met the DSM-III/IV criteria for schizophrenia. These trials highlighted that iloperidone had comparable efficacy to risperidone and ziprasidone as evidenced by changes in scores in the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale from baseline to end of treatment and improvements in Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale scores. Iloperidone also demonstrated a statistically significantly longer time to relapse when compared to patients receiving placebo. For bipolar disorder, iloperidone was studied in a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 4-week study that enrolled adult patients who met the DSM-5 criteria for bipolar I disorder, manic or mixed type. At the end of the study, iloperidone demonstrated superiority over placebo in changes to the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) total score from baseline at Day 28. Both CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 are involved in milsaperidone metabolism; therefore, clinically significant drug interactions may occur with CYP2D6 or CYP3A4 inhibitors. In clinical trials, iloperidone was associated with QTc prolongation, an effect that was enhanced during concurrent use of potent CYP2D6 or CYP3A4 inhibitors. Due to the possibility of arrhythmias and sudden death associated with QTc prolongation, consideration should be given to the use of other treatment options prior to milsaperidone administration. As with all antipsychotics, milsaperidone carries a boxed warning for increased mortality in geriatric patients with dementia-related psychosis. The FDA approved milsaperidone in February 2026.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The exact mechanism responsible for the therapeutic effects of milsaperidone in the treatment of schizophrenia and bipolar I disorder is unknown. However, it is thought that atypical antipsychotics, including milsaperidone, reduce symptoms of schizophrenia and manic or mixed episodes of bipolar disorder through dopamine D2 and serotonin 5-HT2 antagonism. Milsaperidone and iloperidone rapidly interconvert in vivo, and milsaperidone has an in vitro receptor binding profile similar to iloperidone. Milsaperidone acts as an antagonist with moderate to strong affinity at the dopamine D1, D2, D3, and D4 receptors, the histamine H1, serotonin 5-HT1B, 5-HT2A, and 5-HT2C sites, and at the norepinephrine NEalpha1, NEalpha2B, and NEalpha2C receptors. It has weak affinity at the norepinephrine NEalpha2A, serotonin 5-HT1A, and 5-HT6 sites.

    Milsaperidone is well absorbed orally, with peak plasma concentrations occurring 4 hours after a dose.   Text
  Milsaperidone is administered orally. Milsaperidone and its major metabolite (P95) are approximately 92% protein bound. The apparent volume of distribution is 1,715 to 2,343 L. Iloperidone and milsaperidone rapidly interconvert in vivo; Milsaperidone undergoes oxidation to form iloperidone, while iloperidone can undergo a stereospecific carbonyl reduction to form milsaperidone. Elimination of both milsaperidone and iloperidone is mainly through hydroxylation (mediated by CYP2D6) and O-desmethylation (mediated by CYP3A4). After oral administration in CYP2D6 normal metabolizers (NMs), total plasma exposure was 48% P95, 20% milsaperidone, and 9% iloperidone. After oral administration to CYP2D6 poor metabolizers (PMs), plasma exposure was 23% P95, 34% milsaperidone, and 16% iloperidone. The observed mean elimination half-life in CYP2D6 NMs is 26 hours for milsaperidone, 18 hours for iloperidone, and 23 hours for P95. In CYP2D6 PMs, the mean elimination half-lives were 37 hours for milsaperidone, 33 hours for iloperidone, and 31 hours for P95. Milsaperidone has an apparent clearance (clearance/bioavailability) of 32 to 69 L/hour. Excretion data is derived from iloperidone studies, which demonstrated that the majority of radioactive materials were recovered in the urine. Mean recovery was 58% in CYP2D6 NMs and 45% in PMs, with feces accounting for 20% in NMs and 22% in PMs.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP450) isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2D6, CYP3A4
Milsaperidone and iloperidone rapidly interconvert in vivo; in vitro drug interaction studies are based on data from iloperidone studies. Because metabolism occurs via CYP2D6 and CYP3A4, clinically significant drug interactions may occur with strong CYP2D6 inhibitors, strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, and concomitant use of strong CYP2D6 with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors. Iloperidone is an inhibitor of CYP 3A4, 3A5 and 2D6; however, coadministration with sensitive substrates of these enzymes did not appear to result in clinically significant interactions. Iloperidone does not inhibit CYP 1A1, 1A2, 2A6, 2B6, 2C8, 2C9, or 2E1. Iloperidone also does not induce CYP 1A2, 2C8, 2C9, 2C19, 3A4 and 3A5. Iloperidone is not a substrate of CYP 1A1, 1A2, 2A6, 2B6, 2C8, 2C9, 2C19, or 2E1. This suggests that an interaction of iloperidone with inhibitors or inducers of these enzymes, or other factors, is unlikely. Iloperidone and milsaperidone are not substrates of P-gp; iloperidone is a weak P-gp inhibitor.
