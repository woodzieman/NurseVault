Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Reserve opioid analgesics for use in individuals for whom alternative treatment options are ineffective, not tolerated, or would be otherwise inadequate to provide sufficient management of pain.
Individualize dosing; consider severity of pain, clinical response, prior analgesic treatment experience, and risk factors for addiction, abuse, and misuse. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration consistent with individual treatment goals. Reserve titration to higher doses for individuals in whom lower doses are insufficient and in whom expected benefits of using a higher dose opioid clearly outweigh substantial risks.
Monitor individuals closely for respiratory depression, which may occur at any time during therapy. Consider this risk when selecting an initial dosage and when adjusting the dose. Continually reevaluate individuals to assess the maintenance of pain control and the relative incidence of adverse reactions, as well as to monitor for the development of addiction, abuse, or misuse. If the level of pain increases after dosage stabilization, attempt to identify the source of increased pain before increasing the opioid dosage. If unacceptable opioid-related adverse reactions are observed, consider reducing the dosage. Adjust the dosage to obtain an appropriate balance between management of pain and opioid-related adverse reactions.
Many acute pain conditions (e.g., pain occurring with surgical procedures or acute musculoskeletal injuries) require no more than a few days of an opioid. Clinical guidelines on opioid prescribing for some acute pain conditions are available.
There is substantial interpatient variability in the relative potency of different opioid drugs and products. When adjusting butorphanol dosages or converting from another opioid agonist, it is better to underestimate daily dose requirements and give rescue doses than to risk an adverse reaction. If an adverse event occurs, the next dose may be reduced. Refer to the Opioid Agonists Drug Class Overview for approximate equianalgesic doses.
Avoid abrupt discontinuation or rapid dosage reduction in an individual who is physically dependent on opioid analgesics. When discontinuing butorphanol therapy in individuals who are opioid-dependent, taper the dose gradually by 10% to 25% every 2 to 4 weeks. Individuals who have been taking opioids for briefer periods of time may tolerate a more rapid taper. Ensure a multimodal approach to pain management, including mental health support if needed, is in place prior to an opioid analgesic taper.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Despite having weak effects on GI motility when compared to other opiate agonists, butorphanol should be used cautiously in patients with GI disease including GI obstruction or ileus, ulcerative colitis, or pre-existing constipation. Patients with acute ulcerative colitis (UC) or other inflammatory bowel disease may be more sensitive to the constipating effects of opiate agonists. Although opiate agonists are usually contraindicated for use in patients with diarrhea secondary to poisoning or infectious diarrhea, antimotility agents have been used successfully in these patients. If possible, butorphanol should not be given until the toxic substance has been eliminated.
 
Although the incidence is rare, butorphanol may cause psychologic dependence, which may lead to substance abuse in some patients. Patients with a previous history of substance abuse may be at increased risk. The indication for use of butorphanol in patients with a history of alcoholism or drug dependence is for the treatment of moderate to severe pain. Patients with active substance abuse or dependence upon opiate agonist should not receive butorphanol. High doses of butorphanol will precipitate withdrawal symptoms in opiate-dependent patients, due to its opiate antagonist effects.
 
Abrupt discontinuation of prolonged butorphanol therapy may result in withdrawal symptoms (see Adverse Reactions). Patients should be gradually tapered off butorphanol to avoid a withdrawal reaction.
 
In general, butorphanol does not appear to significantly affect biliary flow. Safe use in patients undergoing biliary tract surgery, however, has not been established.
 
Butorphanol can cause respiratory depression, although the risk for this is supposedly less than for morphine. Therefore, butorphanol should be used with caution in patients with known pulmonary disease such as bronchial asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), or respiratory insufficiency due to limited respiratory reserve.
 
Patients with head trauma or intracranial mass, or increased intracranial pressure should only be given butorphanol if the benefits outweigh the risks, because it can compromise the evaluation of neurologic parameters. Respiratory depression can produce cerebral hypoxia and raise CSF pressure, exaggerating the injury.
 
Patients with cardiac disease should be treated with caution. Butorphanol can increase blood pressure and cardiac workload, so its use in patients with acute myocardial infarction, ventricular dysfunction, or coronary insufficiency (e.g., angina, heart failure) must be carefully evaluated.
 
Drug accumulation can occur in patients with renal impairment, renal failure, or hepatic disease, prolonging butorphanol's duration of action and increasing the risk of adverse reactions; lower initial doses are advised (see Dosage). Butorphanol and its metabolites are known to be substantially excreted by the kidney, and the risk of toxic reactions to butorphanol may be greater in patients with impaired renal function.
 
Elderly patients are more susceptible to the adverse reactions of butorphanol and should be carefully monitored; lower initial doses are advised (see Dosage). In clinical studies, elderly patients had an increased incidence of headache, dizziness, drowsiness, vertigo, constipation, nausea/vomiting, and nasal congestion as compared to younger patients.
 
Butorphanol readily crosses the placenta and normally should not be given to pregnant women. It is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. Premature neonates are especially sensitive to respiratory depression and apnea. There have been rare reports of infant respiratory distress/apnea following the administration of butorphanol injection during labor. The reports of respiratory distress/apnea have been associated with administration of a dose within two hours of obstetric delivery, use of multiple doses, use with additional analgesic or sedative drugs, or use in preterm pregnancies. The manufacturer further recommends that butorphanol not be used while breast-feeding.
 
Safe and effective use of butorphanol in children < 18 years old have not been established.
 
Patients who have demonstrated a prior opiate agonist hypersensitivity reaction to butorphanol should not receive butorphanol. These patients are at increased risk of developing anaphylactoid reactions. Some formulations of the butorphanol nasal spray and injectable may contain the preservative benzethonium chloride; such formulations are contraindicated in patients hypersensitive to either the medicine or the preservative.
 
Patients should be warned that butorphanol can impair the mental and physical abilities required for the performance of potentially dangerous tasks such as driving or operating machinery. These effects may persist for varying periods of time after dosing.

    Headline
    brain tumor, coma, head trauma, increased intracranial pressure, loss of consciousness
    General Information
    Avoid opioid use in individuals with loss of consciousness or coma; opioids may obscure the clinical course in an individual with a head trauma injury. Monitor individuals who may be susceptible to the intracranial effect of carbon dioxide retention (e.g., those with evidence of increased intracranial pressure or brain tumor) for signs of sedation and respiratory depression, particularly when initiating opioid therapy. Opioids may reduce respiratory drive and the resultant carbon dioxide retention can further increase intracranial pressure.
    Headline
    seizures
    General Information
    Opioids may increase seizure frequency in individuals with seizure disorders and may increase seizure risk in other clinical settings associated with seizures. Regularly evaluate individuals with a history of seizure disorders for worsened seizure control during opioid therapy.
    Headline
    acute myocardial infarction, coronary insufficiency, ventricular dysfunction
    General Information
    Limit use of butorphanol in individuals with acute myocardial infarction, ventricular dysfunction, or coronary insufficiency to those situations where the benefits clearly outweigh the risk because butorphanol may increase the work of the heart, especially the pulmonary circuit.
    Headline
    asthma exacerbation, cachexia, central sleep apnea, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coadministration with certain medications, cor pulmonale, hypercapnia, hypoxia, respiratory depression, respiratory insufficiency, severe asthma
    General Information
    Butorphanol is contraindicated in individuals with significant respiratory depression and those with asthma exacerbation or severe asthma in an unmonitored setting or in the absence of resuscitative equipment. Avoid opioid coadministration with certain medications, specifically other CNS depressants when possible, as this significantly increases the risk for profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death. Reserve concomitant prescribing of these medications for use in individuals for whom alternative treatment options are inadequate; if concurrent use is necessary, use the lowest effective dosages and minimum treatment durations needed. Consider prescribing an opioid overdose reversal agent if concomitant use is warranted. Monitor individuals closely for signs or symptoms of respiratory depression and sedation. Careful monitoring is required, particularly when CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducers are used concomitantly. Concurrent use of a CYP3A4 inhibitor or discontinuation of a concurrently used CYP3A4 inducer may result in an increase in plasma butorphanol concentrations, which may increase the risk for respiratory depression. Concurrent use of a CYP3A4 inducer or discontinuation of CYP3A4 inhibitor may result in a decrease in plasma butorphanol concentrations, which may be associated with reduced efficacy and/or opioid withdrawal. Individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cor pulmonale, respiratory insufficiency, hypoxia, hypercapnia, or preexisting respiratory depression are at increased risk of decreased respiratory drive even at recommended doses. Individuals with advanced age, cachexia, or debilitation are also at an increased risk for opioid-induced respiratory depression. Monitor such individuals closely, particularly when initiating and titrating the opioid; consider the use of non-opioid analgesics in these individuals. Carbon dioxide retention from opioid-induced respiratory depression may also worsen opioid sedating effects. Consider decreasing the opioid dosage in individuals with central sleep apnea (CSA). Opioids increase the risk of CSA and sleep-related hypoxemia in a dose-dependent fashion.

    Butorphanol is contraindicated in individuals with known or suspected GI obstruction, including paralytic ileus. Opioids may cause spasm of the sphincter of Oddi or increases in serum amylase. Monitor individuals with biliary tract disease, including acute pancreatitis, for worsening symptoms.

    Use butorphanol with caution in individuals with renal impairment, renal failure, or hepatic failure. Dosage adjustment and/or longer dosing interval for butorphanol is necessary in individuals with renal or hepatic impairment.
    Headline
    major psychiatric disorder, opioid overdose, substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    Opioids expose users to the risks of addiction, abuse, and misuse. Although the risk of addiction in any individual is unknown, it can occur in individuals appropriately prescribed an opioid. Addiction can occur at recommended dosages and if the medication is misused or abused. The risk of opioid-related overdose or overdose-related death is increased with higher doses, and this risk persists over the course of therapy. The potential for these risks should not prevent the proper management of pain in any given individual. Assess an individual's risk for opioid addiction, abuse, or misuse before prescribing an opioid, and monitor for the development of these behaviors or conditions. Risks are increased in individuals with a personal or family history of substance abuse disorder (including alcohol or drug abuse or addiction) or major psychiatric disorder (e.g., major depression). Individuals at increased risk may be prescribed opioids, but use in such individuals necessitates intensive counseling about the risks and proper use of the opioid along with frequent reevaluation for signs of addiction, abuse, and misuse. Opioids are sought for nonmedical use and are subject to diversion from legitimate prescribed use. Consider these risks when prescribing or dispensing opioids. Strategies to reduce these risks include prescribing the medication in the smallest appropriate quantity and educating about proper medication storage and disposal. Instruct individuals not to share butorphanol nasal spray with others and to protect butorphanol nasal spray from theft. Keep opioids out of the reach of children, others for whom the medication was not prescribed, and pets as accidental exposure or improper use may cause respiratory failure and a fatal overdose. Accidental exposure of even a single dose of an opioid, especially by younger individuals, can result in a fatal overdose. Discuss the importance of an opioid overdose reversal agent and the options for obtaining an opioid overdose reversal agent (e.g., prescription, over-the-counter, or as part of a community-based program), especially with individuals who are at increased risk of opioid overdose, such as those who are also using other CNS depressants, who have a history of opioid use disorder (OUD), who have experienced a previous opioid overdose, or who have household members or other close contacts at risk for accidental exposure or opioid overdose. Educate individuals and caregivers on how to recognize respiratory depression, and how to use an opioid overdose reversal agent for the emergency treatment of opioid overdose. Emphasize the importance of calling 911 or getting emergency medical help, even if an opioid overdose reversal agent is administered.

    Advise individuals against driving or operating machinery or participating in activities requiring coordination and concentration during opioid therapy unless they are tolerant to the effects of opioids and know how they will react to the medication.     Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Use butorphanol with caution in geriatric adults. Dosage adjustment and/or longer dosing interval for butorphanol is necessary in geriatric adults. Geriatric adults may have increased sensitivity to opioids. In general, use cautious dose selection for opioids in geriatric adults, usually starting at the low end of the dosing range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function and concomitant disease or other medication therapy. Increase the opioid dosage slowly in geriatric adults and frequently reevaluate for signs of CNS and respiratory depression. According to the Beers Criteria, opioid agonists are considered potentially inappropriate medications in geriatric adults with a history of falls or fractures and should be avoided in these populations, except in the setting of severe acute pain, since opioids can produce ataxia, impaired psychomotor function, syncope, and additional falls. If an opioid must be used, consider reducing the use of other CNS-active medications that increase the risk of falls and fractures and implement strategies to reduce fall risk. In individuals receiving palliative care, the balance of benefits and harms of medication management may differ from those of the general population of older adults.
    Headline
    labor, obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate and well-controlled studies evaluating butorphanol use during human pregnancy before 37 weeks of gestation. Opioids cross the placenta. Butorphanol is not recommended for use during and immediately before labor, when other analgesic techniques are more appropriate. Opioids can prolong labor and obstetric delivery by temporarily reducing the strength, duration, and frequency of uterine contractions. This effect is not consistent and may be offset by an increased rate of cervical dilatation, which may shorten labor. In addition, neonates exposed to opioids in utero may present with respiratory depression at birth. Because of this, an opioid antagonist (e.g., naloxone) must be available for reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression in the neonate at delivery. There have been rare reports of infant respiratory distress/apnea after administration of butorphanol injection during labor. The reports of respiratory distress/apnea have been associated with administration of a dose within 2 hours of delivery, use of multiple doses, use with additional analgesic or sedative drugs, or use in preterm pregnancy. In a study of 119 patients, the administration of 1 mg of IV butorphanol during labor was associated with transient (10 to 90 minutes) sinusoidal fetal heart rate patterns, but was not associated with adverse neonatal outcomes. There is no clincial experience with the use of butorphanol nasal spray during labor and delivery.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with opioids. Long-term use of opioids may influence the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, leading to hormonal changes that may manifest as a libido decrease, impotence, erectile dysfunction, amenorrhea, or infertility. The causal role of opioids in the clinical manifestations of hypogonadism is unknown. It is not known whether the effects on fertility are reversible.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero may present with respiratory depression and psycho-physiologic effects at birth. Monitor exposed newborns for signs of excess sedation and respiratory depression. If necessary, administer an opioid antagonist (e.g., naloxone) to reverse the effects of opioid-induced respiratory depression. Long-term use of opioids during pregnancy can result in physical dependence in the exposed infant. Monitor newborns for symptoms of withdrawal and manage accordingly. Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome may present as irritability, hyperactivity, abnormal sleep pattern, high-pitched cry, tremor, vomiting, diarrhea, and failure to gain weight. It can be life-threatening if not recognized and treated. Onset, duration, and severity of opioid withdrawal may vary based on the specific opioid used, duration of use, timing and amount of last maternal use, and rate of elimination by the infant.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Butorphanol is compatible with breast-feeding. Butorphanol is present in human milk in clinically insignificant amounts (estimated 4 mcg/L of milk with a dose of 2 mg IM 4 times a day). There are no data on the use of nasal butorphanol during breast-feeding, but the amount present in milk is likely to be similarly low. Additionally, butorphanol has poor oral absorption, further limiting drug exposure to the breastfed child. Monitor children exposed to opioids through breast milk for excess sedation and respiratory depression. Withdrawal symptoms may occur in the breast-fed child when maternal administration of the opioid is stopped or when breast-feeding is discontinued, particularly with chronic use. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying maternal medical condition.

  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  There appears to be little difference in the incidence of adverse effects reported from either injectable or intranasal butorphanol. Drowsiness is the most frequently reported adverse effect. Over 40% of patients reported somnolence. These effects can be more pronounced if butorphanol is taken in combination with other sedative medications. Dizziness was reported in 19% of patients. Other adverse effects affecting the CNS with an incidence of more than 3% were confusion, and insomnia (11%). Less frequently butorphanol produced tremor, anxiety, nervousness, euphoria, and paresthesias and urinary retention. Patients should be warned that activity requiring mental alertness can be affected because of CNS depression with butorphanol. Other reported effects from butorphanol use occurring in between 3 and 9% of patients include asthenia, headache, diaphoresis or a clammy feeling, tinnitus, and dysgeusia. Occasionally urination can be impaired by butorphanol use, and patients can experience abnormal dreams or hallucinations.Because butorphanol is an antagonist at the µ-opiate receptor, it is less likely to produce respiratory depression than morphine. When it occurs, however, respiratory depression is serious, and patients should be carefully monitored. Respiratory depression can be reversed with the use of naloxone. Routine use of opiate agonists by an expectant mother can lead to respiratory depression in the newborn. In patients using butorphanol by the intranasal route, dyspnea, epistaxis, nasal irritation, pharyngitis, rhinitis, sinus congestion, and upper respiratory tract infection were reported by 3—9% of patients, with generalized nasal congestion reported by 13%.Butorphanol frequently causes nausea/vomiting (13%). Other gastrointestinal effects occurring in more than 3% of patients include anorexia, constipation and xerostomia.Vasodilation occurs with butorphanol use in 3—9% of patients. This may lead to hypotension including orthostatic hypotension, or episodes of syncope. Palpitations, hypertension, sinus bradycardia, and sinus tachycardia occur infrequently with butorphanol but should be considered when treating patients already at cardiovascular risk.Physiological dependence will occur during chronic opiate agonist therapy as evidenced by a withdrawal syndrome occurring after abrupt discontinuation of the drug in these patients. Symptoms of withdrawal include nausea and diarrhea, coughing, lacrimation, yawning, sneezing, rhinorrhea, profuse sweating, twitching muscles, abdominal and muscle pain/cramps, hot and cold flashes, and piloerection. Elevations in body temperature, respiratory rate, heart rate, and blood pressure may also occur. Without treatment most symptoms resolve within 5—14 days; however, there appears to be a secondary or chronic abstinence syndrome which may last 2—6 months, characterized by insomnia, irritability and muscle aches. The shorter the onset and duration of action of the opiate agonist, the greater the intensity and rapidity of onset of withdrawal symptoms. Routine use of opiate agonists by an expectant mother can lead to withdrawal symptoms in the newborn. Withdrawal symptoms in a newborn occur 1—4 days after birth and include generalized tremors, hypertonicity with any form of tactile stimuli, hyperalterness, sleeplessness, excessive crying, vomiting, diarrhea, yawning and fever. It is important to differentiate physiological dependence, the onset of a withdrawal syndrome upon abrupt discontinuation of the drug from psychological dependence. Although the possibility of psychological dependence with butorphanol is less than pure opiate agonists due to the opiate antagonistic effects, opiate agonists-antagonists do have the potential to cause phycological dependence leading to drug abuse. Psychological dependence is a behavioral syndrome characterized by drug craving, overwhelming concern with acquisition of the drug and other drug-related behaviors such as drug selling and seeking the drug from multiple sources. Clinical trials have shown that 3% of patients who use the butorphanol for 2 months or longer reported behavioral problems. The introduction of a nasal spray dosage form prompted the DEA in June 1997 to propose assigning butorphanol into the schedule IV class of controlled substances.Pharmacologic tolerance to the analgesic affects of opiate agonists including butorphanol may occur in some patients, but the incidence is less than that seen with pure opiate agonists. Tolerance is the need for increasing opioid doses to maintain initial pain relief. Typically, tolerance presents as a decrease in the duration of analgesia and is managed by increasing the opioid dose or frequency. When increasing doses of analgesia are required causes may be multi-factorial including tolerance, progression of disease or psychological distress.Like most opiates, butorphanol can cause dermatologic reactions. The most common reactions seen with butorphanol include pruritus and urticaria, but occur in less than 1% of patients.The clinical manifestations of butorphanol overdose are those of opioid drugs in general. Consequences of overdose vary with the amount of butorphanol ingested and the degree of tolerance of the patient to the effects of opiates. The most serious symptoms are hypoventilation, cardiovascular insufficiency and/or coma. Other symptoms of overdose may include excessive drug effect (e.g., sedation, dizziness, nausea or vomiting). This may be associated with transient difficulty speaking and/or executing purposeful movements. Many of the reported cases have involved people who are not tolerant to the effects of opiates and who use larger doses of butorphanol to initiate therapy.

    neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome, physiological dependence, psychological dependence, withdrawal
    General Information
    Physiological dependence can develop during chronic opioid agonist therapy, as evidenced by a withdrawal syndrome occurring after abrupt discontinuation or a significant dose reduction of the drug. Symptoms of withdrawal include nausea and diarrhea, coughing, lacrimation, yawning, sneezing, rhinorrhea, profuse sweating, twitching muscles, abdominal and muscle pain/cramps, hot and cold flashes, and piloerection. Elevations in body temperature, respiratory rate, heart rate, and blood pressure may also occur. In a chronic nonmalignant pain study, symptoms consistent with withdrawal were reported in 8 (2.6%) subjects receiving butorphanol nasal spray compared to 0 subjects receiving placebo; most subjects abruptly discontinued the nasal spray after long-term use or administration of high doses. Without treatment, most symptoms resolve within 5 to 14 days; however, there appears to be a secondary or chronic abstinence syndrome which may last 2 to 6 months, characterized by insomnia, irritability, and muscle aches. The shorter the onset and duration of action of the opioid agonist, the greater the intensity and rapidity of onset of withdrawal symptoms. It is important to differentiate physiological dependence (the onset of a withdrawal syndrome upon abrupt discontinuation of the drug) from psychological dependence. Although the possibility of psychological dependence on butorphanol is less than pure opioid agonists due to the opioid antagonistic effects, opioid agonists-antagonists have the potential to cause psychological dependence, leading to drug abuse. Psychological dependence is a behavioral syndrome characterized by drug craving, overwhelming concern about the acquisition of the drug and other drug-related behaviors such as drug selling and seeking the drug from multiple sources. In postmarketing studies, addiction, abuse, misuse, and fatal and non-fatal opioid overdose were observed in subjects with long-term opioid use. In a prospective, 12-month observational cohort study in individuals initiating long-term use of Schedule II opioid analgesics, 1% to 6% of subjects across 2 cohorts met the criteria for addiction. Approximately 9% and 22% of subjects newly met the criteria for prescription opioid abuse and misuse, respectively. In a retrospective, observational cohort study in individuals with new long-term use of Schedule II opioid analgesics, the 5-year cumulative incidence estimates for opioid-involved overdose or opioid overdose-related death ranged from approximately 1.5% to 4% across study sites, counting only the first event during follow-up. Approximately 17% of first opioid overdoses observed over the entire study period (5 to 11 years) were fatal. Higher baseline opioid dose was the strongest and most consistent predictor of opioid-involved overdose or opioid overdose-related death. During postmarketing surveillance, there were more cases of abuse with the nasal dosage form than with the injectable dosage form. Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) may occur in neonates and infants exposed to opioids in utero. Withdrawal symptoms may include irritability, hyperactivity, abnormal sleep pattern, high-pitched cry, tremor, vomiting, diarrhea, and failure to gain weight. Onset, duration, and severity of opioid withdrawal may vary based on the specific opioid used, duration of use, timing and amount of last maternal use, and rate of elimination by the infant. Exposed newborns should be monitored closely. NOWS can be life-threatening if not recognized and treated. Treatment should be managed by clinicians experienced with neonatal withdrawal.

Description
  Text
  Butorphanol tartrate is a synthetic parenteral opiate agonist-antagonist. Although it is structurally related to morphine, butorphanol is more similar in action to nalbuphine, another agonist-antagonist. Butorphanol is available parenterally for treating moderate to severe pain and as a nasal spray that has been used to treat migraine. Although studies suggest equianalgesic parenteral doses are as effective as morphine, clinical response may indicate otherwise, perhaps due to the development of tolerance. Butorphanol does not offer significant advantages over pure opiate-agonists in the treatment of acute pain or cancer pain. Butorphanol is also used to provide preoperative sedation and analgesia, and to supplement surgical anesthesia. Butorphanol injection was approved by the FDA in 1978; the nasal spray was approved in 1991. Although butorphanol was not a controlled substance in the United States when it was originally introduced, the DEA recommended in June 1997 that both the injection and the nasal spray be classified as a controlled substance.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Butorphanol is a mixed agonist-antagonist opioid analgesic. Specifically, butorphanol is a kappa-receptor agonist and a partial agonist or mixed agonist-antagonist with low intrinsic activity at mu-receptors. Opioids exert their effects by stimulating specific opioid receptors, designated as mu, kappa, and delta. Mu-receptors are considered the classic morphine-receptor type, and stimulation at this receptor produces supraspinal analgesia, respiratory depression, euphoria, and physical dependence. Stimulation of kappa-receptors produces spinal analgesia, miosis, and sedation. Because of limited stimulation at the mu-receptor, butorphanol is believed to produce less respiratory depression and to pose a lower risk of physical dependence than morphine.

  Text
  Butorphanol is administered via oral, intravenous, intramuscular or intranasal route. Butorphanol and its metabolites are widely distributed. The volume of distribution is between 305—901 liters. The drug crosses the blood-brain barrier and the placenta and is distributed into breast milk. Protein binding is 80%. Hepatic metabolism is extensive, primarily producing an inactive metabolite, hydroxybutorphanol. N-dealkylation and conjugation also occur. The metabolites produced during first pass have no analgesic activity. Elimination of butorphanol is via the kidneys and feces. Sixty to 80% of a dose of butorphanol is excreted via the kidneys as inactive metabolites; approximately 5% is excreted as unchanged drug. Between 11% and 14% of a parenteral dose is excreted in the feces.
