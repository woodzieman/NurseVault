Drug Class: Mecasermin (rh-IGF-1) is a recombinant product, manufactured by genetic modification, to be identical to that of endogenous human insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), which is the primary mediator of growth hormone.

Routes: subcutaneous; Common adult doses: Safety and efficacy have not been established.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema, dyspnea, erythema, hematoma, injection site reaction, pruritus, rash, urticaria, dizziness, headache, increased intracranial pressure, papilledema, seizures, visual impairment, hearing loss, infection, otalgia, arthralgia, avascular necrosis, musculoskeletal pain, osteonecrosis

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Mecasermin is contraindicated in patients with mecasermin hypersensitivity or hypersensitivity to any of its inactive ingredients. The manufacturer recommends discontinuation of therapy if sensitivity to mecasermin develops. The Increlex brand of mecasermin contains benzyl alcohol as a preservative and is contraindicated in patients with benzyl alcohol hypersensitivity. The benzoate in this injection may displace bilirubin and induce kernicterus, and has been associated with 'gasping syndrome' in neonates. In addition, because mecasermin is a protein, local or systemic allergic reaction may occur. Local reactions may result in pruritus, burning, and swelling at the injection site. Parents/patients should be advised to seek medical attention if an allergic reaction occurs.
 
Mecasermin should be administered via subcutaneous administration only; intravenous administration of mecasermin is contraindicated.
 
Mecasermin is contraindicated in patients with active or suspected neoplastic disease. Administration of a growth factor in a patient with concurrent neoplastic disease may result in an increase in tumor size and disease progression; in addition, mecasermin therapy should be discontinued if evidence of neoplasia develops.
 
Because mecasermin increases growth rate, administration may exacerbate growth abnormalities and cause growth rate related adverse events. Patients with a history of scoliosis should receive mecasermin with caution as progression of scoliosis is possible. Mecasermin use has not been shown to increase the risk of scoliosis. In addition, slipped capital femoral epiphysis may occur more frequently in patients with endocrine disorders or in patients undergoing rapid growth. Patients and health care providers should watch for the development of a limp with or without hip or knee pain. Linear growth can no longer occur in patients with epiphyseal closure, therefore mecasermin is contraindicated in this population for the treatment of short stature.
 
Mecasermin has not been studied in children under 2 years of age. Also, a child should not receive mecasermin if his bone growth plates are closed (epiphyseal closure).
 
Mecasermin is only for children who are very short for their age due to an endogenous insulin-like growth factor-1 deficiency. Mecasermin should not be used in place of growth hormone and, thus, should not be used in patients with secondary forms of IGF-1 deficiency, such as growth hormone (GH) deficiency, malnutrition, hypothyroidism, or chronic treatment with pharmacologic doses of anti-inflammatory corticosteroid therapy. Thyroid and nutritional deficiencies should be corrected before treatment with mecasermin.
 
Mecasermin can cause hypoglycemia. The hypoglycemic potency of IGF-1 is thought to be about 10 percent of that of insulin. As such, mecasermin should be used cautiously in patients with diabetes mellitus. Patients with diabetes or glucose intolerance should be monitored closely during treatment; dosage adjustments may be required. All patients should be advised to avoid high-risk activities (i.e., driving or operating machinery) within 2—3 hours of administration until a well-tolerated dose has been established. Dosing should occur shortly before or after a meal or snack. If the patient is unable to eat within 20 minutes for any reason, that dose of mecasermin should be withheld. Special attention should be paid to small children because their oral intake may be inconsistent.
 
Lymphoid tissue (i.e., tonsillar) hypertrophy associated with complications, such as snoring, sleep apnea, and chronic middle-ear effusions, have been reported with the use of mecasermin. Patients should have periodic examinations to rule out such potential complications and receive appropriate treatment if necessary.
 
Because mecasermin therapy has been reported to cause benign increased intracranial pressure with papilledema, visual changes, headache, nausea, and vomiting, funduscopic examination is recommended at therapy initiation and periodically thereafter. Symptoms usually occur within the first weeks of therapy and are more common in adult patients. Of patients who experience intracranial hypertension, the majority have resolution of associated symptoms after discontinuation of therapy; intracranial hypertension does not usually recur after restart of therapy at a reduced dosage.
 
Mecasermin is not known to cause an impairment of fertility. Animal studies demonstrate no ill effects on fertility in either male or female animal models. No similar measures in human trials have been determined.
 
Mecasermin is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. Increased fetal loss but no fetal abnormalities were noted in rabbits administered rh-IGF-1 during organogenesis in doses of 0.2, 0.5, or 0.125 mg/kg/day subcutaneously. Additional animal studies found the incidence of fetal loss to be reduced but not insignificant in glucose-supplemented rabbits; increased early resorption and preimplantation loss was not clearly attributable to drug-induced hypoglycemia. There are no adequate and well-controlled human studies. Animal studies are not always predictive of human response to drugs in pregnancy, and mecasermin should be used in pregnancy only if clearly needed.
 
It is not known if mecasermin is excreted into human breast milk. Because many drugs are excreted in human milk, breast-feeding mothers should receive mecasermin therapy with caution.

    Headline
    malignancy
    General Information
    Mecasermin use is contraindicated in children with malignant neoplasia or a history of malignancy. Therapy should be discontinued if evidence of malignant neoplasia or any new primary malignancy develops. There have been postmarketing reports of both benign and malignant neoplasms in pediatric patients who have received treatment with mecasermin, including cysts and polyps. These cases represent a variety of different malignancies. The occurrence of neoplasia was mostly observed in individuals with rare genetic conditions of short stature associated with an increased risk of cancer, or in individuals with other cancer predisposing conditions. The tumors were also observed more frequently in individuals who received mecasermin at higher than recommended doses, or at doses that produced serum IGF-1 levels above the normal reference ranges for age and sex. Monitor all individuals receiving mecasermin carefully for development of neoplasms. Advise individuals and caregivers to report development of new neoplasms.
    Headline
    epiphyseal closure, scoliosis
    General Information
    Mecasermin is contraindicated in individuals with epiphyseal closure. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis may occur more frequently during periods of rapid growth and can lead to osteonecrosis. Cases of slipped capital femoral epiphysis, with or without osteonecrosis, have been reported in pediatric patients with short stature receiving mecasermin. Evaluate pediatric patients presenting with a new limp or complaints of hip or knee pain. Monitor individuals with scoliosis receiving mecasermin for disease progression, which can occur in individuals who experience rapid growth. Growth hormone has not been shown to increase the occurrence of scoliosis.
    Headline
    infants, neonates, premature neonates
    General Information
    Additionally, mecasermin contains benzyl alcohol as a preservative and use should be avoided in neonates and infants. A 'gasping syndrome' characterized by CNS depression, metabolic acidosis, and gasping respirations has been associated with benzyl alcohol dosages more than 99 mg/kg/day in neonates. However, the minimum amount of benzyl alcohol at which toxicity may occur is unknown and low-birth-weight and premature neonates may be more likely to develop toxicity.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no available data on mecasermin use in pregnant women. Exposure to mecasermin during pregnancy is unlikely because the drug is not indicated for use after epiphyseal closure. In animal reproduction studies, there were no observed embryo-fetal development abnormalities with intravenous administration of mecasermin to pregnant rats and rabbits during fetal organogenesis given at exposures up to 11 and 3 times the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) of 0.24 mg/kg/day based on body surface area (BSA), respectively. Animal studies are not always predictive of human response to drugs in pregnancy, and mecasermin should be used in pregnancy only if clearly needed.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Adverse events related to mecasermin use were generally considered mild to moderate by the investigators and no patient drop out secondary to adverse events was noted in any clinical trial.In studies for the use of mecasermin in ALS, injection site reaction was noted following subcutaneous administration. In addition, the manufacturer reports dermatological conditions, including lipohypertrophy and bruising, in greater than 5% of patients; lipodystrophy reactions can be minimized by rotating injection sites. Non-dose-dependent effects were seen in a double-blind placebo controlled trial involving 266 patients; 232 patients (87%) reported pain, inflammation, bleeding, or other non-specific administration site complaints. A small percentage of patients reported systemic injection site reactions (2—5%). Most reactions were considered mild and there was no clinically significant difference in the incidence of reactions between the two treatment groups (86% and 87%) and the control group (89%).Overt changes in facial appearance, dose dependent facial edema (1—9%) and increases in hair growth or texture (7—15%) were observed in the patients of many studies. Soft tissue thickening of the face increased the prominence of the eyebrow ridge (glabella), eyebrows, nasal tip, and lips and occurred most commonly during patients' puberty. These changes subsided after discontinuation of therapy.Neurologic system disorders including headache (64%) , dizziness (> 5%), seizures (> 5%), facial nerve paralysis, and increased intracranial pressure (intracranial hypertension) (4%) with papilledema, visual impairment, headache, and nausea/vomiting have been reported in patients treated with rh-IGF-1. Symptoms usually occur within the first month of treatment and are more common in adult patients. Of patients who experience intracranial hypertension, the majority have resolution of associated symptoms after discontinuation of therapy; symptoms did not recur after restart of therapy at a reduced dosage. Earlier trials with higher doses of mecasermin (120 to 140 mcg/kg/day) noted a deterioration of retinopathy (7%) often with optic disk swelling. Because of these findings, funduscopic examination of patients is recommended at the initiation of therapy and periodically throughout treatment.As with other protein based medications, antibody formation occurred in patients receiving mecasermin. Anti-IGF-1 antibodies were present at one or more of the periodic assessments in 14/23 (61%) of children with Primary IGFD treated for 2 years. However, no clinical consequences of these antibodies were observed (i.e., allergic reactions or attenuation of growth). While not specifically reported with mecasermin, because any medication has the potential to induce a hypersensitivity response, patients should be cautioned to report any signs or symptoms of anaphylactoid reactions.Among 71 patients pooled from five clinical trials, hypoglycemia was reported in 30 patients (42%). Most cases of hypoglycemia were mild or moderate in severity. Five patients had severe hypoglycemia (requiring assistance and treatment) on one or more occasion and 4 patients experienced hypoglycemic seizures/loss of consciousness on one or more occasion. Of the 30 patients reporting hypoglycemia, 14 (47%) had a history of hypoglycemia prior to treatment. The frequency of hypoglycemia was highest in the first month of treatment, and episodes were more frequent in younger children. Symptomatic hypoglycemia was generally avoided when a meal or snack was consumed shortly (i.e., 20 minutes) before or after administration.Lymphadenopathy, specifically tonsillar hypertrophy, was noted in 11/71 (15%) patients in the first 1—2 years of therapy with less tonsillar growth in subsequent years. The hypertrophy caused snoring during sleep and, less commonly, discomfort when eating solid foods. Tonsillectomy or tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy was performed in 7 patients, of which 3 had obstructive sleep apnea with full resolution after the procedure.Ear and labyrinth complications of mecasermin therapy were reported in more than 5% of patients. These included otitis media and serious otitis media, hypoacusis, fluid in the middle ear, ear pain, and abnormal tympanometry. Description
  Text
  Mecasermin (rh-IGF-1) is a recombinant product, manufactured by genetic modification, to be identical to that of endogenous human insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), which is the primary mediator of growth hormone. Mecasermin is indicated for the long-term treatment of growth failure in children 2 years and older with severe primary IGF-1 deficiency (IGFD) or with growth hormone (GH) gene deletion who have developed neutralizing antibodies to GH. Severe primary IGFD is defined by height standard deviation score less than or equal to -3, basal IGF-1 standard deviation score less than or equal to -3 and normal or elevated GH. Mecasermin is not a substitute to GH for approved GH indications and is not indicated for use in patients with secondary forms of IGF-1 deficiency, such as GH deficiency, malnutrition, hypothyroidism, or chronic treatment with pharmacologic doses of anti-inflammatory corticosteroids. In clinical studies, the average height velocity increased from a baseline of 2.8 cm/year to 8 cm/year in the first year (p less than 0.0001). The height velocity ranged from 4.3 to 5.8 cm/year in years 2 though 8 of treatment. The mean change in chronological age was 4.9 years and the mean change in bone age was 5.3 years. The most common adverse reactions associated with mecasermin are hypoglycemia, local and systemic hypersensitivity, and tonsillar hypertrophy.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mecasermin (rh-IGF-1) is a recombinant product consisting of 70 amino acids in a single polypeptide chain. It is identical in chemical structure and activity to that of endogenous human insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), which is the primary mediator of growth hormone. Under normal circumstances, growth hormone (GH) binds to its receptor in the liver, and other tissues, and stimulates the synthesis and secretion of IGF-1. In target tissues, the Type 1 IGF-1 receptor, which is homologous to the insulin receptor, is activated by IGF-1, which leads to intracellular signaling and stimulates multiple processes resulting in statural growth. The metabolic actions of IGF-1 are in part directed at stimulating the uptake of glucose, fatty acids, and amino acids so that metabolism supports growing tissues.
 
Tissue growth: Skeletal growth occurs at the cartilage growth plates of the epiphyses where stem cells divide to produce new cartilage cells or chondrocytes. The growth of chondrocytes is directly stimulated by IGF-1 and growth hormone (GH). The chondrocytes become calcified so that new bone is formed; this allows the length of the bones to increase, resulting in skeletal growth. In addition, mecasermin therapy has been documented to increase bone density. Longitudinal growth occurs until the cartilage growth plates fuse, known as epiphyseal closure, at the end of puberty. Systemic IGF-1 mitogenic activity leads to an increase in size and number of cells thus promoting organ and whole body growth.
 
Metabolic changes: IGF-1 suppresses hepatic glucose production and stimulates peripheral glucose utilization and therefore has a hypoglycemic potential. IGF-1 has inhibitory effects on insulin secretion.

  Mecasermin, recombinant, rh-IGF-1 is administered by subcutaneous injection. The total IGF-1 volume of distribution after subcutaneous administration in patients with severe primary IGFD is estimated to be 0.257 (+/- 0.073) L/kg at a mecasermin dose of 0.045 mg/kg, and is estimated to increase as the dose increases. Both the liver and the kidney have been shown to metabolize IGF-1. IGF-1 is bound to at least six distinct IGF binding proteins (IGFBP) and is expected to be more than 80% bound as a complex with IGFBP-3 and an acid-labile subunit in the blood. IGFBP-3 is greatly reduced in patients with severe primary IGFD, resulting in increased IGF-1 clearance. Clearance of mecasermin is inversely proportional to IGFBP-3 concentrations, and CL/F is estimated to be 0.04 L/hour/kg at 0.5 mcg/mL IGFBP-3 and 0.01 L/hour/kg at 3 mcg/mL IGFBP-3; the latter is the median IGFBP-3 in individuals with normal IGF-1 serum concentrations.
