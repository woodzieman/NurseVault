Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Iodoquinol use is limited by the incidence and severity of adverse effects. Patients may experience mild abdominal pain or cramps, diarrhea, and nausea/vomiting. Nail discoloration, hair discoloration, colored sweat, and skin discoloration may occur with iodoquinol therapy. Patients may notice a yellow-brown staining believed to be due to the drug's high iodine content; the discoloration is temporary and resolves after discontinuation of the drug. Iodine and drugs that contain pharmacologic amounts of iodine (like iodoquinol) may cause overt thyroid disease such as iodine-induced hypothyroidism (with associated goiter) or hyperthyroidism. Thyroid function tests can be altered for months after therapy discontinuation. Peripheral neuropathy has been reported when moderate doses are used for more than 3 weeks. Other adverse effects reported included pruritus, skin irritation, pruritus ani, iodine-related dermatitis (vegetative or tuberous iododerma), rash (unspecified), acneiform rash, bullous rash, urticaria or iodine-related anaphylactoid reactions, fever, chills, headache, vertigo, malaise, and, rarely, alopecia and agranulocytosis. There is one case report of seizures occurring with the use of iodoquinol 420 mg PO three times daily; other causes for seizures were ruled out. Iodoquinol has been associated with serious adverse effects of the eye, leading to blindness. Iodoquinol may cause optic neuritis and optic atrophy when given in large doses (greater than 1.95 g) or with long term use (over months). The mechanism(s) by which optic nerve damage and inflammation occur is/are unknown. Patients usually experience dyschromatopsia of the red/green axis. The syndrome of subacute myelo-optic neuropathy (SMON) has been reported in thousands of cases in Japan and causally linked to iodoquinol when it was available in that country without a prescription. While the dosage regimens used for amebic disease rarely result in these serious ocular effects, iodoquinol dosages and durations of therapy should be limited to recommended regimens.

Description
  Text
  Iodoquinol (diiodohydroxyquin, di-iodohydroxyquinoline) is an oral amebicide used to treat patients infected with protozoa, especially Entamoeba histolytica. Iodoquinol is effective in killing intraluminal parasites in asymptomatic cyst passers and symptomatic patients, and can be used alone in mild cases or in asymptomatic carriers (noninvasive or luminal amebiasis). In more severe cases, iodoquinol should be used after systemically absorbed amebicide therapy (e.g., metronidazole) is completed. Entamoeba histolytica is not endemic in the United States, but travelers to high-risk areas where amebiasis is endemic may acquire the infection during their travel. The incidence of amebiasis has increased in the southwestern United States most likely due to emigration from Mexico. Iodoquinol may also have some antibacterial and antifungal properties. However, due to the potential for serious side effects with longer durations of use, controversies exist concerning iodoquinol for off-label uses such as the treatment of Blastocystis hominis infection, pulmonary aspergillosis, and acrodermatitis enteropathica (a rare, potentially fatal inherited zinc deficiency disorder). Iodoquinol has also been applied topically treat dermatitis and skin infections (see separate Hydrocortisone; Iodoquinol and Aloe polysaccharide; Iodoquinol monographs). The FDA approved iodoquinol in 1953.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The mechanism of amebicide activity is unknown. Iodoquinol is poorly absorbed and remains primarily in the intestinal lumen (luminal amebicide). Iodoquinol is active against both the trophozoite and encysted forms of the parasite. In general, iodoquinol is considered active against the following organisms in vitro: Entamoeba histolytica, Dientamoeba fragilis, Balantidium coli, and Blastocystis hominis. Iodoquinol may have antibacterial and antifungal properties. Iodoquinol has been used in the treatment of diarrhea due to Blastocystis hominis, vaginitis due to Candida sp., and pulmonary aspergillosis; however, its use for these indications is controversial.
 
Iodoquinol is a potent metal binding agent, chelating zinc and other minerals in the intestine. It has been proposed that iodoquinol chelates dietary zinc and aides zinc absorption through the intestinal barrier, allowing zinc to be transported to tissues. Thus iodoquinol serves as a zinc carrier. More likely, iodoquinol affects zinc metabolism or binds a substance in the intestinal tract that competes for zinc, prevents zinc from binding at these sites, and frees the zinc for intestinal absorption. The drug has been studied as a potential treatment for acrodermatitis enteropathica. However, its use in this condition is controversial.
