    Headline
    adolescents, children, infants, neonates
    General Information
    Limit administration of topical corticosteroids in neonates, infants, children, and adolescents to the least amount compatible with an effective therapeutic regimen. Pediatric populations may absorb proportionally larger amounts of topical corticosteroids and thus be more susceptible to systemic toxicity. Pediatric populations may demonstrate greater susceptibility to topical corticosteroid-induced HPA axis suppression and Cushing syndrome than older individuals because of a larger skin surface area to body weight ratio. Additionally, chronic corticosteroid therapy may interfere with the growth and development of pediatric populations.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate and well-controlled studies on the use of tolnaftate; hydrocortisone during human pregnancy. The more potent corticosteroids have been shown to be teratogenic after dermal application in laboratory animals. Do not use topical corticosteroids in large amounts, on large areas, or for prolonged periods in pregnant individuals. Intermittent use of mild to moderate potency topical agents has not been associated with fetal harm. However, the use of high potency topical corticosteroids has been associated with fetal growth restriction, particularly when the cumulative dose throughout pregnancy exceeds 300 grams.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Topical tolnaftate;hydrocortisone is compatible with breast-feeding. Although there are limited data on tolnaftate use during lactation, the drug has poor systemic absorption after topical administration, limiting potential risk to the breastfed child. In vitro data suggest dermal absorption from application of 1% tolnaftate is 0.92 mcg/cm2. Topical corticosteroids can be used safely in lactation as infant exposure through breast milk is minimal. To minimize any potential risk, use the lowest potent corticosteroid necessary on the smallest area of skin possible. Use care to ensure the infant's skin does not come into direct contact with the treated area. Do not apply high potency corticosteroids to the nipple or areola, as the child could ingest medication from the skin, which increases the risk of iatrogenic hypertension. Water-miscible creams are preferred over ointments to reduce exposure to mineral paraffin. Before breastfeeding, thoroughly wipe off the treated area to prevent transfer of medication to the child. 70364

    Headline
    Cushing syndrome, hyperglycemia, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) suppression
    General Information
    Systemic absorption of topical corticosteroids can produce reversible hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) suppression, manifestations of Cushing syndrome, hyperglycemia, and glucosuria. Conditions that augment systemic absorption include application of more potent corticosteroids, use over large surface areas, prolonged use, and the addition of occlusive dressings. Periodically evaluate for evidence of HPA axis suppression in individuals receiving a large dose of a potent topical corticosteroid applied to a large surface area or under an occlusive dressing by using the urinary free cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation tests. If HPA axis suppression is noted, attempt to withdraw the corticosteroid, reduce the frequency of application, or substitute a less potent steroid. Recovery of HPA axis function is generally prompt and complete upon discontinuation of the corticosteroid. Infrequently, signs and symptoms of corticosteroid withdrawal may occur, requiring supplemental systemic corticosteroids. HPA axis suppression, Cushing syndrome, and intracranial hypertension have been reported in children receiving topical corticosteroids. Manifestations of adrenal suppression in children include linear growth retardation, delayed weight gain, low plasma cortisol concentrations, and absence of response to ACTH stimulation. Manifestations of intracranial hypertension include bulging fontanelles, headaches, and bilateral papilledema.

Description
  Text
  Tolnaftate; Hydrocortisone is a topical product indicated for the treatment of fungal infection of the skin, skin around the nail, and for the relief of inflammatory and pruritic manifestations of corticosteroid-responsive dermatoses. Tolnaftate has antifungal properties, and hydrocortisone is a corticosteroid. Topical corticosteroids have anti-inflammatory, anti-pruritic and vasoconstrictive actions.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Tolnaftate: Tolnaftate is a reversible and noncompetitive inhibitor of fungal squalene epoxidase. The membrane bound epoxidase plays a key role in the biosynthetic pathway from acetate to sterols. The blockade of this enzyme results in the accumulation of squalene and a deficiency of ergosterol. Tolnaftate has been shown to distort hyphae and stunt mycelial growth in susceptible fungi. Resistance to tolnaftate has been reported only rarely. However, the potential to develop resistance by the action of multidrug efflux transporters does exist.
Hydrocortisone: Hydrocortisone has anti-inflammatory, antipruritic, and vasoconstrictive properties. The exact mechanism of anti-inflammatory activity is unclear for topical steroids. However, there is some evidence that a correlation exists between vasoconstrictor potency and therapeutic efficacy. Corticosteroids are thought to act by the induction of phospholipase A2 inhibitory proteins (lipocortins). These proteins may control the biosynthesis of potent mediators of inflammation such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes by inhibiting the release of their common precursor, arachidonic acid, which is released from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase A2.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Topical Route
    Description
    Tolnaftate: There is no data regarding the percutaneous absorption of tolnaftate.
Hydrocortisone: The extent of percutaneous absorption of topical steroids is determined by many factors including the vehicle, the integrity of the epidermal barrier, and the use of occlusive dressings. Hydrocortisone is absorbed through normal intact skin. Inflammation or inflammatory disease processes in the skin can increase percutaneous absorption. Occlusive dressings substantially increase percutaneous absorption of steroids.

  Text
  Tolnaftate; hydrocortisone is administered topically. There are no data available on the pharmacokinetics of tolnaftate. It is unknown if tolnaftate is absorbed through the epidermis or if detectable systemic concentrations of tolnaftate are achieved in the blood. Once absorbed through the skin, topical corticosteroids, including hydrocortisone, are handled through pharmacokinetic pathways similar to systemically administered corticosteroids. Corticosteroids are bound to plasma proteins in varying degrees. Corticosteroids are metabolized primarily in the liver and then are excreted by the kidneys. Some topical corticosteroids and their metabolites are also excreted by the bile.
