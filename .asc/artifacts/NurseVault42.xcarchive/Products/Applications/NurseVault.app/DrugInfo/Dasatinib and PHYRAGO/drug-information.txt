Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Treatment with dasatinib is associated with severe bone marrow suppression (Grade 3 or 4) including thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, and anemia. Severe myelosuppression is more frequent in patients with advanced phase chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) and Philadelphia-chromosome (Ph+) acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL) than chronic phase CML. Dasatinib should be used only by clinicians experienced in the use of cancer therapy. Complete blood counts should be done weekly for the first 2 months during dasatinib treatment, and then monthly thereafter, or as clinically indicated. Myelosuppression is generally reversible and is managed by withholding dasatinib or with dose reduction (see Dosage). Hematopoietic growth factors have been used in patients with resistant myelosuppression. Patients with an active infection including fungal infection should be treated prior to receiving dasatinib or therapy should be interrupted. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with chemotherapy.
 
Dasatinib has been associated with platelet dysfunction in vitro. Caution should be exercised if patients are required to take medications that inhibit platelet function or anticoagulant therapy. During clinical trials, patients were excluded if they took anticoagulants or medications that inhibit platelets. Severe CNS, GI, and other severe hemorrhages, including fatalities, have been reported (see Adverse Reactions). Most bleeding events were associated with severe thrombocytopenia.
 
Dasatinib is associated with fluid retention, which can be severe, including edema, ascites, pleural and pericardial effusions, and pulmonary edema (see Adverse Reactions). Patients who develop symptoms suggestive of pleural effusion such as dyspnea or dry cough should be evaluated by chest x-ray.
 
Dasatinib may cause fetal harm when administered to a pregnant woman (FDA pregnancy risk category D). In animals studies at plasma concentrations below those in humans receiving therapeutic doses, fetal toxicity was noted. Embryo-fetal toxicities included skeletal malformations at multiple sites, reduced ossification, edema, and microhepatia. Dasatinib is not recommended in females who are pregnant or considering becoming pregnant. If dasatinib is used during pregnancy, or if the patient becomes pregnant while taking dasatinib, the patient should be apprised of the potential hazard to the fetus. Sexually active male or female patients should use adequate contraception.
 
Dasatinib therapy may cause infertility. Although the effect of dasatinib on human reproduction has not been studied, results of repeat-dose toxicity studies in multiple animal species indicate the potential for dasatinib to impair reproductive function and fertility.
 
It is unknown whether dasatinib is excreted in breast-milk. Breast-feeding is not recommended if the mother is receiving dasatinib.
 
In vitro data suggest that dasatinib has the potential to prolong cardiac ventricular repolarization (QT prolongation). In single-arm clinical trials in patients with leukemia treated with dasatinib, the mean QTc interval changes from baseline were 3—6 msec (using Frederica's method); the upper 95% confidence intervals for all mean changes from baseline were 500 msec. Dasatinib should be administered with caution to patients who have or may develop prolongation of the QTc interval. These include patients with hypokalemia or hypomagnesemia, patients with congenital long QT syndrome, patients taking antiarrhythmic medicines or other medicines that may lead to QT prolongation, and patients who have received high-cumulative dose anthracycline therapy. Hypokalemia or hypomagnesemia should be corrected prior to dasatinib administration.
 
Dasatinib capsules contain 189 mg of lactose monohydrate per 140 mg daily dose and 135 mg of lactose monohydrate per 100 mg daily dose. Patients with lactose intolerance/lactase deficiency should take appropriate precautions with use.
 
There are no clinical studies of dasatinib in patients with hepatic impairment; clinical trials excluded patients with ALT and/or AST > 2.5 times the upper limit of normal and/or total bilirubin > 2 times the upper limit of normal. Dasatinib doses of 50 mg and 20 mg were evaluated in 8 patients with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B) and 7 patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C), respectively. Compared to 15 control subjects with normal hepatic function who were administered dasatinib 70 mg, no clinically significant differences were observed in Cmax or AUC. However, because metabolism of dasatinib is mainly hepatitic, caution is recommended if dasatinib is administered to patients with hepatic disease/impairment (see Dosage).
 
Do not crush or split dasatinib tablets. Use care to avoid accidental exposure during handling and administration. If dasatinib tablets are crushed or broken, personnel should use disposable chemotherapy gloves. Pregnant women should avoid exposure to crushed and/or broken tablets.
 
The safety and efficacy of dasatinib in children = 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    Use dasatinib with caution in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes.     General Information
    Dasatinib may cause fetal harm or neonatal death when administered to a pregnant woman. Females of reproductive potential should avoid pregnancy; apprise women of the potential hazard to the fetus. Dasatinib crosses the placenta and has been measured in fetal plasma and amniotic fluid at concentrations comparable to maternal plasma. Adverse fetal and infant outcomes reported from women treated with dasatinib during pregnancy include congenital malformations (e.g., neural tube defects), hydrops fetalis, fetal leukopenia and thrombocytopenia, and harmful pharmacological effects.

Interactions
Acetaminophen; Ibuprofen: (Major) Due to the thrombocytopenic and possible platelet inhibiting effects of dasatinib, an additive risk of bleeding may be seen in patients receiving concomitant anticoagulants, NSAIDs, platelet inhibitors (including aspirin), strontium-89 chloride, and thrombolytic agents. In addition, large doses of salicylates (>= 3-4 g/day) can cause hypoprothrombinemia, an additional risk factor for bleeding. Caution should be exercised if patients are required to take medications that inhibit platelet function or anticoagulants concomitantly with dasatinib.
Adagrasib: (Major) Avoid coadministration of dasatinib and adagrasib due to the potential for increased dasatinib exposure and additive risk for QT/QTc prolongation and torsade de pointes (TdP). If coadministration cannot be avoided, consider a dasatinib dose reduction to 40 mg PO daily if original dose was 140 mg daily, 20 mg PO daily if original dose was 100 mg daily, or 20 mg PO daily if original dose was 70 mg daily. Stop dasatinib during use of adagrasib in patients receiving dasatinib 60 mg or 40 mg PO daily. Additionally, consider taking steps to minimize the risk for QT/QTc interval prolongation and TdP, such as electrolyte monitoring and repletion and ECG monitoring. If dasatinib is not tolerated after dose reduction, either discontinue adagrasib or stop dasatinib until adagrasib is discontinued. Allow a washout of approximately 1 week after adagrasib is stopped before increasing the dasatinib dose or reinitiating dasatinib. Dasatinib is a CYP3A substrate, adagrasib is a strong CYP3A inhibitor, and both medications may prolong the QT/QTc interval. Coadministration with another strong CYP3A inhibitor increased the AUC of dasatinib by 5-fold.
Alfuzosin: (Moderate) Use dasatinib with caution in combination with alfuzosin. In vitro studies have shown that dasatinib has the potential to prolong the QT interval. Alfuzosin may prolong the QT interval in a dose-dependent manner.
Aluminum Hydroxide: (Moderate) Separate the administration of dasatinib and antacids by at least 2 hours if concomitant use is required.   Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Pancytopenia was reported in 1—10% of patients, and a common adverse effect of dasatinib is myelosuppression. Grade 3 or 4 myelosuppression is more frequent in patients with advanced phase chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) and Philadelphia-chromosome (Ph+) acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL) than chronic phase CML. Severe neutropenia (grade 3 or 4) was reported in 46% of patients with chronic phase CML, in 68% of patients with accelerated phase CML, in 80% of patients with myeloid blast phase CML, and in 78% of patients each with lymphoid blast phase CML or Ph+ ALL. Severe thrombocytopenia (grade 3 or 4) was reported in 41% of patients with chronic phase CML, in 71% of patients with accelerated phase CML, in 81% of patients with myeloid blast phase CML, and in 78% of patients each with lymphoid blast phase CML or Ph+ ALL. Severe anemia (grade 3 or 4) was reported in 18% of patients with chronic phase CML, in 55% of patients with accelerated phase CML, in 75% of patients with myeloid blast phase CML, and in 45% of patients each with lymphoid blast phase CML or Ph+ ALL. Among patients with chronic phase CML, grade 3 or 4 thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, and anemia were less frequent among patients who got 100 mg once daily as compared with patients who got 70 mg twice daily. Specifically, grade 3/4 neutropenia occurred in 34% of the 100 mg once daily group and in 43% of the 70 mg twice daily group. Grade 3/4 thrombocytopenia occurred in 22% of the 100 mg once daily group and in 38% of the 70 mg twice daily group, and grade 3/4 anemia occurred in 10% of the 100 mg once daily group and in 17% of the 70 mg twice daily group. Regularly monitor complete blood counts - weekly for the first 2 months and then monthly thereafter or as clinically indicated. In patients who experience severe myelosuppression, recovery usually occurs following dose interruption and/or reduction (see Dosage); permanent discontinuation of treatment occurred in 1% of patients. Hematopoietic growth factors have been used in patients with resistant myelosuppression. Fever and febrile neutropenia were reported in 39% and 9% of patients, respectively. Infection (including pneumonia, bacterial, viral, fungal, and non-specified) occurred in 34% of patients and upper respiratory tract infection/inflammation was reported in 26% of patients.
 
Bleeding events have been associated with dasatinib therapy. Of 2182 patients who received dasatinib in clinical trials, 21% had bleeding of any severity. Severe central nervous system hemorrhages such as intracranial bleeding, including fatalities, occurred in 500 msec. Other cardiovascular events reported during dasatinib therapy in leukemia patients included chest pain (unspecified) (13%) and arrhythmias (11%), as well as the following reported in 1—10% of patients: palpitations, angina, cardiomegaly, and myocardial infarction. In < 1% of leukemia patients, the following cardiac adverse events have been reported: pericarditis, ventricular tachycardia, acute coronary syndrome, and myocarditis.
 
GI adverse reactions to dasatinib therapy have included diarrhea (49%), nausea/vomiting (34%/22%), abdominal pain (25%), anorexia (19%), mucositis/stomatitis (16%), weight loss (14%), constipation (14%), abdominal distention (11%), and weight gain (11%). Other GI events occurring in 1—10% of dasatinib patients include dyspepsia, gastritis, colitis, anal fissure, and dysphagia. Significantly elevated hepatic enzymes (Grade 3 or 4), including hyperbilirubinemia, were reported in 1—11% of leukemia patients treated with dasatinib. There was an increased frequency in patients with myeloid or lymphoid blast phase chronic myeloid leukemia and Philadelphia chromosome positive acute lymphocytic leukemia. Elevated hepatic enzymes were usually managed with dose reduction or interruption.
 
Other common adverse reactions reported in leukemia patients treated with dasatinib include headache (40%), musculoskeletal pain (39%), fatigue (39%), pain (unspecified), asthenia (19%), arthralgia (19%), dizziness (14%), neuropathy including peripheral neuropathy (13%), myalgia (12%), chills (11%), and pruritus (11%). Skin rash (unspecified) was reported in 35% of leukemia patients and included exfoliative rash, generalized erythema, milia, erythematous rash, follicular rash, macular rash, maculopapular rash, papular rash, pruritic rash, pustular rash, skin exfoliation, systemic lupus erythematosus rash, urticaria, drug eruption, and vesicular rash. Two cases of panniculitis have been reported in patients with chronic myeloid leukemia treated with dasatinib. Both patients experienced recurrence when dasatinib therapy was restarted at lower doses; one patient responded to corticosteroid therapy, which was given concurrently with reduced dose dasatinib.
 
Grade 3 or 4 hypocalcemia and hypophosphatemia were reported in 2—20% and 11—23% of patients treated with dasatinib, respectively. There was an increased frequency in patients with myeloid or lymphoid blast chronic myeloid leukemia and Philadelphia chromosome positive acute lymphocytic leukemia. Patients developing Grade 3 or 4 hypocalcemia during dasatinib therapy were usually treated with oral calcium supplementation.

Description
  Text
  Dasatinib is an oral multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitor. It is indicated for the treatment of adult patients with previously untreated, chronic-phase Philadelphia chromosome-positive (Ph+) chronic myeloid leukemia (CML); chronic-phase, accelerated-phase, or myeloid or lymphoid blast-phase Ph+ CML with resistance or intolerance to prior therapy including imatinib; and Ph+ acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) with resistance or intolerance to prior therapy. Dasatinib is also indicated for use in pediatric patients 1 year of age and older with chronic-phase Ph+ CML (as a single agent) and newly diagnosed Ph+ ALL in combination with chemotherapy. Severe myelosuppression has been reported; transfusions may be necessary.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Dasatinib is an oral multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitor that was specifically designed to inhibit SRC tyrosine kinase, which acts as a signal transducer in several molecular pathways within the cell, but was also found to inhibit BCR-ABL and imatinib-resistant mutations of BCR-ABL. The BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase is the resultant protein of the chromosome translocation (Philadelphia chromosome, Ph) which is associated with chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) and certain types of acute lymphocytic leukemia (Ph+ ALL). Changes in the BCR-ABL protein, such as point mutations and changes in the conformation of the proteins that change the imatinib-binding site, and gene amplification lead to imatinib-resistance; however, dasatinib's chemical structural allows for activity in the majority of imatinib-resistant stages of CML and Ph+ ALL. Dasatinib more effectively inhibits the BCR-ABL kinase than imatinib. Additional rationale for the clinical response seen with dasatinib include the facts that dasatinib is not a substrate for multidrug resistance protein (P-glycoprotein), which can lead to increased concentrations within hematopoietic cells that highly express this protein and dasatinib also targets many other kinases that may contribute to the cytogenetic response. Interestingly there may not be cross-resistance between dasatinib and imatinib, as studies have identified several BCR-ABL mutations that confer resistance to dasatinib but not imatinib. In vitro studies of head and neck squamous cells and non-small cell lung cancer cells indicate that dasatinib inhibited migration and invasion in all cell lines and induced cell cycle arrest (blocking G1-S transition) and apoptosis in some cell lines. These effects are consistent with dasatinib's inhibition of SRC tyrosine kinase.

  Text
  Dasatinib is administered orally. Different formulations of dasatinib have slight differences in pharmacokinetics. For Sprycel, the apparent volume of distribution is 2,505 L (CV, 93%), the mean terminal half-life is 3 to 5 hours, and the mean apparent oral clearance is 363.8 L/hr (CV, 81.3%). For Phyrago, the apparent volume of distribution is 1,850 L (CV, 59.3%), the mean terminal half-life is 5 hours, and the mean apparent clearance is 241 L/hr (CV, 54.4%). Dasatinib and its active metabolite are highly bound to human plasma proteins (96% and 93%, respectively). Formation of the active metabolite occurs via CYP3A4 metabolism. Other enzymes involved in dasatinib metabolism include flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 (FMO-3) and uridine diphosphate-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT). Concentrations of an active metabolite account for about 5% of dasatinib AUC; therefore, the active metabolite is unlikely to play a role in the activity of dasatinib. Several inactive oxidative metabolites are also present. Dasatinib is eliminated primarily via the feces. Following a single dose of radiolabeled dasatinib, 4% of the radioactivity was recovered in the urine and 85% was recovered in the feces within 10 days. Unchanged dasatinib accounted for 0.1% and 19% of the administered dose in the urine and feces, respectively.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes: CYP3A4
Dasatinib is extensively metabolized by CYP3A4. Dasatinib is also a weak time-dependent inhibitor of CYP3A4. In vitro, dasatinib is a substrate for P-glycoprotein (P-gp).
