Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Dexchlorpheniramine use is contraindicated in patients with known dexchlorpheniramine hypersensitivity or chlorpheniramine hypersensitivity. Because dexchlorpheniramine has been shown to be the predominant active isomer of the racemic compound chlorpheniramine, pharmacologic information between the two agents is generally interchangeable.
 
Dexchlorpheniramine may cause drowsiness. Patients receiving dexchlorpheniramine should be advised to avoid driving or operating machinery until the effects of the drug are known. Alcohol consumption may intensify the sedative effects of the drug and should be avoided.
 
Use of dexchlorpheniramine is contraindicated to treat lower respiratory tract symptoms including asthma. The anticholinergic activity of H1-antagonists may result in thickened bronchial secretions in the respiratory tract thereby aggravating an acute asthmatic attack or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Although antihistamines should be avoided during an acute asthmatic attack, these anticholinergic effects do not preclude the use of antihistamines in all asthmatics or COPD patients, particularly if the respiratory symptom is not a primary component of the illness. Because dexchlorpheniramine exhibits a moderate amount of anticholinergic activity, the use of an H1-antagonist with less anticholinergic activity is preferable when therapy is indicated.
 
The exact metabolic pathway of some first generation antihistamines, such as dexchlorpheniramine, is not available. Chlorpheniramine, a racemic mixture containing dexchlorpheniramine, is extensively metabolized in the liver. Therefore, it is possible that metabolism may be reduced in those with significant hepatic disease receiving dexchlorpheniramine. It is suggested that liver function tests be monitored in this patient population. Dosage adjustments may be required, as drug accumulation or prolonged duration of action can occur in patients with hepatic dysfunction.
 
The anticholinergic effects of dexchlorpheniramine may result in a worsening of symptoms in patients with bladder obstruction, GI obstruction or ileus, stenosing peptic ulcer disease, pyloric stenosis, benign prostatic hypertrophy, or urinary retention. The use of an H1-antagonist with less anticholinergic activity is preferable when therapy is indicated. The elderly are more susceptible to the anticholinergic effects of drugs since there is a decline in endogenous cholinergic activity that occurs with age. The quinidine-like local anesthetic and anticholinergic effects of H1-antagonists are responsible for the adverse cardiac effects which have been observed including tachycardia, ECG changes, hypotension, and cardiac arrhythmias. Although these cardiovascular effects are uncommon, dexchlorpheniramine should be used conservatively in those with cardiac disease since this patient population is susceptible to the anticholinergic effects of the drug. Therefore, considerable caution should be used in patients with controlled or mild hypertension, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, or other cardiac disease because of the anticholinergic effects associated with dexchlorpheniramine.
 
Dexchlorpheniramine should be avoided, if possible, in patients with closed-angle glaucoma. An H1-antagonist with less anticholinergic effects should be used in this patient population. An increase in intraocular pressure may occur from the anticholinergic actions of the drug, precipitating an acute attack of glaucoma. Elderly patients are more susceptible to the anticholinergic effects of dexchlorpheniramine, including possible precipitation of undiagnosed glaucoma. Other ocular effects resulting from the anticholinergic effects of dexchlorpheniramine include dry eyes or blurred vision, which may be of significance in the elderly and wearers of contact lenses.
 
Due to the increased risk of paradoxical CNS stimulation and seizures, do not use dexchlorpheniramine in neonates or premature infants (those with neonatal prematurity). It is generally not recommended to administer the extended-release products to children < 6 years of age. Paradoxical CNS stimulation following antihistamine administration is possible in children and infants. The use of the phenothiazine class of antihistamines has resulted in a number of cases of respiratory depression, sleep apnea, and SIDS in children. The mechanism of this reaction is not yet known; therefore, H1-antagonists should be used with extreme caution in children with a family history of SIDS or sleep apnea. In January 2007, the CDC warned caregivers and healthcare providers of the risk for serious injury or fatal overdose from the administration of cough and cold products to children and infants less than 2 years of age. This warning followed an investigation of the deaths of three (3) infants less than 6 months of age that were attributed to the inadvertent inappropriate use of these products. The symptoms preceding these deaths have not been clearly defined, and there is a lack of conclusive data describing the exact cause of death. The report estimated that 1519 children less than 2 years of age were treated in emergency departments during 2004—2005 for adverse events related to cough and cold medications. In October 2007, the FDA Nonprescription Drug Advisory Committee and the Pediatric Advisory Committee recommended that nonprescription cough and cold products containing pseudoephedrine, dextromethorphan, chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, brompheniramine, phenylephrine, clemastine, or guaifenesin not be used in children less than 6 years of age. In January 2008, the FDA issued a Public Health Advisory recommending that OTC cough and cold products not be used in infants and children less than 2 years. An official ruling regarding the use of these products in children greater than 2 years has not yet been announced. The FDA recommends that if parents and caregivers use cough and cold products in children greater than 2 years, labels should be read carefully, caution should be used when administering multiple products, and only measuring devices specifically designed for use with medications should be used. While some combination cough/cold products containing these ingredients are available by prescription only and are not necessarily under scrutiny by the FDA, clinicians should thoroughly assess each patient's use of similar products, both prescription and nonprescription, to avoid duplication of therapy and the potential for inadvertent overdose.
 
Dexchlorpheniramine is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category B. Early data from animal studies indicated a reduction in fertility with chlorpheniramine; however this was not substantiated in more recent studies. No well controlled trials of dexchlorpheniramine use in human gestation are available. However, in general, H1-antagonists are not recommended for use in the last 2 weeks of pregnancy due to a possible association between these drugs and retrolental fibroplasia in premature neonates. Dexchlorpheniramine should be administered during pregnancy only when the benefits of therapy outweigh the potential risks to the fetus, and use should be limited to short-term, 'as needed' administration under the prescription of a qualified health-care professional. Non-pharmacologic methods (e.g., fluids, and rest) are recommended to be tried first for symptomatic relief of colds or allergies during pregnancy.
 
According to the manufacturer, dexchlorpheniramine use is contraindicated during breast-feeding because H1-antagonists can induce hyperexcitability in the infant and seizures in premature infants. Side effects in breast-feeding infants may manifest as irritability, disturbed sleeping patterns, drowsiness, hyperexcitability, or excessive crying. Single bedtime doses of dexchlorpheniramine, which are short-acting, after the last feeding of the day may be suggested and might minimize infant exposure through the breast milk during feeding hours. The frequent use of dexchlorpheniramine or the use of extended-release dexchlorpheniramine products during breast-feeding should be avoided. Non-drug methods or, if medication is needed, nonsedating antihistamines are preferred alternatives. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
Dexchlorpheniramine use is contraindicated in patients on MAOI therapy. Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) may prolong and intensify the anticholinergic effects of antihistamines. The anticholinergic activity of MAOIs is minimal; however, anticholinergic effects sometimes occur. It is recommended that the concurrent use of MAOIs with drugs possessing anticholinergic activity, such as dexchlorpheniramine, be avoided since their effects and those of other anticholinergic drugs are potentiated and may become severe. Some manufacturers recommend that H1-antagonists not be used within two weeks of therapy with an MAOI (see Drug Interactions).

Allergies to one antihistamine can sometimes lead to cross-reactivity with other chemically-related antihistamines.

    General Information
    Dexchlorpheniramine should not be used in neonates or premature neonates/infants. Due to the risk for serious adverse reactions, the FDA recommends against administration of over the counter (OTC) cough and cold products to neonates, infants and children younger than 2 years of age. When administering OTC medications to older pediatric patients, they advise caregivers to read product labels carefully, use caution when administering multiple products to avoid duplication of ingredients, and use only measuring devices specifically designed for use with medications. Care teams should thoroughly assess the use of similar products, both prescription and nonprescription, to avoid duplication of therapy and the potential for inadvertent overdose.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    The Beers Criteria identifies first-generation antihistamines as potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) for geriatric adults, recommending their avoidance due to their high anticholinergic properties, decreased clearance in older age, the development of tolerance when used as sleep aids, and an increased risk of anticholinergic effects and toxicity compared to younger individuals. These medications should particularly be avoided in patients with dementia or cognitive impairment (due to adverse CNS effects), those at high risk for delirium (which can worsen or trigger new-onset delirium), and men with lower urinary tract symptoms or benign prostatic hyperplasia (due to risks of urinary retention or hesitancy). Antihistamines are more likely to cause dizziness, sedation, and hypotension in geriatric patients.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    No well controlled trials of dexchlorpheniramine use during pregnancy are available; however, chlorpheniramine (parent drug) use as a single agent during pregnancy has been associated with reassuring animal and human data. However, second-generation antihistamines are generally preferred over first-generation antihistamines. Loratadine and cetirizine are acceptable antihistamine alternatives based on their excellent safety data and recommendation in multiple guidelines for use during pregnancy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use dexchlorpheniramine with caution during breast-feeding. Some manufacturers recommend avoidance of dexchlorpheniramine use during breast-feeding in their label due to the availability of alternatives with more safety data. Side effects of sedating antihistamines in breast-feeding infants such as irritability, disturbed sleeping patterns, drowsiness, hyperexcitability, and excessive crying have been reported in a number of cases.   Antihistamines can lower basal prolactin secretion and may interfere with the establishment of lactation. Consider treatment alternatives to dexchlorpheniramine. Guidelines recommend loratadine or cetirizine at the lowest dose as preferred antihistamines in breast-feeding individuals because they are safer with only low levels found in breast milk.

  General Information
  Dexchlorpheniramine, as with other alkylamine antihistamines, has been associated with CNS depression, such as drowsiness and dizziness. Elderly patients may be more susceptible to these adverse reactions. There is considerable individual patient response to sedative effects, so patients should be warned of the potential for impaired cognition. These side effects may disappear after a few days of medication; although, if symptoms persist or are severe, a reduction in dosage or a change to another H1-antagonist may be necessary. Other possible CNS effects include confusion, excitability, euphoria, fatigue, headache, hysteria, incoordination, irritability, nervousness, neuritis, paresthesias, tinnitus, tremor, and vertigo.Dexchlorpheniramine possesses a moderate degree of anticholinergic activity. Adverse cardiovascular responses are likely to be associated with the anticholinergic properties of H1-antagonists or the quinidine-like anesthetic effects. These responses may include sinus tachycardia, extrasystoles, palpitations, and/or cardiac arrhythmias. Alpha-adrenergic blockade may lead to orthostatic hypotension. Hypertension can also occur, but is usually not of clinical significance. Other anticholinergic-related effects include thickening of bronchial secretions, dysuria, mydriasis (rare), xerophthalmia, xerostomia, and/or blurred vision. Elderly patients are more susceptible to these adverse reactions, since endogenous cholinergic activity declines with age.H1-antagonists can cause a paradoxical CNS stimulation; however, this is more likely to occur in children and with high doses. Symptoms may include ataxia, hallucinations, insomnia, palpitations, psychosis, restlessness, and, in severe cases, seizures.Dexchlorpheniramine can cause adverse GI effects including anorexia, constipation, diarrhea, epigastric distress, and nausea/vomiting. Taking dexchlorpheniramine with meals or milk may relieve some of these adverse reactions.Rarely, dexchlorpheniramine has been associated with hematologic dysfunction including agranulocytosis, hemolytic anemia, and thrombocytopenia. In most cases, blood counts returned to normal following discontinuation of dexchlorpheniramine.Other adverse reactions which have been reported with dexchlorpheniramine administration include anaphylactic shock, diaphoresis, diplopia, acute labyrinthitis, aseptic meningitis, menstrual irregularity (e.g. early menses), nasal congestion, nasal dryness (xeromycteria), photosensitivity, polyuria, rash (unspecified), tightness of chest, urticaria, and wheezing.

Elderly patients are more susceptible to these adverse reactions, since endogenous cholinergic activity declines with age.

Description
  Text
  Dexchlorpheniramine is an oral first generation, sedating antihistamine (H1-blocker) of the alkylamine class and is the predominant active isomer of the racemic compound chlorpheniramine. It is used in the treatment of symptoms related to allergy and the common cold, including sneezing, allergic rhinitis or vasomotor rhinitis, allergic conjunctivitis, and mild allergic skin manifestations, like urticaria. The FDA approved dexchlorpheniramine in 1981.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Data indicate that dexchlorpheniramine, the d-isomer of the racemic compound chlorpheniramine, is two times more active than chlorpheniramine, although the therapeutic advantage, if any, is unknown. Dexchlorpheniramine does not prevent the release of histamine, as do cromolyn and nedocromil, but rather competes with free histamine for binding at the H1-receptor sites. Like other antihistamines, dexchlorpheniramine competitively antagonizes the effects of histamine on H1-receptors in the GI tract, uterus, large blood vessels, and bronchial muscle. Blockade of H1-receptors also suppresses the formation of edema, flare, and pruritus that result from histaminic activity. Unlike second generation antihistamines, such as loratadine and cetirizine, which selectively block peripheral H1-receptors, first generation antihistamines, such as dexchlorpheniramine, bind to H1-receptors centrally and peripherally. Thus, sedative effects are more likely to occur with first generation antihistamines. Following prolonged use, tolerance can occur which may be beneficial, dependent on the indication for use, because of reduced sedative effects. H1-antagonists are structurally similar to anticholinergic agents and therefore possess anticholinergic properties of varying degrees. They also have antipruritic effects. Dexchlorpheniramine has high antihistaminic activity, moderate anticholinergic effects, and minimal sedative effects. The drug does not possess antiemetic properties.

    Description
    The absorption of dexchlorpheniramine has not been described; however, in general, H1-antagonists are well absorbed from the GI tract. The onset of action of regular release formulations of chlorpheniramine, a racemic mixture containing dexchlorpheniramine, is about 30—60 minutes.   Text
  Note: Dexchlorpheniramine is the primary active isomer of the racemic compound chlorpheniramine; therefore, the pharmacokinetics of dexchlorpheniramine are likely similar to chlorpheniramine. The following information is primarily based on chlorpheniramine pharmacokinetics.
 
Dexchlorpheniramine is administered orally. The duration of action is between 4—8 hours. Protein binding is approximately 72%. Chlorpheniramine is widely distributed in body tissues and fluids, and it crosses the placenta and is excreted into breast milk. The metabolism and elimination of dexchlorpheniramine have not been described. N-dealkylation produces several metabolites, which are excreted in the urine along with the parent compound. Plasma half-life is between 2—4 hours, but the terminal elimination half-life varies with age. The half-life in healthy adults and children is 20—24 hours and 10—13 hours, respectively. Excretion rates are dependent on the pH of urine and urinary flow, with the rate decreasing as the pH rises and urinary flow decreases.
