Drug Class: Rivaroxaban is an oral selective factor Xa inhibitor anticoagulant used for the treatment and prophylaxis of thromboembolism in several clinical contexts.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 30 mg/day PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): bleeding, epistaxis, gi bleeding, hematoma, intracranial bleeding, menorrhagia, ocular hemorrhage, retroperitoneal bleeding, spinal hematoma, subdural hematoma, vaginal bleeding, back pain, muscle cramps, musculoskeletal pain, anxiety, depression, dizziness, insomnia, paresis, syncope, cholestasis, hepatitis, jaundice, splenic rupture

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  Switching from another anticoagulant to rivaroxaban
Converting from warfarin to rivaroxaban:
Discontinue warfarin and start rivaroxaban as soon as the INR is less than 3 in adults and less than 2.5 in pediatric patients to avoid periods of inadequate anticoagulation.
Converting from an anticoagulant other than warfarin to rivaroxaban:
For low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) or non-warfarin oral anticoagulant, initiate rivaroxaban 0 to 2 hours before the next scheduled evening administration of other anticoagulant and omit that dose of other medication.
For continuous infusion of unfractionated heparin, stop the infusion and initiate rivaroxaban simultaneously.
 
Switching from rivaroxaban to another anticoagulant
Converting from rivaroxaban to warfarin:
Rivaroxaban affects INR; therefore, INR measurements obtained during coadministration with warfarin may not be useful for determining the appropriate warfarin dose. Measure INR just before next rivaroxaban dose to limit interference. INR measurement can be considered reliable 24 hours after rivaroxaban discontinuation.
Adults, oral option:
If continuous anticoagulation is necessary, continue rivaroxaban and begin warfarin. Discontinue rivaroxaban when INR is therapeutic.
Adults, parenteral option:
Discontinue rivaroxaban and administer a parenteral anticoagulant and warfarin at the time of the next scheduled rivaroxaban dose. Once a stable INR of 2 or more is achieved, discontinue the parenteral anticoagulant and continue warfarin.
Pediatric patients:
Continue rivaroxaban and begin warfarin. After 2 days of coadministration, obtain INR prior to the next scheduled rivaroxaban dose. Coadminister warfarin and rivaroxaban until the INR is 2 or more.
Converting from rivaroxaban to an anticoagulant with rapid onset (not warfarin):
Discontinue rivaroxaban and administer the first dose of the other anticoagulant (oral or parenteral) at the time that the next dose of rivaroxaban would have been administered.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  Contraindications And Precautions
    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    premature discontinuation
    General Information
    Avoid premature discontinuation of rivaroxaban, as stopping therapy without adequate alternative anticoagulation increases the risk of thrombotic events. During clinical trials in atrial fibrillation, an increased rate of stroke was observed in individuals transitioned from rivaroxaban to warfarin. If discontinuation is necessary for reasons other than pathological bleeding or completion of therapy, consider alternative anticoagulation.
    Headline
    neuraxial procedure, spinal deformity, spinal hematoma, spinal surgery
    General Information
    Carefully consider the potential benefits and risks before performing a neuraxial procedure in individuals who are receiving or are expected to receive rivaroxaban. Epidural or spinal hematoma, which may result in long-term or permanent paralysis, can occur in anticoagulated individuals undergoing neuraxial anesthesia or spinal puncture. Risk factors for epidural or spinal hematoma include use of an indwelling epidural catheter, concomitant use of other agents that affect hemostasis (e.g., NSAIDs, platelet inhibitors, other anticoagulants), a history of spinal deformity or spinal surgery, and traumatic or repeated epidural or spinal puncture. To reduce the risk of bleeding, consider the pharmacokinetic profile of rivaroxaban and perform placement or removal of an epidural catheter or lumbar puncture when the anticoagulant effect is expected to be low; however, the exact timing to reach low anticoagulant effect is not known. Do not remove an epidural or intrathecal catheter until at least 2 half-lives have elapsed after the last dose of rivaroxaban (approximately 18 hours in patients aged 20 to 45 years and 26 hours in patients aged 60 to 76 years), and do not administer the next dose until at least 6 hours after catheter removal. Delay rivaroxaban administration for 24 hours if a traumatic puncture occurs. Monitor individuals undergoing these procedures frequently for neurological impairment and instruct them to report symptoms immediately. If an epidural or spinal hematoma is suspected, urgently diagnose and treat, including consideration of spinal cord decompression.
    Headline
    bleeding, bleeding disorder, major bleeding
    General Information
    Rivaroxaban is contraindicated in individuals with active major bleeding. It increases the risk of bleeding and can cause serious and potentially fatal bleeding. The risk of bleeding is higher in individuals with a history of bleeding or a bleeding disorder. Additionally, the risk may be increased with concomitant use of drugs affecting hemostasis, such as aspirin and other antiplatelet agents, other antithrombotic agents, thrombolytic agents, chronic use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), and serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs). Rivaroxaban should not be used for primary venous thromboembolism prophylaxis in acutely ill medical individuals at high risk for bleeding, which includes the following conditions: history of bronchiectasis, pulmonary cavitation, pulmonary hemorrhage, active cancer (i.e. undergoing acute, in-hospital cancer therapy), active gastrointestinal ulcer or history of bleeding within 3 months of starting treatment, or dual antiplatelet therapy.
    Headline
    planned procedure
    General Information
    Discontinue rivaroxaban at least 24 hours before a planned procedure if anticoagulation must be discontinued to reduce the risk of bleeding. When deciding whether a procedure should be delayed until 24 hours after the last rivaroxaban dose was administered, weigh the increased risk of bleeding against the urgency of the surgical procedure or intervention. Following the surgical procedure or intervention, restart rivaroxaban as soon as hemostasis has been established. If oral therapy is not possible, consider administration of a parenteral anticoagulant.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Avoid use of rivaroxaban in individuals with moderate or severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B or Child-Pugh class C), hepatic failure or any hepatic disease associated with coagulopathy, as drug exposure and bleeding risk may be increased in these individuals.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Rivaroxaban dosage adjustments or treatment avoidance may be required in adults with renal impairment or renal failure depending on the indication and concomitant use of combined P-gp and moderate CYP3A inhibitors. In children and adolescents, avoid rivaroxaban in those with moderate or severe impairment (eGFR less than 50 mL/minute/1.73m2) due to limited clinical data. In neonates and infants, avoid rivaroxaban if serum creatinine is above the 97.5th percentile, as no clinical data are available. Rivaroxaban exposure and pharmacodynamic effects are increased in individuals with renal impairment. Assess renal function before initiating therapy and periodically during treatment, particularly in situations that may lead to renal decline. Discontinue rivaroxaban if acute renal failure develops during therapy, except in cases of nonvalvular atrial fibrillation where dose adjustment may be appropriate.
    Headline
    mitral stenosis, prosthetic heart valves
    General Information
    Rivaroxaban is not recommended in individuals with prosthetic heart valves, moderate to severe mitral stenosis, or those who have undergone transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Use in individuals with other valve procedures has not been studied. In the GALILEO study, individuals who received rivaroxaban after TAVR experienced higher rates of death and bleeding compared with those who received an antiplatelet regimen.
    Headline
    antiphospholipid antibody syndrome
    General Information
    Rivaroxaban is not recommended in individuals with triple-positive antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS), defined as positivity for lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, and anti-beta-2 glycoprotein I antibodies. Higher rates of recurrent thrombotic and bleeding events have occurred with use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in this population compared with vitamin K antagonists.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    According to the Beers Criteria, avoid rivaroxaban in geriatric adults for long-term treatment of nonvalvular atrial fibrillation or VTE in favor of safer alternatives. At doses used for long-term treatment of VTE or nonvalvular atrial fibrillation, rivaroxaban appears to have a higher risk of major bleeding and GI bleeding in older adults than other DOACs, particularly apixaban. However, use of rivaroxaban may be reasonable in certain situations, as all DOACs confer a lower risk of intracranial hemorrhage than warfarin. Avoid use in patients with CrCl less than 15 mL/minute due to lack of safety and efficacy evidence. Reduce the rivaroxaban dosage in patients with CrCl 15 to 50 mL/minute, following manufacturer recommendations based on indication-specific dosing.
    Headline
    labor, obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    Use of rivaroxaban during pregnancy and obstetric delivery may increase the risk of bleeding in both the pregnant person and the fetus or neonate. This may result in pregnancy-related hemorrhage and/or emergent delivery. Dosing for rivaroxaban during pregnancy has not been established. Because the anticoagulant effect of rivaroxaban cannot be monitored with standard laboratory testing, any symptoms of bleeding or blood loss (e.g., drop in hemoglobin and/or hematocrit, hypotension, fetal distress) should be promptly evaluated. In addition, there is a risk of epidural or spinal hematoma associated with the use of rivaroxaban in patients receiving neuraxial anesthesia during labor and obstetric delivery. There are limited data on the use of rivaroxaban during pregnancy to inform a drug-associated risk of adverse developmental outcomes. Rivaroxaban crosses the placenta in in vitro human placenta perfusion models and in animals. In animal studies, increased fetal toxicity (increased resorptions, decreased number of live fetuses, and decreased fetal body weight) was observed in rabbits exposed to rivaroxaban during organogenesis at exposures approximately 4 times the human exposure based on AUC. Decreased fetal body weight was observed in rats exposed to rivaroxaban during organogenesis at exposures approximately 14 times the human exposure. Additionally, peripartal maternal bleeding and maternal and fetal death occurred in rats at a rivaroxaban dose approximately 6 times the maximum human exposure.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with rivaroxaban. Discuss pregnancy planning with people of reproductive potential requiring anticoagulation. Assess the risk of clinically significant uterine bleeding, potentially requiring gynecological surgical interventions, in people of reproductive potential and those with abnormal uterine bleeding.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Monitor neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero for bleeding. Based on the pharmacologic activity of Factor Xa inhibitors and their potential to cross the placenta, bleeding may occur at any site in the neonate.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use rivaroxaban with caution during breast-feeding. Rivaroxaban is present in human milk. Limited data report that rivaroxaban is present in human milk in small amounts, with a relative infant dose of 0.68% to 3.78%. Plasma rivaroxaban concentrations in 2 breastfed infants exposed to rivaroxaban via human milk were undetectable. There are no data on its effects on the breastfed child or its effects on milk production.

Intravenous Lipid Emulsions: (Moderate) Drug interactions with fish oil, omega-3 fatty acids (Dietary Supplements) or fish oil, omega-3 fatty acids (FDA-approved) are unclear at this time. However, because fish oil, omega-3 fatty acids inhibit platelet aggregation, caution is advised when fish oils are used concurrently with anticoagulants, platelet inhibitors, or thrombolytic agents. Theoretically, the risk of bleeding may be increased, but some studies that combined these agents did not produce clinically significant bleeding events. In one placebo-controlled, randomized, double-blinded, parallel study, patients receiving stable, chronic warfarin therapy were administered various doses of fish oil supplements to determine the effect on INR determinations. Patients were randomized to receive a 4-week treatment period of either placebo or 3 or 6 grams of fish oil daily. Patients were followed on a twice-weekly basis for INR determinations and adverse reactions. There was no statistically significant difference in INRs between the placebo or treatment period within each group. There was also no difference in INRs found between groups. One episode of ecchymosis was reported, but no major bleeding episodes occurred. The authors concluded that fish oil supplementation in doses of 3-6 grams per day does not have a statistically significant effect on the INR of patients receiving chronic warfarin therapy. However, an increase in INR from 2.8 to 4.3 in a patient stable on warfarin therapy has been reported when increasing the dose of fish oil, omega-3 fatty acids from 1 gram/day to 2 grams/day. The INR decreased once the patient decreased her dose of fish oil to 1 gram/day. This implies that a dose-related effect of fish oil on warfarin may be possible. Patients receiving warfarin that initiate concomitant fish oil therapy should have their INR monitored more closely and the dose of warfarin adjusted accordingly.

In the first study, the increase in bleeding time to 45 minutes was observed in approximately 45% of patients. Approximately 30% of patients in the second study had the event. The change in bleeding time was approximately twice the maximum increase seen with either drug alone. No change in the pharmacokinetic parameters of either drug were noted.
Primidone: (Major) Avoid concomitant use of rivaroxaban with drugs that are combined P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and strong CYP3A4 inducers, such as primidone. Phenobarbital is a strong inducer of both CYP3A4 and P-gp. Because primidone is metabolized to phenobarbital, drug interactions occurring with phenobarbital must be considered when primidone is administered. In a drug interaction study, coadministration of a single dose of rivaroxaban 20 mg with food with a drug that is a combined P-gp and strong CYP3A4 inducer (rifampicin titrated up to 600 mg once daily) led to an approximate decrease of 50% in AUC and an approximate decrease of 22% in Cmax of rivaroxaban. Similar decreases in pharmacodynamic effects were also observed. These decreases in exposure to rivaroxaban may decrease efficacy.

    Headline
    bleeding, epistaxis, GI bleeding, hematoma, intracranial bleeding, menorrhagia, ocular hemorrhage, retroperitoneal bleeding, spinal hematoma, subdural hematoma, vaginal bleeding
    General Information
    Bleeding (up to 28% in adult clinical trials) has been reported during clinical trials of rivaroxaban, and cases of serious, sometimes fatal, bleeding have occurred. Major bleeding, including intracranial bleeding (intraparenchymal, intraventricular, subarachnoid, epidural, and/or subdural hematoma), gastrointestinal bleeding (upper, lower, and rectal GI bleeding), retroperitoneal bleeding, ocular hemorrhage, intra-articular bleeding, pulmonary hemorrhage (including cases associated with bronchiectasis) was reported in less than 1% to 4% of adult patients, and fatal bleeding occurred in 0.2% or less of patients. Rare cases of spinal hematoma have also been reported with rivaroxaban, particularly in association with neuraxial anesthesia or spinal puncture; if signs or symptoms of spinal hematoma are suspected, initiate urgent diagnosis and treatment. In acutely ill medical patients with risk factors such as bronchiectasis, pulmonary cavitation, active cancer, recent or active gastrointestinal bleeding, or dual antiplatelet therapy, rivaroxaban was associated with excess bleeding compared with enoxaparin. Increased bleeding and mortality were also observed in patients who received rivaroxaban after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) compared with an antiplatelet regimen. In pediatric trials, any bleeding was reported in 36% of patients treated with rivaroxaban, with trivial bleeding in 33% to 34%, clinically relevant non-major bleeding in 3% to 6%, and major bleeding (epistaxis) in 1 patient; among patients who had experienced menarche, menorrhagia occurred in 27%. Vaginal bleeding, including abnormal uterine bleeding, has been reported in females of reproductive potential receiving direct oral anticoagulants; some cases required surgical or interventional treatment. Promptly evaluate any signs or symptoms of blood loss and discontinue rivaroxaban in patients with active pathological bleeding. A specific agent to reverse the anti-factor Xa activity of rivaroxaban is not available. Since rivaroxaban is highly protein bound, it is not expected to be dialyzable. Protamine sulfate and vitamin K are not expected to reverse the anticoagulant activity of rivaroxaban. The efficacy and safety of procoagulant reversal agents such as prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs), activated PCC (APCC), or recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) have not been established in clinical studies. Monitoring the anticoagulant effect of rivaroxaban using clotting tests such as PT, INR, aPTT, or anti-factor Xa activity is not recommended.

Description
  Text
  Rivaroxaban is an oral selective factor Xa inhibitor anticoagulant used for the treatment and prophylaxis of thromboembolism in several clinical contexts. Similar to apixaban and dabigatran, rivaroxaban has a rapid onset of effect and predictable anticoagulant effects; routine laboratory monitoring is not required. Rivaroxaban was FDA-approved in 2011.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Rivaroxaban is an oral factor Xa inhibitor that selectively blocks the active site of factor Xa and does not require a cofactor such as Anti-thrombin III for activity. Rivaroxaban inhibits free factor Xa and prothrombinase activity; inhibition of factor Xa decreases the generation of thrombin. Rivaroxaban has no direct effect on platelet aggregation, but indirectly inhibits platelet aggregation induced by thrombin.]

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Rate and extent of absorption are similar between the tablet and oral suspension. Absolute bioavailability is dose-dependent. Bioavailability of the 2.5 mg and 10 mg tablets is estimated to be 80% and 100%, respectively, and is not affected by food. Rivaroxaban 20 mg, administered in the fasted state, has a bioavailability of approximately 66%; coadministration with food increases the bioavailability (the mean AUC and Cmax increased by 39% and 76%, respectively). The Tmax of rivaroxaban occurs 2 to 4 hours after oral administration. Absorption is dependent on the site of drug release in the gastrointestinal tract. A 29% and 56% decrease in AUC and Cmax was reported when rivaroxaban granulate was released in the proximal small intestine compared to values obtained with tablet administration. Exposure is further reduced when drug is released in the distal small intestine or ascending colon. Mean AUC and Cmax of a crushed 20 mg tablet mixed in applesauce was comparable to that of a whole tablet. When the crushed tablet was suspended in water and given via nasogastric tube followed by a liquid meal, AUC was comparable but Cmax was 18% lower.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    Mild hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh A) did not significantly affect the pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic parameters of rivaroxaban after a single 10 mg dose. However, in patients with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh B), total body clearance was reduced leading to increased exposure to rivaroxaban; the AUC and Cmax were increased by 2.27-fold and 1.27-fold, respectively, compared with healthy subjects. In addition, for inhibition of Factor Xa, increases in the area under the effect-time curve and the maximum effect were observed in patients with moderate hepatic impairment. Prolongation of prothrombin time was also significantly increased in patients with moderate hepatic impairment. No patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C) were studied.
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    Rivaroxaban systemic exposure and pharmacodynamic activity increases in patients with renal impairment compared to healthy controls. The AUC increased 44% to 64%, factor Xa inhibition increased 33% to 100%, and PT prolongation increased 33% to 158% in subjects with renal impairment. After a single rivaroxaban 15 mg dose in end-stage renal disease (ESRD) subjects given 3 hours after the completion of a 4-hour hemodialysis session, rivaroxaban exposure was 56% higher than in subjects with normal renal function. When rivaroxaban was given 2 hours before a 4-hour hemodialysis session with a dialysate flow rate of 600 mL/minute and a blood flow rate of 320 to 400 mL/minute, rivaroxaban exposure was 47% higher compared to those with normal renal function. Hemodialysis had no significant impact on rivaroxaban exposure. Protein binding is similar in healthy patients and those with ESRD (86% to 89%).
    Name
    Pediatrics
    Description
    In vitro plasma binding of rivaroxaban is approximately 90% in pediatric patients 6 months to 9 years. Like adults, the rate and extent of absorption are similar between the tablet and oral suspension in pediatric patients. Tmax occurs approximately 1.5 to 2.2 hours after repeated dosing. The half-life of rivaroxaban increases with increasing age; the mean half-life is 1.6 hours in neonates and infants younger than 6 months, 1.9 hours in infants and children 6 to 23 months, 3 hours in children 2 to 12 years, and 4.2 hours in adolescents. Rivaroxaban produces dose-dependent inhibition of factor Xa activity. In children, the correlation between anti-factor Xa to plasma concentrations is linear with a slope close to 1.
    Name
    Geriatric

    The terminal elimination half-life of rivaroxaban is 11 to 13 hours in the elderly aged 60 to 76 years as compared with 5 to 9 hours in healthy subjects aged 20 to 45 years.

  Text
  Rivaroxaban is administered orally. Plasma protein binding is approximately 92% to 95%, primarily to albumin. Vd at steady state is approximately 50 L in healthy adult subjects. Oxidative degradation catalyzed by CYP3A4/5 and CYP2J2 and hydrolysis are the major sites of biotransformation. Unchanged rivaroxaban is the predominant moiety in plasma with no major or active circulating metabolites. Unchanged drug is excreted into urine, mainly via active tubular secretion and to a lesser extent via glomerular filtration (approximate 5:1 ratio); 36% and 7% is recovered in the urine and feces, respectively, as unchanged drug. Rivaroxaban is a low-clearance drug, with a systemic clearance of approximately 10 L/hour. The terminal elimination half-life of rivaroxaban is 5 to 9 hours in healthy patients aged 20 to 45 years.
 
Rivaroxaban produces dose-dependent inhibition of factor Xa activity. Clotting tests such as prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), and HepTest are also prolonged dose-dependently. Monitoring for safety and efficacy with these parameters or the international normalized ratio (INR) is not recommended.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4, CYP3A5, CYP2J2, P-gp, ABCG2/BCRP
Rivaroxaban is a substrate of CYP3A4/5, CYP2J2, and the P-gp and ATP-binding cassette G2 (ABCG2) transporters (also known as breast cancer resistance protein [BCRP]). Rivaroxaban's affinity for influx transporter proteins is unknown. Inhibitors and inducers of these CYP450 enzymes or transporters may result in changes in rivaroxaban exposure. Concomitant use of combined P-gp and strong CYP3A inhibitors can increase rivaroxaban exposure while concomitant use of combined P-gp and strong CYP3A inducers can decrease exposure. In vitro studies indicate that rivaroxaban neither inhibits the major cytochrome P450 enzymes CYP1A2, 2C8, 2C9, 2C19, 2D6, 2J2, and 3A nor induces CYP1A2, 2B6, 2C19, or 3A. In vitro data also indicates a low rivaroxaban inhibitory potential for P-gp and ABCG2/BCRP transporters. However, no significant pharmacokinetic interactions were observed in studies comparing concomitant use of rivaroxaban with midazolam (substrate of CYP3A4), digoxin (substrate of P-gp), or atorvastatin (substrate of CYP3A4 and P-gp) in healthy volunteers.

Administration of 1 mg/mL oral suspension to children
Suspension is for oral use only. Only use the oral dosing syringe provided with the suspension to administer the dose. If the oral dosing syringe is missing, lost, or damaged, contact a physician or pharmacist. The oral dosing syringe has both dosing in milliliters and with a color band. The color band is an alternate aid to ensure that the correct, prescribed dose is withdrawn from the bottle.
If using the mL side of the syringe, the top of the plunger should line up with the prescribed mL indicator.
If using the color side of the syringe, the top of the plunger should line up with the prescribed mL indicator dose line at the bottom of the color band.
If the prescribed dose is more than 5 mL, the dosing syringe will have to be used more than once.
For a 7.5 mL dose, measure 5 mL and 2.5 mL.
For a 10 mL dose, measure 5 mL and 5 mL.
For a 15 mL dose, measure 5 mL, 5 mL, and 5 mL.
For a 20 mL dose, measure 5 mL, 5 mL, 5 mL, and 5 mL.
Shake bottle containing suspension slowly for 10 seconds. Do not shake the bottle too quickly as the suspension will foam; foaming may lead to the administration of the incorrect dose. If lumps or granules remain at the bottom of the bottle, shake again slowly for an additional 10 seconds.
Push the plunger of the oral syringe all the way down to remove any air.
Insert the oral syringe into bottle adaptor and turn the bottle upside down with the syring inserted. Pull the plunger slightly past the prescribed dose line to fill the oral syringe; this will help when removing any air bubbles.
While the bottle remains upside down with the oral syringe inserted, tap the syringe to air bubbles to the top. Then push the plunger to align with the prescribed dose line.
Turn the bottle right side up and remove the oral syringe. Do not remove the bottle adaptor from the bottle.
To administer the dose, place the oral syringe into the mouth with the tip of the syringe pointing toward the cheek and slowly press the plunger to allow the child to swallow naturally. Make sure the child swallows the full dose.
Once the full dose has been administered, rinse the oral dosing syringe with tap water and allow it to air dry. Do not place the oral dosing syringe in the dishwasher.
Disposal of the suspension bottle and syringe may be done with household trash; throw away any used oral dosing syringe with the opening of a new suspension bottle. Do not pour suspension down the drain (e.g. sink, toilet, shower, or tub). Do not recycle the bottle.

Missed doses
For adults receiving 2.5 mg twice daily: Take a single 2.5 mg dose at the next scheduled time.
For adults receiving 15 mg twice daily: Take 15 mg immediately to ensure intake of 30 mg/day. Two 15 mg tablets may be taken at once.
For adults receiving 20 mg, 15 mg, or 10 mg once daily: Take the missed dose immediately. Do not double the dose within the same day to make up for a missed dose.
For pediatric patients receiving once daily dosing: Take the missed dose as soon as possible once it is noticed, but only on the same day. If this is not possible, skip the dose and continue with the next dose as prescribed. Do not take 2 doses to make up for a missed dose.
For pediatric patients receiving twice daily dosing: Take the missed morning dose as soon as possible once it is noticed. A missed morning dose may be taken together with the evening dose. A missed evening dose can only be taken in the same evening.
For pediatric patients receiving 3 times daily dosing: Skip the missed dose and go back to the regular dosing schedule at the usual time without compensating for the missed dose.
 
Vomit or spit up doses
If the patient vomits or spits up the dose within 30 minutes of administration, give a new dose.
If the patient vomits or spits up more than 30 minutes after administration, do not re-administer the dose; take the next dose as scheduled.
If the patient vomits or spits up repeatedly, the caregiver should contact the patient's doctor immediately.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Mild impairment (Child-Pugh Class A): No dosage adjustment needed, but avoidance of rivaroxaban is recommended for any degree of hepatic disease associated with coagulopathy.
Moderate impairment (Child-Pugh Class B): Avoid use.
Severe impairment (Child-Pugh Class C): Avoid use.
  Renal Impairment
  CrCl 15 to 79 mL/minute: Do not administer rivaroxaban in patients receiving a combined P-gp and moderate CYP3A inhibitor unless the potential benefit justifies the potential risk.
Adults
Nonvalvular Atrial Fibrillation
CrCl greater than 50 mL/minute: No dosage adjustment needed.
CrCl 50 mL/minute or less: 15 mg PO once daily.
 
Treatment and Prophylaxis of Venous Thromboembolism (e.g., after hip or knee replacement surgery, during acute medical illness)
CrCl 30 mL/minute or more: No dosage adjustment needed.
CrCl 15 to 29 mL/minute: Monitor closely and promptly evaluate signs or symptoms of blood loss.
CrCl less than 15 mL/minute: Avoid use.
 
Coronary Artery Disease and Peripheral Artery/Vascular Disease
No dosage adjustment is needed based on CrCl.
 
Children and Adolescents
eGFR 50 mL/minute/1.73 m2 or more: No dosage adjustment needed.
eGFR less than 50 mL/minute/1.73 m2: Avoid use; limited clinical data available.
 
Neonates and Infants
Determine renal function using serum creatinine (SCr). Avoid use of rivaroxaban in neonates and infants with SCr above the 97.5th percentile as limited clinical data are available. Reference values of the 97.5th percentile of SCr in neonates and infants, based on age are as follows:
Week 2: 0.52 mg/dL (46 micromol/L)
Week 3: 0.46 mg/dL (41 micromol/L)
Week 4: 0.42 mg/dL (37 micromol/L)
Month 2: 0.37 mg/dL (33 micromol/L)
Month 3 to 9: 0.34 mg/dL (30 micromol/L)
Month 10 to 12: 0.36 mg/dL (32 micromol/L)
 
Hemodialysis
Systemic concentrations of rivaroxaban in patients with end-stage renal disease receiving intermittent dialysis were similar to those with moderate renal impairment. Hemodialysis had no significant impact on rivaroxaban exposure.
