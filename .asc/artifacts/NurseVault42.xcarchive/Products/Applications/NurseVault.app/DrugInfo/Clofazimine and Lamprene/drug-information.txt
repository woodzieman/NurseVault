Drug Class: Clofazimine is an oral antimycobacterial agent indicated for dapsone-sensitive or dapsone-resistant lepromatous leprosy as part of a combination regimen as well as lepromatous leprosy complicated by erythema nodosum leprosum.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 200 mg/day PO; 300 mg PO once monthly plus daily dosing has also been used.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): elevated hepatic enzymes, gi bleeding, gi obstruction, hepatitis, hepatomegaly, hyperbilirubinemia, jaundice, weight loss, acneiform rash, ichthyosis, photosensitivity, pruritus, rash, skin discoloration, stool discoloration, urine discoloration, xerosis, corneal deposits, ocular irritation, ocular pruritus, retinopathy, visual impairment, dizziness, drowsiness, dysgeusia, fatigue, headache, anemia, bone pain, cystitis

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  NOTE: This drug is discontinued in the U.S.Clofazimine should be used with extreme caution in patients with a history of GI problems because it can cause splenic infarction, GI obstruction or ileus, any type of GI bleeding, abdominal pain (colicky or burning pain in the abdomen), enteritis, diarrhea, and, in severe cases, death. Exploratory laparotomies have been done in some patients because of severe abdominal symptoms. Clofazimine can also cause dark, black, or tarry stools that may be misinterpreted as GI hemorrhage.Clofazimine should be used cautiously in patients with hepatic disease. On rare occasions, clofazimine has caused hepatitis and jaundice.Because clofazimine is a dye, most bodily secretions will be discolored during therapy including the sweat, tears, urine, feces, lining of the eye lids, and sputum. Patients should be advised of this effect prior to beginning therapy. The feces can appear black or tarry and can be misinterpreted as GI hemorrhage. Wearers of soft contact lenses should be cautioned that discoloration of the contact lens can occur. Skin discoloration also occurs with clofazimine and may trigger depression in patients with a history of depression; two cases of suicide have been reported in patients taking clofazimine.Elderly patients age 65 or older were not included in sufficient numbers in clinical trials of clofazimine. Therefore, it has not been determined if these patients respond differently from younger patients. Based on other clinical data, no differences in response between the elderly and younger patients have been identified. Generally, dosing in elderly patients should be done with caution. They should receive doses at the lower end of the dosing range because of the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function, and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy.Clofazimine is a FDA pregnancy risk category C drug. No complete or well-controlled studies have been done in humans. Clofazimine is known to cross the human placenta. Women receiving clofazimine during pregnancy had infants with deeply pigmented skin at birth; there was no evidence of teratogenicity. Clofazimine should only be used during pregnancy if the potential benefit justifies the risk to the fetus.Breast-feeding is not recommended during clofazimine therapy. Clofazimine is excreted into the breast milk. Discoloration of skin and fatty tissue has been observed in animals, secondary to clofazimine in maternal milk.

    Headline
    bradycardia, cardiomyopathy, congenital long QT syndrome, coronary artery disease, females, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, QT prolongation
    General Information
    Use clofazimine with caution in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are studies on the use of clofazimine during pregnancy to inform drug-associated risk. Few cases of clofazimine use during pregnancy have been reported in the literature. These cases describe deep pigmentation of the infants' skin at birth. Limited data are available regarding the reversibility of discoloration. Based on previous observations, discoloration may gradually fade over the first year of life. Embryotoxicity and fetotoxicity were evident in animal studies. Delayed fetal skull ossification, increased incidences of abortions and stillbirths, and impaired neonatal survival were observed in mice after prenatal exposure at doses equivalent to 0.6 times the maximum recommended human daily dose, based on body surface area comparisons.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with clofazimine. Clofazimine may cause fetal harm. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective (methods that result in less than 1% pregnancy rates) contraception while taking clofazimine and for at least 4 months after the last dose. For those that can cause pregnancy in others, contraception should be used during clofazimine treatment and for at least 4 months after the last dose. Impaired female fertility was observed in a study involving rats. It is not known how clofazimine may affect human fertility.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use clofazimine with caution during breast-feeding. Clofazimine appears in breast milk in relatively high amounts, and human milk may enhance the drug's solubilization and possibly infant absorption. Milk can become pink to red, and the breastfed infant may develop a reddish skin discoloration similar to what is seen in people taking the drug. No serious or permanent toxicity has been reported in breastfed infants. Because data are limited, weigh the benefits of clofazimine therapy against potential risks to the child and consider an alternative treatment if feasible.

Adagrasib: (Major) Concomitant use of adagrasib and clofazimine increases the risk of QT/QTc prolongation and torsade de pointes (TdP). Avoid concomitant use if possible, especially in patients with additional risk factors for TdP. Consider taking steps to minimize the risk for QT/QTc interval prolongation and TdP, such as electrolyte monitoring and repletion and ECG monitoring, if concomitant use is necessary.

Description
  Text
  Clofazimine is an oral antimycobacterial agent indicated for dapsone-sensitive or dapsone-resistant lepromatous leprosy as part of a combination regimen as well as lepromatous leprosy complicated by erythema nodosum leprosum. Because of resistance, clofazimine is not used alone for leprosy. Clofazimine exerts a slow bactericidal effect on Mycobacterium leprae and possesses anti-inflammatory properties in treating erythema nodosum leprosum reactions. Cases of QT prolongation with torsade de pointes have been reported with clofazimine; ECG monitoring is recommended. Other significant adverse reactions associated with clofazimine include skin discoloration that has been reported to result in depression and suicide and intestinal obstruction.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Clofazimine is an antimycobacterial agent that exerts a slow bactericidal effect on Mycobacterium leprae. Clofazimine binds preferentially to mycobacterial DNA and inhibits growth. The mechanism of action is postulated through its membrane-directed activity including the bacterial respiratory chain and ion transporters. Intracellular redox cycling, involving oxidation of reduced clofazimine, leads to the generation of antimicrobial reactive oxygen species (ROS), superoxide-hydrogen peroxide (H202). Interaction of clofazimine with membrane phospholipids results in the generation of antimicrobial lysophospholipids, which promote membrane dysfunction, resulting in interference with potassium uptake. Both mechanisms cause interference with cellular energy metabolism by disrupting ATP production. Clofazimine also exerts anti-inflammatory properties in treating erythema nodosum leprosum reactions. Anti-inflammatory activity is likely primarily through inhibition of T lymphocyte activation and proliferation. Clofazimine may indirectly interfere with the proliferation of T cells by promoting the release of ROS and E-series prostaglandins (PGs), especially PGE2 from neutrophils and monocytes.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Clofazimine absorption ranges from 45% to 62% in leprosy patients. The average serum concentrations of clofazimine in leprosy patients treated with 100 and 300 mg daily were 0.7 mcg/mL and 1 mcg/mL, respectively. Time to reach peak plasma concentration (median Tmax) decreases from 12 hours to 8 hours under fed conditions relative to the fasted state.
  Text
  Clofazimine is administered orally. The highly lipophilic drug is distributed mainly to fatty tissue and the reticuloendothelial system. It is taken up by macrophages throughout the body. Clofazimine is bound to alpha- and primarily beta-lipoproteins in serum, and the binding is saturable at plasma concentrations of approximately 10 mcg/mL. Binding to gamma-globulin and albumin is negligible. At autopsy, clofazimine crystals have been found in mesenteric lymph nodes, adrenals, subcutaneous fat, liver, bile, gallbladder, spleen, small intestine, muscles, bones, and skin.
 
Information on the metabolism of clofazimine is limited; however, 3 metabolites were found in urine after repeated oral doses. After a single 300 mg dose, elimination of unchanged clofazimine and its metabolites was negligible in a 24-hour urine collection. Part of the ingested drug recovered from feces may represent excretion via the bile. A small amount is also eliminated in the sputum, sebum, and sweat. The mean elimination half-life of clofazimine is approximately 25 days (range 6.5 to 160 days) after repeated oral doses of 50 or 100 mg in leprosy patients.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2C8, CYP2D6, CYP3A4/5
Clofazimine inhibits the metabolism of CYP2C8, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4/5 in vitro. It may increase the concentration of CYP3A4/5 substrates if used concomitantly.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Avoid clofazimine in patients with hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A, B, or C) unless the benefit outweighs the risk.
  Renal Impairment
  No dosage adjustment needed. Use clofazimine with caution in patients with severe renal impairment.
