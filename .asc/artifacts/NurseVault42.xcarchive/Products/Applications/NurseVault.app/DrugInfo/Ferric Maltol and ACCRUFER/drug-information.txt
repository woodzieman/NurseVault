Drug Class: Ferric maltol is an oral iron replacement therapy indicated for the treatment of iron-deficiency anemia.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 60 mg/day PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): stool discoloration

In an Overdose: Accidental exposure of iron-containing products (i.e., overdose) is the leading cause of fatal poisoning in children younger than 6 years.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Ferric maltol is contraindicated in patients with iron overload disorder (e.g., hemochromatosis) and those receiving a repeated blood transfusion as use may result in iron overload. Do not administer ferric maltol to patients receiving intravenous iron.

    Headline
    blood transfusion, iron overload disorder
    General Information
    Ferric maltol is contraindicated in patients with iron overload disorder (e.g., hemochromatosis) and those receiving a repeated blood transfusion as use may result in iron overload. Do not administer ferric maltol to patients receiving intravenous iron. Excessive therapy with iron products may lead to excess storage of iron and iatrogenic hemosiderosis. Monitor iron parameters prior to initiating ferric maltol and during therapy.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    inflammatory bowel disease
    General Information
    Avoid use of ferric maltol in patients with an active inflammatory bowel disease flare, as there is a potential risk for increased inflammation in the gastrointestinal tract.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Use of ferric maltol during pregnancy is not expected to result in fetal exposure to the drug, as it is not absorbed systemically as an intact complex after oral administration. Untreated iron deficiency in pregnancy is associated with adverse outcomes, including postpartum anemia, increased risk for preterm delivery, and low birth weight. Iron overdose during pregnancy may increase the risk of spontaneous abortion, gestational diabetes, and fetal malformations. Animal reproduction studies with oral ferric maltol resulted in no adverse developmental outcomes.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Ferric maltol is compatible with breast-feeding. Ferric maltol is not absorbed systemically as an intact complex after oral administration. Use during lactation is not expected to result in exposure to the breastfed child. There are no data on the presence of ferric maltol in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production.

Description
  Text
  Ferric maltol is an oral iron replacement therapy indicated for the treatment of iron-deficiency anemia. Ferric maltol contains iron in a stable ferric state as a complex with a trimaltol ligand. The safety and efficacy of ferric maltol for the treatment of iron-deficiency anemia were demonstrated in clinical studies in adults and pediatric subjects aged 10 to 17 years. Across studies, ferric maltol significantly increased hemoglobin and ferritin concentrations compared with placebo. As with other iron supplements, gastrointestinal adverse effects are common with ferric maltol; however, it is recommended that ferric maltol be taken on an empty stomach to increase absorption. Ferric maltol was FDA-approved in 2019.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Ferric maltol delivers iron for uptake across the intestinal wall and transfers to transferrin and ferritin. Ferric maltol has been shown to increase serum iron parameters, including ferritin and transferrin saturation (TSAT).
 
Normal erythropoiesis is dependent on the concentration of iron and erythropoietin available in the plasma. Approximately two-thirds of total body iron is in the circulating erythrocytes as hemoglobin, the major factor in oxygen transport to the tissues. Stored iron, in the form of ferritin and hemosiderin, accumulates during periods of positive iron balance and can be mobilized to meet iron requirements when iron intake is low. Administration of iron does not stimulate the production of red blood cells, nor does it correct abnormalities not caused by iron deficiency. A therapeutic response to treatment with iron products is dependent on the patient's ability to absorb and use the iron. The response to iron therapy is also influenced by the cause of the deficiency as well as other illnesses that can affect normal erythropoiesis. A positive response to iron treatment can be noted by an increase in the hemoglobin concentration by 1 g/dL or more 4 weeks after initiating oral therapy, assuming that transfusion or other interventions cannot explain the improvements in the patients clinical status.
 
Iron-containing proteins and enzymes are important in oxidation-reduction reactions, especially those of the mitochondria. Iron is a component of iron-containing heme proteins (e.g., hemoglobin, myoglobin), iron-sulfur enzymes (e.g., flavoproteins), proteins for iron storage and transport (e.g., transferrin) and other iron-containing enzymes. Iron deficiency not only causes anemia and decreased oxygen delivery, but it also reduces the metabolism of muscle and decreases mitochondrial activity. Thus, iron is important to several metabolic functions which are independent of its importance to erythropoiesis.

    In a pharmacokinetic study in pediatric subjects (10 to 17 years) with iron-deficiency anemia, ferric maltol 15 mg or 30 mg was administered twice daily for 7 to 10 days.   Text
  Ferric maltol is administered orally. It dissociates upon intake from the gastrointestinal tract, allowing iron and maltol to be absorbed separately. Maltol is metabolized through glucuronidation (UGT1A6) and sulphation in vitro. Of the total maltol ingested, a mean of between 40% and 60% is excreted in the urine as maltol glucuronide. Total serum iron concentrations increase in a less than dose-proportional manner with increasing ferric maltol doses.
