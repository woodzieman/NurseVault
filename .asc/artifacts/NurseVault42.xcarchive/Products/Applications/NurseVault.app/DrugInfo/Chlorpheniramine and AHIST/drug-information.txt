Drug Class: Chlorpheniramine is a first generation, oral sedating antihistamine (H1-blocker) of the alkylamine class.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 24 mg/day PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): confusion, dizziness, drowsiness, headache, impaired cognition, weakness, hypertension, hypotension, palpitations, sinus tachycardia, ataxia, hallucinations, insomnia, psychosis, restlessness, seizures, blurred vision, urinary retention, xerophthalmia, agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, thrombocytopenia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Use antihistamines with caution in patients with asthma. Chlorpheniramine should be avoided during an acute asthmatic attack.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Use antihistamines with caution in patients with asthma. Chlorpheniramine should be avoided during an acute asthmatic attack. The anticholinergic activity of H1-antagonists may result in thickened bronchial secretions in the respiratory tract thereby aggravating an acute asthmatic attack or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Although H1-antagonists should be avoided during an acute asthmatic attack, these anticholinergic effects do not preclude the use of H1-antagonists in all asthmatic or COPD patients, particularly if thickened bronchial secretions are not a primary component of the illness. Chlorpheniramine exhibits a moderate amount of anticholinergic activity, and the use of an H1-antagonist with less anticholinergic effects may be preferable in some instances.
 
Antihistamines may induce paradoxical CNS stimulation in some children. It is generally not recommended to administer the 8-hour or 12-hour sustained release products to children < 6 years of age. Antihistamines, including chlorpheniramine, are contraindicated in neonates, neonatal prematurity and infants due to the possibility of paradoxical CNS stimulation or seizures. In January 2007, the CDC warned caregivers and healthcare providers of the risk for serious injury or fatal overdose from the administration of cough and cold products to children and infants less than 2 years of age. This warning followed an investigation of the deaths of three (3) infants less than 6 months of age that were attributed to the inadvertent inappropriate use of these products. The symptoms preceding these deaths have not been clearly defined, and there is a lack of conclusive data describing the exact cause of death. The report estimated that 1519 children less than 2 years of age were treated in emergency departments during 2004—2005 for adverse events related to cough and cold medications. In October 2007, the FDA Nonprescription Drug Advisory Committee and the Pediatric Advisory Committee recommended that nonprescription cough and cold products containing pseudoephedrine, dextromethorphan, chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, brompheniramine, phenylephrine, clemastine, or guaifenesin not be used in children less than 6 years of age. In January 2008, the FDA issued a Public Health Advisory recommending that OTC cough and cold products not be used in infants and children less than 2 years. An official ruling regarding the use of these products in children greater than 2 years has not yet been announced. The FDA recommends that if parents and caregivers use cough and cold products in children greater than 2 years, labels should be read carefully, caution should be used when administering multiple products, and only measuring devices specifically designed for use with medications should be used. While some combination cough/cold products containing these ingredients are available by prescription only and are not necessarily under scrutiny by the FDA, clinicians should thoroughly assess each patient's use of similar products, both prescription and nonprescription, to avoid duplication of therapy and the potential for inadvertent overdose.
 
Chlorpheniramine is classified as FDA pregnancy category B. No well controlled trials of chlorpheniramine use in human gestation are available, although data available from reports of use in human pregnancy, particularly in the first and second trimesters, do not support an association between chlorpheniramine use and congenital defects. However, in general, H1-antagonists are not recommended for use in the last 2 weeks of pregnancy due to a possible association between these drugs and retrolental fibroplasia in premature neonates. Chlorpheniramine should be administered during pregnancy only when the benefits of therapy outweigh the potential risks to the fetus, and use should be limited to short-term, 'as needed' administration under the prescription of a qualified health-care professional. Non-pharmacologic methods (e.g., fluids, and rest) are recommended to be tried first for symptomatic relief of colds or allergies during pregnancy.
 
Chlorpheniramine should be used with caution during breast-feeding. Side effects in breast-feeding infants may manifest as irritability, disturbed sleeping patterns, drowsiness, hyperexcitability, or excessive crying. Single bedtime doses of chlorpheniramine, which are short-acting, after the last feeding of the day may be suggested and might minimize infant exposure through the breast milk during feeding hours. The frequent use of chlorpheniramine or extended-release chlorpheniramine products during breast-feeding should be avoided. Alternative methods of feeding should be used if routine chlorpheniramine therapy is necessary in the breast-feeding mother.
 
Chlorpheniramine should be used conservatively in patients with closed-angle glaucoma. An increase in intraocular pressure may occur from the anticholinergic actions of chlorpheniramine, precipitating an acute attack of glaucoma. Elderly patients are more susceptible to the anticholinergic effects of chlorpheniramine, including possible precipitation of undiagnosed glaucoma. Other ocular effects resulting from the anticholinergic effects of chlorpheniramine include dry eyes or blurred vision. This may be of significance in the elderly and wearers of contact lenses.
 
Although chlorpheniramine has only moderate anticholinergic effects, a worsening of symptoms may be seen in patients with bladder obstruction, GI obstruction or ileus, benign prostatic hypertrophy, or urinary retention. The elderly are more susceptible to the anticholinergic effects of drugs since there is a decline in endogenous cholinergic activity that occurs with age. As with all H1-antagonists, chlorpheniramine should be avoided in patients with peptic ulcer disease.
 
Chlorpheniramine is extensively metabolized in the liver. The metabolism of chlorpheniramine may be reduced in the presence of hepatic impairment. Those with significant hepatic disease receiving chlorpheniramine should be monitored for liver function and side effects. Dosage adjustments may be required, as drug accumulation or prolonged duration of action can occur in patients with hepatic dysfunction.
 
The quinidine-like local anesthetic and anticholinergic effects of H1-antagonists are responsible for the adverse cardiac effects that have been observed with such drugs, including tachycardia, ECG changes, hypotension, and arrhythmias. Although these cardiovascular effects are uncommon, antihistamines should be used conservatively in patients with cardiac disease. In addition, considerable caution should be used in patients with controlled or mild hypertension, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, or other cardiac disease because of the anticholinergic effects associated with chlorpheniramine.
 
Chlorpheniramine can cause drowsiness. Patients receiving chlorpheniramine should be advised to avoid driving or operating machinery until the effects of the drug are known. Additive drowsiness may be seen other CNS depressant agents including ethanol.

    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
    General Information
    Use sedating antihistamines such as chlorpheniramine with caution in people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), including emphysema or chronic bronchitis. The anticholinergic activity of first-generation H1-antagonists may result in thickened bronchial secretions in the respiratory tract, thereby aggravating COPD.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Chlorpheniramine is a first-generation antihistamine of the alkylamine class. Allergies to one antihistamine can sometimes lead to cross-reactivity with other chemically-related antihistamines.
    Headline
    glaucoma
    General Information
    Chlorpheniramine should be used with caution in people with glaucoma, as an increase in intraocular pressure may occur from the anticholinergic actions of the medication. Older adults are more susceptible to the anticholinergic effects of chlorpheniramine, including possible precipitation of undiagnosed glaucoma.
    Headline
    prostatic hypertrophy
    General Information
    Due to the anticholinergic effects which may aggravate urinary retention, first-generation antihistamines, such as chlorpheniramine, should be used with caution in people with trouble urinating due to prostatic hypertrophy.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Chlorpheniramine can cause drowsiness. Advise individuals receiving chlorpheniramine to avoid driving or operating machinery and activities requiring coordination and concentration until the effects of the drug are known. Additive drowsiness may be seen during coadministration with other CNS depressants, including ethanol ingestion.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    The Beers Criteria identifies first-generation antihistamines as potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) for geriatric adults, recommending their avoidance due to their high anticholinergic properties, decreased clearance in older age, the development of tolerance when used as sleep aids, and an increased risk of anticholinergic effects and toxicity compared to younger individuals. These medications should particularly be avoided in patients with dementia or cognitive impairment (due to adverse CNS effects), those at high risk for delirium (which can worsen or trigger new-onset delirium), and men with lower urinary tract symptoms or benign prostatic hyperplasia (due to risks of urinary retention or hesitancy).
    Headline
    children, infants, neonates
    General Information
    Due to the risk for serious adverse reactions, the FDA recommends against administration of over the counter (OTC) cough and cold products to neonates, infants and children younger than 2 years of age. When administering OTC medications to older pediatric patients, they advise caregivers to read product labels carefully, use caution when administering multiple products to avoid duplication of ingredients, and use only measuring devices specifically designed for use with medications. Care teams should thoroughly assess the use of similar products, both prescription and nonprescription, to avoid duplication of therapy and the potential for inadvertent overdose.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Chlorpheniramine use as a single agent during pregnancy has been associated with reassuring animal and human data; however, second-generation antihistamines are generally preferred over first-generation antihistamines. Loratadine and cetirizine are acceptable antihistamine alternatives based on their excellent safety data and recommendation in multiple guidelines for use during pregnancy. Non-pharmacologic methods (e.g., fluids, rest) are recommended to be tried first for symptomatic relief of colds or allergies during pregnancy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Chlorpheniramine should be used with caution during breast-feeding. Small, occasional doses of chlorpheniramine would not be expected to cause adverse effects in breastfed infants. Larger doses or more prolonged use may cause effects in the infant or decrease the milk supply. Side effects of sedating first-generation antihistamines in breastfed infants such as irritability, disturbed sleeping patterns, drowsiness, hyperexcitability, and excessive crying have been reported in a number of cases. Single bedtime doses of short-acting chlorpheniramine, after the last feeding of the day may be suggested and might minimize infant exposure through the breast milk during feeding hours. Antihistamines can lower basal prolactin secretion and may interfere with the establishment of lactation. Consider alternatives if antihistamine treatment is necessary. Guidelines recommend loratadine or cetirizine at the lowest dose as preferred antihistamines in breast-feeding individuals because they are safer with only low levels found in breast milk.

Description
  Text
  Chlorpheniramine is a first generation, oral sedating antihistamine (H1-blocker) of the alkylamine class. In contrast to first generation antihistamines from the ethanolamine class, such as diphenhydramine, the alkylamine antihistamines, such as chlorpheniramine, are considered less sedating and have less anticholinergic effects. However, these effects are still substantial for some patients compared to newer, non-sedating antihistamines. Chlorpheniramine is used to relieve seasonal allergic rhinitis and to treat other allergic conditions. Chlorpheniramine was originally marketed as a prescription-only drug in 1950 and was later moved to non-prescription (OTC) status.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  H1-antagonists do not prevent the release of histamine, as do cromolyn and nedocromil, but rather compete with free histamine for binding at H1-receptor sites. These drugs competitively antagonize the effects of histamine on H1-receptors in the GI tract, uterus, large blood vessels, and bronchial smooth muscle. Blockade of H1-receptors also suppresses the formation of edema, flare, and pruritus that result from histaminic activity. H1-antagonists also possess anticholinergic properties in varying degrees. The anticholinergic activity of propylamine derivatives such as chlorpheniramine is moderate. This anticholinergic action appears to be due to a central antimuscarinic effect. Sedative effects from chlorpheniramine result from antagonism at central histaminergic receptors. Chronic administration may lead to some degree of tolerance.

  Text
  Chlorpheniramine is administered orally. Chlorpheniramine is widely distributed in body tissues and fluids, including the central nervous system, and it crosses the placenta and is excreted into human breast milk. Protein-binding is approximately 70%. Chlorpheniramine is rapidly and extensively metabolized via demethylation in the liver, forming mono-and didesmethyl derivatives. Oxidative metabolism of chlorpheniramine is catalyzed by CYP2D6. Chlorpheniramine and its metabolites are cleared substantially by the kidney, with large individual variation. Excretion rates are dependent on the pH of urine and urinary flow, with the rate decreasing as the pH rises and urinary flow decreases. The mean plasma half-life of chlorpheniramine is approximately 21 to 24 hours.
