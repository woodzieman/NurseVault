    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    It is not known if iodoquinol; hydrocortisone; aloe polysaccharides can cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy. Animal reproduction studies have not been conducted. The more potent corticosteroids have been shown to be teratogenic after dermal application in laboratory animals. Do not use topical corticosteroids in large amounts, on large areas, or for prolonged periods in pregnant individuals. Intermittent use of mild to moderate potency topical agents has not been associated with fetal harm. However, the use of high potency topical corticosteroids has been associated with fetal growth restriction, particularly when the cumulative dose throughout pregnancy exceeds 300 grams.
    Headline
    children, infants, neonates
    General Information
    The Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) list includes topical hydrocortisone as a potentially inappropriate medication for use in neonates, infants, and children younger than 2 years of age and use should be avoided. If avoidance is not possible, use iodoquinol; hydrocortisone; aloe polysaccharides with caution and appropriate monitoring.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use iodoquinol; hydrocortisone; aloe polysaccharides with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of topically applied iodoquinol in breast milk. Topical corticosteroids, such as hydrocortisone, can be used safely during lactation because exposure through human milk is minimal. Topical aloe vera is likely acceptable for use during lactation, provided the child does not ingest it. To minimize potential risk, ensure the child's skin does not come into direct contact with the area treated with iodoquinol; hydrocortisone; aloe polysaccharides. Do not apply the medication to the nipple or areola, as the child could ingest medication from the skin. Before breast-feeding, thoroughly wipe off the treated area to prevent transfer of medication to the child.

    laboratory test interference
    General Information
    Iodoquinol can cause laboratory test interference. Iodoquinol may be absorbed through the skin and interfere with thyroid function tests. If such tests are to be conducted, wait at least 1 month after discontinuation of hydrocortisone; iodoquinol; aloe polysaccharides to perform these tests. Additionally, the ferric chloride test for phenylketonuria can yield a false positive result if iodoquinol is present in the diaper or urine.

Description
  Text
  Iodoquinol; hydrocortisone; aloe polysaccharides is a topical combination of an antifungal and antibacterial agent, a corticosteroid, and skin soother or moisturizer indicated for the treatment of acne urticata, anogenital pruritus, bacterial dermatoses, contact or atopic dermatitis, chronic infectious dermatitis, stasis dermatitis, eczema, chronic eczematoid otitis externa, folliculitis, intertrigo, lichen simplex chronicus, localized or disseminated neurodermatitis, monilasis, mycotic dermatoses such as tinea (capitis, cruris, corporis, pedis), or pyoderma in adults and pediatric patients as young as 12 years. Hydrocortisone has anti-inflammatory, antipruritic, and vasoconstrictive properties, and iodoquinol has both antifungal and antibacterial properties. Iodoquinol may be absorbed through the skin and interfere with thyroid function tests. Prolonged use of iodoquinol; hydrocortisone; aloe polysaccharides may result in overgrowth of non-susceptible fungal or bacterial organisms requiring appropriate therapy.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Topical Route
    Description
    Iodoquinol: There are no data available regarding the percutaneous absorption of iodoquinol.
Hydrocortisone: Hydrocortisone can be absorbed from normal intact skin after topical application. Inflammation and/or other inflammatory disease processes in the skin increase percutaneous absorption. The extent of percutaneous absorption of topical corticosteroids is determined by many factors including vehicle, the integrity of the epidermal barrier, and the use of occlusive dressings. Occlusive dressings substantially increase the percutaneous absorption of topical corticosteroids.
  Text
  Iodoquinol; hydrocortisone; aloe polysaccharides is administered topically.
Iodoquinol: There are no data available regarding the pharmacokinetics of topical iodoquinol; however, following oral administration, 3 to 5% of the dose was recovered in the urine as a glucuronide.
Hydrocortisone: Once absorbed through the skin, hydrocortisone is metabolized in the liver and most body tissues to hydrogenated and degraded forms, such as tetrahydrocortisone and tetrahydrocortisol. These metabolites are excreted in the urine, mainly as conjugated glucuronides. A small portion is excreted as unchanged hydrocortisone acetate.
