Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  This drug does not treat viral infections (e.g., common cold). Prescribing nitrofurantoin in the absence of a proven or strongly suspected bacterial infection or a prophylactic indication is unlikely to provide benefit to the patient and increases the risk of the development of drug-resistant bacteria. Patients should be told to complete the full course of treatment, even if they feel better earlier.
 
The success of nitrofurantoin therapy depends on achieving adequate urinary concentrations of the drug. Nitrofurantoin is contraindicated in patients with anuria, oliguria, or severe renal impairment where creatinine clearance is less than 60 ml/min and should be used with extreme caution in patients with renal disease.
 
Patients with G6PD deficiency should not use nitrofurantoin because of the risk of developing hemolytic anemia. Patients with anemia also have a propensity to develop hemolytic anemia and nitrofurantoin should be used with caution.
 
Nitrofurantoin is contraindicated in neonates < 1 month old. Nitrofurantoin can induce hemolytic anemia in G6PD-deficient patients and in patients whose RBCs lack sufficient quantities of reduced glutathione, including neonates.
 
Nitrofurantoin is contraindicated in pregnancy at term (38—42 weeks gestation) and during labor or obstetric delivery. Although it is classified as a pregnancy category B drug for use during pregnancy, nitrofurantoin can induce hemolytic anemia in G6PD-deficient patients and in patients whose RBCs lack sufficient quantities of reduced glutathione. Because neonates exhibit this latter condition, generally it is wise to avoid the use of this drug during late pregnancy; however, hemolytic anemia in the newborn as a result of in utero exposure to nitrofurantoin has not been reported.
 
Nitrofurantoin is distributed into breast milk and should be used with caution in breast-feeding women. The drug is especially problematic for infants with G6PD deficiency, because of the risk of hemolytic anemia. Because of the potential for serious adverse reactions from nitrofurantoin in nursing infants < 1 month old, a decision should be made whether to discontinue nursing or to discontinue the drug, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother. Nitrofurantoin is contraindicated in neonates < 1 month old.
 
Patients most likely to develop peripheral neuropathy, including optic neuritis, when receiving nitrofurantoin are those with pre-existing peripheral neuropathy, anemia, diabetes mellitus, electrolyte imbalance, vitamin B12 deficiency, or otherwise debilitated patients. These populations should be treated with caution.
 
Nitrofurantoin is contraindicated in patients with a history of cholestatic jaundice or hepatic dysfunction associated with previous nitrofurantoin therapy. In general, nitrofurantoin should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease due to the possibility of developing drug-induced hepatotoxicity. Liver-function tests should be performed periodically during long-term therapy.
 
Nitrofurantoin use is associated with acute and chronic pulmonary reactions (see Adverse Reactions). While acute reactions (i.e., those occurring within several days after beginning therapy) are usually reversible, pulmonary reactions occurring after prolonged therapy (i.e., those occurring after many months of continuous therapy) may not resolve. Patients receiving chronic therapy should be monitored carefully because delayed pulmonary reactions can be insidious. Nitrofurantoin should be used with caution in patients with any preexisting pulmonary disease.
 
Use nitrofurantoin with caution in elderly patients, especially if given for long term. Clinical studies of nitrofurantoin did not include sufficient numbers of subjects aged 65 and over to determine whether they respond differently from younger subjects. However, based on other reported clinical experience, no differences in response have been identified. Spontaneous reports suggest a higher proportion of pulmonary reactions, including fatalities, in elderly patients; these differences appear to be related to the higher proportion of elderly patients receiving long-term nitrofurantoin therapy. As in younger patients, chronic pulmonary reactions generally are observed in patients receiving therapy for six months or longer. Spontaneous reports also suggest an increased proportion of severe hepatic reactions, including fatalities, in elderly patients. In general, the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function, and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy in elderly patients should be considered when prescribing nitrofurantoin. This drug is known to be substantially excreted by the kidney, and the risk of toxic reactions to this drug may be greater in patients with impaired renal function. Anuria, oliguria, or significant impairment of renal function (creatinine clearance under 60 mL per minute or clinically significant elevated serum creatinine) are contraindications. Because elderly patients are more likely to have decreased renal function, it may be useful to monitor renal function.
 
Almost all antibacterial agents have been associated with pseudomembranous colitis (antibiotic-associated colitis) which may range in severity from mild to life-threatening. In the colon, overgrowth of Clostridia may exist when normal flora is altered subsequent to antibacterial administration. The toxin produced by Clostridium difficile is a primary cause of pseudomembranous colitis. It is known that systemic use of antibiotics predisposes patients to development of pseudomembranous colitis. Consideration should be given to the diagnosis of pseudomembranous colitis in patients presenting with diarrhea following antibacterial administration. Systemic antibiotics should be prescribed with caution to patients with inflammatory bowel disease such as ulcerative colitis or other GI disease. If diarrhea develops during therapy, the drug should be discontinued. Following diagnosis of pseudomembranous colitis, therapeutic measures should be instituted. In milder cases, the colitis may respond to discontinuation of the offending agent. In moderate to severe cases, fluids and electrolytes, protein supplementation, and treatment with an antibacterial effective against Clostridium difficile may be warranted. Products inhibiting peristalsis are contraindicated in this clinical situation. Practitioners should be aware that antibiotic-associated colitis has been observed to occur over two months or more following discontinuation of systemic antibiotic therapy; a careful medical history should be taken.
 
Patients receiving nitrofurantoin may present with a false-positive reaction for glucose in the urine. Benedict's and Fehling's solutions are affected but not the glucose enzymatic test.

    General Information

    Headline
    oliguria, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Although data are lacking, the FDA-approved labeling states that nitrofurantoin is contraindicated in persons with oliguria (including anuria), renal failure, or severe renal impairment where the CrCl is less than 60 mL/minute. Retrospective studies have shown that short-term nitrofurantoin use is effective and generally well tolerated in persons with a CrCl of 30 mL/minute or more, although higher incidences of adverse reactions have been noted in persons with renal impairment. Conditions such as renal impairment (creatinine clearance less than 60 mL/minute or clinically significant elevated serum creatinine) may potentiate the risk for peripheral neuropathy. Monitor persons receiving long-term nitrofurantoin therapy periodically for changes in renal function.
    Headline
    G6PD deficiency
    General Information
    Use nitrofurantoin with caution in persons with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. Cases of hemolytic anemia have been induced by nitrofurantoin, and hemolysis appears to be linked to G6PD deficiency in the affected patients.
    Headline
    anemia, diabetes mellitus, electrolyte imbalance, vitamin B deficiency
    General Information
    Conditions such as renal impairment (creatinine clearance less than 60 mL/minute or clinically significant elevated serum creatinine), anemia, diabetes mellitus, electrolyte imbalance, vitamin B deficiency, and debilitating disease may potentiate the risk for peripheral neuropathy.
    Headline
    history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use
    General Information
    Nitrofurantoin is contraindicated in individuals with a history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use, including cholestatic jaundice or hepatic dysfunction.
    Headline
    neonates
    General Information
    All forms of nitrofurantoin are contraindicated in neonates because of the risk of hemolytic anemia due to immature erythrocyte enzyme systems (glutathione instability). The Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) list includes nitrofurantoin as a potentially inappropriate medication for use in neonates and use should be avoided. If avoidance is not possible, use nitrofurantoin with caution and appropriate monitoring.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Use nitrofurantoin with caution in geriatric adults. Spontaneous reports suggest a higher proportion of pulmonary reactions and hepatic reaction, including fatalities, in older adults; these differences appear to be related to the higher proportion of older patients receiving long-term nitrofurantoin therapy. Nitrofurantoin is substantially excreted by the kidney and the risk for adverse reactions is greater in those individuals with substantial renal impairment; older adults are more likely to have renal dysfunction. According to the Beers Criteria, nitrofurantoin is considered a potentially inappropriate medication (PIM) in geriatric adults due to the possibility of pulmonary toxicity, hepatotoxicity, and peripheral neuropathy, especially with long-term use, and the availability of safer alternatives. Avoid as a long-term bacterial suppressant and avoid use in geriatric patients with a CrCl less than 30 mL/minute.
    Headline
    labor, obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    Nitrofurantoin is contraindicated in pregnancy at term (38 to 42 weeks gestation), when onset of labor is imminent, and during labor or obstetric delivery because of the possibility of hemolytic anemia due to immature erythrocyte enzyme systems (glutathione instability). Hemolytic anemia of the primaquine-sensitivity type has been induced by nitrofurantoin; hemolysis appears to be linked to a glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency in the red blood cells of the affected individuals. Published epidemiological studies, including cohort studies and case control studies, have reported inconsistent findings related to nitrofurantoin use during the first trimester and the risk of major birth defects. These studies cannot definitively establish the presence or absence of risk. The limited number of studies available have not identified a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. Animal studies have revealed no evidence of fetal harm at doses up to 25 times the human dose. Nitrofurantoin is recommended for treatment of urinary tract infections during pregnancy, except in pregnancy at term.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with nitrofurantoin. Nitrofurantoin may reversibly decrease spermatogenesis, based on findings from a fertility study in 36 healthy male volunteers and an animal fertility study. The effect on fertility is unknown.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use nitrofurantoin with caution during breast-feeding. Nitrofurantoin is present in human milk in low concentrations. Breast-feeding is not recommended in children younger than 1 month of age and in those who have glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, due to the potential for drug-induced hemolytic anemia. The time of greatest risk for hemolysis in full term newborns without G6PD deficiency may be as short as 8 days after birth. In children older than 1 month and with normal G6PD, consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the medication or from the underlying patient's medical condition. Monitor the breastfed child for vomiting, diarrhea, and rash. Cephalexin, trimethoprim, or sulfamethoxazole; trimethoprim may be potential alternatives to consider during breast-feeding. However, site of infection, patient factors, local susceptibility patterns, and specific microbial susceptibility should be assessed before choosing an alternative agent.

Interactions
Aluminum Hydroxide; Magnesium Trisilicate: (Major) Avoid concomitant use of antacids containing magnesium trisilicate during treatment with nitrofurantoin. Antacids containing magnesium trisilicate reduce both the rate and extent of nitrofurantoin absorption. The mechanism for this interaction is unknown but likely due to adsorption of nitrofurantoin onto the surface of magnesium trisilicate.
Articaine; EPINEPHrine: (Moderate) Coadministration of articaine with oxidizing agents, such as nitrofurantoin, may increase the risk of developing methemoglobinemia. Monitor patients closely for signs and symptoms of methemoglobinemia if coadministration is necessary. If methemoglobinemia occurs or is suspected, discontinue articaine and any other oxidizing agents. Depending on the severity of symptoms, patients may respond to supportive care; more severe symptoms may require treatment with methylene blue, exchange transfusion, or hyperbaric oxygen.
Aspirin, ASA; Citric Acid; Sodium Bicarbonate: (Major) Antacids can delay both the rate and the extent of GI absorption of nitrofurantoin. This interaction may be due to surface absorption of the antibacterial onto the antacid. Separate administration by at least 1 hour.
Bortezomib: (Minor) Monitor patients for the development of peripheral neuropathy when receiving bortezomib in combination with other drugs that can cause peripheral neuropathy like nitrofurantoin; the risk of peripheral neuropathy may be additive.

Desogestrel: Ethinyl Estradiol; Ferrous Bisglycinate: (Moderate) It was previously thought that antibiotics may decrease the effectiveness of oral contraceptives containing estrogens due to stimulation of estrogen metabolism or a reduction in estrogen enterohepatic circulation via changes in GI flora. One retrospective study reviewed the literature to determine the effects of oral antibiotics on the pharmacokinetics of contraceptive estrogens and progestins, and also examined clinical studies in which the incidence of pregnancy with oral contraceptives (OCs) and antibiotics was reported. It was concluded that the antibiotics ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin, doxycycline, metronidazole, ofloxacin, roxithromycin, temafloxacin, and tetracycline did not alter plasma levels of oral contraceptives. Antituberculous drugs (e.g., rifampin) were the only agents associated with OC failure and pregnancy. Based on the study results, these authors recommended that back-up contraception may not be necessary if OCs are used reliably during oral antibiotic use. Another review of the subject concurred with these data, but noted that individual patients have been identified who experienced significant decreases in plasma concentrations of combined OC components and who appeared to ovulate; the agents most often associated with these changes were rifampin, tetracyclines and penicillin derivatives. These authors concluded that because females most at risk for OC failure or noncompliance may not be easily identified and the true incidence of such events may be under-reported, and given the serious consequence of unwanted pregnancy, that recommending an additional method of contraception during short-term antibiotic use may be justified. During long-term antibiotic administration, the risk for drug interaction with OCs is less clear, but alternative or additional contraception may be advisable in selected circumstances. Data regarding progestin-only contraceptives or for newer combined contraceptive deliveries (e.g., patches, rings) are not available.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Nitrofurantoin can cause serious pulmonary reactions that can present as acute, subacute, or chronic. Acute reactions usually manifest within hours of initiation of therapy if the patient has been previously sensitized, and within days for those who develop sensitivity during therapy. Lupus-like symptoms have been reported. Symptoms, often accompanied by eosinophilia, include dyspnea, chills, fever, angina, chest pain (unspecified), malaise, and cough. On chest x-ray, pulmonary infiltration with consolidation or pleural effusion may be seen. Allergic dermatological reactions also can occur, including exfoliative dermatitis, pruritus, maculopapular rash, and erythema multiforme (including Stevens-Johnson syndrome). Subacute reactions can become manifest after about 1 month of treatment, and symptoms include dyspnea, tachypnea, persistent cough, and interstitial pneumonitis. Electrocardiograph changes (e.g., non-specific ST/T wave changes, bundle-branch block) have also been reported in association with pulmonary reactions. Withdrawal of the drug in acute and subacute conditions usually resolves the condition within 1 week. The onset of chronic reactions can be insidious and may not manifest until after several months to several years of therapy. If chronic pulmonary reactions are unrecognized and remain untreated, they can cause permanent impairment of pulmonary function, and possibly pulmonary fibrosis or respiratory failure, even if the drug is withdrawn. Other allergic reactions have been reported and include angioedema, maculopapular rash, erythematous rash, eczematous rash, pruritus, urticaria, anaphylaxis, arthralgia, myalgia, drug fever, and chills. Transient alopecia has also been reported.Asthenia, dizziness, drowsiness, nystagmus, and vertigo have been reported with the use of nitrofurantoin. Hypersensitivity reactions are the most frequently spontaneously-reported adverse reactions in worldwide post-marketing experience with nitrofurantoin formulations. In almost all cases the events were reversible after discontinuation of the drug.Peripheral neuropathy can develop during therapy with nitrofurantoin, irrespective of dosage or length of treatment. The neuropathy can become severe and irreversible and is more likely to occur in patients with existing conditions, such as diabetes mellitus, electrolyte imbalance, or vitamin B deficiency, or in otherwise debilitated patients. Manifestations can include paresthesias. Headache is the most common nervous system effect.Hemolytic anemia can occur during therapy with nitrofurantoin, most likely in patients with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency. Neonates under 1 month old are especially vulnerable. Hemolysis necessitates withdrawal of the drug. Cyanosis secondary to methemoglobinemia has been rarely reported. Other hematologic adverse reactions reported with nitrofurantoin include decreased hemoglobin, increased serum phosphorus, eosinophilia, agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia (rare), leukopenia, granulocytopenia, thrombocytopenia, megaloblastic anemia. In most cases, hematologic reactions resolved after discontinuing nitrofurantoin.Nitrofurantoin is commonly associated with adverse GI effects which can include nausea/vomiting, anorexia, diarrhea, and abdominal pain. The macrocrystalline form appears to cause fewer adverse GI effects. These effects also can be minimized by a dose reduction or by taking the drug with food or milk. Sialadenitis and pancreatitis have also been reported. Additionally, there have been sporadic reports of Clostridium difficile superinfection or pseudomembranous colitis with the use of nitrofurantoin.Hepatotoxicity is infrequent during therapy with nitrofurantoin, but it can lead to jaundice with cholestasis, hepatic necrosis, hepatitis or chronic active hepatitis, which can be fatal. Hepatotoxicity can be insidious. Elevated hepatic enzymes indicate hepatotoxicity and the drug should be withdrawn.Treatment with nitrofurantoin can cause urine discoloration to a dark yellow or brown.Nitrofurantoin has been infrequently (< 1%) associated with pseudotumor cerebri (benign intracranial hypertension), confusion depression, optic neuritis, and psychotic reactions. Bulging fontanels, as a sign of benign intracranial hypertension in infants, have been rarely reported.

    Headline
    C. difficile-associated diarrhea, pseudomembranous colitis, superinfection
    General Information
    Microbial overgrowth and superinfection can occur with antibiotic use. C. difficile-associated diarrhea (CDAD) or pseudomembranous colitis has been reported for nearly all antibacterial drugs, including nitrofurantoin, which may range in severity from mild to life-threatening. Treatment with antibacterial agents alters the normal flora of the colon leading to overgrowth of C. difficile. If pseudomembranous colitis is suspected or confirmed, ongoing antibacterial therapy not directed against C. difficile may need to be discontinued. Institute appropriate fluid and electrolyte management, protein supplementation, C. difficile-directed antibacterial therapy, and surgical evaluation as clinically appropriate. Careful medical history is necessary as pseudomembranous colitis has been reported to occur over 2 months after the administration of antibacterial agents.

    General Information
    Administration of nitrofurantoin may result in laboratory test interference.     Headline

    General Information
    Nitrofurantoin may cause male infertility. Based on findings from a fertility study in 36 healthy volunteers and animal studies, nitrofurantoin may reversibly decrease spermatogenesis. Nitrofurantoin doses of 10 mg/kg/day or more in healthy individuals can produce a slight to moderate spermatogenic arrest with a reduction in sperm count. The effect on fertility is unknown.

Description
  Text
  Nitrofurantoin is a nitrofuran antibacterial agent. It is specifically used to treat urinary tract infections caused by many gram-negative and some gram-positive bacteria in adults and pediatric patients 1 month and older. Nitrofurantoin is contraindicated for use in pregnant individuals at term and neonates due to the risk of hemolytic anemia.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Nitrofurantoin is reduced by bacterial flavoproteins to reactive intermediates which inactivate or alter bacterial ribosomal proteins and other macromolecules. As a result of these inactivations, the vital biochemical processes of protein synthesis, aerobic energy metabolism, DNA synthesis, RNA synthesis, and cell wall synthesis are inhibited. Nitrofurantoin is bactericidal in urine at therapeutic doses. This broad-based mode of action may explain the lack of acquired bacterial resistance to nitrofurantoin, as the necessary multiple and simultaneous mutations of the target macromolecules would likely be lethal to the bacteria.
 
The susceptibility interpretive criteria for nitrofurantoin are delineated by pathogen. The MICs are defined for Enterobacterales, Enterococcus sp., and Staphylococcus sp. as susceptible at 32 mcg/mL or less, intermediate at 64 mcg/mL, and resistant at 128 mcg/mL or more.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Nitrofurantoin is readily absorbed after oral administration. Peak concentrations are achieved approximately 2 hours after administration. The macrocrystalline form is more slowly absorbed due to a slower rate of dissolution compared with older, microcrystalline formulations. Bioavailability is increased by approximately 40% when administered with food.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    In patients with impaired renal function, excretion of nitrofurantoin is impaired, and the plasma half-life can be significantly prolonged.
  Text
  Nitrofurantoin is administered orally. Protein binding is approximately 60% to 77%. Nitrofurantoin is highly soluble in urine and is not widely distributed to other tissues. It crosses the placenta and is distributed into breast milk. Nitrofurantoin is rapidly excreted and is concentrated in the urine; approximately 20% to 25% of a single dose of nitrofurantoin is recovered from the urine unchanged over 24 hours. The plasma half-life is approximately 0.3 to 1 hour. After a dose regimen of 100 mg 4 times a day for 7 days, average urinary drug recoveries (0 to 24 hours) on Day 1 and Day 7 were approximately 43% and 44%, respectively.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: none

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  CrCl less than 60 mL/minute: Although the FDA-approved labeling states that use is contraindicated in patients with a CrCl less than 60 mL/minute, data are lacking to support this recommendation. Retrospective studies have shown that short-term nitrofurantoin use is effective and generally well tolerated in patients with a CrCl of 30 mL/minute or more, although higher adverse events have been noted in patients with renal impairment.
