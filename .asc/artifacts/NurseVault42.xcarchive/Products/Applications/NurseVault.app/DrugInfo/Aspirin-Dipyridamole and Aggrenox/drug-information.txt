Drug Class: Aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole is an oral antiplatelet combination medication.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 50 mg/day PO aspirin and 400 mg/day PO dipyridamole.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): bleeding, ecchymosis, epistaxis, gi bleeding, gi perforation, hematemesis, hematoma, intracranial bleeding, peptic ulcer, purpura, hemorrhoids, pancreatitis, pyrosis (heartburn), anemia, aplastic anemia, coagulopathy, disseminated intravascular coagulation (dic), pancytopenia, platelet dysfunction, prolonged bleeding time, thrombocytopenia, thrombocytosis, angina, chest pain (unspecified), flushing, heart failure, hypotension, palpitations, peripheral vasodilation, sinus tachycardia

In an Overdose: The excretion of salicylic acid (but not other metabolites) is enhanced by alkalinization of the urine which is used in the management of aspirin overdose.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Aspirin; dipyridamole is also contraindicated in individuals with a known allergy to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).

Brand Names

Aggrenox

Indication Specific Dosing

For secondary stroke prophylaxis in patients who have had a transient ischemic attack (TIA) or ischemic stroke due to thrombosis

Oral dosage
Adults


25 mg aspirin/200 mg dipyridamole PO twice daily. For stroke prophylaxis in patients with a history of noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or TIA, guidelines recommend long-term aspirin; dipyridamole as a first-line antiplatelet regimen.


Contraindications And Precaution
Drug Interactions

The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.

Hypersensitivity

This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Aspirin; dipyridamole is also contraindicated in individuals with a known allergy to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).

aspirin exacerbated respiratory disease, asthma


Aspirin is contraindicated in individuals with aspirin exacerbated respiratory disease, which may include chronic rhinosinusitis complicated by nasal polyps, severe and potentially fatal acute bronchospasm, and/or intolerance to aspirin and other NSAIDs. When aspirin is used in individuals with pre-existing asthma without known aspirin sensitivity, monitor for changes in the signs and symptoms of asthma.


bleeding disorder, ethanol ingestion, peptic ulcer disease


Avoid aspirin in individuals with a history of or active peptic ulcer disease due to the risk of gastric ulceration and bleeding. Aspirin and dipyridamole increase bleeding risk; risk factors for bleeding include history of bleeding disorder, ethanol ingestion, age 60 years and older, and the use of other drugs that increase the risk of bleeding (e.g., anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, heparin, anagrelide, fibrinolytic therapy and chronic NSAID therapy). Counsel individuals who consume 3 or more alcohol-containing drinks per day about the bleeding risks involved with chronic, heavy alcohol use while taking aspirin.


coronary artery disease, hypotension


Dipyridamole produces peripheral vasodilation, which can exacerbate pre-existing hypotension. Similarly, dipyridamole may precipitate or exacerbate chest pain in individuals with underlying coronary artery disease given its vasodilatory effect. For individuals with stroke or transient ischemic attack for whom aspirin is indicated to prevent recurrent myocardial infarction or angina, the low-dose of aspirin in aspirin; dipyridamole may not provide adequate treatment for cardiac indications.


influenza, varicella


Aspirin; dipyridamole is contraindicated in pediatric populations who have or are recovering from varicella (chicken pox), influenza, or influenza-like symptoms due to the risk of Reye syndrome.


hepatic failure


Avoid aspirin in individuals with severe hepatic dysfunction or hepatic failure.


renal disease


Avoid aspirin in individuals with severe renal failure (i.e., eGFR less than 10 mL/minute). However, guidelines recommend aspirin for primary and secondary prevention of atherosclerotic events with careful monitoring for bleeding complications in individuals undergoing dialysis. Aspirin use decreases GFR and renal blood flow, especially in individuals with pre-existing renal disease. The regular use of aspirin is associated with a dose-dependent increased risk of chronic renal failure.


geriatric


Aspirin increases bleeding risk; risk factors for bleeding include geriatric age of 60 years and older. According to the Beers Criteria, extended-release aspirin; dipyridamole extended-release is considered acceptable. There is an increased risk of GI bleeding and peptic ulcer disease due to aspirin in high-risk groups including those older than 75 years, those taking more than 325 mg/day of aspirin chronically, or those taking oral or parenteral corticosteroids, anticoagulants, or antiplatelet medications concurrently.


labor, obstetric delivery, pregnancy


Available data with aspirin; dipyridamole use during human pregnancy have not identified a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. In general, the use of NSAIDs, including low-dose aspirin for indications other than preeclampsia prophylaxis, is not recommended during the second half of pregnancy. Avoid the use of NSAIDs during pregnancy starting at 30 weeks gestation due to the risk of premature closure of the fetal ductus arteriosus. When use is necessary between 20 and 30 weeks gestation, limit use to the lowest effective dose and shortest duration possible. Consider ultrasound monitoring of amniotic fluid if treatment exceeds 48 hours. Discontinue the NSAID if oligohydramnios develops, and follow up according to clinical practice. Use of NSAIDs around 20 weeks gestation and later may also cause fetal renal dysfunction, leading to oligohydramnios, and in some cases, neonatal renal impairment. These adverse outcomes are seen, on average, after days to weeks of treatment, although oligohydramnios has been infrequently reported as soon as 48 hours after NSAID initiation. Oligohydramnios is often, but not always, reversible with treatment discontinuation. Complications of prolonged oligohydramnios may include limb contractures and delayed lung maturation. In some cases, invasive neonatal procedures such as exchange transfusion or dialysis have been required. Animal reproduction studies with dipyridamole in mice, rabbits, and rats have revealed no evidence of fetal harm at doses up to approximately 25 times the maximum recommended human daily dose of aspirin; dipyridamole. However, nonclinical data are suggestive of a possible potentiation of aspirin-related fetal toxicity when combined with dipyridamole. Additionally, aspirin and dipyridamole increase the risk for bleeding during labor and obstetric delivery. Salicylates have been associated with alterations in maternal and neonatal hemostasis mechanisms, decreased birth weight, and perinatal mortality. Prolonged gestation and labor due to prostaglandin inhibition have been reported.


people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk


Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with aspirin. NSAIDs may delay or prevent prostaglandin-mediated rupture of ovarian follicles, which has been associated with reversible infertility. Small studies with NSAIDs demonstrated a reversible delay in ovulation. Consider withdrawal of NSAIDs in people who may become pregnant who have difficulties conceiving or who are undergoing infertility evaluation.


breast-feeding


Use aspirin; dipyridamole with caution during breast-feeding. Both components are present in human milk. Daily low-dose (e.g., 75 to 162 mg/day) aspirin therapy for vascular indications may be continued during breast-feeding. Aspirin is not present in human milk after daily low-dose aspirin (75 to 325 mg/day) use and salicylate concentrations are low. However, long term use of higher doses are not recommended as salicylates are excreted into human milk, with higher doses resulting in disproportionately higher milk levels which could cause adverse effects in the breastfed child. There is not published data on the use of dipyridamole during breast-feeding. Based on its large molecular weight and high protein binding, concentrations in milk are likely to be low. If aspirin; dipyridamole is used during lactation, monitor the child for bruising and bleeding.


adolescents, children, infants


The Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) list includes aspirin a potentially inappropriate medication for use in infants, children, and adolescents with suspicion of a viral illness and use should be avoided. If avoidance is not possible, use aspirin; dipyridamole with caution and appropriate monitoring.

Pharmacodynamic studies have demonstrated interference with the antiplatelet activity of aspirin when ibuprofen 400 mg 3 times daily is administered with enteric-coated low-dose aspirin. The interaction exists even after ibuprofen 400 mg once daily, particularly when ibuprofen is dosed prior to aspirin. The interaction is alleviated if immediate-release low-dose aspirin is dosed at least 2 hours prior to a once daily regimen of ibuprofen; however, this finding cannot be extended to enteric-coated low-dose aspirin. A decrease in antiplatelet activity (53%) was observed when ibuprofen 400 mg once daily was administered 2 hours before low-dose immediate-release aspirin 81 mg/day for 6 days. An interaction was still observed, but minimized, when ibuprofen 400 mg once daily was administered as early as 8 hours before immediate-release aspirin (90.7%). There was no interaction with the antiplatelet activity of aspirin when ibuprofen 400 mg once daily was administered 2 hours after immediate-release aspirin (99.2%). In another study of low-dose immediate-release aspirin 81 mg/day and ibuprofen 400 mg 3 times daily (1, 7, and 13 hours post-aspirin dose) for 10 consecutive days, there was no interaction with the antiplatelet activity of aspirin (98.3%); however, there were individuals with aspirin antiplatelet activity below 95%, with the lowest being 90.2%. Controlled clinical studies showed that the concomitant use of NSAIDs and analgesic doses of aspirin does not produce any greater therapeutic effect than the use of NSAIDs alone. In a clinical study, the concomitant use of an NSAID and aspirin was associated with a significantly increased incidence of GI adverse reactions as compared to use of the NSAID alone. Ibuprofen is not a substitute for low dose aspirin for cardiovascular protection. Icosapent ethyl: (Moderate) Icosapent ethyl is an ethyl ester of the omega-3 fatty acid eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA). Because omega-3 fatty acids inhibit platelet aggregation, caution is advised when icosapent ethyl is used concurrently with anticoagulants, platelet inhibitors, or thrombolytic agents. Theoretically, the risk of bleeding may be increased, but some studies that combined these agents did not produce clinically significant bleeding events. In one placebo-controlled, randomized, double-blinded, parallel study, patients receiving stable, chronic warfarin therapy were administered various doses of fish oil supplements to determine the effect on INR determinations. Patients were randomized to receive a 4-week treatment period of either placebo or 3 or 6 grams of fish oil daily. Patients were followed on a twice-weekly basis for INR determinations and adverse reactions. There was no statistically significant difference in INRs between the placebo or treatment period within each group. There was also no difference in INRs found between groups. One episode of ecchymosis was reported, but no major bleeding episodes occurred. The authors concluded that fish oil supplementation in doses of 36 grams per day does not have a statistically significant effect on the INR of patients receiving chronic warfarin therapy. However, an increase in INR from 2.8 to 4.3 in a patient stable on warfarin therapy has been reported when increasing the dose of fish oil, omega-3 fatty acids from 1 gram/day to 2 grams/day. The INR decreased once the patient decreased her dose of fish oil to 1 gram/day. This implies that a dose-related effect of fish oil on warfarin may be possible. Patients receiving warfarin that initiate concomitant icosapent ethyl therapy should have their INR monitored more closely and the dose of warfarin adjusted accordingly.

Adverse Reaction
General Information

Most adverse events with the combination product in the ESPS2 study were mild and similar to those with either agent alone.

headache


The most frequently reported adverse effect with aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole was headache (28.25%/year) which was similar to dipyridamole monotherapy (27.91%/year), but higher than for aspirin alone (22.1%/year). Headache was most notable in the first month and the annualized incidence of patient discontinuation of therapy due to headache was 7.2%. After a month, the incidence of headache in the combination group was similar to frequency in the placebo group. It is postulated that tolerance may develop for this adverse effect over time. For intolerable headaches during initial treatment, change to low-dose aspirin in the morning and 1 aspirin; dipyridamole capsule PO once daily at bedtime. Resume the usual regimen as soon as possible, usually within 1 week.


bleeding, ecchymosis, epistaxis, GI bleeding, GI perforation, hematemesis, hematoma, intracranial bleeding, melena, peptic ulcer, purpura


There is an increased bleeding risk with the use of aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole. General bleeding such as unspecified hemorrhage was reported at an annualized incidence of 2.27% per year with aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole compared to 1.06% per with extended-release dipyridamole, 1.82% per year with aspirin, and 0.99% per year with placebo. In the ESPS2 study, the annualized event rate for intracranial bleeding was 0.39%/year, 0.26%/year, 0.24%/year, and 0.29%/year for the aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole group, extended-release dipyridamole group, aspirin group, and placebo group, respectively. Additionally, the annualized event rate for GI bleeding was 2.97%/year for aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole, 1.58%/year for extended-release dipyridamole, 2.06%/year for aspirin, and 1.4%/year for placebo. Other bleeding events reported in ESPS2 study include epistaxis (2%), purpura (1%), hematoma (less than 1%), gingival bleeding (less than 1%), cerebral hemorrhage (less than 1%), subarachnoid hemorrhage (less than 1%), rectal hemorrhage (2%), melena (2%), GI ulceration/peptic ulcer (less than 1%), and GI perforation (less than 1%). Bruising, ecchymosis, and hematemesis have been noted in post-marketing reports.


abdominal pain, diarrhea, dyspepsia, hemorrhoids, nausea, pancreatitis, pyrosis (heartburn), vomiting


Aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole was associated with GI disorders not related to bleeding which included nausea (11.53%/year), vomiting (6.03%/year), abdominal pain (12.62%), dyspepsia (13.23%/year), and diarrhea (9.17%/year) in the ESPS2 study. Hemorrhoids were also reported in 1% of patients in each of the study groups (aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole, extended-release dipyridamole, aspirin, and placebo). Pyrosis (heartburn), pancreatitis, and gastritis have also been reported with aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole therapy. GI side effects should be monitored due to the potential for GI bleeding.


anemia, aplastic anemia, coagulopathy, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), pancytopenia, platelet dysfunction, prolonged bleeding time, thrombocytopenia, thrombocytosis


Aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole can cause platelet dysfunction (i.e., decreased platelet aggregation) leading to prolonged bleeding time. Anemia has been reported in 2% of patients during clinical trials. Over the course of a 24-month study, hemoglobin dropped 0.25 g/dL, hematocrit declined 0.75%, and erythrocyte counts deceased 0.13 x 106/mm3. Coagulopathy, prolonged prothrombin time, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), thrombocytopenia, aplastic anemia, pancytopenia, and thrombocytosis have been noted in post-marketing reports.   


angina, chest pain (unspecified), flushing, heart failure, hypotension, palpitations, peripheral vasodilation, sinus tachycardia, supraventricular tachycardia (SVT)


Cardiovascular adverse events have been reported with the use of aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole. Heart failure was reported in 2% and hypotension occurred in less than 1% of patients treated with aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole for secondary stroke prevention. Dipyridamole produces peripheral vasodilation, which can exacerbate pre-existing hypotension. Dipyridamole's vasodilatory effects may also precipitate or aggravate chest pain (unspecified) in patients with underlying coronary artery disease. Other adverse events reported in less than 1% of patients during clinical trials include sinus tachycardia, palpitations, arrhythmia, supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), and flushing. Angina pectoris has been noted in post-marketing reports.


hearing loss, tinnitus


Tinnitus and deafness/hearing loss occurred in less than 1% of aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole treated patients. Tinnitus is commonly associated with salicylate concentrations more than 200 to 300 mcg/mL. Maximum hearing loss occurs most frequently at salicylate concentrations of 400 mcg/mL or more.


cholelithiasis, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatic failure, hepatitis, jaundice, Reye's syndrome


Aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole has been associated with elevated hepatic enzymes and hepatic failure. Cholelithiasis, jaundice, and abnormal hepatic function have been reported in less than 1% of patients in clinical trials. Hepatitis, hepatic failure, and Reye's syndrome have been noted in post-marketing reports. Reye's syndrome is a multisystem disorder evidenced by persistent vomiting, altered sensorium, elevated hepatic enzymes, hypoprothrombinemia, hyperammonemia, convulsions, and encephalopathy. 


hematuria, interstitial nephritis, proteinuria, renal failure (unspecified)


Renal insufficiency or renal failure (unspecified) occurred in less than 1% and hematuria in less than 1% of patients treated with aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole for secondary stroke prevention. Interstitial nephritis, papillary necrosis, and proteinuria have been noted in post-marketing reports. 


bronchospasm, cough, dyspnea, hemoptysis, infection, pulmonary edema, tachypnea


Respiratory adverse events reported during aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole clinical trials include cough (2%) and upper respiratory tract infection (1%). Other adverse events reported in less than 1% of patients include hyperpnea, asthma, bronchospasm, hemoptysis, and pulmonary edema. Tachypnea and dyspnea have been noted in post-marketing reports.


angioedema, laryngeal edema, pruritus, urticaria, vasculitis


Hypersensitivity reactions can occur with the use of aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole. Aspirin may cause severe urticaria, angioedema, or bronchospasm. Allergic reaction, urticaria, and pruritus have been reported in less than 1% of patients during aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole clinical trials. Acute anaphylaxis, hypersensitivity, laryngeal edema, and allergic vasculitis have been noted in post-marketing reports.


alopecia, rash, Stevens-Johnson syndrome


Dermatologic reactions to aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole can occur. Adverse reactions reported during postmarketing surveillance include rash, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, and alopecia.


hyperuricemia


Asprin; dipyridamole may cause hyperuricemia. Salicylates can affect uric acid secretion and reabsorption by the kidney. Low doses inhibit tubular uric acid secretion whereas high doses may increase uric acid secretion and reabsorption so that the net effect is uricosuric.


hypoglycemia


Hypoglycemia has been noted in post-marketing reports with aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole.


dehydration, hyperglycemia, hyperkalemia, hypokalemia, metabolic acidosis


Aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole use may result in alterations of fluid and electrolytes as well as metabolic disorders. Hyperglycemia and thirst have been reported in less than 1% of patients during clinical trials. Dehydration, hyperkalemia, hypokalemia, metabolic acidosis, and respiratory alkalosis have been noted in post-marketing reports.


agitation, amnesia, anorexia, cerebral edema, coma, confusion, dizziness, drowsiness, migraine, paresthesias, seizures


Nervous system or psychiatric adverse events reported during aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole clinical trials include convulsions/seizures (2%), amnesia (2%), confusion (1%), anorexia (1%), and somnolence/drowsiness (1%). Other adverse events reported in less than 1% of patients during trials include coma, dizziness, paresthesias, and agitation. Cerebral edema and migraine have been noted in post-marketing reports.


asthenia, back pain, fatigue, fever, hypothermia, malaise, syncope


General adverse events reported during aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole clinical trials include pain (6%), fatigue (6%), back pain (5%), accidental injury (3%), malaise (2%), asthenia (2%), syncope (1%), and unspecified neoplasm (2%). Fever and taste loss occurred in less than 1% of patients. Hypothermia was noted in post-marketing reports.


arthralgia, myalgia, rhabdomyolysis


Musculoskeletal adverse events reported during aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole clinical trials include arthralgia (6%), arthritis (2%), arthrosis (1%), and myalgia (1%). Rhabdomyolysis was noted in post-marketing reports.


fetal death


Reproductive adverse events have been reported during aspirin; dipyridamole clinical trials. Uterine hemorrhage was noted in less than 1% of patients, while prolonged pregnancy and labor, stillbirths (fetal death), lower birth weight infants, and antepartum/postpartum bleeding have been noted in post-marketing reports.


Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS)


Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS), a multi-organ hypersensitivity reaction, has occurred with NSAIDs. Some of these events have been life-threatening or fatal. DRESS typically presents as fever, rash, and/or lymphadenopathy in conjunction with other organ system involvement including hepatitis, nephritis, hematologic abnormalities, myocarditis, or myositis sometimes resembling an acute viral infection. Eosinophilia is often present. Early manifestations such as fever and lymphadenopathy may be present without evidence of a rash. Discontinue the NSAID in patients presenting with such signs and symptoms in whom an alternative etiology cannot be identified. DRESS has been reported with postmarketing use of aspirin; dipyridamole.


infertility


NSAIDs, such as aspirin, may delay or prevent prostaglandin-mediated rupture of ovarian follicles, which has been associated with reversible infertility. Small studies with NSAIDs demonstrated a reversible delay in ovulation. Consider withdrawal of NSAIDs in people who may become pregnant who have difficulties conceiving or who are undergoing infertility evaluation.


Description

Aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole is an oral antiplatelet combination medication. Aspirin is an acetylated salicylate antiplatelet agent, which also has analgesic, antiinflammatory, and antipyretic effects. Dipyridamole is a non-nitrate coronary vasodilator with antiplatelet properties. Aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole is indicated to reduce the risk of stroke in patients who have had transient ischemia of the brain or completed ischemic stroke due to thrombosis. For patients with a history of noncardioembolic transient ischemic attack (TIA) or ischemic stroke, the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association recommend aspirin; dipyridamole as a first-line antiplatelet regimen (Class I; Level of Evidence of B). For stroke or TIA patients for whom aspirin is indicated to prevent recurrent myocardial infarction (MI) or angina pectoris, the low aspirin dosage in asipirin, ASA; dipyridamole may not be adequate for the cardiac indications.

Mechanism Of Action

Aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole is a combination product that combines different mechanisms of action to inhibit platelet aggregation, leading to additive effects on stroke reduction.
Aspirin, ASA: Aspirin-induced inhibition of thromboxane A2 (TXA2) and prostacyclin (PGI-2) has opposing effects on hemostasis. TXA2 is a potent vasoconstrictor and platelet agonist, while PGI-2 inhibits platelet aggregation and vascular smooth muscle contraction. However, data suggest that the effects of aspirin-induced TXA2 inhibition predominate clinically. This may be due to the ability of vascular endothelial cells to regenerate new COX and recover normal function, while COX inhibition in platelets is irreversible due to the limited amount of mRNA and protein synthesis in these cells. This distinction also allows for the use of very low doses of aspirin to retard platelet aggregation. The antithrombotic actions of aspirin are primarily mediated by COX-1 inhibition; COX-1 produces TXA2. Aspirin may also inhibit platelet activation by neutrophils. The antiplatelet effects of aspirin result in a prolonged bleeding time, which returns to normal roughly 36 hours after the last dose of the drug. Antiplatelet effects occur before acetylsalicylic acid is detectable in the peripheral blood due to exposure of platelets in the portal circulation. In very high and toxic doses, aspirin also exerts a direct inhibitory effect on vitamin K-dependent hemostasis by inhibiting the synthesis of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors. Prothrombin synthesis is impaired, resulting in hypoprothrombinemia.
Dipyridamole: Dipyridamole inhibits adenosine uptake into platelets, endothelial cells, and erythrocytes. Adenosine induces vasodilation directly by stimulating adenosine receptors on the smooth muscle membrane and/or indirectly by increasing the synthesis of cyclic-3',5'-adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), an inhibitor of platelet function. Increased levels of adenosine act on the platelet A2-receptor stimulating platelet adenylate cyclase and increasing platelet cAMP levels; thereby, inhibiting platelet aggregation in response to various stimuli, such as platelet activating factor (PAF), collagen, and adenosine diphosphate (ADP). Adenosine also interferes with enzymatic degradation of cAMP by phosphodiesterase. Dipyridamole presumably inhibits adenosine deaminase as well as phosphodiesterase, allowing levels of cAMP to remain increased.


Pharmacokinetics

Aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole is administered orally. Aggrenox is formulated to provide 25 mg of aspirin for immediate-release and 200 mg of dipyridamole for extended-release. There are no significant interactions between aspirin and dipyridamole. The kinetics of the components are unchanged by their coadministration as Aggrenox.
Aspirin: Aspirin is poorly bound to plasma proteins with an apparent volume of distribution (Vd) of 10 L. Its metabolite, salicylic acid, is highly bound to plasma proteins, but its binding is concentration-dependent (nonlinear). At low concentrations (less than 100 mcg/mL), approximately 90% of salicylic acid is bound to albumin. Salicylic acid is widely distributed to all tissues and fluids in the body, including the central nervous system, breast milk, and fetal tissues. Aspirin is rapidly hydrolyzed to salicylic acid and other metabolites, with a half-life of about 20 minutes. Salicylic acid, but not aspirin itself, undergoes saturable kinetics resulting in a decrease in total body clearance at higher serum concentrations. At low doses, the elimination is first-order and the half-life remains constant at 2 to 3 hours. At higher doses, salicylic acid elimination follows zero-order kinetics, with an apparent half-life of 6 hours or greater. Salicylic acid and its metabolites are excreted primarily by the kidneys. Almost all the ingested dose is excreted in the urine. Following therapeutic doses, about 75% is excreted as salicyluric acid, as phenolic and acyl glucuronides, and 10% as salicylic acid in the urine. The excretion of salicylic acid (but not other metabolites) is enhanced by alkalinization of the urine which is used in the management of aspirin overdose. As urinary pH rises above 6.5, the renal clearance of free salicylate increases from less than 5% to greater than 80%.
Dipyridamole: Dipyridamole distributes widely throughout the body tissues, crosses the placenta, and is secreted into breast milk. Approximately 99% of dipyridamole is bound to plasma proteins, predominantly to alpha 1-acid glycoprotein and albumin. Dipyridamole is highly lipophilic; however, the drug does not cross the blood-brain barrier to any significant extent in animal models. The steady-state Vd of dipyridamole is approximately 92 L. Dipyridamole undergoes hepatic metabolism, primarily glucuronidation, and these glucuronide conjugates are eliminated mainly in the feces, although enterohepatic circulation can occur. Renal excretion of dipyridamole is negligible and urinary excretion of the glucuronide metabolite is low (about 5%). The terminal phase plasma half-life following oral administration is 13.6 hours.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP) isoenzymes and drug transporters: none

Route-Specific Pharmacokinetics
Oral Route

Aspirin; dipyridamole: When aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole capsules are taken with a high-fat meal, the AUC for aspirin is unchanged and the Cmax for aspirin is decreased by 50%; this effect is not considered clinically significant based on a similar degree of cyclooxygenase inhibition for the fed versus the fasted state. Coadministration of aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole capsules with a high-fat meal decreases dipyridamole Cmax and AUC values by 20 to 30% compared to fasting; this effect is not considered clinically significant based on similar inhibition of adenosine uptake at these dipyridamole plasma concentrations. Following twice-daily oral administration of aspirin, ASA 25 mg; dipyridamole 200 mg, peak plasma levels of salicylic acid and dipyridamole are achieved in 0.63 hours (0.5 to 1 hours) and 2 hours (range 1 to 6 hours), respectively. The peak plasma concentration at steady-state for salicylic acid is 319 ng/mL (175 to 463 ng/ml); the dipyridamole peak plasma concentration at steady-state is 1.98 mcg/mL (1.01 to 3.99 mcg/mL), and the steady-state trough concentration is 0.53 mcg/mL (0.18 to 1.01 mcg/mL).

Hepatic Impairment

Studies of aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole in patients with hepatic impairment have not been conducted. The package labeling recommends avoiding the use of aspirin-containing products, such as aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole, in patients with severe hepatic impairment.
Aspirin: Pharmacokinetic data are unavailable in patients with hepatic impairment; however, aspirin is extensively metabolized in the liver, and patients with hepatic impairment may have decreased elimination.
Dipyridamole: In patients with mild to severe hepatic insufficiency who received intravenous dipyridamole, there was no change in plasma concentrations of dipyridamole.


Renal Impairment

The package labeling recommends avoiding the use of aspirin-containing products, such as aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole, in patients with severe renal impairment (CrCl less than 10 mL/minute).
Aspirin: Pharmacokinetic data are unavailable in patients with renal impairment. Aspirin is renally excreted and patients with renal impairment may have decreased elimination. Aspirin is 50% to 100% hemodialyzable.
Dipyridamole: After the data from the ESPS2 study was corrected for age, renal impairment (i.e., defined as CrCl ranging from approximately 15 mL/min to greater than 100 mL/min) had no effect on the pharmacokinetics of dipyridamole or its glucuronide metabolite. Dipyridamole is unlikely to be significantly removed by hemodialysis due to its high protein binding.


Geriatric

The AUC of dipyridamole in healthy elderly subjects (greater than 65 years) was about 40% higher than in subjects younger than 55 years receiving treatment with aspirin, ASA; dipyridamole in the ESPS2 study.
