Drug Class: Hydroxychloroquine is an oral, 4-aminoquinoline derivative used as an antimalarial drug and also as a disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) used to treat rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Patients with chloroquine hypersensitivity may have cross sensitivity to hydroxychloroquine. Hydroxychloroquine should not be used in patients with known hydroxychloroquine hypersensitivity, or with a known hypersensitivity to 4-aminoquinolines.
 
Use of hydroxychloroquine is contraindicated in the presence of retinal or visual field changes attributable to any 4-aminoquinoline compound (see Adverse Reactions). Also, hydroxychloroquine should not be used by patients who have ocular disease because the drug can cause corneal opacities, keratopathy, or retinopathy. In patients with normal vision, these effects are generally dose-related and, except for retinopathy, are generally reversible upon discontinuation of the drug. Retinopathy can lead to blindness and may progress even after the drug is discontinued. Ophthalmological examinations should be performed before and during treatment at regular intervals if the drug is used; the drug should be discontinued at the first signs of visual change. The use of hydroxychloroquine should be approached with caution in patients with Fabry disease, particularly those with ocular symptoms. The drug can cause a keratopathy that is clinically and ultrastructurally indistinguishable from keratopathy caused by Fabry disease; this drug-induced keratopathy is reversible with drug cessation. In addition, hydroxychloroquine poses a theoretical risk of decreased intracellular alpha-galactosidase A activity in Fabry disease patients. Prior to prescribing hydroxychloroquine, physicians should completely familiarize themselves with the current prescribing information for hydroxychloroquine.
 
Hydroxychloroquine should be used with extreme caution in patients with psoriasis or porphyria because it has been shown to precipitate severe attacks.
 
Hydroxychloroquine should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease or a history of alcoholism because the drug concentrates in the liver and can produce toxic effects. Patients receiving other hepatotoxic drugs also should be treated with caution.
 
Hydroxychloroquine can cause gastric irritation and should be used with caution in patients with GI disease. It can be taken with meals or milk to minimize gastric irritation.
 
Hydroxychloroquine also can cause blood dyscrasias and anemias and should be used with caution in patients with preexisting hematological disease. Regular complete blood counts should be made for patients on long-term therapy and the drug discontinued immediately if abnormalities appear. Patients with known glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency (G6PD deficiency) should not receive hydroxychloroquine.
 
Hydroxychloroquine should be used with caution in patients with dental disease. Hydroxychloroquine can cause myelosuppression and there may be an increased risk of infection. Dental work should be performed prior to initiating hydroxychloroquine therapy or deferred until blood counts return to normal. Patients should be instructed on proper oral hygiene.
 
Hydroxychloroquine should be used with caution in patients with neurological disease because polyneuritis, ototoxicity, seizures, or neuromyopathy can develop. Symptoms of muscle weakness and response of knee and ankle reflexes should be investigated regularly. If muscle weakness develops, the drug should be discontinued.
 
Long-term hydroxychloroquine therapy is contraindicated in children. Children are especially more sensitive to the 4-aminoquinoline compounds than are adults. Fatalities have been reported after accidental ingestion of chloroquine, in some cases, relatively small doses (e.g., 0.75 g or 1 g in one 3-year-old child) were involved. Strongly warn patients to keep hydroxychloroquine out of the reach of infants and children. Prior to prescribing hydroxychloroquine, physicians should completely familiarize themselves with the current prescribing information for hydroxychloroquine.
 
In general, avoid the use of hydroxychloroquine during pregnancy. If the physician determines that the benefit outweighs the possible hazard, hydroxychloroquine may be used for the suppression or treatment of malaria. Hydroxychloroquine may also be appropriate for pregnancies complicated by lupus. In mice, radioactively-tagged chloroquine administered intravenously passed rapidly across the placenta and accumulated selectively in the melanin structures of the fetal eyes. Chloroquine was retained in the ocular tissues for five months after the drug had been eliminated from the rest of the body.
 
The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) lists hydroxychloroquine as a maternal medication usually compatible with breast-feeding; hydroxychloroquine is concentrated in human milk. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Hydroxychloroquine is also contraindicated in individuals with known hypersensitivity to 4-aminoquinoline compounds.
    Headline
    Asian patients, macular disease
    General Information
    Irreversible retinal damage was observed in some individuals treated with hydroxychloroquine. Risk factors for retinal damage include hydroxychloroquine dosages of 6.5 mg/kg/day (5 mg/kg/day base) or more of actual body weight, durations of use longer than 5 years, renal impairment, use of concomitant medications such as tamoxifen, and concurrent macular disease. Perform a baseline ocular exam, including best corrected distance visual acuity (BCVA), automated threshold visual field (VF) of the central 10 degrees (with retesting if an abnormality is noted), and spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT), within the first year of hydroxychloroquine treatment. In Asian patients, retinal toxicity may first be noticed outside the macula; perform VF testing in the central 24 degrees instead of the central 10 degrees in Asians. For individuals at higher risk of retinal damage, monitoring should include annual examinations which include BCVA, VF, and SD-OCT. For individuals without significant risk factors, annual exams can usually be deferred until 5 years of treatment.
    Headline
    bradycardia, cardiac disease, cardiomyopathy, congenital long QT syndrome, coronary artery disease, females, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, QT prolongation, ventricular arrhythmias
    General Information
    Avoid use of hydroxychloroquine in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk. Also avoid hydroxychloroquine in individuals with cardiac disease or a history of ventricular arrhythmias. Correct electrolyte imbalances prior to hydroxychloroquine use.
    Headline
    hepatic disease
    General Information
    A hydroxychloroquine dose reduction may be necessary in individuals with hepatic disease.
    Headline
    renal disease
    General Information
    A hydroxychloroquine dose reduction may be necessary in individuals with renal disease. Irreversible retinal damage was observed in some individuals treated with hydroxychloroquine. Risk factors for retinal damage include renal impairment.
    Headline
    porphyria
    General Information
    Avoid hydroxychloroquine in individuals with porphyria, as it may exacerbate the condition.
    Headline
    psoriasis
    General Information
    Avoid hydroxychloroquine in individuals with psoriasis unless the benefit outweighs the possible risk, as it may precipitate a severe exacerbation of the disease.
    Headline
    G6PD deficiency
    General Information
    Hemolysis has been reported with hydroxychloroquine in individuals with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency (G6PD deficiency). Monitor for hemolytic anemia as this can occur, particularly in association with other medications that cause hemolysis.
    Headline
    major psychiatric disorder
    General Information
    Advise individuals with a major psychiatric disorder to contact their health care provider promptly if they experience worsening neuropsychiatric symptoms, such as depression, mood changes, or suicidal thoughts or behavior. Suicidal behavior, suicidal ideation, and other neuropsychiatric adverse reactions have been reported with hydroxychloroquine.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, hypoglycemia
    General Information
    Monitor blood sugar concentrations in individuals with diabetes mellitus or individuals presenting with clinical symptoms suggestive of hypoglycemia who are receiving hydroxychloroquine. Hydroxychloroquine can cause severe, life-threatening hypoglycemia in the presence or absence of antidiabetic medications. Adjust antidiabetic medication as necessary.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Use cautious dose selection for hydroxychloroquine in geriatric adults, starting with the lowest recommended dose and taking into consideration the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function, and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Prolonged clinical experience over decades of use and available data from published epidemiologic and clinical studies with hydroxychloroquine use during pregnancy have not identified a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. Hydroxychloroquine readily crosses the placenta with cord blood levels corresponding to plasma levels in the pregnant individual. No retinal toxicity, ototoxicity, cardiotoxicity, or growth and developmental abnormalities have been observed in children who were exposed to hydroxychloroquine in utero. Available epidemiologic and clinical studies have methodological limitations, including small sample size and study design. There are risks to the patient and fetus associated with untreated or increased disease activity from malaria, rheumatoid arthritis, and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) in the pregnant person that should be considered. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors pregnancy outcomes in patients exposed to hydroxychloroquine during pregnancy. Encourage patients to register by contacting 1-877-311-8972. Hydroxychloroquine is considered to be compatible for use during pregnancy in some anti-rheumatic guidelines, and may be continued in pregnancy for maintenance of remission or treatment of a disease flare. Hydroxychloroquine may also be appropriate for pregnancies complicated by lupus. Guidelines also recommend hydroxychloroquine as an alternative to chloroquine as a treatment option for acute malaria and for prophylaxis in pregnant people during all trimesters. Animal reproductive studies have not been conducted.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Hydroxychloroquine is compatible with breast-feeding. Hydroxychloroquine is present in human milk at low levels. During lactation studies, breast milk concentrations ranged from 10.6 to 1,392 mcg/L. A breastfed child would likely receive 0.2 mg/kg or less of hydroxychloroquine. No adverse reactions have been reported in breast-fed children exposed to hydroxychloroquine. In infants monitored for up to at least 1 year of age, careful follow-up found no adverse effects on growth, vision, or hearing. Anti-rheumatic guidelines recommend continuing hydroxychloroquine during lactation for maintenance of remission or treatment of a disease flare. There is no information on the effect of hydroxychloroquine on milk production.

Acarbose: (Moderate) Careful monitoring of blood glucose is recommended when hydroxychloroquine and antidiabetic agents, including the alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, are coadministered. A decreased dose of the antidiabetic agent may be necessary as severe hypoglycemia has been reported in patients treated concomitantly with hydroxychloroquine and an antidiabetic agent.
acetaZOLAMIDE: (Moderate) Caution is warranted with the coadministration of hydroxychloroquine and antiepileptic drugs, such as acetazolamide. Hydroxychloroquine can lower the seizure threshold; therefore, the activity of antiepileptic drugs may be impaired with concomitant use.

  General Information
  Hydroxychloroquine can cause ocular toxicity, but the incidence of corneal changes and visual side effects appears to be considerably lower with hydroxychloroquine than with chloroquine. Corneal changes such as transient edema, punctate to lineal opacities, and decreased corneal sensitivity are fairly common and are reversible; symptoms may include blurred vision, halos around lights, and photophobia. Corneal deposits of hydroxychloroquine can also lead to blurred vision and appearance of halos around lights. Corneal deposits may appear as early as three weeks after hydroxychloroquine initiation. Patients may also have blurred vision because of a disturbance of accommodation; the reaction is dose-related and is reversible with hydroxychloroquine cessation. Patients may have visual field defects such as pericentral or paracentral scotoma, central scotoma with decreased visual acuity (visual impairment), rarely field constriction, or abnormal color vision. Retinopathy is the most likely visual disturbance to occur following long-term therapy with hydroxychloroquine, although, as of 1991, only 18 cases of retinopathy causing vision loss have been reported, mainly in patients receiving more than 200 mg/day. Retinopathy appears to be dose related and has occurred within several months to several years of daily therapy; a small number of cases have been reported several years after antimalarial drug therapy was discontinued. Patients with retinal changes may have visual symptoms or may be asymptomatic. Symptoms may include reading and seeing difficulties such as words, letters, or parts of objects missing; photophobia; blurred distance vision; missing or blacked out areas in the central or peripheral visual field; light flashes; and streaks. Rarely, scotomatous vision or field defects may occur without obvious retinal change. Paracentral scotoma to red targets is indicative of early retinal dysfunction, which is usually reversible with cessation of therapy. In a number of patients, early retinopathy such as macular pigmentation sometimes with central field defects diminished or regressed completely after therapy was discontinued. Retinopathy can continue to progress even after the drug has been discontinued, and irreversible retinal damage has been observed in some patients who received long-term or high-dosage 4-aminoquinoline therapy for discoid and systemic lupus erythematosus or for rheumatoid arthritis. Furthermore, a case of delayed retinopathy has been reported; a patient had vision loss that started one year after hydroxychloroquine discontinuation. Other adverse effects may include macular edema; macular atrophy; abnormal pigmentation ranging from mild pigment stippling to a 'bull's-eye' appearance; loss of foveal reflex; increased macular recovery time after bright light exposure; and elevated retinal threshold to red light in macular, paramacular, and peripheral retinal areas. Other fundus changes include optic disc pallor, optic atrophy, attenuation of retinal arterioles, fine granular pigmentary disturbances in the peripheral retina, and prominent choroidal patterns in advanced stage. Ophthalmologic examinations including visual acuity, expert slit-lamp, funduscopic, and visual field tests should be performed at baseline and every 3 months during hydroxychloroquine receipt. If there is any indication of abnormality in the visual acuity, visual field, or retinal macular areas such as pigmentary changes or loss of foveal reflex or if the patient has any visual symptoms such as light flashes and streaks that are not fully explainable by difficulties of accommodation or corneal opacities, immediately discontinue hydroxychloroquine, and closely observe the patient for possible progression. Retinal changes in a small number of patients who received hydroxychloroquine usually consisted of alteration in retinal pigmentation that was detected on periodic ophthalmologic examination. Hydroxychloroquine is contraindicated in the presence of retinal or visual field changes attributable to any 4-aminoquinoline compound.Allergic reactions such as urticaria, angioedema, and bronchospasm have been reported. Allergic skin eruptions are common and may be in the form of urticarial, morbilliform, lichenoid, maculopapular, purpuric, erythema annulare centrifugum, and exfoliative dermatitis. Pruritus and pigmentary skin changes can occur. Pruritus is not necessarily accompanied by rash and is not alleviated by antihistamines. It generally occurs within 6—48 hours of ingestion of the drug and affects any area of the body. Pruritus is more common in black patients, but is not a cause for discontinuation of therapy. Due to high affinity of hydroxychloroquine for melanin-containing cells, blue-black skin discoloration can persist after discontinuation of therapy. Alopecia, bleaching of hair, photosensitivity, and Stevens-Johnson syndrome have also been reported.An acute attack of nonlight sensitive psoriasis can be precipitated by hydroxychloroquine in predisposed patients. Hydroxychloroquine may also exacerbate or precipitate porphyria.Hydroxychloroquine has been associated with acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP). The nonfollicular, pustular, erythematous rash starts suddenly, is associated with fever above 38 degrees C, and is distinct from pustular psoriasis, although biopsy results in each reveal spongiform subcorneal pustules. Drugs are the main cause of AGEP. A period of 2—3 weeks after an inciting drug exposure appears necessary for a first episode of AGEP. Unintentional reexposure may cause a second episode within 2 days. Clinical presentation is diverse with cutaneous lesions beyond erythema and pustules present in half of the cases. For example, bullous lesions, edema, purpura, pruritus, and mucosal erosions are possible. The mean duration of the pustules is 9.7 days followed by an annular desquamation, as long as the causative drug or factor is discontinued. The physiopathological mechanisms of AGEP have not been determined but the pathological criteria of edema, leukocytoclastic vasculitis, eosinophil exocytosis, and keratinocyte focal necrosis are distinctive. Pustule confluence or very small pustules may lead a clinician to make an incorrect diagnosis of TEN, of drug-induced erythroderma, or of staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome.Skeletal muscle palsies or skeletal muscle myopathy or neuromyopathy generally result from prolonged treatment with hydroxychloroquine and is reversible. Progressive weakness and atrophy of proximal muscle groups may occur and may be associated with mild sensory changes, tendon reflex depression, and abnormal nerve conduction. Periodic examination of knee and ankle reflexes should be undertaken, and any signs of muscle weakness should be investigated. Discontinue hydroxychloroquine if weakness ocurs.Hematological adverse reactions of hydroxychloroquine include anemia, agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, pancytopenia, neutropenia, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia. Hematological toxicity is rare, but abnormal bleeding or bruising, fever or sore throat, and unusual weakness and tiredness should be investigated promptly.Hearing loss and tinnitus may become apparent only after discontinuation of the drug and is generally associated only with high doses of hydroxychloroquine. Patients with preexisting hearing difficulties are most at risk.Cardiomyopathy has been rarely reported with high daily dosages of hydroxychloroquine. Other cardiovascular adverse reactions include ECG changes and hypotension. These reactions are more likely during long-term therapy or high doses. Patients should be monitored for cardiac arrhythmias.Adverse GI effects include nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain (cramps), diarrhea, and anorexia. These effects are associated with oral administration and can be minimized by taking hydroxychloroquine with food. Also, isolated cases of abnormal liver function and fulminant hepatic failure have been reported.CNS adverse reactions can include headache, dizziness, confusion, emotional changes, irritability, psychosis, nervousness, nightmares, vertigo, nystagmus, ataxia, and seizures.

    Hydroxychloroquine can cause ocular toxicity including irreversible retinal damage related to cumulative dose and treatment duration. A baseline ocular exam should be performed within the first year of hydroxychloroquine treatment. This baseline exam should include best corrected distance visual acuity (BCVA), an automated threshold visual field (VF) of the central 10 degrees (with retesting if an abnormality is noted), and spectral domain ocular coherence tomography (SD-OCT). Description
  Text
  Hydroxychloroquine is an oral, 4-aminoquinoline derivative used as an antimalarial drug and also as a disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) used to treat rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus. Hydroxychloroquine is used mostly in individuals with mild or new-onset RA or as an add-on therapy; it is often preferred over methotrexate for DMARD-naive individuals with low RA disease activity because it lacks liver toxicity. Irreversible retinal damage has been observed with use, and cases of life-threatening and fatal cardiomyopathy, including ventricular arrhythmias and torsade de pointes, have been reported. Hydroxychloroquine was initially FDA-approved in 1955.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Hydroxychloroquine, a 4-aminoquinoline antimalarial, is a weak base and may exert its antimalarial effect by concentrating in the acid vesicles of the plasmodia and by inhibiting the polymerization of heme. It can also inhibit certain enzymes by its interaction with DNA. Organisms with reduced susceptibilities to chloroquine also show reduced susceptibilities to hydroxychloroquine.
 
Although the mechanisms underlying the antiinflammatory and immunomodulatory effects of hydroxychloroquine in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis, chronic discoid lupus erythematous, and systemic lupus erythematosus are not fully known, several possible mechanisms of action have been proposed. It is unclear if these mechanisms work similarly for rheumatic and autoimmune diseases. Potential mechanisms include reduced cytokine production, inhibition of immune effector cells, inhibition of platelet function, protection of the cell surface from external disturbances, competitive binding to nucleic acid ligands or toll-like receptors (TLRs), interference with lysosomal function, reduction of leakage of lysosomal enzymes, and interference with endosomal NADPH oxidase (NOX).

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    The mean absolute oral bioavailability of hydroxychloroquine is 79% under fasting conditions. In rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients, there is a large variability in absorption (30% to 100%) with mean concentrations significantly higher in patients with less disease activity. After a singe 200 mg dose to healthy volunteers, the peak whole blood concentration (Cmax) was 129.6 ng/mL (plasma Cmax was 50.3 ng/mL) and the time to reach peak blood concentration (Tmax) was 3.3 hours (plasma Tmax was 3.7 hours). Peak blood concentrations of the metabolites were observed at the same time as peak hydroxychloroquine concentrations. Steady state concentrations are achieved by 6 weeks after oral administration of 400 mg daily in RA patients. At steady state, the whole blood concentration of hydroxychloroquine is dose proportional over a range of 200 to 400 mg daily in patients with RA and lupus.
    Name
    Intravenous Route
    Description
    After a single 155 mg intravenous infusion of hydroxychloroquine in healthy volunteers, peak blood concentrations (Cmax) ranged from 1,161 ng/mL to 2,436 ng/mL (mean 1,918 ng/mL). The Cmax range after a single 310 mg infusion was 2,290 ng/mL and 4,211 ng/mL; thus, indicating linear pharmacokinetics.
  Text
  Hydroxychloroquine is administered orally. It is approximately 50% bound to plasma proteins and is widely distributed into body tissues, with high concentrations in the bone marrow, liver, kidneys, lungs, adrenal gland, and pituitary gland. Hydroxychloroquine has a high affinity for melanin and thus is concentrated in the choroid and ciliary body of the eye, which may account for the retinal toxicity. Cellular concentrations have been shown to be higher in mononuclear cells than in polymorphonuclear leukocytes. Hydroxychloroquine is primarily metabolized by CYP2C8, CYP3A4, and CYP2D6 as well as by flavin-containing monooxygenase 1 (FMO-1) and monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A). Three metabolites have been identified, including the major metabolite, desethylhydroxychloroquine (DHCQ), as well as desethylchloroquine (DCQ) and bidesethylhydroxychloroquine (BDCQ). Renal clearance of unchanged drug ranges from 16% to 30% and does not change with chronic dosing. The half-life of a single 200 mg oral dose is approximately 40 days. After chronic administration, the absorption half-life is approximately 3 to 4 hours and the terminal half-life in whole blood ranges from 40 to 50 days. In rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients, the effective half-life is likely to be shorter.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2C8, CYP2D6, CYP3A4, P-gp, MATE1, MATE2-K
In vitro data suggest that hydroxychloroquine is metabolized primarily by CYP2C8 and CYP3A4, and to a much lesser extent, by CYP2D6. It has also been shown to be an inhibitor of the drug transporter P-glycoprotein (P-gp). An in vitro study suggests that hydroxychloroquine also has the potential to inhibit CYP2D6, CYP3A4, and multidrug and toxin extrusion transporters MATE1 and MATE2-K. In vitro data suggest that hydroxychloroquine does not inhibit CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, OATP1B1, OATP1B3, OAT1, OAT3, OCT1, and OCT2. In vitro data suggest that hydroxychloroquine has no significant potential to induce CYP1A2, CYP2B6, and CYP3A4.
