Eighty percent of Cogan syndrome patients are between 14 and 47 years (1). Cogan syndrome affects males and females equally. The disease appears to result from an autoimmune reaction directed against an unknown common autoantigen in the cornea and inner ear. Some patients may also have severe systemic vasculitis, which may include life-threatening aortitis.

Symptoms and Signs of Cogan Syndrome

Cogan syndrome can be categorized into 2 subgroups based on clinical presentation. The first subgroup is typical Cogan syndrome in which ophthalmic and vestibulo-auditory symptoms arise within 2 years of each other. In contrast, in atypical Cogan syndrome, there is a delay between these symptoms of greater than 2 years (1).

The presenting symptoms involve the ocular system, the vestibuloauditory system, or both. By 5 months of onset, most patients have both ocular and vestibuloauditory symptoms. Nonspecific systemic complaints include fever, headache, joint pain, and myalgia.

Ocular

Ocular involvement includes any combination of the following:

Interstitial keratitis (the main ophthalmological finding in Cogan syndrome, typically bilateral but may be unilateral)

Other types of corneal stromal keratitis

Episcleritis or scleritis

Uveitis

Retinal vasculitis

Papillitis

Other orbital inflammation (eg, vitritis, choroiditis)

Ocular symptoms include irritation, pain, redness, photophobia, and decreased vision. Ocular examination shows a patchy corneal stromal infiltrate typical of interstitial keratitis, ocular redness, optic nerve edema, proptosis, or a combination of these symptoms.

Vestibuloauditory

Vestibuloauditory symptoms can include sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, and/or a sensation of fullness or pressure. Patients may demonstrate ataxia or nystagmus on clinical examination.

Vascular

A diastolic heart murmur may be present when aortitis is significant. Claudication may be present if limb vessels are affected.

Diagnosis of Cogan Syndrome

History and physical examination

Urgent evaluation by an ophthalmologist and otolaryngologist is indicated when Cogan syndrome is suspected. Diagnosis is based on clinical findings and exclusion of other causes of stromal keratitis (eg, syphilis, Lyme disease, Epstein-Barr virus infection, herpes, tuberculosis) by appropriate serologic tests. Antibodies to inner ear antigens, anti-Hsp70 (associated with sensorineural hearing loss), and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (associated with vasculitis) have been found in patients with Cogan syndrome. CT and MRI should be performed and may be normal or demonstrate labyrinthitis, inflammation, or calcification of the semicircular canals, vestibule, or cochlea.

Treatment of Cogan Syndrome

Initially topical and sometimes subconjunctival or systemic glucocorticoids for ocular involvement

Untreated disease may lead to corneal scarring and vision loss. Keratitis usually responds well to topical steroids. With treatment, the risk of permanent hearing loss decreases to approximately 50% (1). Keratitis (and symptoms of corneal involvement), episcleritis, and anterior uveitis can usually be treated with topical prednisolone acetate 1%, 1 drop 4 times daily up to once every hour while awake, for 2 to 3 weeks. To treat deeper ocular inflammation and to treat vestibuloauditory symptoms before they become permanent, prednisone 1 mg/kg orally once a day is begun as soon as possible and continued for 2 to 6 months. Some clinicians add cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, cyclosporine, infliximab, or rituximab for any recalcitrant manifestations of Cogan syndrome.

Key Points

Cogan syndrome is a rare autoimmune syndrome affecting mainly the ocular and vestibuloauditory systems.

Life-threatening aortitis sometimes occurs.

If untreated, permanent vision and hearing loss can result.

Urgent evaluation by an ophthalmologist and otolaryngologist is indicated.

Treatment is typically with glucocorticoids (topical and systemic).
