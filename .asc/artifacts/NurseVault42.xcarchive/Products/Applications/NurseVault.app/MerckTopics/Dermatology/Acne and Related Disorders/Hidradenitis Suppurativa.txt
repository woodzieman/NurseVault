=== Hidradenitis Suppurativa (Merck Manual Professional) ===
Section: Dermatology > Acne and Related Disorders

Retrieved: 2026-09-25

Hidradenitis suppurativa is a chronic inflammatory condition of the hair follicle and associated structures. In a large meta-analysis of several population-based, cross-sectional prevalence studies, the global prevalence of hidradenitis suppurativa was estimated to be 0.99% (95% CI, 0.67%–1.46%), with a female predominance (1). Younger adults, women, and Black people are disproportionately affected (2). There is a higher prevalence in patients with metabolic syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease (particularly Crohn disease), spondyloarthropathy (3), rheumatoid arthritis, and some variants of PAPA syndrome.

Complex interplays between genetic predisposition, lifestyle factors (eg, obesity, smoking), hormonal influences, and immune dysregulation (eg, upregulated Th1- and Th17-derived cytokines, downregulated regulatory T cells and Th22 cells) contribute to the development of disease (4). Bacterial dysbiosis (overgrowth of anaerobic, pathogenic bacteria and depletion of normal skin commensals) is thought to occur (5). Follicular inflammation and subsequent occlusion leads to rupture of the follicle and recurrent development of abscesses, sinus tracts, and scarring.

Symptoms and Signs of Hidradenitis Suppurativa

Swollen, tender masses resembling cutaneous abscesses develop. These lesions are often sterile. Pain, pruritus, fluctuance, discharge, and sinus tract formation (sometimes called epithelialized tunnels) are characteristic in chronic cases. Bacterial infection may occur in deep abscesses and sinus tracts. In chronic axillary cases, coalescence of inflamed nodules causes palpable cordlike fibrotic bands. The condition may become debilitating because of pain and foul odor.

Rarely, longstanding, severe cases, particularly in the gluteal and perianal region can undergo malignant transformation (typically into a squamous cell carcinoma) (1). Such transformation is more frequently observed in males.

The diagnosis of hidradenitis suppurativa is primarily based on history and physical examination (1). Cultures should be taken from deep abscesses and sinus tracts in patients who have chronic disease, but often no pathogens will be found. The Hurley staging system describes the severity of disease (2):

Stage I: Abscess formation, single or multiple, without sinus tracts or scarring

Stage II: Single or multiple, widely separated, recurrent abscesses with sinus tract formation or scarring

Stage III: Diffuse or near diffuse involvement or multiple interconnected sinus tracts and abscesses across the entire area

Treatment of Hidradenitis Suppurativa

Stage I: Topical clindamycin, intralesional glucocorticoids, and oral antibiotics

Stage II: Longer courses of oral antibiotics, antiandrogenic agents, and sometimes drainage, de-roofing, or punch debridement

Stage III: Biologic agents (eg, infliximab, adalimumab, secukinumab, bimekizumab) and often wide surgical excision and repair or grafting

Treatment goals in hidradenitis suppurativa are to prevent new lesions, reduce inflammation, and remove sinus tracts (1). Treatment approaches typically involve a combination of pharmacologic agents (eg, topical and oral antibiotics, oral antiandrogens, intralesional glucocorticoids, biologics) and surgical interventions.

For Hurley stage I disease, typical treatment includes topical 1% clindamycin solution 2 times a day, topical resorcinol 15% cream once a day, oral zinc gluconate (90 mg once a day), intralesional glucocorticoids (eg, 0.1 to 0.5 mL of a 5- to 10-mg/mL solution of triamcinolone acetonide once a month), and short (eg, 7- to 10-day) courses of oral antibiotics. Tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline, or, less commonly, erythromycin are used until the lesions resolve. A typical regimen could include one topical treatment (eg, based on the patient's skin sensitivity) and an oral antibiotic; however, all treatments can be used in combination or alone. Skin affected by hidradenitis suppurativa can be washed with benzoyl peroxide washes; this includes the axillae, inframammary, groin, external genitalia, and the buttocks. Skin should be rinsed well to avoid peroxide-induced bleaching of the clothing.

For Hurley stage II disease, treatment is with a longer (eg, 2- to 3-month) course of the same oral antibiotics used to treat stage I disease; if response is incomplete, clindamycin and/or rifampin may be added to the regimen. Adding antiandrogen therapy (eg, with oral estrogen or combination oral contraceptives, spironolactone, cyproterone acetate [not available in the United States], finasteride, or combinations) may be helpful in women. Incision and drainage may reduce the pain of an abscess but are insufficient for disease control (unlike in common cutaneous abscesses). For acute inflammatory lesions that are not excessively deep, punch debridement (ie, excision with a 5- to 7-mm punch instrument followed by digital debridement and curettage or scrubbing) is preferable. Sinus tracts should be unroofed and debrided. Patients whose lesions are deeper should be evaluated by a plastic surgeon for consideration of excision and grafting.

For Hurley Stage III disease, medical and surgical therapy should be more aggressive (2). Evidence of efficacy in reducing inflammation is strongest for infliximab (3). Alternatively, adalimumab, secukinumab, or bimekizumab are also efficacious (4, 5, 6). Oral retinoids (isotretinoin for 4 to 6 months or acitretin for 9 to 12 months) have been effective in some patients (7). Wide surgical excision and repair or grafting of the affected areas is often necessary if the disease persists. Ablative laser therapy (CO2 or erbium:YAG) is an alternate surgical treatment (8). Laser hair removal has also been used with some success (9).

Recommended adjunctive measures for all patients with hidradenitis suppurativa include maintaining good skin hygiene, minimizing trauma, providing psychological support, and possibly avoiding high glycemic–load foods.

Key Points

Lesions are usually sterile except for deep abscesses and sinus tracts in chronic disease.

Hidradenitis suppurativa can be disabling.

The diagnosis and treatment of hidradenitis suppurativa are based on the Hurley staging system.

Adjunctive measures include maintaining good skin hygiene, minimizing trauma, providing psychological support, and possibly avoiding a high glycemic-load diet.
