Kaposi sarcoma originates from endothelial cells in response to infection by human herpesvirus type 8 (HHV-8). Although it is rare in the general population, immunosuppression (particularly due to organ transplantation or advanced HIV) markedly increases the likelihood of Kaposi sarcoma in patients infected with HHV-8. In one population-based surveillance study, the rate of Kaposi sarcoma was elevated > 500-fold in patients with HIV infection in the United States; however, rates have seen a consistent, albeit gradual, decline over time (1). Some forms of Kaposi Sarcoma are endemic in some countries around the Mediterranean sea and in Southern and Eastern Africa (2).

The tumor cells have a spindle shape, resembling smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts, and myofibroblasts.

Classification of Kaposi Sarcoma

Classic Kaposi sarcoma

This type occurs most often in older (> 60 years) men of Mediterranean, Eastern European, or Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry.

The course is indolent, and the disease is usually confined to a small number of lesions on the skin of the lower extremities; visceral involvement occurs in approximately 10% of cases (1).

This type is usually not fatal.

Advanced HIV-associated Kaposi sarcoma (epidemic Kaposi sarcoma)

This type is the most common advanced HIV-associated cancer and is more aggressive than classic Kaposi sarcoma.

Multiple cutaneous lesions are typically present, often involving the face and trunk. Mucosal, lymph node, and gastrointestinal (GI) involvement is common. Sometimes Kaposi sarcoma is the first manifestation of advanced HIV.

Non-epidemic Kaposi sarcoma

An additional type of Kaposi sarcoma that has been increasingly recognized occurs in men who are HIV-negative and who have sex with men (2). The reasons for increased prevalence in this group is uncertain and remains an area of ongoing investigation.

Endemic Kaposi sarcoma

This type occurs in areas around the Mediterranean sea and Africa independent of HIV infection. There are 2 main forms:

Prepubertal lymphadenopathic form: This form predominantly affects children; primary tumors involve lymph nodes, with or without skin lesions. The course is usually fulminant and fatal.

Adult form: This form resembles classic Kaposi sarcoma.

Iatrogenic Kaposi sarcoma (immunosuppressive Kaposi sarcoma)

This type typically develops several years after organ transplantation. The course is more or less fulminant, depending on the degree of immunosuppression.

Symptoms and Signs of Kaposi Sarcoma

Cutaneous lesions are purple, pink, brown, or red macules that may coalesce into blue-violet to black plaques and nodules, but these are otherwise asymptomatic (eg, painless, lacking pruritus). Some edema may be present. Occasionally, nodules fungate or penetrate soft tissue and invade bone.

Although less common, visceral involvement most often involves the oral cavity, gastrointestinal (GI) tract, and the lungs. Symptoms depend on specific organ involvement. Mucosal lesions appear as bluish to violaceous macules, plaques, and tumors. GI lesions can bleed, sometimes extensively, but usually are asymptomatic.

Diagnosis of Kaposi Sarcoma

Biopsy

Imaging (CT of the chest or abdomen)

Rarely, endoscopy

The diagnosis of Kaposi sarcoma is confirmed by punch biopsy.

Patients who present with Kaposi Sarcoma as the initial manifestation require screening for HIV infection. Patients with known advanced HIV or immunosuppression require evaluation for visceral spread by CT of the chest and abdomen.

If CT is negative but pulmonary or GI symptoms are present, bronchoscopy or endoscopy should be considered.

Treatment of Kaposi Sarcoma

Surgical excision, cryotherapy, electrocoagulation, intralesional chemotherapy, or possibly topical imiquimod or alitretinoin for superficial lesions

Local radiation therapy and chemotherapy for multiple lesions, diffuse involvement, or lymph node disease

Antiretroviral therapy with similar local treatments or chemotherapy depending on extent of disease for advanced HIV-associated Kaposi sarcoma

Reduction of immunosuppressants for iatrogenic Kaposi sarcoma

Kaposi sarcoma treatment is generally tailored to the clinical subtype, disease extent, and patient comorbidities. Treatments for classic Kaposi sarcoma and advanced HIV-associated Kaposi sarcoma overlap considerably.

Indolent lesions (if lesions are limited) often require no treatment. One or a few superficial lesions can be removed by excision, cryotherapy, or electrocoagulation or treated with intralesional vinblastine or interferon alfa. Topical imiquimod or alitretinoin has also been reported to be effective. Multiple lesions, diffuse involvement, and lymph node disease are treated locally with 10 to 20 Gy of radiation therapy and chemotherapy. Recurrence is common, and it is difficult to obtain a complete cure.

Advanced HIV-associated Kaposi sarcoma responds markedly to antiretroviral therapy (ART), probably because the CD4+ count increases and the HIV viral load decreases (1). However, there is some evidence that protease inhibitors in this regimen may block angiogenesis (although this has not been shown to have beneficial clinical effects in humans) (2).

In patients with advanced HIV who have limited cutaneous disease, intralesional vinblastine or bleomycin can be added (3). Pomalidomide can also be used for more indolent lesions.

Patients with more extensive or visceral disease can be given pegylated liposomal doxorubicin 20 mg/m2 IV every 2 to 3 weeks (3). Paclitaxel is an alternative first-line treatment or can be given if pegylated liposomal doxorubicin fails.

Treatment of Kaposi sarcoma does not prolong life in most patients with advanced HIV because infections dominate the clinical course.

Non-epidemic classic Kaposi sarcoma requires treatment of indolent lesions as described above.

Iatrogenic Kaposi sarcoma responds best to stopping immunosuppressants. In patients who have had an organ transplant, reduction of immunosuppressant dosage often results in reduction of Kaposi sarcoma lesions. If dosage reduction is not possible, conventional local and systemic therapies used in other types of Kaposi sarcoma should be instituted. Substitution of immunosuppressant regimens to sirolimus may also improve iatrogenic Kaposi sarcoma (3).

Endemic Kaposi sarcoma treatment is challenging and typically palliative.

Key Points

Consider Kaposi sarcoma in older men, people from endemic areas, and patients with organ transplants or advanced HIV.

Test patients with immunosuppression (including advanced HIV) for metastases.

Treat superficial lesions with locally ablative methods.

Treat multiple lesions, diffuse involvement, or lymph node disease with local radiation therapy and chemotherapy.
