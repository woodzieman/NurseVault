Epidemiology of Community-Acquired Pneumonia

Community-acquired pneumonia is the leading infectious cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide (1). In 2021, lower respiratory infections (including pneumonia) accounted for approximately 4,350 new cases per 100,000 people worldwide, or 344 million new cases overall. The global mortality rate due to lower respiratory infection was approximately 27.7 deaths per 100,000 people, or approximately 2.2 million deaths overall.

In the United States, community-acquired pneumonia results in > 1.5 million hospitalizations annually, with an age-adjusted incidence of approximately 650 hospitalizations per 100,000 adults annually (2). Mortality rates have declined in recent decades, with overall age-adjusted mortality due to pneumonia declining from 35.9 to 17.2 per 100,000 between 1999 and 2022 (3). However, pneumonia remains a leading cause of death in the United States; approximately 1 of 3 patients hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia die within 1 year (2).

Children < 5 years of age, adults aged > 70 years, and immunocompromised patients are at the highest risk of mortality due to community-acquired pneumonia (4).

Etiology of Community-Acquired Pneumonia

Many organisms cause community-acquired pneumonia, including bacteria, viruses, and fungi. Pathogens vary by patient age and other factors (see table Community-Acquired Pneumonia in Adults), but the relative importance of each as a cause of community-acquired pneumonia is uncertain because most patients do not undergo testing to determine the causative organism. Even with testing, specific organisms are identified in only approximately 40% of hospitalized patients (1).

Bacterial causes

The most common bacterial causes are (2):

Streptococcus pneumoniae

Hemophilus influenzae

Staphylococcus aureus

Enterobacterales species

Atypical organisms causing pneumonia include (2):

Chlamydia pneumoniae

Mycoplasma pneumoniae

Legionella pneumophila

Streptococcus pneumoniae is the most common bacterial cause of community-acquired pneumonia worldwide (albeit declining in the United States [3]), followed by Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus, and Gram-negative organisms (2). S. pneumoniae and S. aureus (including MRSA) can also cause necrotizing pneumonia.

Identification of possible MRSA pneumonia is based on an assessment of risk factors that include known MRSA colonization (nasal), prior MRSA infection, compatible gram stain, hospitalization or antibiotic use in the previous 3 months, immunosuppression, necrotizing or cavitary pneumonia, recent influenza, or empyema (4).

The atypical bacterial pathogens Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Legionella pneumophila account for 3 to11% of cases of community-acquired pneumonia (2). Pneumonias caused by chlamydia and mycoplasma are often clinically indistinguishable from other pneumonias.

C. pneumoniae is commonly responsible for outbreaks of respiratory infection within families, in college dormitories, and in military training camps. It causes a relatively benign form of pneumonia that infrequently requires hospitalization. Chlamydia psittaci pneumonia (psittacosis) is rare and occurs in patients who own or are often exposed to psittacine birds (ie, parrots, parakeets, macaws).

Since initial increases beginning in the late 1990s, the incidence of community-acquired methicillin-resistant S. aureus (CA-MRSA) skin infections has plateaued and declined in resource-rich settings (5). This pathogen can rarely cause severe, cavitating pneumonia and tends to affect young adults.

S. pneumoniae and S. aureus (including MRSA) can cause necrotizing pneumonia.

Identification of possible MRSA pneumonia is based on an assessment of risk factors that include known MRSA colonization (nasal), prior MRSA infection, compatible gram stain, hospitalization or antibiotic use in the previous 3 months, immunosuppression, necrotizing or cavitary pneumonia, recent influenza, empyema.

Gram-negative organisms are important pathogens in selected patients. For example, identification of possible P. aeruginosa pneumonia is based on assessment of risk factors that include known Pseudomonas colonization, prior Pseudomonas infection, hospitalization or antibiotic use in the previous 3 months, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or other structural lung disease, and immunosuppression.

Q fever, tularemia, anthrax, and plague are uncommon bacterial syndromes that can occur in humans subsequent to exposure to infected animals or animal products; pneumonia may be a prominent feature of such syndromes. In particular, tularemia, anthrax, and plague should raise the suspicion of bioterrorism.

For information on related veterinary disease, see Coxiellosis in Animals, Tularemia in Animals, Anthrax in Animals, and Plague in Animals.

Viral causes

Bacterial superinfection can make distinguishing viral from bacterial infection difficult in clinical practice. However, it is likely that a significant proportion (up to 30%) of pneumonias are caused by a virus alone (6).

Common viral causes include:

Influenza viruses

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)

Human rhinovirus

Adenoviruses

Coronaviruses

Metapneumovirus

Parainfluenza viruses

Seasonal influenza can rarely cause a direct viral pneumonia but often predisposes patients to the development of a serious secondary bacterial pneumonia (7). Lower respiratory tract bacterial coinfection with influenza carries significant morbidity (ie, development of severe or critical illness) and mortality. Evidence suggests that the mortality risk is increased approximately 3-fold in patients hospitalized with influenza and bacterial pneumonia, compared to those with influenza alone (8).

Epstein-Barr virus and coxsackievirus are common viruses that can rarely cause pneumonia. Varicella virus can also cause lung infection as part of the clinical course of adult chickenpox. Coronaviruses cause severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), the Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), and COVID-19.

Hantavirus pulmonary syndrome is a viral zoonotic disease transmitted via inhalation of aerosolized rodent excreta (particularly deer mice), characterized by the development on noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, and in untreated cases, by high mortality.

Other causes

Common fungal pathogens include Histoplasma capsulatum (histoplasmosis) and Coccidioides immitis (coccidioidomycosis). Less common fungal pathogens include Blastomyces dermatitidis (blastomycosis) and Paracoccidioides braziliensis (paracoccidioidomycosis). These infections are primarily contracted via contact with specific environmental exposures (eg, contact with soil containing infected bird or bat droppings in caves) and are considered fungal sapronoses (ie, infections caused by fungi that arise from the environment, such as soil, water, or decaying organic matter).

Pneumocystis jirovecii commonly causes pneumonia in patients who have human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection or are immunosuppressed (see Pneumonia in Immunocompromised Patients).

Parasites causing lung infection in higher income countries include Toxocara canis or T. catis (toxocariasis), Dirofilaria immitis (dirofilariasis), and Paragonimus westermani (paragonimiasis).

Pulmonary tuberculosis and nontuberculous mycobacterial infections are discussed elsewhere.

Pneumonia in children

In children, the most common causes of pneumonia depend on age:

< 5 years: Most often viruses; among bacteria, S. pneumoniae, S. aureus, and S. pyogenes, are common

≥ 5 years: Most often the bacteria S. pneumoniae, M. pneumoniae, or Chlamydia pneumoniae

Viral etiologies are especially predominant in very young children < 2 years of age, with respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), adenoviruses, human metapneumovirus (hMPV), influenza A and B viruses, parainfluenza viruses, coronaviruses, and rhinovirus contributing to the development of pneumonia (9). Some children may be infected with 2 or more viruses simultaneously, or they may have a bacterial coinfection with viral pneumonia.

Pneumonia in neonates is discussed elsewhere.

Symptoms and Signs of Community-Acquired Pneumonia

Symptoms of community-acquired pneumonia include malaise, chills, rigor, fever, cough, dyspnea, and chest pain. Cough is typically productive in older children and adults and dry in infants, young children, and older adults. Dyspnea is usually mild and exertional and is rarely present at rest. Chest pain is pleuritic and is adjacent to the infected area. Pneumonia may manifest as upper abdominal pain when lower lobe infection irritates the diaphragm. Gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) are not uncommon.

Symptoms are variable at the extremes of age. Infection in infants may manifest as nonspecific irritability, restlessness, and altered (typically reduced) feeding and voiding (1). In older adults, infection may manifest as confusion and obtundation. Delirium is present in approximately 20% of patients; its presence is a significant risk factor for increased mortality (2).

Signs of community-acquired pneumonia include fever, tachypnea, tachycardia, crackles, bronchial breath sounds, egophony (E to A change— when, during auscultation, a patient says the letter “E” and through the stethoscope the examiner hears the letter “A”), and dullness to percussion. Signs of pleural effusion may also be present. Nasal flaring, use of accessory muscles, and cyanosis are common among infants. Fever is frequently absent in older patients.

The clinical features of community acquired pneumonia have been thought to differ by type of pathogen. However, manifestations in patients with typical and atypical pathogens overlap considerably. In addition, no single symptom or sign is considered sensitive or specific enough to identify the underlying pathogen (3). Symptoms and signs are of community-acquired pneumonia may also be similar to those of other noninfective inflammatory lung diseases such as acute hypersensitivity pneumonitis and organizing pneumonia.

Diagnosis of Community-Acquired Pneumonia

Chest imaging

Consideration of alternative diagnoses (eg, heart failure, pulmonary embolism, inflammatory lung conditions)

Sometimes identification of pathogen

Evaluation of severity and risk stratification

The diagnosis of pneumonia is suspected on the basis of clinical presentation and findings on chest imaging (1). The severity of pneumonia is estimated using a variety of clinical and laboratory factors (see Risk Stratification), which are sometimes organized using quantitative scoring systems.

The initial imaging test commonly performed is chest radiography, on which infiltrates may be seen (1). Lung ultrasound is considered an acceptable alternative to chest radiography to diagnose pneumonia when performed by clinicians with appropriate expertise. When there is high clinical suspicion of pneumonia and the chest radiograph and/or lung ultrasound does not reveal an infiltrate, performing computed tomography (CT) or repeating the chest radiograph in 24 to 48 hours is recommended. Testing typically also includes measurements of oxygen saturation, complete and differential blood count, and a complete metabolic profile.

In general, the clinical severity (ie, risk stratification) of the pneumonia determines the treatment setting (ie, outpatient versus inpatient). This in turn dictates the need for microbiological confirmation of the causative organism and further associated testing. Microbiologic confirmation with respiratory panels (using nucleic acid amplification testing) can detect viral and atypical pathogens that blood and sputum cultures cannot easily identify; however, thus far the impact of this testing on reducing antibiotic use and improving outcomes has been limited (2).

In outpatients with mild pneumonia, with the exception of COVID-19, influenza, and RSV testing during the appropriate seasons, no further diagnostic testing is typically needed (see table Risk Stratification for Community-Acquired Pneumonia). For patients with moderate or severe pneumonia who require hospitalization, 2 sets of blood cultures are obtained to assess for bacteremia and sepsis. Additional tests for these patients include sputum Gram stain and culture, urine pneumococcal and Legionella antigen testing, and HIV screening. A respiratory viral panel that relies on nucleic acid amplification testing is indicated in severely ill patients and those who are immunocompromised (3). In selected severely ill patients, especially if they are immunocompromised, bronchoscopy to obtain lower airway specimens is indicated as the spectrum of possible pathogens is much wider in these patients than immunocompetent patients (4).

Pathogen identification

A thorough history must be obtained. Specifically, identification of relevant exposures, travel, pets, and hobbies, is essential to raise suspicion of less common organisms. For example, exposure to farm animals may suggest Q fever, and a recent hotel or cruise ship stay may suggest Legionella infection.

Diagnosing the underlying infectious organism can be difficult. In particular, distinguishing between bacterial and viral causes is frequently challenging. Identification of a virus also does not always preclude concomitant infection with a bacterium. Many studies have investigated the utility of clinical, imaging, and routine blood tests in differentiating viral from bacterial causes, but no single test is considered absolutely reliable in making this differentiation (4). However, identification of the pathogen can be useful to direct therapy and verify bacterial susceptibilities to antibiotics.

Because of the limitations of current diagnostic tests and the success of empiric antibiotic treatment, experts recommend limiting attempts at microbiologic identification (eg, cultures, specific antigen testing) unless patients are at high risk or have complications (eg, severe pneumonia, immunocompromise, asplenia, failure to respond to empiric therapy). In general, the milder the pneumonia, the less such diagnostic testing is required. Critically ill patients, patients in whom an antibiotic-resistant or unusual organism (eg, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, P. jirovecii) is suspected, and patients whose condition is deteriorating or who are not responding to treatment within 72 hours require the most intensive testing.

Chest radiography findings generally cannot distinguish one type of infection from another, although the following findings are suggestive:

Multilobar opacities suggest S. pneumoniae or Legionella pneumophila infection.

Interstitial pneumonia (on chest radiography, appearing as increased interstitial markings and subpleural reticular opacities that increase from the apex to the bases of the lungs) suggests viral or mycoplasmal etiology.

Cavitating pneumonia suggests S. aureus or infection due to anaerobes, fungi, or mycobacteria.

Nasopharyngeal swabs, sputum, and bronchoscopic samples can also be tested using nucleic-acid amplification tests (NAAT) such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for other viruses, including influenza, RSV, and other respiratory viruses, atypical pathogens, and bacteria (6). These results should be interpreted with caution because of baseline colonization and incomplete validation with lower respiratory tract samples. Routine PCR testing for non-influenza viral etiologies is not suggested for outpatients or most inpatients with suspected community-acquired pneumonia; however, it is recommended for those hospitalized patients who are immunocompromised or have severe community-acquired pneumonia (3). Blood cultures, which are often obtained in patients hospitalized for pneumonia, can identify causative bacterial pathogens if bacteremia is present. Approximately 7 to 12% of all patients hospitalized with pneumonia have bacteremia; S. pneumoniae is the most frequent pathogen in such cases (5).

Sputum testing usually includes Gram stain and culture with antibiotic susceptibility testing for identification of any pathogen isolated. Sputum testing is recommended for patients hospitalized for pneumonia (4). The clinical value of sputum testing is uncertain because specimens often are contaminated with oral flora and the overall diagnostic yield is low. To mitigate these concerns and optimize pathogen yield, it is recommended that laboratories have a mechanism in place that screen sputum specimens for acceptability (2). Regardless, typical Gram stain findings can raise suspicion for MRSA or P. aeruginosa, and identification of a bacterial pathogen in sputum cultures allows for susceptibility testing. Obtaining sputum samples also allows for testing for viral pathogens via direct fluorescence antibody testing or polymerase chain reaction (PCR), but caution needs to be exercised in interpretation because up to 15% of healthy adults carry a respiratory virus or potential bacterial pathogen. In patients whose condition is deteriorating and in those unresponsive to broad-spectrum antibiotics, sputum should be tested with mycobacterial and fungal stains and cultures.

Sputum samples can be obtained, ideally before antibiotic administration, via noninvasive means such as simple expectoration or, for patients unable to produce sputum, after hypertonic saline nebulization (induced sputum). Alternatively, patients can undergo bronchoscopy or endotracheal suctioning. Either of these procedures can be readily done through an endotracheal tube in patients who are being mechanically ventilated. Otherwise, bronchoscopic sampling is usually done only for patients with other risk factors (eg, immunocompromise, failure of empiric therapy). Quantitative cultures of lower respiratory tract specimens can help distinguish between bacterial colonization (ie, presence of microorganisms at levels that provoke neither symptoms nor an inflammatory response) and infection.

Urine testing for Legionella antigen and pneumococcal antigen should be considered in patients hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia (4). These tests are simple and rapid and have high sensitivity and specificity (7, 8). Urinary Legionella antigen testing is indicated in non-severe cases in outbreak settings or when there is a history of travel (eg, on cruise ships) (2); however, it should be noted that the test detects only L. pneumophila serogroup 1.

COVID-19 test using reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) testing of respiratory secretions (nasopharyngeal specimen is preferred) is recommended in patients presenting with pneumonia.

Serum procalcitonin can help distinguish bacterial infection from other causes of infection or inflammation. However, use of the serum procalcitonin level as a criterion to initiate antibiotic therapy in community-acquired pneumonia is not recommended. It can be used, along with clinical judgement, to guide early discontinuation of antibiotics in lower respiratory tract infections.

Differential diagnosis

Several conditions should be considered among the differential diagnosis in patients presenting with pneumonia-like symptoms (eg, cough, fever, dyspnea).

The most serious common misdiagnosis is pulmonary embolism, which may be more likely in patients with acute onset of dyspnea, minimal sputum production, no accompanying upper respiratory infection or systemic symptoms, and risk factors for thromboembolism (see table Risk Factors for Deep Venous Thrombosis and Pulmonary Embolism); thus, testing for pulmonary embolism should be considered in all patients with such symptoms and risk factors.

Other conditions that may mimic community-acquired pneumonia include:

Acute bronchitis (infiltrates are absent)

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbation (infiltrates are absent)

Heart failure (presence of edema of the extremities or elevated jugular venous pressure)

Organizing pneumonia (failure of response to antibiotics)

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (presence of triggering occupational or other exposure, centrilobular nodules and/or fibrosis on imaging)

Treatment of Community-Acquired Pneumonia

Risk stratification for determination of site of care

Antibiotics

Antivirals for influenza or varicella

Systemic glucocorticoids in selected critically ill patients

Supportive measures

Treatment approaches in community-acquired pneumonia generally depend on risk stratification (eg, patient age, clinical status, comorbidities), the clinical setting (ie, outpatient vs inpatient), a history of recent antibiotic use, and knowledge of local resistance patterns.

Risk stratification

Risk stratification via risk prediction rules may be used to estimate mortality risk and thus help guide decisions regarding hospitalization (1). These rules have been used to identify patients who can be safely treated as outpatients and those who require hospitalization because of high risk of complications (see table Risk Stratification for Community-Acquired Pneumonia). However, these prediction rules should supplement, not replace, clinical judgment because many unrepresented factors, such as likelihood of adherence, ability to care for self, and ability to maintain oral intake, should also influence triage decisions.

The Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) is the preferred validated prediction score (1). However, because the PSI is complex and requires several laboratory assessments, simpler rules such as CURB-65 are useful alternatives. Use of these prediction rules has led to a reduction in unnecessary hospitalizations for patients who have milder illness.

Clinical Calculators

Community-Acquired Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) for Adults

In CURB-65, 1 point is allotted for each of the following risk factors:

Confusion

Uremia (blood urea nitrogen [BUN] ≥ 19 mg/dL [6.8 mmol/L])

Respiratory rate > 30 breaths/minute

Systolic Blood pressure < 90 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≤ 60 mm Hg

Age ≥ 65 years

Scores can be used as follows:

0 or 1 points: Risk of death is < 3%. Outpatient therapy is usually appropriate, especially if the only point is related to age.

2 points: Risk of death is 9%. Hospitalization should be considered.

≥ 3 points: Risk of death is 15 to 40%. Hospitalization is indicated, and, particularly with 4 or 5 points, ICU admission should be considered.

In clinical settings where access to a BUN measurement is not readily available, a CRB-65 score can be used instead. Use of CRB-65 scores is similar to that for CURB-65, with 0 points: appropriate for outpatient therapy; 1 to 2 points: consider hospitalization; ≥ 3 points: consider intensive care unit (ICU) admission. An international validation study has been performed on the CURB-65 score (2).

Antimicrobials

Antibiotics

Antibiotic therapy is the mainstay of treatment for most patients with community-acquired pneumonia (1). Patients with community-acquired pneumonia who have a positive respiratory viral panel, no comorbidities (eg, underlying respiratory disease, immunocompromise), no clinical indicators of bacterial infection or complications of pneumonia, and who do not require hospitalization may not require antibiotic therapy (3). In immunocompromised patients, empiric broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy is usually necessary (4).

For patients in whom antibiotics are indicated, appropriate treatment involves starting empiric antibiotics as soon as possible, preferably ≤ 4 hours after presentation. Because pathogen identification is difficult and takes time, the empiric antibiotic regimen is selected based on likely pathogens, presence of comorbidities, and severity of illness. Consensus guidelines have been developed by many professional organizations (1, 3, 4); one widely used set is detailed in the table Antibiotic Therapy for Bacterial Community-Acquired Pneumonia in Adults. In addition to the antibiotics outlined in the table, the broad-spectrum antibiotics omadacycline and lefamulin can be considered, especially when the first-line agents are contraindicated or ineffective. Anaerobic antibiotic coverage for suspected aspiration pneumonia should not be routinely added unless lung abscess or empyema is suspected (1).

Traditionally, at least 5 days of antibiotics have been recommended in the treatment of pneumonia. The 2025 guideline update from the ATS recommends a shorter duration (3 to 4 days) for outpatients or inpatients with nonsevere community-acquired pneumonia, provided they reach clinical stability and have close follow-up (3). In all other circumstances, a minimum of 5 days is still recommended.

Guidelines should be adapted to local susceptibility patterns, drug formularies, and individual patient circumstances (eg, specific exposures). If a pathogen is subsequently identified, the results of antibiotic susceptibility testing can help guide any changes in antibiotic therapy.

With empiric treatment, the majority of patients with bacterial pneumonia improve. Clinical improvement typically manifests as decreased cough and dyspnea, defervescence, relief of chest pain, and a decline in white blood cell count. Failure to improve should trigger suspicion of:

An unusual organism

Resistance to the antimicrobial used for treatment

Empyema

Coinfection or superinfection with a second infectious agent

An obstructive endobronchial lesion

Immunosuppression

Metastatic focus of infection with reseeding (in the case of pneumococcal infection)

Nonadherence to treatment (in the case of outpatients)

Wrong diagnosis (ie, a noninfectious cause of the illness such as acute hypersensitivity pneumonitis)

When usual therapy has failed, consultation with a pulmonary and/or infectious disease specialist is indicated.

Antivirals

Antiviral therapy may be indicated for select viral pneumonias (eg, pneumonias due to influenza, varicella, and RSV).

For influenza, neuraminidase inhibitors (oral oseltamivir, aerosolized zanamivir) given for 5 days reduce the duration and severity of symptoms in patients who develop influenza infection, regardless of the duration of illness before symptom onset. However, initiating treatment within 48 hours of onset has been associated with the best outcomes (1). Alternatively, IV peramivir or oral baloxavir started within 48 hours of symptom onset can be given as single doses. Patients hospitalized with confirmed influenza infection should receive neuraminidase inhibitors; in such patients, administration may be beneficial even if initiated 48 hours after symptom onset (1).

For varicella lung infections, acyclovir is recommended for children and adults (5).

For respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) pneumonia, adults and children are generally managed with supportive care alone.

Management in children

For children, treatment depends on age, previous vaccinations, and whether treatment is to be initiated in the outpatient or inpatient setting (6). Antimicrobial therapy is not routinely required for preschool-aged children with community-acquired pneumonia, because there is strong evidence to support that viral pathogens are responsible for the great majority of clinical disease.

If antibiotic therapy is appropriate, several agents are available. In addition to antibiotic therapy, antiviral coverage with neuraminidase inhibitors (eg, oseltamivir) should be administered as quickly as possible, seasonally (during known periods of circulation), in children with symptoms compatible with viral moderate to severe community-acquired pneumonia (6).

For children treated as outpatients, treatments are dictated by age (6):

< 5 years: Amoxicillin is usually the medication of choice. Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid is an alternative. If epidemiology suggests an atypical pathogen as the cause and clinical findings are compatible, a macrolide (eg, azithromycin, clarithromycin) can be used instead. Some experts suggest not using antibiotics if clinical features strongly suggest viral pneumonia.

≥ 5 years: Amoxicillin or (particularly if an atypical pathogen cannot be excluded) amoxicillin plus a macrolide. Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid is an alternative. If the cause appears to be an atypical pathogen, a macrolide alone can be used.

For children treated as inpatients, antibiotic therapy tends to be more broad-spectrum and depends on the child's previous vaccinations:

Fully immunized (against S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae type b): Ampicillin or penicillin G. Alternatives are ceftriaxone or cefotaxime. If S. Aureus (including MRSA) is suspected, vancomycin or clindamycin is added. If an atypical pathogen cannot be excluded, a macrolide is added.

Not fully immunized: Ceftriaxone or cefotaxime (alternative is levofloxacin). If MRSA is suspected, vancomycin or clindamycin is added. If an atypical pathogen cannot be excluded, a macrolide is added.

Supportive care

Supportive care includes fluids, antipyretics (as needed for high-grade fever), analgesics, and, for patients with hypoxemia, oxygen. Prophylaxis against thromboembolic disease and early mobilization improve outcomes for patients hospitalized with pneumonia. Cessation counseling should also be done for patients who smoke.

Adjunctive glucocorticoids are recommended in immunocompetent inpatients who have severe community-acquired pneumonia (7). Randomized trials have demonstrated reduced mortality and morbidity with early initiation of intravenous hydrocortisone 200 mg daily by continuous infusion for 4 to 7 days in immunocompetent patients with severe pneumonia (7). However, a subsequent randomized trial in hospitalized adults admitted to the intensive care unit with hydrocortisone use demonstrated a less favorable benefit-risk ratio than was previously reported (8). Such treatment is not advised in influenza-associated pneumonia, for less severe pneumonia, in immunocompromised patients, or when viral or fungal pneumonia is suspected.

Follow-up

Early outpatient follow-up with a primary care clinician is encouraged to reduce readmission risk (9) and assess for post-acute sequelae (eg, myocardial infarction, stroke, post-intensive care syndrome) (10). Follow-up radiographs are generally not recommended in patients whose pneumonia resolves clinically as expected within 5 to 7 days (1). Resolution of radiographic abnormalities can lag behind clinical resolution by several weeks. Chest radiographs should be considered in patients with pneumonia symptoms that do not resolve or that worsen over time.

Prognosis for Community-Acquired Pneumonia

Short-term mortality is related to severity of illness. Mortality is very low in patients who are candidates for outpatient treatment. Among patients hospitalized for community-acquired pneumonia, 30-day mortality ranges between 4.1% to 9.6% ; however, patients hospitalized in intensive care units may experience much higher mortality (1). Death may be caused by pneumonia itself, progression to sepsis syndrome, or exacerbation of coexisting conditions. In patients hospitalized for pneumonia, risk of death is increased substantially during the year after hospital discharge; about 1 in 3 patients die within 1 year (2).

Mortality varies to some extent by pathogen. Mortality rates are highest with gram-negative bacteria and MRSA. However, because these pathogens are relatively infrequent causes of community-acquired pneumonia, S. pneumoniae remains the most common cause of death in patients with community-acquired pneumonia. Atypical pathogens such as Mycoplasma have a good prognosis. Mortality is higher in patients who do not respond to initial empiric antibiotics and in those whose treatment regimen does not conform with guidelines.

Prevention of Community-Acquired Pneumonia

Several vaccines are available to prevent some forms of community-acquired pneumonia (1, 2, 3):

Pneumococcal vaccine

H. influenzae type b (Hib) vaccine

Varicella vaccine

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccine

Influenza vaccine

Available vaccine formulations and dosing schedules are discussed in detail separately. For more information, see Immunization.

In high-risk patients who are not vaccinated against influenza and household contacts of patients with influenza, oseltamivir 75 mg orally once/day or zanamivir 10 mg orally mg once/day can be given as prophylaxis for 2 weeks, which differs from the dosing regimens for treatment. If started within 48 hours of exposure, these antivirals may prevent influenza (although resistance has been described for oseltamivir).

Smoking cessation can reduce the risk of developing pneumonia.

Key Points

Community-acquired pneumonia is a leading cause of death in the United States and around the world.

Common symptoms and signs include cough (nonproductive in children and older adults), fever, chills, fatigue, dyspnea, and pleuritic chest pain.

Treat patients with mild or moderate risk pneumonia with empiric antibiotics without testing designed to identify the underlying pathogen.

Hospitalize patients at high risk, as delineated by risk assessment tools; severe pneumonia requires prompt identification and management.

Consider alternate diagnoses, including pulmonary embolism, particularly if pneumonia-like signs and symptoms are not typical.
