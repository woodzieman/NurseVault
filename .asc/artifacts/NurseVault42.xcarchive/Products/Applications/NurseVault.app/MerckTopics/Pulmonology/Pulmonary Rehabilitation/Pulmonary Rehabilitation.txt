=== Pulmonary Rehabilitation (Merck Manual Professional) ===
Section: Pulmonology > Pulmonary Rehabilitation
Source: https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/pulmonary-disorders/pulmonary-rehabilitation/pulmonary-rehabilitation
Retrieved: 2026-09-25

For many patients with chronic respiratory disorders, medical therapy may only partially allay the symptoms and complications of the disorder. A comprehensive program of pulmonary rehabilitation may lead to significant clinical improvement by:

Reducing shortness of breath

Increasing exercise tolerance

Pulmonary rehabilitation has been associated with a reduced the likelihood of hospitalization and reduced mortality in patients hospitalized for an exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (1). Evidence from meta-analyses of smaller randomized trials (2) has been supported by subsequent analyses from a large retrospective cohort of patients with COPD, showing that the initiation of pulmonary rehabilitation within 3 months of hospital discharge significantly reduced both the risk of 1-year mortality and the number of rehospitalizations (3, 4).

Pulmonary rehabilitation programs

Pulmonary rehabilitation is most often conducted in a hospital or clinic; however, alternative strategies include home-based care, telerehabilitation, internet-based programs, and programs that require minimal resources (see also Overview of Rehabilitation). Some programs combine cardiac rehabilitation and pulmonary rehabilitation (5). Some evidence also supports the use of virtually delivered pulmonary rehabilitation, with improvements in functional capacity, quality of life, and adherence (6).

Regardless of which model of healthcare delivery is used, expert consensus has established the essential components of pulmonary rehabilitation, which are divided into 4 domains (1):

Patient assessment

Program components

Method of delivery

Quality assurance

Before pulmonary rehabilitation begins, a clinician makes an initial assessment of the patient's baseline functional status and pulmonary rehabilitation needs. This assessment is conducted in a hospital, ambulatory care setting, or pulmonary rehabilitation center and includes the following:

A laboratory-based exercise test (incremental cycle ergometer test, endurance cycle ergometer test, incremental treadmill test, or endurance treadmill test)

A field exercise test (6- or 12-minute walk test, incremental or endurance shuttle walk test)

Quality-of-life measurements

Dyspnea assessment

Nutritional status evaluation

Occupational status evaluation

An adequate pulmonary rehabilitation program includes both endurance training and resistance training. The prescription is tailored to the patient's status and goals and progress is assessed regularly. In addition to exercise training, other core rehabilitative interventions include education and self-management support. The care team should ideally include an individual who has expertise in exercise and clinicians who are trained in delivering rehabilitation.

Quality assurance emphasizes the safe delivery of care, ongoing monitoring of outcomes, adherence to evidence-based standards, and continuous evaluation of program performance.

Indications

In the past, pulmonary rehabilitation was reserved for patients with (7):

Severe COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease)

However, an increasing body of evidence suggests a benefit to patients with (8):

Bronchiectasis

COVID-19

Cystic fibrosis (CF)

Interstitial lung disease (ILD)

Lung cancer

Neuromuscular disorders

Pulmonary hypertension

Sarcoidosis

Asthma

Patients with pulmonary hypertension benefit from exercise-based rehabilitation with improvements in exercise capacity (9).

Most patients with COPD benefit from pulmonary rehabilitation at any stage in the disease. Guidelines recommend that adult patients with stable COPD participate in pulmonary rehabilitation (7, 10). In addition, patients with COPD hospitalized with an acute exacerbation should be referred within 3 weeks of hospital discharge, because pulmonary rehabilitation has the potential to reduce readmission rates (7). Pulmonary rehabilitation should be considered early in the course of COPD because the degree of airflow obstruction correlates only weakly with symptoms, functional capacity, and exercise limitation; therefore, patients may experience substantial impairment and benefit from rehabilitation even before disease becomes severe (5). Furthermore, even patients with less severe disease are likely to benefit from reduced dyspnea, improved exercise tolerance, improved muscle strength, conditioning, improved cardiac and pulmonary physiology, reduced dynamic hyperinflation, and the psychosocial benefits that accompany pulmonary rehabilitation (11). Despite the numerous clear benefits to participating in pulmonary rehabilitation, such programs are a limited resource that is underutilized by the patients who would benefit from them. In the United States, utilization rates in older adults have modestly increased but generally remain low, with referrals and adherence tied to well-established social determinants of health (12).

Participation in pulmonary rehabilitation prior to and after lung transplantation is perceived to be beneficial and is widely recommended (13).

Contraindications

Contraindications are relative and include comorbidities (eg, untreated angina, left ventricular dysfunction) that could complicate attempts to increase a patient’s level of exercise. However, these comorbidities do not preclude application of other components of pulmonary rehabilitation.

Complications

There are no complications of pulmonary rehabilitation beyond those expected from physical exertion and exercise.

Procedure

Pulmonary rehabilitation as an integrated program typically includes:

Exercise training

Inspiratory muscle training

Education

Nutritional evaluation and counseling

Psychosocial and behavioral interventions

Pulmonary rehabilitation is delivered by a multidisciplinary team that includes physicians, nurses, respiratory therapists, physical and occupational therapists, and psychologists or social workers. The intervention should be individualized and targeted to the patient's needs. Pulmonary rehabilitation can be initiated at any stage of disease with the goal of minimizing disease burden and symptoms. A typical outpatient pulmonary rehabilitation schedule consists of 36 sessions, 2 to 3 days per week, spanning a period of 8 to 12 weeks.

Exercise training involves aerobic exercise and respiratory muscle and upper and lower extremity strength training. There is increasing evidence supporting the use of both strength training and interval training of the extremities (14). Interval training alternates short bursts (eg, 30 seconds) of intense activity with longer periods (eg, 2 minutes) of less intense activity.

Inspiratory muscle training (IMT) is often a component of pulmonary rehabilitation. IMT uses handheld breathing devices that impose a resistive load set to a fraction of an individual's maximal inspiratory pressure. Common devices include threshold-loading devices and resistive loading devices; these devices are designed to strengthen the diaphragm and accessory inspiratory muscles through repeated breathing against resistance. When combined with other elements of pulmonary rehabilitation, IMT may improve a patient's inspiratory muscle pressure, but this improvement does not result in decreased shortness of breath or in improvements in quality of life or functional parameters (15).

Education has many components. Smoking cessation is considered a key component of pulmonary rehabilitation. Teaching breathing strategies (such as pursed-lip breathing, in which exhalations are initiated against closed lips to decrease respiratory rate, thereby decreasing gas trapping) and the principles of conserving physical energy are helpful. Education about treatments, including indication, appropriate use, and delivery of inhaled medications correctly, is important as it can promote self-management, improve adherence, and may help patients recognize and respond to worsening symptoms.

Nutritional evaluation and counseling are often required for many patients with chronic lung disease, particularly patients with severe COPD who are susceptible to significant anorexia and cachexia. Guidance on maintaining adequate caloric intake without causing excessive fullness that impairs breathing is important. Conversely, for patients with weight gain due to reduced physical activity, weight management helps reduce the respiratory burden associated with excess body mass.

Psychosocial interventions involve counseling and feedback for the depression, anxieties, and fears that hinder the patient’s full participation in activities. Behavioral modification strategies and an emphasis on self-management are critical components of pulmonary rehabilitation. Strategies include techniques for goal-setting and problem-solving, decision-making, medication adherence, and the maintenance of routine exercise and physical activity (1).

Although the most optimal maintenance strategy is unknown, continued participation in an exercise program is essential to sustain the benefits of pulmonary rehabilitation.
