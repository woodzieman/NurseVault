(See also Overview of Fractures.)

The usual injury mechanism for femoral shaft fractures is severe direct force or an axial load to the flexed knee (typically in a motor vehicle crash or automobile-pedestrian collision). Other serious injuries are often present (eg pelvic ring fractures).

Symptoms and Signs of Femoral Shaft Fractures

Fracture causes obvious swelling, deformity (often with shortening), and instability. Up to 1.5 L of blood for each fracture may be lost. Hemorrhagic shock is possible, particularly when the cause is blunt trauma and there are other injuries.

Diagnosis of Femoral Shaft Fractures

Radiographs

Anteroposterior and lateral radiographs are diagnostic.

If the fracture resulted from great force, hip radiographs should always be performed to look for an ipsilateral femoral neck fracture. The knee also needs to be carefully evaluated.

Treatment of Femoral Shaft Fractures

Immediate splinting with traction

Open reduction with internal fixation (ORIF)

Immediate treatment is splinting, usually with a commercially available femoral traction splint (such as a Hare traction or Sager traction splint), followed by ORIF. Because traction splints apply traction to the lower leg, they should not be used if patients also have a tibial fracture.

Key Points

Femoral shaft fractures usually result from severe forces and are not clinically subtle.

For each fracture, up to 1.5 L of blood may be lost.

Perform radiographs of the hip and evaluate the knee if the femoral shaft is fractured.

Splint femoral shaft fractures as soon as possible.
