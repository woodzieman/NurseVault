Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Feverfew has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. Products containing feverfew are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Consumers should also be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for dietary supplements and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.Feverfew, Tanacetum parthenium is a member of the Asteraceae (formerly Compositae) family. Cross-reactivity may occur in those individuals allergic to other plants in the Asteraceae family. Members include ragweed, chrysanthemums, marigolds, daisies, many other herbs (e.g., chamomile, echinacea or milk thistle), and the pyrethrins. Allergic reactions, though infrequent, may be severe. Patients with a history of atopy or childhood eczema may have a greater likelihood of developing sensitivity reactions to feverfew.Not every patient is a candidate for self-prophylaxis of headache with feverfew; and feverfew is not to be used as an acute self-treatment to abort a migraine. Health Canada's labeling standards suggest a patient should not use feverfew for more than 4 months except on advice of a qualified health care prescriber. The National Headache Foundation suggests that a patient should seek medical attention for the following circumstances: 1) headache (pain, duration and frequency) has gotten worse over days or weeks; 2) headaches occur suddenly in a person who has previously not experienced them; 3) headaches are accompanied by weakness, numbness in an extremity or a change in hearing or sight (associated symptoms of stroke or basilar/hemiplegic migraine); 4) the headache is unrelenting, and over-the-counter analgesics do not relieve the pain; 5) changes in memory, personality or cognitive abilities occur in association with headaches; or 6) the headache is accompanied by a stiff neck, rash, nausea, vomiting, fever, breathing problems, painful sinuses, or a history of head trauma.Feverfew, Tanacetum parthenium should not be used by any patient with active bleeding, hemophilia, or by patients who are currently receiving anticoagulant therapy or thrombolytic therapy. Impaired platelet aggregation may result from the depletion of platelet serotonin by feverfew. Use with caution in those patients with a known coagulopathy or vitamin K deficiency. Because of the potential effects on platelet aggregation, feverfew should generally be discontinued prior to surgery. It may be wise to discontinue this herb 2—3 weeks prior to an elective procedure. Use caution in those patients taking this herb who will need extensive dental work.Feverfew, Tanacetum parthenium reportedly stimulates premature uterine contractions, which could induce spontaneous abortion during pregnancy. Consider the use of this drug to be equivalent to a FDA pregnancy risk category X classification. Do not use this herb during pregnancy.It is not known if feverfew, Tanacetum parthenium is excreted into breast-milk. Because of the potential and unknown risks to the breast-feeding infant, feverfew should be avoided during lactation.There is no information on the safety of feverfew in those patients with hepatic disease or renal disease; use with caution. Some feverfew products contain therapeutic amounts of magnesium and should be avoided by patients with renal impairment or renal failure.Due to a lack of scientific data and an unknown potential for side effects, feverfew is not recommended for use in children without the approval and observation of a health care professional. According to many herbal reviews, feverfew should not be used in children or infants younger than two years of age. In available clinical trials, the youngest child administered feverfew was 9 years of age, and overall few children or adolescents have been evaluated. Canadian authorities do not allow feverfew products to be labeled with indications for children. Caution is also warranted in the elderly due to limited study of feverfew in this patient population at this time. If migraine-type symptoms occur in children or in geriatric patients, it is important to rule out the potential cause(s) of the problem.Patients taking feverfew for prolonged periods of time for prophylaxis of migraine should avoid abrupt discontinuation of the herb. Gradual reduction is recommended unless there is an immediate clinical need to discontinue use (i.e., hypersensitivity). Rebound withdrawal headaches may occur and may be associated with anxiety, insomnia and muscle and joint soreness. Such events have been noted on abrupt discontinuation of feverfew in some clinical trials.Feverfew should be used with caution by persons with rheumatoid arthritis; clinical studies to date suggest that feverfew is not an effective treatment for this condition. Use only under the advice of a qualified health care professional.

Description
  Text
  Feverfew, Tanacetum parthenium is an ornamental flowering plant, also known as Chrysanthemum parthenium, that is commonly cultivated in the United States and many other countries worldwide. The plant is commonly known as bachelor's button, featherfew, mutterkraut and Santa Maria. The flowers of feverfew bear close resemblance to those of chamomile; chamomile has been botanically misidentified as feverfew. There are many different species of feverfew worldwide; the chemical, flower and leaf morphology are different among these species. The leaves of the feverfew species, T. parthenium are used medicinally; the leaves contain the majority of bioactive compounds. Feverfew has been used historically since at least 78 A.D. as a treatment for fever; other traditional claimed uses include the relief of dyspepsia, acute headache, or arthritis pain, but data of sufficient quality are not available for these uses. One randomized, controlled trial found feverfew ineffective for the treatment of the signs and symptoms of rheumatoid arthritis. Today, feverfew is used primarily as migraine headache prophylaxis; several clinical trials have supported the use of feverfew for this purpose. Comparative trials of feverfew with FDA-approved agents for migraine prophylaxis are not available and there is no evidence the herb is effective for acute migraine treatment. Official herbal monographs exist in the European Union and Canada for feverfew, and the use of a standardized feverfew product (containing at least 0.2% parthenolide) from a reliable manufacturer is recommended. In the U.S., feverfew is regulated as a herbal dietary supplement and is not FDA-approved for any medical use.
 
NOTE: In the U.S., herbal products and dietary supplements are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for dietary supplements as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since supplements are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for these products and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The exact mechanisms of action of feverfew remain unknown. Over 30 chemical constituents of Tanacetum parthenium have been identified. The best-characterized compounds are contained in the feverfew leaf. Feverfew leaves contain 3 different types of sesquiterpene lactones: the germacranolides, the eudesmanolides, and the guaianolides. Parthenolide, a germacranolide, is the most abundant and the principal bioactive ingredient, but additional components of feverfew may be pharmacologically important. Other non-sesquiterpene constituents of feverfew include borneol, camphor, flavonoids, pyrethrins, and volatile essential oils (e.g., chrysanthenyl acetate); some of these are active. Melatonin has also been found during analysis of some feverfew species.
 
Parthenolide may inhibit prostaglandin production, have antispasmodic activity in blood vessels, and may antagonize the actions of serotonin (5-HT). Unlike nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, feverfew is not an inhibitor of cyclooxygenase (COX-1 or COX-2). Parthenolide appears to be a weak 5-HT2A receptor antagonist which may contribute to anti-migraine actions. Feverfew also appears to inhibit vascular smooth muscle spasm by blocking voltage-dependent potassium channels; this spasmolytic activity may reduce the reactivity of the cerebral blood vessels to endogenous vasoconstrictive or vasodilatory compounds like norepinephrine, acetylcholine, bradykinins, prostaglandins, histamine and serotonin. Feverfew may inhibit platelet aggregation in vitro; however, the effects during clinical use are not clear.
 
Parthenolide is not the only active constituent in feverfew. Chrysanthenyl acetate, an essential oil in the feverfew leaf, appears to inhibit prostaglandin synthetase in vitro and to possess analgesic properties. Melatonin has also been found during analysis of some feverfew species, but it is unclear if melatonin contributes to the herb's effects in migraine. Feverfew leaves and flowers have some antioxidant, antimicrobial and cytoprotective activities which continue to be investigated.

Pharmacokinetics
  Text
  Feverfew is administered orally in a variety of dosage forms including dried extract capsules, tinctures and teas; the fresh leaves have also been masticated. Human clinical pharmacokinetic data are not available, and preclinical pharmacokinetic data are limited in mice. The use of standardized formulations is important to herb efficacy. It is recommended that feverfew products come from authenticated Tanacetum parthenium standardized to contain a minimum of 0.2% parthenolide. This amount of parthenolide is considered a minimum concentration for efficacy in migraine prophylaxis. The parthenolide content of over 35 different commercial preparations of feverfew assayed in one study indicated a wide variation in the amounts of parthenolide in marketed supplements. Ethanolic feverfew extracts appear ineffective; chemical constituents of feverfew appear to either rapidly degrade or do not survive the ethanolic extraction process.
