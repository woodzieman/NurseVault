Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Fludarabine should be administered under the supervision of a qualified physician experienced in the use of antineoplastic therapy. Fludarabine can cause severe bone marrow suppression. In addition, fludarabine should be used cautiously in patients who have had previous myelosuppressive therapy such as chemotherapy or radiation therapy. Severe bone marrow suppression, including anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia, is a relative contraindication to fludarabine depending upon the disease. Patients with aggressive leukemia or lymphoma may require treatment with fludarabine despite severe bone marrow suppression. Patients with preexisting bone marrow suppression due to treatment should be allowed to recover their blood cell counts prior to fludarabine administration. The hematological status (hemoglobin, WBC, and platelet counts) of the patient should be monitored closely during fludarabine therapy. Prolonged or persistent bone marrow suppression should be assessed to differentiate between residual bone marrow disease, drug-induced hypoplasia, or autoimmune complications. In addition, patients undergoing treatment with fludarabine should be evaluated and closely monitored for hemolysis. Fludarabine is contraindicated in decompensated hemolytic anemia. Life-threatening and sometimes fatal autoimmune hemolytic anemia and immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) have been reported following fludarabine therapy. Patients with an active infection should be treated prior to receiving fludarabine whenever possible. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with chemotherapy. Studies have indicated an increased frequency of herpes zoster during fludarabine therapy in untreated and previously treated patients. Data supporting the use of prophylactic antibiotics during fludarabine therapy are limited. Prophylaxis for Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia (PCP) should be considered in high-risk patients who are heavily pre-treated or require concurrent corticosteroids; previously untreated patients, receiving single-agent fludarabine are probably not candidates for PCP prophylaxis.
 
During clinical studies, 36% of patients with acute leukemia treated with high-dose fludarabine (approximately 4 times the recommended dose) experienced severe neurologic toxicity, including visual disturbance (blindness), coma, and death. In the pivotal trial of fludarabine tablets, severe impairment of consciousness was reported in 1 patient. Similar severe toxicities, including coma, seizures, agitation, and confusion have been reported when used at recommended doses for the treatment of chronic lymphocytic leukemia.
 
During a clinical study in patients with refractory chronic lymphocytic leukemia, combination therapy of fludarabine plus pentostatin (deoxycoformycin) caused an unacceptably high incidence of fatal pulmonary toxicity. Use with pentostatin is not recommended.
 
Myelosuppressive effects of fludarabine can increase the risk of infection or bleeding; therefore, dental work should be delayed until blood counts have returned to normal. Patients, especially those with dental disease, should be instructed in proper oral hygiene, including caution in use of regular toothbrushes, dental floss, and toothpicks.
 
Intramuscular injections should not be administered to patients with platelet counts = 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    General Information
    Use fludarabine with caution in patients with renal impairment; an initial dose adjustment may be necessary. A dosage has not been established in patients with severe renal impairment (CrCl less than 30 mL/min), including renal failure. Evaluate renal function prior to and during fludarabine therapy. Monitor patients with renal impairment closely for adverse reactions.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Fludarabine may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy, based on its mechanism of action and data from animal studies. Advise pregnant people of the potential fetal risk. In animal studies, fludarabine was embryotoxic and teratogenic at doses below (rabbits) and above (rats) the recommended human dose (RHD). In rats, repeated intravenous doses given during organogenesis at 2.4 and 7.2 times the RHD caused increased resorptions, skeletal and visceral malformations, and decreased fetal body weights. In rabbits, a significant increase in malformations was seen at doses 0.5 times the RHD and above. Embryo-fetal lethality was observed at doses 3.8 times the RHD.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with fludarabine. Fludarabine can cause fetal harm. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking fludarabine and for 6 months after the last dose. For those that can cause pregnancy in others, contraception should be used during fludarabine treatment and for 3 months after the last dose. Based on animal data, fludarabine may cause male infertility due to testicular tissue and spermatozoa damage. It is not known if these effects are reversible.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking fludarabine and for 1 week after the last dose, due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in the breastfed child. There are no data on the presence of fludarabine or its metabolites in human milk, their effects on the breastfed child, or their effects on milk production.

  Using currently recommended doses of fludarabine, bone marrow suppression is a commonly reported adverse event. Myelosuppression may be severe, cumulative, and affect multiple cell lines. Hematologic adverse reactions, including leukopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia, were reported in the majority of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) patients treated with fludarabine. During clinical trials, the absolute neutrophil count decreased to 40—125 mg/m2/day for 5—7 days) were associated with a progressive, irreversible syndrome characterized by optic neuritis, cortical blindness, altered mental status, seizures, muscle paralysis, coma, and death. Symptoms appeared 21—60 days following the last dose. Thirteen of 36 patients (36%) who received high-dose fludarabine (96 mg/m2/day for 5—7 days) developed this neurotoxicity. Autopsies have shown severe demyelination and vacuolar changes of white matter throughout the central nervous system. Similar severe central nervous system toxicity has been reported in patients treated with doses recommended for CLL. In the pivotal trial of fludarabine tablets, severe impairment of consciousness was reported in 1 patient. The effect of chronic administration of fludarabine on the central nervous system is unknown; however, patients have received fludarabine at recommended doses for up to 15 cycles. Neurological toxicity reported during fludarabine therapy at recommended doses for CLL include agitation, asthenia (weakness), confusion, coma, seizures, peripheral neuropathy, paresthesias, auditory hallucinations, headache, visual impairment/disturbances, hearing loss, sleep disorder, depression, cerebellar syndrome, and impaired cognition.
 
Rare cases of hemorrhagic cystitis have been reported in patients treated with fludarabine. Other genitourinary adverse events reported during clinical trials with fludarabine, regardless of causality, include dysuria (4%), hematuria (3%), renal failure (unspecified) (1%), abnormal renal function test (1%), proteinuria (1%), and urinary hesitancy (3%).
 
Other adverse reactions reported during fludarabine therapy for chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) include fever (60%), chills (11—19%), fatigue (10—38%), pain (unspecified) (22%), rash (unspecified) (18%), myalgia (16%), diaphoresis (13%), malaise (8%), hyperglycemia (6%), alopecia (3%), pruritus (3%), osteoporosis (2%), anaphylactoid reactions (1%), bleeding (1%), arthralgia (1%), dehydration (1%), and seborrhea (1%). Rarely, patients receiving fludarabine have developed transfusion-related graft-versus-host disease after receiving non-irradiated blood. Consider the use of irradiated blood products in patients receiving fludarabine.
 
In a large series of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) patients randomized to receive chlorambucil, fludarabine, or fludarabine plus chlorambucil, treatment-related myeloid malignancies, specifically treatment-related myelodysplastic syndrome (t-MDS), treatment-related acute myeloid leukemia (t-AML), or t-MDS evolving to t-AML, have been reported. Patients with CLL may have disease transformation to non-Hodgkin's lymphoma or prolymphocytic leukemia; however, development of secondary malignancy of myeloid origin is unusual. These patients either received fludarabine plus chlorambucil (5 of 142 patients, 3.5%) and one patient was treated with fludarabine alone (1 of 188 patients, 0.5%); no patients treated with chlorambucil alone have developed secondary malignancies following a median follow-up of 4.2 years. These findings suggest that the combination of fludarabine and chlorambucil, and possibly other alkylating agents, may increase the risk of treatment-related secondary malignancies.

Description
  Text
  Fludarabine is a purine nucleoside antineoplastic agent. It is indicated as single agent therapy for the treatment of adults with B-cell chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) who have not responded to or whose disease has progressed during treatment with at least 1 standard alkylating agent-containing regimen and as a component of a combination regimen for the treatment of B-cell CLL. Fludarabine may cause severe myelosuppression and autoimmune cytopenias (e.g., hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenic purpura, acquired hemophilia); monitor blood counts before and during treatment.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Fludarabine is a purine nucleoside metabolic inhibitor. It is rapidly converted to active 2-fluoro-ara-ATP, which appears to inhibit DNA synthesis through inhibition of DNA polymerase alpha, ribonucleotide reductase and DNA primase.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Intravenous Route
    Description
    Fludarabine phosphate is dephosphorylated to 2-fluoro-ara-A and then phosphorylated intracellularly by deoxycytidine kinase to the active triphosphate, 2-fluoro-ara-ATP.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    The total body clearance of the active metabolite 2-fluoro-ara-A is correlated with the creatinine clearance; however, the effect of varying degrees of renal impairment on the pharmacokinetics of this metabolite has not been fully characterized.
  Text
  Fludarabine is administered intravenously. Fludarabine phosphate is a prodrug. It is rapidly converted to its active metabolite, 2-fluoro-ara-A, with plasma trough concentration accumulated 2-fold. Plasma protein binding ranges between 19% and 29% in vitro. Renal clearance represents approximately 40% of the total body clearance and the terminal half-life is approximately 20 hours.
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: None.
