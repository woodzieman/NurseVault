  General Information
  NOTE: The Society of Gynecologic Oncology (SGO) has issued a statement detailing platinum dosing and sparing recommendations to overcome challenges of limited drug availability. This statement and disease-specific recommendations can be found at https://www.sgo.org/chemotherapy-drug-shortage/.
 
Carboplatin Dosing with the Calvert equation:
NOTE: For all equations, AUC (area under the plasma concentration vs. time curve) is expressed in mg/ml x min and GFR (glomerular filtration rate) in ml/min.
 
The GFR should be calculated using creatinine clearance determined by actual measurements or estimated by using the Cockroft-Gault or Jeliffe methods. It should be noted that these methods of determining creatinine clearance may not be as accurate, especially in patients with renal dysfunction or patients with low body weight, as the (51)Cr-EDTA method used in the original study. Currently, (51)Cr-EDTA is not available in the United States. The following are general recommendations for target AUC; however, exact dosing guidelines have not been established and may vary depending upon the disease state and protocol.
For chemotherapy naive patients receiving carboplatin: A target AUC of 6-8 mg/ml x min is recommended.
For previously treated patients receiving carboplatin alone: An AUC of 4-6 mg/ml x min is recommended.
For patients receiving carboplatin with other antineoplastics: An AUC of 4-7 mg/ml x min is recommended.
NOTE: By the end of 2010, all clinical laboratories in the US will use the standardized Isotope Dilution Mass Spectrometry (IDMS) method to measure serum creatinine. Compared to older methods of measuring serum creatinine, the IDMS method may underestimate serum creatinine when values are relatively low (e.g. about 0.7 mg/dl). This could result in an overestimation of GFR in patients with normal renal function and when using the Calvert formula for carboplatin dosing (see below), may result in a higher than desired dose and increased drug-related toxicity. If a patient's GFR is calculated based on a serum creatinine measured using the IDMS method, the FDA recommends that physicians consider capping the dose of carboplatin for desired exposure to avoid potential overdosing. Based on the Calvert formula, maximum doses can be calculated as follows:
Target AUC = 6, the maximum dose is 6 x 150 = 900 mg
Target AUC = 5, the maximum dose is 5 x 150 = 750 mg
Target AUC = 4, the maximum dose is 4 x 150 = 600 mg
Maximum dose recommendations are based on a maximum estimated GFR = 125 ml/min. Higher estimated GFR values should not be used.
 
Adults: The total carboplatin dose in mg for adults may be calculated using the Calvert equation:
Total dose (mg) = target AUC x (GFR + 25)
 
Children: The following equation should be used to calculate carboplatin dosage (mg/m2) in children. This equation accounts for differences in the renal elimination of carboplatin between adults and children:
Total dose (mg/m2) = target AUC x [(0.93 x GFR) + 15]

Chemotherapy cycles were started on days 1, 22, and 43 and were administered concurrently with radiotherapy (RT). In a phase III trial, 226 patients with stage III or IV squamous cell carcinoma of the oropharynx (no evidence of distant metastasis) were randomized to receive RT alone or concomitantly with carboplatin/5-FU. Hematologic and skin toxicities were more common in chemoradiotherapy arm. In addition, grades 3 and 4 mucositis and poor nutritional status occurred more frequently with concomitant therapy. There were no significant differences in late toxic effects between the arms when assessed at 5 years.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Carboplatin should be administered under the supervision of a physician experienced in the management of cancer chemotherapy. Adequate diagnostic and treatment facilities are needed for appropriate management of carboplatin therapy and complications.
 
Carboplatin is contraindicated in patients who exhibit platinum compound hypersensitivity. For example, patients who have worked with platinum in the electronics industry can develop sensitivity to platinum. There is an increased incidence of hypersensitivity reactions in patients previously exposed to platinum therapy. Anaphylactic reactions may occur within minutes of carboplatin administration. Epinephrine, corticosteroids, and antihistamines have been utilized to treat these reactions.
 
Contraindications to carboplatin include patients with severe bone marrow suppression and significant bleeding; patients with acute leukemia may require treatment with carboplatin despite severe bone marrow suppression. Carboplatin-induced bone marrow suppression is dose-related and is dose limiting. Complete blood counts should be frequently monitored during carboplatin treatment and, when appropriate, until recovery is achieved. Single intermittent courses of carboplatin should not be repeated until leukocyte, neutrophil, and platelet counts have recovered. When used in combination with other myelosuppressive chemotherapy agents, the dose and administration of carboplatin must be carefully reviewed and adjusted to minimize additive effects. Carboplatin should be used cautiously in patients with coagulopathy, reduced renal function, or in those who have received previous cisplatin or myelosuppressive therapy such as chemotherapy or radiation therapy; these patients may require reduced dosages (i.e., lower target AUC values). Carboplatin is a radiation sensitizer and patients may experience severe myelosuppression or other toxicities with concurrent use. Patients with preexisting bone marrow suppression should be allowed to recover their counts prior to carboplatin administration. Severe thrombocytopenia (= 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. It is also contraindicated in patients with a history of severe allergic reactions to cisplatin or other platinum-containing compounds.
    Headline
    serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis
    General Information
    Serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis may occur within minutes of carboplatin administration during any cycle; treatment with carboplatin requires an experienced clinician in the management of cancer chemotherapy. There is an increased risk of allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis, in patients previously exposed to platinum-based therapy or after 6 cycles of carboplatin. Monitor patients for hypersensitivity reactions; ensure supportive equipment and medications are available to treat severe hypersensitivity reactions.
    Headline
    bleeding, bone marrow suppression, serious adverse effects
    General Information
    Carboplatin can cause dose-dependent bone marrow suppression and is contraindicated in patients with severe bone marrow suppression or significant bleeding. Carboplatin may cause serious adverse effects including anaphylactic-like reactions and vomiting; dose-dependent bone marrow suppression may result in infections and/or bleeding. Monitor blood counts at baseline, weekly during treatment, and as clinically indicated. Bone marrow suppression is increased in patients who have received prior therapy (e.g., cisplatin regimens) and in patients with impaired renal function; dosages in these patients should be appropriately reduced.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Use carboplatin with caution in patients with renal impairment; patients with creatinine clearance below 60 mL/minute are at increased risk of severe bone marrow suppression. A dose reduction is recommended for patients with creatinine clearance 16 to 59 mL/minute when dosing based on body surface area (BSA); a recommended dose has not been established for creatinine clearance less than 16 mL/minute, including renal failure. No dose modification is recommended for renal impairment when dosing based on area under the curve (AUC) as the calculation includes glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Assess renal function at baseline and monitor closely during treatment.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Carboplatin may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy, based on human and animal data and its mechanism of action. When administered to rats, carboplatin was embryolethal and teratogenic. Advise pregnant people of the potential fetal risk. The American Society of Clinical Oncology provides additional information on the use of certain medications for the management of cancer during pregnancy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking carboplatin and for 1 week after the last dose, due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in the breastfed child. There are no data on the presence of carboplatin or its metabolites in human milk, their effects on the breastfed child, or their effects on milk production.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with carboplatin. Carboplatin can cause fetal harm. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking carboplatin and for 6 months after the last dose. For those that can cause pregnancy in others, contraception should be used during carboplatin treatment and for 3 months after the last dose.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Myelosuppression is the dose-limiting toxicity of carboplatin and includes pancytopenia, leukopenia, neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia. Carboplatin-induced myelosuppression is dose-dependent, cumulative, and correlates with renal function. Myelosuppression also occurs following intraperitoneal administration of carboplatin. Previously treated patients and patients with decreased performance status have more severe myelosuppression. In previously treated patients, an AUC of 4—5 mg/ml x min resulted in grade 3 or 4 leukopenia or thrombocytopenia in 13% and 16% of patients, respectively. A carboplatin AUC of 6—7 mg/ml x min resulted in grade 3 or 4 leukopenia and thrombocytopenia in 34% and 33% of previously treated patients, respectively. Thrombocytopenia is the most significant myelosuppressive effect of carboplatin. The platelet nadir is 3 weeks after therapy and recovers within 4—5 weeks. Anemia occurs in up to 70% of patients and as many as 25% of patients require transfusions. the incidence of anemia increases with increasing exposure to carboplatin. Neutropenia ( 90% of patients receiving carboplatin and requires prophylactic therapy with serotonin antagonists and dexamethasone. Vomiting occurs in 65% of patients and in about one-third it is severe. Carboplatin is less emetogenic than cisplatin; however, patients previously treated with other emetogenic agents, especially cisplatin, are more likely to experience vomiting (> 80%). Nausea alone occurs in another 10—15% of patients. Symptoms of nausea and vomiting usually resolve within 24 hours and delayed nausea/vomiting usually is not seen. Prolonged administration of carboplatin either by continuos 24-hour infusion or by daily pulse doses has been associated with less severe vomiting than a single-dose intermittent schedule. Other GI effects of carboplatin include abdominal pain (17%), constipation (6%), and diarrhea (6%). Dysgeusia (changes in taste), a metallic taste, and anorexia have been reported in patients treated with carboplatin.Due to the stability of carboplatin, less carboplatin is activated in the renal tubules. Therefore, the incidence of carboplatin-induced nephrotoxicity is much less than with cisplatin. However in high doses, carboplatin may cause similar nephrotoxic effects, especially in patients with renal impairment or those receiving high doses. During therapy with standard doses of carboplatin, azotemia (14%) and increases in serum creatinine (6%) may occur and are usually reversible. An increased incidence of elevated serum creatinine or azotemia may be seen in patients previously treated with other chemotherapy, especially cisplatin. Creatinine clearance is the most sensitive measure of kidney function in patients receiving carboplatin. In patients with a baseline creatinine clearance > 60 ml/min, 27% demonstrated a reduction below this value during carboplatin therapy. Hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hyponatremia, hypophosphatemia, and/or hypocalcemia occur in about 30% of patients during therapy with carboplatin but are not as common or severe as with cisplatin. Hyperuricemia has been reported to occur in approximately 7% of patients on carboplatin. Increased uric acid concentrations usually occur after 3—5 days of therapy.Anaphylactoid reactions can occur rarely during therapy with carboplatin in patients with prior exposure to platinum or platinum-containing compounds, including cisplatin. There is an increasing incidence of hypersensitivity reactions in patients receiving repeated courses of carboplatin. Hypersensitivity usually develops after >= 6 courses of carboplatin. Reactions may occur up to 3 days after therapy and desensitization is not usually successful. Other hypersensitivity reactions (2%) including rash (unspecified), urticaria, erythema, pruritus, and, rarely, bronchospasm and hypotension have been reported.In contrast to cisplatin therapy, tinnitus, hearing loss, and peripheral neuropathy are rare with carboplatin. Peripheral neuropathy consisting of mild paresthesias occurs in about 4% of patients treated with carboplatin. Carboplatin may worsen cisplatin-induced peripheral neuropathy in 30% of cases. Prolonged treatment of carboplatin may result in cumulative neurotoxicity, especially in patients who have received prior cisplatin therapy or elderly patients. Ototoxicity is most apparent in pediatric patients who receive high doses of carboplatin in combination with other ototoxic agents.Visual impairment, including loss of vision and/or changes in the perception of light and colors, has been reported with high-dose carboplatin. Vision tends to recover totally or to a significant extent within weeks of stopping high-dose carboplatin therapy.Elevated hepatic enzymes (e.g., AST), alkaline phosphatase, and/or hyperbilirubinemia is seen in approximately 15% of patients during therapy with carboplatin. These elevations tend to be reversible.In the treatment of ovarian cancer, platinum-based therapy has been associated with an increased risk of secondary malignancy, primarily acute myelogenous leukemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, acute lymphocytic leukemia and chronic myelogenous leukemia. The risk of secondary malignancy increases with cumulative carboplatin doses > 2000 mg. However, in evaluating the risks versus benefits of platinum-based chemotherapy in the treatment of ovarian cancer, the survival benefits exceed the risk of secondary malignancy.Injection site reaction, such as erythema, swelling, and pain, has been reported. Tissue necrosis associated with extravasation of carboplatin has been reported during post-marketing experience with carboplatin.Pain and asthenia are commonly reported during carboplatin therapy and are probably related to the tumor and anemia. Cardiovascular, respiratory, genitourinary and mucosal side effects (including stomatitis or esophagitis) have been reported in <= 6% of patients. Cardiovascular events (e.g., heart failure, thromboembolism, and stroke) were fatal in < 1% of patients and did not appear to be related to chemotherapy. Cancer-associated hemolytic anemia has been reported rarely. Other adverse effects noted with carboplatin include alopecia (3%), malaise, and hypertension.

Description
  Text
  Carboplatin (CBDCA) is a platinum compound related to cisplatin. Carboplatin is formed by replacing the chloride leaving groups of cisplatin with 1, 1-cyclobutanedicarboxylato ligand, which increases the stability of the leaving groups. On a molar basis, carboplatin is 45 times less cytotoxic than cisplatin. Carboplatin has a more favorable adverse effect profile than cisplatin, which has led to the investigation of the replacement of cisplatin with carboplatin in many regimens. Carboplatin and cisplatin seem to be equally effective in ovarian, non-small cell and small cell lung cancers. Comparative trials are ongoing in these cancers and others to determine if there is an advantage of carboplatin over cisplatin. Most tumors that are resistant to cisplatin will be resistant to carboplatin. Currently, there does not seem to be a role for carboplatin in place of cisplatin in the routine management of testicular or head and neck cancers. Due to its myelosuppressive effects, carboplatin has been used in bone marrow ablation protocols and in the treatment of relapsed leukemias. The FDA approved carboplatin for the treatment of ovarian cancer in March 1989.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Carboplatin cytotoxic activity is similar to cisplatin as it binds with DNA to form intrastrand crosslinks and adducts that cause changes in the conformation of the DNA and affect DNA replication. Carboplatin readily crosses the cell membrane. Once inside the cell, the ring structure of carboplatin is hydroxylated by water to form the active moiety. This reaction occurs more slowly than the activation of cisplatin. Therefore, 4—6 times the amount of carboplatin is required to produce the same cytotoxic effects as cisplatin. Once in the active form, carboplatin functions similarly to cisplatin and binds with DNA, RNA, or other macromolecules at two sites to form interstrand and intrastrand links. Carboplatin forms irreversible covalent bonds which inhibit DNA replication, RNA transcription, and protein synthesis. Intrastrand crosslinks at the N7 position of guanine are the predominant binding sites. The maximal DNA crosslinks occur 18 hours after exposure to carboplatin compared to 6—12 hours for cisplatin. Carboplatin crosslinks have a slower removal rate than do cisplatin-induced crosslinks. This slower rate of onset and removal of carboplatin crosslinks is thought to be due to a slow rate of monofunctional adduct formation and/or a slower rate of conversion of monoadducts to crosslinks. While considered cell cycle non-specific, carboplatin cytotoxicity is increased with exposure during the S-phase and with increased infusion rates (24 hours versus 1 hour). Carboplatin causes cell cycle arrest in the G2-phase and then induces programmed cell death or apoptosis.

  Text
  Carboplatin is primarily administered by IV infusion. The pharmacokinetics of carboplatin and cisplatin differ greatly. Very little protein binding occurs. Carboplatin distributes well into ascites and pleural fluid. CSF concentrations are approximately 30% of plasma concentrations. Carboplatin is not metabolized but undergoes spontaneous hydrolysis to form the active compound. The excretion of carboplatin is biphasic with half-lives of 90 and 180 minutes Carboplatin is excreted primarily by the kidneys via tubular filtration and secretion. Sixty-five percent of a dose can be recovered in the urine in the first 24 hours.
 
The exposure to carboplatin or AUC is associated with myelosuppressive and cytotoxic effects. In general, carboplatin AUC of 4—7 is associated with acceptable myelosuppressive effects and efficacy. A carboplatin AUC of > 7 is associated with an increased incidence of severe myelosuppression with no added efficacy. Carboplatin bolus doses of 400—600 mg/m2 are associated with an AUC of 6—8 mg/mL x min in previously untreated patients with normal creatinine clearance. Several equations have been developed to individualize carboplatin dosing. The Calvert equation is the simplest equation and was approved by the FDA in 1995 for dosing of carboplatin. In the determination of this equation, creatinine clearance was determined using Cr-EDTA, which is thought to more accurately reflect glomerular filtration rate in patients with altered renal function than does a measured or a calculated creatinine clearance. Currently, Cr-EDTA is not available in the United States and creatinine clearance is determined using measured or calculated methods, which may not be as accurate. The Egorin equations use creatinine clearance and a relationship with platelet nadir to determine the carboplatin dosage. These equations also differentiate between previously treated and untreated patients to take into account the cumulative bone marrow toxicity of carboplatin. The dosing of carboplatin in many disease states is now almost entirely based upon AUC and not body surface area.

Intermittent hemodialysis
Multiple reports document the use of carboplatin in patients who are receiving concurrent hemodialysis. Most of the reports indicate that carboplatin is removed by hemodialysis. Studies indicate that anuric patients may receive carboplatin at initial doses not exceeding 150 mg/m2 IV followed by dialysis within 24—48 hours of treatment (written communication, Bristol-Myers Squibb Oncology/Immunology Division, September 1999).
