The absolute improvement in 5-year OS was 11% at a median follow-up time of 9.3 years. In a post-hoc analysis, no OS benefit was observed in patients with stage IB disease. Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Vinorelbine is an extremely toxic drug with a low therapeutic index, and therapeutic doses are likely to cause toxicity. Therefore, this drug should be used only by clinicians experienced in administering chemotherapy.
 
Myelosuppressive drugs, such as vinorelbine, can cause an increased incidence of fungal, viral, and bacterial infections, bleeding and fatigue due to neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia, respectively. Because these adverse effects can be fatal, the patient must be warned to promptly report any signs such as fever, sore throat, or abnormal pain. Hematological status (hematocrit, hemoglobin concentration, leukocyte and platelet counts) should be monitored weekly during vinorelbine therapy, and patients should be observed for signs of infection. Vinorelbine should not be administered to patients with pre-treatment neutropenia (= 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    bone marrow suppression
    General Information
    Severe bone marrow suppression including neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia occurs in patients treated with vinorelbine and can lead to infection, septic shock, hospitalization, and death. Neutropenia is the major dose-limiting toxicity, with the nadir between days 7 and 10, and recovery typically within the following 7 to 14 days. Monitor complete blood counts prior to each dose of vinorelbine; a dose adjustment may be necessary based on neutrophil counts on the day of treatment. Do not administer vinorelbine to patients with neutrophil counts less than 1,000 cells/mm3.
    Headline
    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use vinorelbine with caution in patients with hepatic impairment, including hepatic failure; drug-induced liver injury manifested by increased AST and hyperbilirubinemia can occur during treatment. Assess hepatic function prior to initiation of vinorelbine therapy and periodically during treatment. A dose reduction may be necessary for patients with hyperbilirubinemia.
    Headline
    autonomic neuropathy, peripheral neuropathy
    General Information
    Use vinorelbine with caution in patients with a history of peripheral neuropathy or autonomic neuropathy causing constipation. Neurotoxicity including sensory and motor neuropathy, in some cases severe, has occurred with vinorelbine treatment. Monitor for new or worsening signs and symptoms of neuropathy, such as paresthesia, hyperesthesia, hyporeflexia, and muscle weakness. Discontinue vinorelbine for grade 2 or higher peripheral neuropathy or autonomic neuropathy causing constipation.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Based on its mechanism of action and findings from animal studies, vinorelbine may cause fetal harm when given during pregnancy. Vinorelbine was embryo- and fetotoxic in mice when given as a single dose approximately 0.33 times the recommended human dose (RHD) based on body surface area. Vinorelbine was also embryo- and fetotoxic in rabbits given vinorelbine given a dose approximately 0.18 the RHD every 6 days during organogenesis. At doses that did not cause maternal toxicity in either species, vinorelbine administration caused reduced fetal weight and delayed ossification. The American Society of Clinical Oncology provides additional information on the use of certain medications for the management of cancer during pregnancy.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with vinorelbine. Vinorelbine may cause fetal harm. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking vinorelbine and for 6 months after the last dose. For those that can cause pregnancy in others, contraception should be used during vinorelbine treatment and for 3 months after the last dose. Additionally, male infertility has been observed in animal studies.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking vinorelbine and for 9 days after the last dose. There are no data on the presence of vinorelbine in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. However, vinorelbine has the potential to cause serious side effects in the breastfed child.

  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Similar to vinblastine, the dose limiting toxicity of vinorelbine is myelosuppression consisting of leukopenia (< 1000 cells/mm3, 15%), specifically neutropenia (< 500/mm3, 29—36%). The onset of neutropenia usually occurs around day 7—14 and recovery is seen by day 14—24. Vinorelbine-induced granulocytopenia is generally reversible and not cumulative over time. Hospitalizations due to neutropenia complications (i.e., infection, sepsis, or fever) have been reported in about 9% of patients receiving vinorelbine. Septic deaths have occurred in about 1% of patients receiving vinorelbine alone. Anemia is mild and common (hemoglobin < 8 g/dl, 9%). Thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 50,000/mm3) is relatively rare (1%). Anemia, severe granulocytopenia, and thrombocytopenia are more common when vinorelbine is given in combination with cisplatin (24%, 82%, and 29%, respectively). Fever and/or sepsis related to neutropenia occurred in 11% of patients receiving vinorelbine and cisplatin during clinical trials. Routine use of prophylactic hematopoietic growth factors is not required.Neurotoxicity is much less common during therapy with vinorelbine than with vincristine. Low grade peripheral neuropathy, manifested by ataxia, hyperesthesia, paresthesias or muscle weakness, may occur in up to 25% of patients; however, severe (grade 3—4) peripheral neuropathy occurs <= 1% of patients receiving vinorelbine as a single agent and is often reversible. Concurrent administration with cisplatin does not seem to increase the incidence of severe peripheral neuropathy. Loss of deep tendon reflexes occurs in fewer than 5% of patients. Uncommonly back pain, jaw pain, or tumor pain may occur. Vinorelbine's neurotoxicity appears to be reversible after discontinuation. Vestibular and auditory deficits (i.e., dizziness or hearing loss) have been observed with vinorelbine, usually when used in combination with cisplatin. Headache has been reported, with and without musculoskeletal pain, arthralgia or myalgia.Injection site reactions including pain at the injection site, occur in 28—38% of patients receiving vinorelbine; severe reactions occur in <= 5% of patients. Extravasation of vinorelbine can lead to severe pain, skin ulcer, and tissue necrosis. Patients should be observed for any injection site reaction especially severe pain, swelling, erythema and poor blood return. Chemical phlebitis along the vein proximal to the injection site is reported in 10% of patients. Extravasation of vinca alkaloids cause blisters after a few weeks and evolve slowly into necrotic ulcers. Development of ulcers following vinorelbine extravasation may be delayed. Some healing may occur; however, neurologic symptoms including paresthesias and sensory deficits at the site may be permanent. If extravasation occurs, treatment with warm compresses and hyaluronidase is effective in preventing ulcer formation. Cold compresses significantly increase vinca alkaloid toxicity. Skin discoloration along the vein is reported in one-third of patients.Patients who have received prior radiation therapy may develop a radiation recall reaction at the previous radiation site (e.g., dermatitis or esophagitis). Vinorelbine may also act as a radiation sensitizer causing increased adverse effects such as dermatitis and esophagitis in patient receiving concomitant radiation therapy.Generalized symptoms such as asthenia (36%) and fatigue (27%) are common with vinorelbine. These symptoms may worsen with cumulative dosing. Moderate alopecia occurs in 12% of patients. Systemic allergic reactions have been reported with vinorelbine. These adverse reactions include anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema, flushing, pruritus, and urticaria. Other toxicities that have been reported in < 5% of patients include jaw pain and rash (unspecified). Hemorrhagic cystitis occurred in < 1% of patients.Mild nausea/vomiting can occur and responds to conventional antiemetics. Diarrhea, anorexia, and stomatitis occur in less than 20% of patients and are usually not severe. Severe constipation, paralytic ileus, GI obstruction, GI necrosis, and/or GI perforation have been association with vinorelbine; some events have been fatal. Constipation or a paralytic ileus occurs in less than 33% of patients, but is similar to the toxicity of vincristine. Abdominal pain dysphagia, and pancreatitis have also been reported. Transient elevated hepatic enzymes (67%) have been reported without clinical symptoms. Hyperbilirubinemia has been reported in 13% of patients, including grade 3—4 elevations (jaundice) in 2—4% of patients.Cardiotoxicity is rare, however, chest pain (unspecified) (5%), hypertension, hypotension, myocardial infarction, sinus tachycardia, and vasodilation have been reported in patients receiving vinorelbine. In some cases, the chest pain responded to sublingual nitrates. Many of these patients had a history of tobacco smoking, underlying cardiovascular disease, and/or thoracic malignancies.Thrombotic events such as pulmonary embolism and deep venous thrombosis have been reported with vinorelbine therapy, especially in combination with cisplatin. Thromboembolism occurred primarily in seriously ill and debilitated patients with known predisposing factors for these conditions.Electrolyte abnormalities, including hyponatremia with or without the syndrome of inappropriate ADH (SIADH) secretion, have been reported in seriously ill and debilitated patients (< 1%).Pulmonary hypersensitivity occurs in fewer than 5% of patients receiving vinorelbine. Either an acute or subacute course may occur. Acute bronchospasm and shortness of breath/wheezing (3%) have been reported; severe cases were seen in 2% of patients. These symptoms occurred most commonly with concurrent administration with mitomycin. These events may require treatment with supplemental oxygen, bronchodilators, and/or corticosteroids, especially if there is underlying pulmonary disease. The subacute toxicity is manifested by dyspnea (7%), cough, hypoxia, and pulmonary infiltrates on chest X-ray. Pneumonia and pulmonary edema have also been reported. Reported cases of interstitial pulmonary changes and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), most of which were fatal, occurred in patients treated with single-agent vinorelbine. The mean onset of these symptoms after vinorelbine administration was one week (range 3—8 days). Patients with alterations in their baseline pulmonary symptoms or with new onset dyspnea, cough, hypoxia, wheezing, or other symptoms should be evaluated promptly.

Description
  Text
  Vinorelbine is a semi-synthetic vinca alkaloid derived from the Catharanthus roseus or Madagascar periwinkle plant. It acts as a microtubule inhibitor, causing cellular instability and preventing mitosis at metaphase. Microtubules are responsible for intracellular transport, maintenance of cell shape, polarity, cell signaling, and mitosis. During mitosis, microtubules form the mitotic spindle that transports daughter chromosomes to separate poles of the dividing cell. Vinorelbine is more potent and less neurotoxic than vinblastine and vincristine. Vinorelbine is indicated for the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) as monotherapy and in combination with cisplatin. The primary dose-limiting toxicity is neutropenia. Monitor complete blood counts prior to each dose; an adjustment to the dose of vinorelbine may be necessary for neutropenia. Vinorelbine is also a vesicant; monitor closely for signs of extravasation.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Vinorelbine is a semi-synthetic vinca alkaloid. It acts as a microtubule inhibitor, causing cellular instability and preventing mitosis at metaphase. Microtubules are responsible for intracellular transport, maintenance of cell shape, polarity, cell signaling, and mitosis. During mitosis, microtubules form the mitotic spindle that transports daughter chromosomes to separate poles of the dividing cell. Vinorelbine may also interfere with amino acid, cyclic AMP, and glutathione metabolism; calmodulin-dependent calcium-transport ATPase activity; cellular respiration; and nucleic acid and lipid biosynthesis. Vinorelbine inhibited mitotic microtubule formation in intact mouse embryo tectal plates at a concentration of 2 micromolar, inducing a blockade of cells at metaphase; however, it produced depolymerization of axonal microtubules at a concentration of 40 micromolar. This suggests a modest selectivity of vinorelbine for mitotic microtubules.

  Text
  Vinorelbine is administered intravenously. It demonstrates high binding to human platelets and lymphocytes. The free fraction was approximately 0.11 in human plasma over a concentration range of 234 ng/mL to 1,169 ng/mL. The binding to plasma constituents in cancer patients ranged from 79.6% to 91.2%. The steady-state volume of distribution (Vss) of vinorelbine ranged from 25.4 L/kg to 40.1 L/kg. Vinorelbine plasma concentrations decay in a triphasic manner. The terminal phase half-life averaged 27.7 to 43.6 hours and the mean plasma clearance ranged from 0.97 to 1.26 L/hour/kg. Vinorelbine undergoes substantial hepatic elimination to 2 metabolites: vinorelbine N-oxide and deacetylvinorelbine. Deacetylvinorelbine is the primary metabolite in humans and has antitumor activity similar to vinorelbine; however, therapeutic doses of vinorelbine (30 mg/m2) yield very small, if any, quantifiable levels of either metabolite in blood or urine. After IV administration, approximately 46% of a radioactive dose was recovered in the feces and 18% in the urine. In a different study, 10.9% (+/- 0.7%) of a single IV dose was excreted as parent drug in the urine.

Dilution
In a flexible plastic container (i.e., minibag), dilute the calculated dose/volume of vinorelbine in a compatible IV fluid (e.g., 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection, 0.45% Sodium Chloride Injection, 5% Dextrose Injection, 5% Dextrose and 0.45% Sodium Chloride Injection, Ringer's Injection, or Lactated Ringer's Injection) to a final concentration between 0.5 and 2 mg/mL.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Total bilirubin 2 mg/dL or less: No dosage adjustment required.
Total bilirubin 2.1 to 3 mg/dL: Reduce the dose of vinorelbine to 50% of the original dose.
Total bilirubin greater than 3 mg/dL: Reduce the dose of vinorelbine to 25% of the original dose.
Note: For patients with concurrent hematologic toxicity and hepatic impairment/hepatotoxicity, administer the lower of the recommended dose adjustments based on the corresponding starting dose of vinorelbine.
Patients with diffuse liver metastases (more than 75% of liver volume replaced by tumor): Consider reducing the dose of vinorelbine to 50% of the original dose.
