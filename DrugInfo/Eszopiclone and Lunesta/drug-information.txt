Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  NOTE: Timing of eszopiclone administration is important. Eszopiclone should be given immediately prior to retiring. Taking a sedative/hypnotic while the patient is still up and about may induce short-term memory impairment, hallucinations, impaired coordination, dizziness, and lightheadedness.
 
This medication is contraindicated in those with eszopiclone hypersensitivity or hypersensitivity to any ingredient in the product. Reactions including anaphylaxis or angioedema may occur with sedative-hypnotics, and may become evident as early as the initial dose. Patients should be instructed on the appropriate action in the event of an allergic reaction. Treatment with eszopiclone should not be re-initiated in patients who experience angioedema after administration of the drug.
 
Eszopiclone may be used for the long-term treatment of insomnia. However, problems associated with abrupt discontinuation of hypnotic drugs are more likely to occur following chronic therapy. In a clinical study, patients received either placebo (n=44), eszopiclone 2 mg (n=104), or eszopiclone 3 mg (n=105) nightly for 6-weeks. On days 45 and 46, the first and second days of withdrawal from study drug, patients received single-blind placebo. During this withdrawal period, patients in the 3 mg group spontaneously reported anxiety (1%), abnormal dreams (1.9%), hyperesthesia (1%), and neurosis (1%), while none previously receiving placebo reported these symptoms. Withdrawal of some hypnotics also precipitates a rebound insomnia. If therapy is continued for more than 2 weeks, the possibility of a withdrawal syndrome should be considered and abrupt discontinuation of therapy avoided. The possibility of physical and psychological dependence to eszopiclone requires close monitoring. Eszopiclone should be used cautiously in patients with a history of alcoholism or substance abuse.
 
Mental status changes have been reported to occur with the use of sedatives/hypnotics, including the emergence of bizarre behaviors or amnesia. While these are relatively infrequent with eszopiclone, any emergence of changes in thinking or behavior should be evaluated. Sleep disturbances may indicate an underlying physical or psychiatric problem. The failure of insomnia to remit after 7—10 days of eszopiclone treatment may indicate the presence of a primary medical or psychiatric illness.
 
Eszopiclone should be used with caution in patients exhibiting signs and symptoms of depression. Worsening of pre-existing depression, including suicidal ideation and completed suicides, has occurred in association with the use of sedative/hypnotics. The exact incidences among various sedative products are unknown; however patients should be informed of the risks prior to receiving any medication from this class, including eszopiclone. If use is warranted in this patient group, the least amount of eszopiclone feasible should be prescribed at any one time.
 
Eszopiclone has a rapid onset of action and should only be administered prior to retiring. Patients should be instructed to avoid driving or operating machinery or performing other tasks requiring mental alertness after taking eszopiclone. Sedative-hypnotic medications have the potential to cause sleep-related behaviors such as sleep-driving, a state of driving after ingestion of a sedative-hypnotic while not fully awake and having no memory of the event. Other sleep-related behaviors may include making phone calls or eating while asleep. The exact incidences among various sedative products are unknown; however patients should be informed of the risks prior to receiving any medication from this class. Next-day residual effects are not common with eszopiclone. Additive effects may occur with alcohol or other CNS depressant medications. Patients should avoid ethanol intoxication while taking this medication. Lower initial dosages of eszopiclone should be considered in patients taking other CNS depressant therapies concurrently.
 
Elderly and/or debilitated patients may be more sensitive to the effects of eszopiclone. The impairment of cognitive and motor function may be more marked in this patient group which could result in falls or mental status changes. A lower initial dosage is recommended in this patient group with close monitoring (see Dosage). Federal OBRA regulations recommend maximum dosage limits for hypnotic use in the nursing home resident.
 
Eszopiclone is classified as FDA pregnancy category C. No published reports of eszopiclone use in human pregnancy are available; however, human reports on the racemic zopiclone (available outside the US) are published. In an observational study (n = 40), an active treatment and control group was compared to determine pregnancy outcomes with 1st trimester exposure to zopiclone. The outcomes included: spontaneous abortion (7 in the active treatment group vs. 3 in the control group), elective abortion (1 vs. 0, respectively), and congenital dislocation of the hip (1 vs. 1, respectively). No major defects were observed in either group. The low molecular weight (389) and low protein binding suggest eszopiclone will cross the human placenta. Animal studies have shown no evidence of teratogenicity or structural defects but decreased fetal weights and evidence of developmental delay were seen with doses up to 400 times the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD). Decreased fetal weights and developmental delays were not observed with doses of roughly 200 times the MRHD. When eszopiclone was given to rats throughout pregnancy and lactation at doses ranging from 200—600 times the MRHD, increased postimplantation loss, decreased postnatal pup weights, survival, and increased pup startle response was seen at all doses. An increase in adenocarcinomas (mammary gland, thyroid gland follicular cell, pulmonary) has been noted in both male and female rats at doses ranging from 70—150 times the MRHD. The available data suggest that eszopiclone use in pregnancy is probably low risk; however, eszopiclone should only be used during pregnancy if the potential benefit to the mother justifies a potential risk to the fetus. If a hypnotic is required in pregnancy, it may be prudent to use a hypnotic with more published human pregnancy safety literature. Additionally, there is some risk that babies born to mothers taking sedative/hypnotics may suffer withdrawal symptoms and possible neonatal flaccidity. Eszopiclone has no established use during labor or obstetric delivery.
 
It is not known if eszopiclone is excreted in human milk. However, racemic zopiclone (available outside the US) is excreted into human milk. The milk:plasma AUC ratio for zopiclone has been reported as 0.6/0.8 after a 7.5 mg PO dose. Eszopiclone has a low molecular weight (389), low protein binding, and moderately long half-life of 6 hours, suggesting excretion into the breast milk is likely. Sedation and/or CNS toxicity may be of concern in the nursing infant. Caution should be exercised if eszopiclone is administered to a woman who is breast-feeding.
 
The safety and efficacy of eszopiclone in children under the age of 18 years have not been evaluated.
 
Studies of healthy subjects receiving doses of eszopiclone 2.5 times higher than the recommended therapeutic doses did not reveal any respiratory-depressant effects. However, eszopiclone should be used with caution in patients with pre-existing respiratory depression, such as severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), or sleep apnea to avoid the risk of depressing ventilatory function.
 
In patients with severe hepatic disease the dose of eszopiclone should be reduced as recommended by the manufacturer (see Dosage). In these patients the plasma concentration of eszopiclone was double that of normal subjects.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. While eszopiclone belongs to the nonbenzodiazepine, benzodiazepine receptor agonist (NBRA) class, the chemical structures of the agents in this class are distinct. Cross-sensitivity to one NBRA does not imply cross-sensitivity will occur to another agent in the class.
    Headline
    depression, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Worsening of depression and suicidal ideation, including completed suicides, have been reported during the use of hypnotics, primarily in people with pre-existing depression. Immediately evaluate patients with worsening depression, emergent suicidal ideation (suicidal thoughts or actions), or other new or worsening adverse behaviors during treatment with eszopiclone. A variety of abnormal thinking and behavioral changes have been reported to occur in association with the use of sedative/hypnotics, including eszopiclone. Some of these changes included decreased inhibition (e.g., aggressiveness and extroversion that seemed out of character), bizarre behavior, agitation and depersonalization. Visual and auditory hallucinations have been reported. Amnesia and other neuropsychiatric symptoms may occur unpredictably. Some adverse effects of eszopiclone appear to be dose-related; use of the lowest effective dose may mitigate these effects. Because intentional overdose is more common in people with pre-existing depression, eszopiclone should be prescribed in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management to reduce the risk of overdose.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery, drug-induced complex sleep-related behaviors
    General Information
    Eszopiclone and other non-benzodiazepine receptor agonists (NBRAs) are contraindicated in people with a history of drug-induced complex sleep-related behaviors. Sedative-hypnotics, especially NBRAs like eszopiclone, can cause complex sleep-related behaviors (e.g., sleep-driving, eating, sexual activity, or making phone calls) with no memory of these events. These effects can occur with or without concomitant use of alcohol or other CNS depressants. Rarely, serious injuries or death have occurred. Patients should discontinue eszopiclone immediately and contact their healthcare provider if such episodes occur. Care teams and patients are encouraged to report adverse events to the FDA MedWatch Safety Information and Adverse Event Reporting Program. Eszopiclone has rapid onset and CNS depressant effects, and should be taken immediately before bed, with at least 7 to 8 hours remaining for sleep. Patients should avoid driving or operating machinery, or engaging in activities requiring coordination and concentration the day after use. The risk for next-day impairment is increased if eszopiclone is taken without adherence to recommended hours for sleep or at doses higher than recommended. Anterograde amnesia may occur and can be particularly evident at eszopiclone doses above the recommended maximum. Use with alcohol or other CNS depressants increases the risk of impairment and adverse effects, and patients should avoid alcoholic beverages. Consider lower doses of eszopiclone when other CNS depressants are prescribed concurrently.
    Headline
    respiratory insufficiency
    General Information
    Sedative-hypnotics have the potential to suppress respiratory drive, and caution is advised when using eszopiclone in people with compromised respiratory function (respiratory insufficiency). While respiratory depressant effects were not seen in a study of healthy volunteers, caution is advised when considering the use of eszopiclone in people with respiratory insufficiency, such as severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), sleep apnea, or individuals with concomitant opioid use.
    Headline
    substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    Eszopiclone should be used with caution in people with a history of a substance abuse disorder. People with a history of addiction or abuse of drugs or alcohol are at increased risk for misuse, abuse, or addiction and should be monitored carefully when receiving eszopiclone or any hypnotic. Additionally, people taking eszopiclone are at risk of physical and/or psychological dependence and this risk increases with dose and duration of treatment. People with dependence on eszopiclone may experience withdrawal signs and symptoms following abrupt discontinuation. To minimize these risks, eszopiclone should be used at the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration of treatment possible, and reassess the patient periodically before continuing treatment for an extended period of time.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Eszopiclone should be used with caution in people with hepatic impairment since the drug is extensively metabolized in the liver. People with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure should receive a lower dosage due to increased systemic exposure in this population, which may increase the risk of next-day psychomotor and memory impairment. No dosage adjustments are necessary in people with mild or moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A or B).
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Geriatric adults are more sensitive to the effects of eszopiclone, necessitating lower doses and close monitoring. Since eszopiclone can cause drowsiness and impairs consciousness, there is an increased risk of falls and severe injuries in older adults. Discontinuing eszopiclone, especially at higher doses, can lead to rebound insomnia; however, at recommended doses, no long-term evidence of rebound insomnia has been observed. The Beers Criteria state that eszopiclone and other nonbenzodiazepine, benzodiazepine-receptor agonists (NBRAs), are potentially inappropriate for use in geriatric adults. NBRAs may cause adverse effects similar to benzodiazepines, including falls, fractures, and delirium, which are particularly concerning in older adults. Their use is associated with increased emergency department visits, hospitalizations, and motor vehicle crashes, with minimal benefit in improving sleep latency and duration. Avoid NBRA use in individuals with dementia or cognitive impairment, or those at high risk of delirium. Additionally, avoid NBRAs in geriatric adults with a history of falls or fractures unless no safer alternatives are available. If an NBRA must be prescribed, consider reducing other CNS-active medications and employing strategies to mitigate fall risk. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates the use of sedative/hypnotic agents for residents of long-term care facilities (LTCFs); when a drug is being used to induce sleep or treat a sleep disorder, the facility should attempt periodic tapering of the medication as stated in the OBRA guidelines or provide documentation of continued medical necessity.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    The use of eszopiclone during pregnancy is not usually recommended. The low molecular weight of eszopiclone and low protein binding suggest eszopiclone will cross the human placenta. Zopiclone (the parent racemic agent, not available in the U.S.) is known to cross the placenta. In an observational study (n = 40), no major defects were observed, and the authors concluded that use of zopiclone or eszopiclone during human pregnancy is likely not associated with congenital malformations. In a study examining data from the Swedish Medical Birth Registry, the birth outcomes for 692 infants who were exposed to zopiclone in early pregnancy were compared to nonexposed infants and zopiclone use was not associated with an increased risk for congenital malformations. There were 4 cases of intestinal malformations reported in infants exposed to hypnotic benzodiazepine receptor agonists (HBRAs); 2 of these cases were in infants exposed to zopiclone and 2 were exposed to zolpidem. The authors noted the presence of several potential confounders, such as exposure to other medications, smoking status, and maternal age, that may have contributed to these malformations. Published studies of other HBRAs do not indicate an increased risk of congenital malformations at typical doses. However, one study has reported neural tube defects in an infant following high-dose exposure to zolpidem in the first trimester of pregnancy. In a systematic review and meta-analysis developed to assess for risks associated with use of HBRAs in pregnancy, the authors noted that pregnancy exposure to HBRAs was not associated with an increased risk of congenital malformations but was associated with increased risks of preterm birth, low birth weight, and being small for gestational age compared to unexposed infants. Similarly, a population-based cohort study determined that HBRA use in early pregnancy may be associated with an increased risk of infants being born small for gestational age, even after controlling for numerous confounding variables. Additional studies are needed to determine the true risk and incidence of these effects.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    There is a risk of respiratory depression and sedation in neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero during the third trimester. Monitor neonates exposed to hypnotic benzodiazepine receptor agonists in utero for signs and symptoms of respiratory depression and sedation at birth, and manage accordingly. Withdrawal symptoms may also be possible during the postnatal period. A limited number of exposed neonatal cases of moderate to severe respiratory depression requiring aspiration, artificial ventilation, or intratracheal intubation have been reported. The majority of neonates recovered within hours to a few weeks after birth once treated. Neonatal flaccidity has also been reported in newborns after maternal sedative-hypnotic use during pregnancy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Eszopiclone should be used with caution during breast-feeding. The low molecular weight and low protein binding of the drug suggest that excretion into the breast milk is likely, and the relatively long half-life (mean half-life, 6 hours) should be considered. Sedation and CNS toxicity may be of concern in the breastfed child. Racemic zopiclone (parent drug available outside the U.S.) is excreted into human milk and the milk: plasma AUC ratio for zopiclone has been reported as 0.6/0.8 after a 7.5 mg oral dose. Although the concentration excreted in breast milk is low, the European zopiclone label recommends avoidance of breast-feeding during use. Consider the developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding along with the clinical need for eszopiclone and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the drug or the underlying maternal condition. Alternatives, such as zaleplon or zolpidem, may be preferred; however, the effects of these medications on the breastfed infant have not been formally evaluated.

Description
  Text
  Eszopiclone is a short-acting, non-benzodiazepine sedative-hypnotic and was the first indicated for chronic treatment of insomnia. It has been shown to decrease sleep latency as well as improving sleep maintenance. Although chemically unrelated to other hypnotics, such as the benzodiazepines or barbiturates, eszopiclone does share some pharmacological actions with these drugs in terms of its sedative and hypnotic properties. Unlike the benzodiazepines, it produces muscle relaxation and anticonvulsant effects only at doses much higher than the hypnotic dose. Eszopiclone has a rapid onset of action and should only be taken immediately before going to sleep. Eszopiclone has two primary metabolites with little or no activity at recommended therapeutic doses, which reduces the risk of residual next-day effects from prolonged or excessive sedation. As with all medications in this class, eszopiclone has a boxed warning for an increased risk of complex sleep behaviors, which may include sleep-walking, sleep-driving, and engaging in other activities while not fully awake. Eszopiclone also has CNS-depressant effects and should only be taken when there is enough time for a full night's rest to avoid next day sedation. Patients should be made aware of these risks prior to starting treatment with eszopiclone. The Veterans Affairs/Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guidelines for management of chronic insomnia suggest eszopiclone may be considered for short-term use in adults who are candidates for pharmacotherapy, but also indicate patients must be specifically counseled regarding the risk of complex sleep-related behaviors. The FDA approved eszopiclone in December 2004.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Eszopiclone is a gamma-aminobutyric acid-A (GABA-A) receptor positive modulator thought to exert its therapeutic effect by binding at the alpha-subunit within the GABAA receptor complex, also known as the benzodiazepine (BZ) or omega receptor. Differences in sedative, anticonvulsant, anxiolytic, and myorelaxant properties among the sedative-hypnotic medications are thought to be related to differences in selectivity at the various subunits within this GABAA receptor chloride channel macromolecular complex. Zopiclone, a racemic mixture of (R)- and (S)-isomers marketed in Europe for insomnia, is believed to have higher affinity for the alpha1 subunit, making it more selective for sleep effects. The (S)-zopiclone isomer (eszopiclone) appears to be less selective and interacts nonspecifically with alpha1, alpha2, and alpha3 subunits, leading to a broader range of sedative and hypnotic effects.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Eszopiclone is rapidly absorbed from the GI tract. Peak plasma concentrations occur within 1 hour of a single oral dose. The presence of food reduces the amount of drug absorbed and increases the time to achieve maximum concentration, resulting in delayed sleep onset.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    Eszopiclone is extensively metabolized in the liver, and the pharmacokinetic parameters of eszopiclone are altered with changes in hepatic function. In patients with severe hepatic impairment, eszopiclone AUC is 2 times higher than in normal controls; Cmax and half-life are unchanged. The maximum daily dose in patients with severe hepatic impairment is 2 mg/day; however, dosage adjustments are not necessary in those with mild to moderate hepatic impairment.
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    The pharmacokinetics of eszopiclone were studied in 24 patients with mild, moderate, or severe renal impairment. AUC and Cmax were similar in the patients compared with demographically matched healthy control subjects. No dose adjustment is necessary in patients with renal impairment, as less than 10% of the orally administered eszopiclone dose is excreted in the urine as parent drug. Eszopiclone is not removed by hemodialysis.
    Name
    Geriatric
    Description
    The pharmacokinetic parameters of eszopiclone are altered with advanced age. In elderly patients the mean half-life is increased to 9 hours and the AUC is increased by 41%. Therefore, the maximum daily dose of eszopiclone in elderly patients should not exceed 2 mg/day.
    Name
    Gender Differences
    Description
    There are no pharmacokinetic differences based upon gender.
  Text
  Eszopiclone is administered orally. It is 52% to 59% protein bound. Hepatic oxidation and demethylation occur via CYP3A4 and CYP2E1 metabolism; two primary metabolites are produced, one of which has substantially lower potency than eszopiclone and the other is essentially inactive. The elimination half-life is approximately 6 hours in patients with normal hepatic function. Up to 75% of an oral dose is excreted as metabolites in the urine and less than 10% is excreted as unchanged drug.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes:CYP3A4, CYP2E1
CYP3A4 is a major metabolic pathway for the elimination of eszopiclone. In pharmacokinetic studies, eszopiclone did not show any inhibitory potential of CYP450 isoenzymes.
