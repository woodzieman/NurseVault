Drug Class: Chloroquine is a 4-aminoquinoline anti-protozoal agent indicated for the treatment and prophylaxis of susceptible malaria strains and for the treatment of extraintestinal amebiasis.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 1,000 mg/dose (600 mg base/dose) PO for malaria up to a total of 2.5 g (1.5 g base) PO in 48 hours; 500 mg/week (300 mg base/week) PO for malaria prophylaxis; 1,000 mg/day (600 mg base/day) PO for other indications.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): blurred vision, corneal deposits, corneal opacification, macular degeneration, night blindness, retinopathy, scotomata, visual impairment, agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, hemolysis, hemolytic anemia, neutropenia, pancytopenia, thrombocytopenia, hyporeflexia, myasthenia, myopathy, weakness, av block, bundle-branch block, cardiomyopathy, hypotension, qt prolongation, torsade de pointes, ventricular fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatitis, agitation

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Chloroquine is only indicated for malaria and extraintestinal amebiasis infection. Antimicrobial resistance to chloroquine therapy is widespread. Resistance is particularly prominent in various parts of the world including sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, the Indian subcontinent, and over large portions of South America, including the Amazon basin. Chloroquine should not be used for treatment of P. falciparum infection acquired in areas of chloroquine resistance or malaria occurring in patients where chloroquine prophylaxis has failed.
 
Chloroquine use is contraindicated in patients with ocular disease, especially those who have retinal or visual field changes, because the drug can cause corneal opacities, keratopathy, or retinopathy. In patients with normal vision, these effects are generally dose-related and, except for retinopathy, are generally reversible on discontinuation of the drug. Retinopathy can lead to blindness and can progress even after the drug is discontinued. For the treatment of acute malaria, healthcare providers should weigh the benefit of treatment with chloroquine versus the risks to the patient. The use of chloroquine should be approached with caution in patients with Fabry disease, particularly those with ocular symptoms. The drug can cause a keratopathy that is clinically and ultrastructurally indistinguishable from keratopathy caused by Fabry disease; this drug-induced keratopathy is reversible with drug cessation. In addition, chloroquine poses a theoretical risk of decreased intracellular α-galactosidase A activity in Fabry disease patients. Chloroquine has been reported to induce clinical symptoms that mimic those of Fabry disease, including formation of inclusion bodies that are biochemically and ultrastructurally similar in most of the cells affected by Fabry disease (e.g., striated muscle, smooth muscle, etc.). The distinguishing factor is that the ultrastructural features of chloroquine toxicity in striated muscle, curvilinear bodies, are not present in renal cells.
 
Chloroquine is contraindicated in patients with known chloroquine hypersensitivity, or with a known allergy to 4-aminoquinolines. Patients with hydroxychloroquine hypersensitivity may have cross sensitivity to chloroquine.
 
Chloroquine is considered to be associated with an increased risk for QT prolongation and torsades de pointes (TdP). Chloroquine should be avoided where possible in patients with QT prolongation (congenital or acquired QT prolongation syndromes) or patients with a history of torsade de pointes. The need to co-administer chloroquine with drugs known to prolong the QT interval should be done with a careful assessment of risks versus benefits, and should be avoided when possible.
 
Chloroquine should be used with extreme caution in patients with psoriasis because it has been shown to precipitate severe attacks.
 
Chloroquine should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease or alcoholism because the drug is metabolized in the liver, accumulation can occur producing toxic effects. Patients receiving other hepatotoxic drugs also should be treated with caution.
 
Chloroquine should be used with caution in patients with GI disease. Chloroquine can cause gastric irritation and further exacerbate these conditions.
 
Chloroquine also can cause blood dyscrasias and anemias, so it should be used with caution in patients with preexisting hematological disease. Regular complete blood counts should be made for patients on long-term therapy, and the drug should be discontinued immediately if abnormalities appear (e.g., agranulocytosis).
 
Chloroquine should be used with caution in patients with dental disease. Chloroquine can cause myelosuppression and there may be an increased risk of infection. Dental work should be performed prior to initiating chloroquine therapy or deferred until blood counts return to normal. Patients should be instructed on proper oral hygiene.
 
Chloroquine should be used with caution in patients with neurological disease including preexisting hearing impairment or seizure disorder. Polyneuritis, ototoxicity, seizures, and neuromyopathy have occurred with chloroquine therapy. Symptoms of muscle weakness and response of knee and ankle reflexes should be investigated regularly. If muscle weakness or any defects in hearing occur during chloroquine therapy, the drug should be discontinued immediately and the patient observed closely.
 
Chloroquine can exacerbate porphyria or glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency (G6PD deficiency) and should be used with caution in patients with these conditions.
 
Chloroquine crosses the placenta, but the potential damage to the mother from malaria is greater than the drug's risk to the fetus. Weekly prophylactic doses appear to have minimal adverse effects when administered during pregnancy. The clinician must weigh risks to benefits of treatment and consider the drug's potential to remain in the body for several months after discontinuation of therapy.
 
Chloroquine is excreted into breast milk. Because infants and children are particularly sensitive to the effects of chloroquine, risks to benefits must be considered when using chloroquine in breast-feeding mothers.
 
Clinical studies of chloroquine did not include sufficient numbers of subjects >= 65 years of age to determine whether they respond differently from younger subjects. However, chloroquine is known to be substantially excreted by the kidney, and the risk of toxic reactions to this drug may be greater in patients with impaired renal function. Because elderly patients are more likely to have decreased renal function, care should be taken in dose selection. Monitoring of renal function may be useful.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Chloroquine is also contraindicated in individuals with known hypersensitivity to 4-aminoquinoline compounds.
    Headline
    Asian patients, renal failure, renal impairment, retinal disease, visual disturbance
    General Information
    Use of chloroquine for indications other than acute malaria is contraindicated in individuals with retinal disease or visual disturbance of any etiology, as irreversible retinal damage has been reported. Risk factors for chloroquine-associated retinopathy include daily doses exceeding 2.3 mg/kg of actual body weight, treatment duration longer than 5 years, renal impairment or renal failure, concomitant use of tamoxifen, and preexisting macular disease. Perform a baseline ophthalmological exam, consisting of best corrected distance visual acuity (BCVA), automated threshold visual field (VF) of the central 10 degrees (with retesting if an abnormality is noted), and spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT), within the first year of treatment. In Asian patients, retinal toxicity may first be noticed outside the macula, and VF testing should be performed in the central 24 degrees instead of the central 10 degrees. For patients with significant risk factors, monitoring should include annual examinations which include BCVA, VF, and SD-OCT. For individuals without significant risk factors, annual exams can usually be deferred until 5 years of treatment. Discontinue chloroquine if ocular toxicity is suspected, and monitor the patient closely as retinal changes and visual disturbances may progress after cessation of therapy.
    Headline
    bradycardia, cardiac disease, cardiomyopathy, congenital long QT syndrome, coronary artery disease, females, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, QT prolongation, ventricular arrhythmias
    General Information
    Avoid use of chloroquine in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk. Additional risk factors may include cardiac disease and a history of ventricular arrhythmias.
    Headline
    psoriasis
    General Information
    Avoid chloroquine in individuals with psoriasis unless the potential benefit clearly outweighs the risk, as it may precipitate a severe exacerbation of the disease.
    Headline
    alcohol use disorder, hepatic disease
    General Information
    Use chloroquine with caution in individuals with hepatic disease, alcohol use disorder, or those receiving other hepatotoxic medications. Chloroquine is metabolized in the liver, and impaired hepatic function may result in drug accumulation and an increased risk of toxicity.
    Headline
    hearing impairment, seizure disorder
    General Information
    Use chloroquine with caution in individuals with a seizure disorder, as it may lower the seizure threshold and increase the risk of seizures. Additionally, use caution in individuals with preexisting hearing impairment, as chloroquine may potentially worsen auditory dysfunction.
    Headline
    porphyria
    General Information
    Avoid chloroquine in individuals with porphyria unless the potential benefit clearly outweighs the risk, as it may exacerbate the condition.
    Headline
    G6PD deficiency
    General Information
    Administer chloroquine with caution in individuals with G6PD deficiency due to the risk of hemolysis. Monitor for signs of hemolytic anemia, particularly in individuals receiving concomitant medications known to cause hemolysis.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Use cautious dose selection for chloroquine in geriatric adults due to a greater likelihood of having decreased renal function and increased risk of adverse reactions. Geriatric adults are at increased risk for QT prolongation.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Weigh the benefit of chloroquine use in the treatment and prophylaxis of malaria against the potential risk to the fetus. Consider the drug's potential to remain in the body for several months after treatment discontinuation. When used at recommended doses for the prophylaxis and treatment of malaria, human observational studies and a meta-analysis including a small number of prospective studies have shown no increase in the rate of birth defects or spontaneous abortions associated with chloroquine. Guidelines recommend chloroquine as an option for the treatment of acute malaria and for prophylaxis in pregnant individuals during all trimesters. Chloroquine crosses the placenta, but the potential risk to the patient from malaria is greater than the drug's risk to the fetus. Weekly prophylactic doses appear to have minimal adverse effects when given during pregnancy. Animal studies showed embryo-fetal developmental toxicity at doses 3 to 16 times the maximum recommended therapeutic dose, based on body surface area. Preclinical data have shown a potential risk of genotoxicity in some test systems. Autoradiographic studies have shown accumulation in the eyes and ears when chloroquine is administered at the start or end of gestation in animal studies.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with chloroquine. In animal studies, testosterone levels and the weight of testes, epididymis, seminal vesicles, and prostate decreased after 30 days of oral treatment. Discuss the potential impact on male fertility with patients.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use chloroquine with caution during breast-feeding. Chloroquine is present in breast milk. The relative infant dose is estimated to be 0.68% to 7.71%.  When given once weekly, the amount of drug is not sufficient to harm the infant, nor is it sufficient to protect the child from malaria. There is no information on the daily use of chloroquine during breast-feeding.

Interactions
Acarbose: (Minor) Careful monitoring of blood glucose is recommended when chloroquine and antidiabetic agents are coadministered. Hypoglycemia has been reported in patients treated concomitantly with chloroquine and an antidiabetic agent. However, alpha-glucosidase inhibitors are not usually associated with hypoglycemia due to their mechanism and use to lower postprandial blood glucose.
Adagrasib: (Major) Concomitant use of adagrasib and chloroquine increases the risk of QT/QTc prolongation and torsade de pointes (TdP). Avoid concomitant use if possible, especially in patients with additional risk factors for TdP. Consider taking steps to minimize the risk for QT/QTc interval prolongation and TdP, such as electrolyte monitoring and repletion and ECG monitoring, if concomitant use is necessary.
Alfuzosin: (Major) Avoid coadministration of chloroquine with alfuzosin due to the increased risk of QT prolongation. If use together is necessary, obtain an ECG at baseline to assess initial QT interval and determine frequency of subsequent ECG monitoring, avoid any non-essential QT prolonging drugs, and correct electrolyte imbalances. Chloroquine is associated with an increased risk of QT prolongation and torsade de pointes (TdP); the risk of QT prolongation is increased with higher chloroquine doses. Alfuzosin may prolong the QT interval in a dose-dependent manner.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Visual impairment occurs most often following long-term or high-dose therapy with chloroquine and other 4-aminoquinolones. The effects can include blurred vision; difficulty in focusing or accommodation; corneal deposits; nyctalopia (night blindness); retinal changes; patchy retinal pigmentation (which can lead to blindness); optic atrophy; macular lesions; and scotomatous vision with field defects of paracentral, pericentral ring types, and typically temporal scotomata, e.g., difficulty in reading with words tending to disappear, seeing half an object, misty vision, and fog before the eyes. Reversible corneal opacification has been reported. With early detection, most adverse effects are reversible, but retinal changes can be permanent. Serious retinopathy can continue to progress even after the drug has been discontinued. Regular ophthalmological examinations should be undertaken for all patients receiving long-term therapy and the drug discontinued at the first sign of any adverse reaction.
 
There have been rare reports of erythema multiforme, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, exfoliative dermatitis, and similar desquamation-type events associated with chloroquine use. Pruritus and pigmentary skin discoloration can occur. Pruritus is not necessarily accompanied by rash and is not alleviated by antihistamines. Onset is generally within 6—48 hours of ingestion of the drug and affects the whole body. Various dermatoses, which can be aggravated by exposure to ultraviolet light, have been observed in patients during chloroquine therapy. It is not known if these reactions are true photosensitivity reactions. An acute attack of psoriasis can be precipitated by chloroquine in predisposed patients. Pleomorphic skin eruptions, lichen planus-like eruptions, urticaria, anaphylactic/anaphylactoid reactions (including angioedema), alopecia, and hair discoloration have been reported. Occasionally, chloroquine can result in bleaching of all body hair. This effect is more likely to occur following several months of treatment.
 
Chloroquine has been associated with acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP). The nonfollicular, pustular, erythematous rash starts suddenly, is associated with fever above 38 degrees C, and is distinct from pustular psoriasis, although biopsy results in each reveal spongiform subcorneal pustules. Drugs are the main cause of AGEP. A period of 2—3 weeks after an inciting drug exposure appears necessary for a first episode of AGEP. Unintentional reexposure may cause a second episode within 2 days. Clinical presentation is diverse with cutaneous lesions beyond erythema and pustules present in half of the cases. For example, bullous lesions, edema, purpura, pruritus, and mucosal erosions are possible. The mean duration of the pustules is 9.7 days followed by an annular desquamation, as long as the causative drug or factor is discontinued. The physiopathological mechanisms of AGEP have not been determined but the pathological criteria of edema, leukocytoclastic vasculitis, eosinophil exocytosis, and keratinocyte focal necrosis are distinctive. Pustule confluence or very small pustules may lead a clinician to make an incorrect diagnosis of TEN, of drug-induced erythroderma, or of staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome.
 
Hematological adverse reactions of chloroquine include agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, pancytopenia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia. Because of the serious nature of possible reactions, any signs of abnormal bleeding or bruising, fever, or sore throat should be investigated promptly and the patient carefully monitored.
 
Skeletal muscle myopathy or neuromyopathy generally results from prolonged treatment with chloroquine and is reversible. This leads to progressive weakness (myasthenia) and atrophy of proximal muscle groups which may be associated with mild sensory changes, depressed tendon reflexes (hyporeflexia), and abnormal nerve conduction. Periodic examination of patients should be undertaken for response to knee and ankle reflexes, and any signs of muscle weakness should be investigated. The drug should be discontinued if myopathy develops.
 
Hearing loss and tinnitus may become apparent only after discontinuation of the drug and is generally associated only with high doses of chloroquine. Patients with preexisting hearing difficulties are most at risk.
 
Cardiovascular adverse reactions associated with chloroquine are rare and include cardiomyopathy, ECG changes (particularly, inversion or flattening of the T-wave with widening of the QRS complex), and hypotension. Long-term therapy or high doses are mainly responsible for changes.
 
Adverse GI effects include hepatitis, elevated hepatic enzymes, nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, anorexia, and epigastric distress. These effects are associated with oral administration and can be minimized by taking chloroquine with food.
 
Nervous system adverse reactions can include drowsiness, headache (usually mild and transient), convulsive seizures, neuritis (polyneuritis), and neuropsychiatric changes including psychosis, delirium, personality changes, anxiety, agitation, insomnia, hallucinations, confusion, and depression. These effects are usually mild.
 
Chloroquine is considered to be associated with an increased risk for QT prolongation and torsades de pointes (TdP). Chloroquine should be avoided where possible in patients with QT prolongation (congenital or acquired QT prolongation syndromes) or patients with a history of torsade de pointes. The need to co-administer chloroquine with drugs known to prolong the QT interval should be done with a careful assessment of risks versus benefits, and should be avoided when possible.

Description
  Text
  Chloroquine is a 4-aminoquinoline anti-protozoal agent indicated for the treatment and prophylaxis of susceptible malaria strains and for the treatment of extraintestinal amebiasis. Chloroquine is not active against gametocytes and the exoerythrocytic forms including the hypnozoite stage of the Plasmodium parasites. Resistance to chloroquine is widespread. Irreversible retinal damage has been observed with use, and postmarketing cases of life-threatening and fatal cardiomyopathy, including ventricular arrhythmias and torsade de pointes (TdP), have been reported.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Chloroquine, a 4-aminoquinoline, is an anti-protozoal agent. The precise mechanism is unknown. Chloroquine may exert its effect against Plasmodium species by concentrating in the acid vesicles of the parasite and by inhibiting polymerization of heme. It can also inhibit certain enzymes by its interaction with DNA. Chloroquine is not active against gametocytes and the exoerythrocytic forms, including the hypnozoite stage (P. vivax and P. ovale) of the Plasmodium parasites. Organisms with reduced susceptibilities to hydroxychloroquine also show reduced susceptibilities to chloroquine.
 
Although the mechanisms underlying the antiinflammatory and immunomodulatory effects of chloroquine are unknown, several possible mechanisms of action have been proposed. It is unclear if these mechanisms work similarly for rheumatic and autoimmune diseases. Potential mechanisms include reduced cytokine production, inhibition of immune effector cells, inhibition of platelet function, protection of the cell surface from external disturbances, competitive binding to nucleic acid ligands or toll-like receptors (TLRs), interference with lysosomal function, reduction of leakage of lysosomal enzymes, and interference with endosomal NADPH oxidase (NOX).

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    After oral administration, chloroquine is rapidly and almost completely absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. The Tmax is 2.7 to 6.9 hours with a Cmax of 283 to 1,430 ng/mL and an AUC of 8.2 to 140 mcg x hour/mL. A single study showed that the AUC in patients with malaria was higher than in normal volunteers (281 vs. 122 mcg x mL/L x hour).
  Text
  Chloroquine is administered orally. It is widely distributed into body tissues, with higher concentrations in the liver, kidneys, spleen, and lungs. Leukocytes also concentrate the drug. Smaller amounts of the drug are found in the brain and spinal cord. Cells containing melanin in the eyes and skin bind strongly to chloroquine. The drug also concentrates in erythrocytes and is bound to platelets and granulocytes. It is about 55% bound to plasma protein.
 
Excretion of chloroquine is largely through urine, but this is a slow process and may be increased by acidification of the urine. Chloroquine undergoes appreciable degradation in the body, and the major metabolite is desethylchloroquine. Slightly more than half of a dose is excreted in urine as unchanged drug and about 25% as the major metabolite; bisdesethylchloroquine and other metabolic products are found in small amounts. A small portion of the unabsorbed drug is excreted in the feces. Elimination appears to take place in a biphasic manner. The elimination half-life is 108 to 291 hours.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2C8, CYP2D6, CYP3A4, P-gp
In vitro data suggest that chloroquine is metabolized primarily by CYP2C8 and CYP3A4, and to a much lesser extent, by CYP2D6. It has also been shown to be an inhibitor of the drug transporter P-glycoprotein (P-gp).

  Renal Impairment
  CrCl 10 mL/minute or more: No dosage adjustment necessary.
CrCl less than 10 mL/minute: Decrease dose by 50%.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis:
Decrease dose by 50%.
 
Peritoneal dialysis:
Decrease dose by 50%.
 
Continuous renal replacement therapy:
No dosage adjustment necessary.
