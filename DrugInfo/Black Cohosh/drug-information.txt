Products containing black cohosh are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. The FDA does not recognize black cohosh as 'GRAS' (generally safe and effective) for any purpose at this time. Consumers should be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products. The German Commission E has only approved alcoholic extracts of black cohosh of a certain potency. Tyler recommends that females not use this herb continuously for longer than 6 months since no long-term toxicity studies have been conducted. This caution is consistent with guidelines for conventional hormone-replacement therapy in females. Clinical evaluation of menopausal patients every 3—6 months is recommended.Black Cohosh is contraindicated for use in patients with known hypersensitivity to any component of these products. Some black cohosh formulations contain tartrazine dye and can cause allergic responses in patients with a history of tartrazine dye hypersensitivity. Although the incidence of this dye hypersensitivity is low, it can be serious. Because FD&C yellow No. 5 (tartrazine) dye sensitivity may occur more frequently in those patients with salicylate hypersensitivity, black cohosh products containing tartrazine should be used cautiously in this population. Allergic-type reactions, including bronchial asthma, may occur in certain susceptible individuals.Some black cohosh preparations are formulated in combination with soy isoflavones. Use black cohosh combinations that contain soy or soybean components cautiously in those patients who have soya lecithin hypersensitivity.Women already on female hormonal regimens for endometriosis, infertility, or uterine leiomyomata (fibroids) should consult their health care professional prior to use of black cohosh. It is possible that black cohosh may interfere with the appropriate therapeutic action of some prescribed medications for these problems.Roughly 3% of breast-cancer patients report the use of black cohosh as an adjunct to their conventional chemotherapy. Patients with estrogen-responsive carcinomas (e.g., breast cancer, ovarian cancer, etc.) should discuss black cohosh with their health care provider prior to use; no definitive, long-term controlled or epidemiologic human studies exist regarding this herb's effects on these tissues. Certain extracts of black cohosh have inhibited the growth of T-47D and MCF-7 human breast cancer cells in vitro; however, translation of these results to in vivo use is not possible at this time. The effects of black cohosh on these cell lines continues to be an active area of research. No data exists regarding black cohosh's effects on gynecological cancers (e.g., cervical cancer, endometrial cancer, uterine cancer, vaginal cancer, etc.).Use black cohosh with caution in those patients with hypertension or cardiac disease; the vasodilatory effects of black cohosh may affect blood pressure control.Black cohosh should be considered to be classified in pregnancy category X for use during the first, second, and the majority of the third trimester of pregnancy. The potential effects on the endocrine cycle, particularly on luteinizing hormone (LH), could potentially harm the fetus. In addition, the effects of black cohosh on uterine contractions could be detrimental to the maintenance of the pregnancy (i.e., may contribute to spontaneous abortion risk). Black cohosh has received widespread use historically as a part of combined herbal regimens used to augment labor and obstetric delivery in normal term pregnancies; however, no controlled clinical trials have evaluated black cohosh's use for this purpose. In February 2000, the FDA released a warning letter to dietary supplement manufacturers that advised against making any claims related to pregnancy on their products. The letter was released following concerns of the potential for birth defects from the use of untested products. Complications in neonates have been reported (see Adverse Reactions). Maternal complications have also been noted in select cases. When used in the last 5 weeks of gestation, black cohosh may induce precipitous labor. It is unknown if this herb is excreted in breast milk.Because of the potential effects on luteinizing hormone (LH) and estrogen receptors, black cohosh is not recommended for use in children or adolescents under the age of 18 years. The effect of black cohosh on prepubertal and pubertal maturation is uncertain.Prior to undergoing surgery, the surgeon and anesthesiologist should be notified of the use of black cohosh. Some herbal products have been reported to induce changes in heart rate or blood pressure during surgery, or intensify the effects of narcotic drugs and anesthetic agents. It may be wise to discontinue this herb 2—3 weeks prior to an elective procedure.Precise clinical metabolism and pharmacokinetic data are not available for black cohosh, therefore it should be used with caution in those patients with pre-existing hepatic disease or renal disease. There have been rare case reports of hepatic adverse events associated with black cohosh. Discontinue use in patients presenting with symptoms of hepatitis or jaundice until the cause has been evaluated.

Description
  Text
  Cimicifuga racemosa is commonly known as black cohosh, and is sometimes referred to as black snakeroot, squaw root, or bugbane. The part of the black cohosh plant used in herbal preparations is the root or rhizome (underground stem). In herbal lore, black cohosh is described as an 'emmenagogue', an agent used to stimulate menstrual and uterine flow, tone, and activity. It has several historical uses in women's health. Native Americans used black cohosh dried rhizome and roots to aid childbirth, and treat female complaints and snakebites; black cohosh was an official drug in the U.S. Pharmacopoeia from 1830 to 1926. Black cohosh was one of the ingredients in the combination herb product known as Lydia E. Pinkham's Vegetable Compound of the late 1800s and early 1900s; the product claimed to treat a multitude of female health problems, including infertility. In February 2000, the FDA released a warning letter to dietary supplement manufacturers that advised against making any claims related to use of black cohosh during pregnancy due to expressed concerns of the potential for birth defects from the use of untested products. Black cohosh dietary supplements marketed in the U.S. are primarily promoted for the relief of symptoms related to menopause and perimenopause. Germany's Commission E has found a standardized, proprietary extracts of black cohosh effective for the treatment of dysmenorrhea, premenstrual syndrome, and climacteric neurovegetative ailments. A 2017 review of a black cohosh standardized extract supported use for menopausal symptoms such as hot flashes and sleep disturbances, and included data from 60-years of medicinal use in Europe and many clinical studies with this extract since the 1980s. However, the North American Menopause Society (NAMS) has refrained from recommending black cohosh in the non-hormonal management of vasomotor symptoms (VMS) related to menopause and considers available safety and efficacy data inconclusive. Possible hepatotoxicity due to black cohosh has been reported; however, an association of the herb to liver problems is not confirmed. After examining reported cases, the U.S. Pharmacopeial Convention's Dietary Supplements- Botanicals Expert Committee found only 30 reports possibly related to black cohosh; however, patients are advised to discontinue use and consult a health care practitioner if they develop symptoms of liver dysfunction, such as abdominal pain, dark urine, or jaundice. Data from 60-years of use of a standardized black cohosh extract in over 12,000 women in clinical trials revealed no evidence of hepatotoxicity, emphasizing the need for patients to select a black cohosh product from those manufacturers that adhere to good manufacturing practices.
 
NOTE: In the U.S., herbal products and dietary supplements are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for these products as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since dietary supplements are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The active ingredients in black cohosh extract are unknown, and its mechanism of action is unclear. At one time it was thought to be estrogenic, with in vitro and in vivo assays indicating estrogen-like activity. Other studies indicate activity similar to selective estrogen-receptor modulators (SERMs) or modulation of serotonergic pathways, as well as antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. In one controlled clinical study of 152 postmenopausal women, an extract of black cohosh did not change serum levels of estradiol, LH, FSH, sex-hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), or prolactin. Black cohosh did however, suppress hot flashes, improve menopausal symptoms, and decrease vaginal dryness in these women. Black cohosh does not appear to help preserve bone mineral density in menopausal women when compared to exercise alone.
 
There are several triterpene glycosides of black cohosh rhizome that may be a source of pharmacologic activity in vitro. The triterpenoids include actein, cimifugoside, deoxyacetylacteol, and 23-epi-26-deoxyactein (also known as 27-deoxyactein). Marketed proprietary products usually standardize dietary supplements to these glycosides, and particularly the 23-epi-26-deoxyactein component. Other constituents include various acids, including acetic and salicylic acids, and a resinous substance known as cimicifugin.

Pharmacokinetics
  Text
  Black cohosh is administered orally. Clinical variability may be expected due to bioavailability and purity differences among products. In producing standardized products, it is common practice to chemically standardize black cohosh dietary supplements to the characteristic spiroketal triterpene glycosides in black cohosh; the extracts are usually standardized with reference to 23-epi-26-deoxyactein (also known as 27-deoxyactein). The pharmacokinetics of black cohosh constituents have been described from a phase I study. Following oral administration of a standardized 75% ethanolic extract of black cohosh roots/rhizomes (32, 64 or 128 mg/dose and confirmed to contain 1.4, 2.8 or 5.6 mg 23-epi-26-deoxyactein per dose) to 15 women (n = 5 per group; 3 dosage groups), the 23-epi-26-deoxyactein was detected during the first 24 hours of dosing in all serum and urine samples from every subject regardless of dosage. The maximum concentration (Cmax) was attained between 2 and 2.9 hours (Tmax) for all 3 dosages. The peak serum concentration of 23-epi-26-deoxyactein was dose-dependent and ranged from 2.2 ng/mL to 12.4 ng/mL. The exposure (AUC) for each of the 3 dosages increased linearly with dosage. No metabolites of 23-epi-26-deoxyactein were detected, and the intact compound was excreted in the urine. However, less than 0.01% of any dose was recovered in urine during the first 24 hours. There was no saturation of the urinary excretion pathway with increasing dose. Instead of urinary excretion, most 23-epi-26-deoxyactein was probably excreted intact in bile and degraded in the gastrointestinal tract. The 23-epi-26-deoxyactein half-life observed in this study was approximately 2 to 3 hours. The half-life of 23-epi-26-deoxyactein was nearly constant across all dosages and was 2.1 +/- 0.4 hours, 2.7 +/- 0.4 hours and 3 +/- 1 hours following administration of 1.4, 2.8 and 5.6 mg, respectively.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes and drug transporters: Uncertain; possibly CYP3A4 and OATP2B1
There was no phase I or II metabolism detected of 23-epi-26-deoxyactein following the administration of black cohosh. Therefore, other drugs are unlikely to have an inhibitory or inducing effect on the metabolism of black cohosh. Certain triterpene glycosides isolated from black cohosh modestly inhibited human CYP3A4 in vitro. In vitro research shows that black cohosh modestly inhibits organic anion-transporting polypeptide substrate 2B1 (OATP2B1); inhibition may reduce the oral bioavailability of oral drugs that are substrates of this drug transporter in the small intestine. However, evidence of in vitro inhibition may not accurately predict in vivo effects observed in humans. In vitro models have suggested that black cohosh might weakly inhibit CYP2D6; however, an in vivo study in young healthy adults has determined that a standardized extract exerted no significant effects on CYP2D6 activity.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Patients with existing hepatic impairment or any liver disorder should use black cohosh with caution. If signs or symptoms suggestive of hepatic dysfunction occur during black cohosh use, the patient should discontinue the supplement and contact a health care provider for evaluation.
  Renal Impairment
  No data are available regarding use of black cohosh in patients with renal impairment.
