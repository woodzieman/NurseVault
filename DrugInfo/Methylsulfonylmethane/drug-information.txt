In addition, it may be prudent to avoid methylsulfonylmethane, MSM in patients with active bleeding, including GI bleeding, intracranial bleeding, retinal bleeding, or retroperitoneal bleeding.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Adverse reactions to methylsulfonylmethane, MSM appear to be rare. The absence of alterations in intrinsic cerebral metabolites, such as myo-inositol, phosphocreatine, N-acetylaspartate, glutamine/glutamate, and choline, suggests that there are no adverse neurochemical effects in humans. The absence of reported toxicity should be interpreted in conjunction with the limited availability of published human data.In a clinical study comparing MSM, glucosamine, the combination, or placebo, the only reported adverse events were mild gastrointestinal disturbances; diarrhea was the most common adverse event reported, with the highest incidence occurring in the glucosamine only group. Furthermore, no patients discontinued the study due to adverse events. In a study comparing MSM 3000 mg PO bid to placebo, the gastrointestinal adverse events of bloating (25% for MSM vs. 18% for placebo) and constipation (17% for MSM vs. 18% for placebo) were reported in patients assigned to either drug. Fatigue was reported more commonly in patients taking placebo (17% for MSM vs. 27% for placebo), while insomnia was reported more commonly in patients taking MSM (17% for MSM vs. 9% for placebo). Changes in concentration, headache, and indigestion were each reported by 1 patient in each group. Because of the overall small numbers of patients in this clinical trial (n=118), differences in the incidence of these adverse reactions were not statistically significant.Increased bleeding from concomitant anticoagulant drugs described as increased bruising (ecchymosis) and blood in the stool have been reported in patients taking methylsulfonylmethane, MSM. Although these effects have not been confirmed in published medical literature or during clinical studies, it may be prudent to monitor patients for increased bleeding, especially in those patients taking other drugs know to have anticoagulant or antiplatelet activity (see Drug Interactions).

Description
  Text
  Methylsulfonylmethane (MSM) is a naturally occurring, organic sulfur-containing compound. Synonyms for MSM include methyl sulfone, and dimethyl sulfone (DMSO2). Methylsulfonylmethane is derived from dimethylsulfide (DMS), which is a result of oceanic phytoplankton production. When DMS oxidizes photochemically, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and MSM are produced. MSM contains roughly 34% elemental sulfur, and because sulfur bonds are essential structural features in all connective tissues, methylsulfonylmethane, MSM has been claimed to have the potential to benefit people with muscle and joint-cartilage conditions. MSM is most often promoted as a dietary supplement for the treatment of osteoarthritis (OA). Most published clinical studies of MSM for OA have suffered from methodological defects but have reported small improvements in pain and physical function scores; these improvements are thought to be unlikely to be clinically significant for patients. Topical MSM products are also marketed for osteoarthritis; however, it is unknown if MSM is absorbed locally or systemically, and no published human data suggest the efficacy of topical MSM for any use. Dietary supplementation with MSM remains of interest for sports medicine applications; it is postulated that MSM may attenuate oxidative muscle stress (e.g., myalgia, muscle inflammation) following high-intensity, long-duration exercise (e.g., marathon running), but studies are not sufficient to definitively determine efficacy. Oral MSM has also been studied for relief of seasonal allergic rhinitis; data are very preliminary but suggest improvements in nasal symptoms with daily use. MSM is naturally found in small quantities in some grains, fruits, vegetables, tea, milk, and coffee, but has no necessary nutritive value. In the U.S., methylsulfonylmethane is sold as a non-prescription oral dietary supplement and as a topical product. MSM is generally recognized as safe or "GRAS" per the FDA. However, MSM is not regulated as a drug and, therefore, is not intended to treat, diagnose, mitigate, prevent, or cure diseases.
 
Methylsulfonylmethane (MSM) is considered a dietary supplement in the U.S., and commercial products are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available since MSM is not regulated as a drug. Rigid quality control standards are not required for dietary supplements, and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The exact mechanism of action of MSM is unknown. Methylsulfonylmethane (MSM) is a sulfur-based nutritional supplement that is purported to have pain and inflammation-reducing effects. Sulfur in the human diet is obtained from ingestion of sulfur-containing amino acids. Methylsulfonylmethane contains 34% elemental sulfur. Sulfur is important for the synthesis of glycosaminoglycans, and sulfate depletion inhibits glycosaminoglycan synthesis in human articular cartilage. Glycosaminoglycans are the structural components of proteoglycans that yield the pressure-resistent properties of articular cartilage. Proteoglycans, along with collagen type II, are the major structural components of articular cartilage. In vitro, a reduction in the sulfate concentration from 0.3 mM (physiological) to 0.2 mM resulted in a 33% reduction in glycosaminoglycan synthesis in one study. Expression of interleukin-1, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and lipopolysaccharide-induced cyclooxygenase 2 is inhibited in vitro by DMSO 1%. Although MSM is a metabolite of DMSO, it is unknown if certain inflammatory mediators are modulated by MSM. In a sports medicine study, the ingestion of MSM had no effect on muscle markers of exercise-related oxidative stress. No alterations from baseline in the plasma IgE or histamine concentrations were found in one study of MSM use in allergic rhinitis.

Pharmacokinetics
  Text
  The dietary supplement methylsulfonylmethane, MSM is typically administered orally. Although topical formulations of MSM are also available, no information is available on the percutaneous transport or systemic availability of MSM by this route of administration. Methylsulfonylmethane is naturally present in the body and is excreted in the urine. Little information regarding how endogenous MSM is distributed or metabolized is available; pharmacokinetic data following the oral supplementation of MSM are also scant.
 
Concentrations of methylsulfonymethane (MSM) in the brains of children, healthy adults, and Alzheimer disease-afflicted adults indicate that MSM does cross the blood-brain barrier; in particular, supplementation of MSM in children and adults results in quantifiable brain concentrations. MSM was detected in the brain of a healthy 62-year-old man that received 2000 mg per 11 kg of body weight for 7 days and 2000 mg daily thereafter. The half-life of MSM in the brain was 7.5 days. However, no evidence of MSM on magnetic resonance spectroscopy was found 6 months after cessation of MSM supplementation in the same individual. In a 20 kg child that took 1250 mg of MSM daily for about a year, a similar concentration of MSM was found in the basal ganglia and white matter. Concentrations of MSM were also detected in the cerebra of 6 adults that took between 1.5 g and 6 g (0.03—0.1 g/kg of body weight) of MSM daily for 5 weeks up to 2 years. Methylsulfonymethane, MSM was detected by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) the day after a single dose of 0.03 g/kg of body weight. The highest concentration was found in a patient with evidence of a prior stroke on MRI. Steady-state cerebral MSM concentrations occurred within 2 weeks in a healthy patient that received 44 mg/kg daily over 2.5 months. Distribution of MSM throughout the brain and brainstem was even with equal concentrations in the gray and white matter. The clinical significance of these findings is not clear.
