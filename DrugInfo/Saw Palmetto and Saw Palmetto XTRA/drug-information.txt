Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Saw palmetto has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration at this time. Products containing saw palmetto are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Consumers should also be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.Benign prostatic hypertrophy is not a self-limiting condition and should not be self-diagnosed. The decision to self-treat symptoms of prostate enlargement with saw palmetto may prevent proper medical treatment. A potential problem with self-treatment could be delay in detection of prostate cancer or other serious urinary tract problem. Patients are advised to see their health care professional for evaluation prior to the use of saw palmetto for BPH. Saw palmetto relieves only the symptoms associated with an enlarged prostate, but does not reduce prostate size. Patients should be advised to continue to consult a health care professional on a regular basis for evaluation of prostate size and treatment response.Saw palmetto is not recommended for use during pregnancy. Clinicians should note that finasteride, a drug for BPH that also inhibits 5-alpha-reductase, is classified as pregnancy category X, due to abnormal development of genitalia that might occur in a male fetus. Such effects have occurred in animals exposed to low doses of finasteride. It is unknown if such a risk would be present with exposure to saw palmetto products. Pregnant women should be cautioned to avoid handling crushed saw palmetto berries or opened capsules as a precautionary measure. There is no objective safety information on the use of saw palmetto in breast-feeding. The use of saw palmetto is discouraged during lactation.Due to lack of scientific data on safety and efficacy, and the potential effects on hormones, saw palmetto is not recommended for use in children or infants.

Pregnancy And Lactation
  Pregnancy
    General Information
    Saw palmetto should be avoided during pregnancy, due to its purported mechanism of action. Clinicians should note that finasteride, a drug for BPH that also inhibits 5-alpha-reductase, is classified as pregnancy category X, due to abnormal development of genitalia that might occur in a male fetus; such effects have occurred in animals exposed to low doses of finasteride. It is unknown if such a risk would be present with exposure to saw palmetto products. Pregnant women should also be cautioned to avoid handling crushed saw palmetto berries or opened capsules as a precautionary measure.
  Breastfeeding
    General Information
    Saw palmetto should be avoided in women who are breast-feeding; the supplement is not usually recommended for consumption by females. It is not known whether saw palmetto is excreted in human milk, and the purported mechanism of action could have potential unwanted effects on infants if ingested. The effects of saw palmetto on infants during breast-feeding cannot be determined.

5-Alpha reductase inhibitors: (Moderate) Saw palmetto may inhibit 5 alpha-reductase, preventing the conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone. This action is similar to the action of 5-alpha reductase inhibitors, such as dutasteride and finasteride. Co-use is likely to be common by patients, but the effects of co-use are not known. In theory, the effects could be additive, but it is not known if the added effects would be beneficial or harmful. Clinicians should be alert for any unusual effects if patients ingest saw palmetto supplements while taking 5-alpha reductase inhibitors.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: Some saw palmetto preparations, are a combination of several herbs. Only adverse reactions pertaining to saw palmetto are discussed in this monograph.Adverse reactions to herbal products are not always documented. Clinical trials to date have been of short duration and have noted that adverse events to saw palmetto have been mild and comparable to both placebo and finasteride. Erectile dysfunction and impotence (erectile dysfunction) appeared to be less common with saw palmetto (1.1%) than with finasteride (4.9%) in a recent meta-analysis of available trials; this difference was clinically significant. Based on in vitro data, saw palmetto does not appear to impair sperm viability or activity. Gastrointestinal disturbances (e.g., dyspepsia) are reported in < 4% of patients on saw palmetto, and can be minimized by administering oral doses with food. Headache and diarrhea have also been reported with short-term use.Acute cholestatic hepatitis in a 65 year-old male has been associated with a combination product (Prostata by Gero Vita International) containing several herbs, one of which is Serenoa serrulata (saw palmetto). While the presumed offending agent was saw palmetto, the potential effects of the other herbs in the product cannot be discounted. Clinicians should note that both estrogens and antiandrogens (e.g., flutamide, a testosterone receptor antagonist) have been shown to be hepatotoxic; the antiandrogenic effects of saw palmetto may mimick the actions of these drugs. Clinicians should be alert for the development of cholestasis in patients taking saw palmetto.One case of gynecomastia related to saw palmetto use has been reported to the FDA special nutritional adverse event monitoring system. The relationship of this event to the use of the herb cannot be established at this time, and details regarding the case are unavailable. Given the folk use of saw palmetto for breast enlargement, clinicians should be aware of this potential side effect.

Description
  Text
  NOTE: In the US, nutraceuticals are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for nutraceuticals as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since nutraceuticals are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products. Monographs on nutraceuticals are included in CP when reliable clinical data are available. The information presented below is condensed from the best clinical data we could find.Description: Saw palmetto is derived from the partially dried, ripe fruit of Serenoa repens, a palm plant of the family Arecaceae that grows from South Carolina to Florida. The medicinal use of this herb has been documented since the 1800's. Native Americans used the berries to treat testicular atrophy, infertility, and inflammation of the prostate. It is reported that native American women used the herb for painful menstruation, problems with lactation, and to induce breast enlargement. Placebo-controlled, double-blind studies of its use in benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) have been carried out in > 2000 patients in Germany. A 1998 meta-analysis of published trials concluded that compared to finasteride, saw palmetto appears to produce similar improvements (28%) in urinary tract symptoms and flow measures. Men taking saw palmetto were twice as likely to report improvement in BPH symptoms versus those taking placebo. There is no evidence that treatment with saw palmetto decreases prostate enlargement. Due to the short duration (usually less than 3 months) of these studies, the ability of saw palmetto to prevent complications of BPH (acute urinary retention or the need for surgical intervention) is unknown. Saw palmetto was listed as an official drug in the United States Pharmacopeia from 1906—1917. PharmaPrint, Inc. submitted an IND to the FDA in July 1997 to study saw palmetto, known as PPRT-321, for the treatment of BPH. A randomized, placebo controlled, phase II pilot investigation of PPRT-321 was completed in March 1999; the results in 50 men suggested that the product is safe and that PPRT-321 is efficacious for the symptoms of BPH. A double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial published in 2006 evaluated saw palmetto in patients with moderate to severe BPH as defined by the American Urological Association Symptom Index (AUASI). Using a single standardized product, patients were randomized to receive saw palmetto 160 mg PO bid (n=113) or placebo (n=112) for one year. The primary objective, as measured by the AUASI and peak urinary flow rates, was to determine if saw palmetto reduced the symptoms of BPH as compared to placebo. Secondary objectives included quality of life changes prostate size, residual volume after voiding, self-reported adverse reactions, and changes in levels of PSA, creatinine, testosterone, and other lab values. Mean changes in AUASI scores and peak urinary flow rates were not significant; no significant differences were also noted in secondary outcome measures. Based on the results, the authors concluded that saw palmetto was no more effective than placebo in these patients. The study, however, neither confirms or disputes the benefit of saw palmetto in patients with mild BPH nor does it address other saw palmetto products. In 2000, the FDA denied any health claim benefits of saw palmetto for BPH because the claims were related to effects on an existing disease not a health claim; lawsuits were filed but all FDA decisions were upheld. The role of saw palmetto in BPH is uncertain.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Saw palmetto appears to contract prostate epithelial cells and suppress tissue dihydrotestosterone (DHT) levels in men with symptomatic benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), but the mechanism of action remains elusive and has been debated. According to the German Commission E, the beneficial effects of saw palmetto for treating BPH are thought to be due to both the antiandrogenic and antiinflammatory actions. The active principle is uncertain, but the volatile and fatty oils of saw palmetto berries contain a large number of compounds, including beta-sitosterol-3-D-glucoside, tannin, sugars, polysaccharides, and various acids including anthranilic, caffeic, and chlorogeneic. Saw palmetto inhibits the enzyme 5-alpha-reductase (an antiandrogenic mechanism similar to finasteride), thereby inhibiting conversion of testosterone to the more active DHT in vitro; however, the activity is thought to be much less potent than that of finasteride and similar drugs, and clinically, may not be significant. In vivo, saw palmetto extract does not alter plasma concentrations of testosterone or prostate-specific antigen (PSA), nor does the extract have significant effect on prostate size. A study has reported a modest reduction in prostate tissue DHT concentrations when a saw palmetto extract treatment was compared to placebo for 6 months, the reductions were not as potent as the reductions seen with finasteride treatment in a control group; the authors postulated the effects were due to inhibition of 5-α reductase activity. A spasmolytic effect has also been reported, which may help relieve obstruction of urinary flow. As a result of inhibition of cyclooxygenase and 5-lipoxygenase, the synthesis of prostaglandins and leukotrienes are inhibited, which may be a mechanism of anti-inflammatory activity. One study reports that the contraction of prostate tissue may be due to an alteration in DNA chromatin structure and organization within the prostate epithelial cells; these structural changes were present after 6-months of therapy but not discernable at earlier points of treatment.
