Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Diazoxide can cause severe hyperglycemia. Patients receiving the drug should be monitored carefully for this effect, especially patients with diabetes mellitus or hepatic disease, or those receiving antihypoglycemic agents. It is recommended that blood glucose be monitored closely in patients receiving intravenous diazoxide. It is contraindicated as a treatment for functional hypoglycemia (e.g., reactive or post-prandial hypoglycemia; or hypoglycemia due to diabetic therapy). These conditions are more effectively treated with conventional measures. Hypoglycemic effects of diazoxide can be potentiated in patients with hypokalemia.
 
Diazoxide should be administered cautiously to patients in whom sodium and water retention could be detrimental (e.g., those with poor cardiac reserves, including those with uncompensated congestive heart failure). Sudden reductions in blood pressure secondary to diazoxide can be hazardous to patients with cardiac disease or coronary artery disease, renal disease, cerebrovascular disease or insufficiency, or mild tachycardia. Prolonged hypoglycemia can exacerbate renal failure.
 
Diazoxide should be used cautiously in patients with uremia because protein binding of the drug is reduced, and clinical effects can be more pronounced.
 
Parenteral diazoxide is contraindicated in patients with acute aortic dissection. Diazoxide is contraindicated in the management of hypertension due to aortic coarctation or arteriovenous shunt, The drug is ineffective against these conditions, and diazoxide-induced increases in cardiac output can be harmful.
 
Diazoxide is contraindicated in patients with hypertension secondary to pheochromocytoma because the drug is ineffective against this disorder.
 
The half-life of diazoxide may be prolonged in patients with renal impairment. Accumulation of the drug and possible toxicity may result. Dosage adjustments may be required.
 
Diazoxide should be used with caution in patients with sulfonamide hypersensitivity or thiazide diuretic hypersensitivity. Chemical structure similarities makes cross sensitivity reactions possible.
 
Diazoxide is classified as pregnancy risk category C. Although no adequate human studies have examined the effects of this drug on the fetus, animal reproduction studies have shown adverse fetal effects. Therefore, in making the decision to administer this drug during pregnancy, the potential risks to the fetus must be weighed against the potential benefits to the mother. Intravenous diazoxide should not be used during labor and delivery because it can inhibit uterine contractions. It is not known if diazoxide is found in breast milk. Because of the potential risk for adverse reactions in nursing infants, diazoxide is not recommended concurrently with breast-feeding.

    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.     Headline
    alcohol use disorder, hypokalemia, hypovolemia, surgery
    General Information
    Diazoxide increases blood glucose, due to inhibiting the release of insulin from the pancreas, and may lead to diabetic ketoacidosis or nonketotic hyperosmolar coma. Precipitating conditions for diabetic ketoacidosis may include reduction in the dosages of concomitant antihyperglycemic medications, increase in the dosages of concomitant growth hormone, intercurrent illness, surgery, hypovolemia, or alcohol use disorder. Prior to diazoxide therapy for hyperphagia, evaluate fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and HbA1c; optimize blood glucose in people who have hyperglycemia. In the presence of hypokalemia, hyperglycemic effects are also potentiated. Monitor fasting glucose frequently in people with risk factors for hyperglycemia, such as obesity, elevated FPG, HbA1c at the upper limit of normal or above, concomitant use of growth hormone, or concomitant use of systemic corticosteroids. 72066

    General Information
    Use diazoxide oral suspension with caution in neonates and infants, especially those with risk factors for pulmonary hypertension, such as meconium aspiration syndrome, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, transient tachypnea of the newborn, pneumonia, sepsis, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, and congenital heart disease.     Headline
    labor, pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on the use of diazoxide during pregnancy are insufficient to determine any drug-associated risks for major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. In animal reproduction studies, oral administration of diazoxide choline to pregnant rats during the organogenesis phase, at doses of 40, 100, and 160 mg/kg/day (0.3, 0.6, and 1.2 times the MRHD of 525 mg based on AUC), did not result in any malformations. However, decreased fetal body weights, delayed skeletal ossification, and increased fetal resorptions were observed at 160 mg/kg/day (a dose approximately equal to the MRHD based on AUC), which is a maternally toxic dose. Evidence of skeletal and cardiac teratogenic effects in rabbits has been noted with intravenous administration. Intravenous administration of diazoxide during labor may lead to the cessation of uterine contractions, requiring the use of oxytocic agents to reinstate labor. This effect has not been reported with oral administration of diazoxide; however, caution is advised.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Monitor neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero for hyperbilirubinemia, thrombocytopenia, and altered carbohydrate metabolism. Diazoxide crosses the placenta and has been detected in cord blood. An infant born to a pregnant person who was treated with oral diazoxide 150 mg daily for 47 days prior to delivery developed hyperglycemia, which resolved after a 6-hour insulin infusion. Because there was an inappropriately low plasma insulin concentration for the degree of hyperglycemia, it was considered compatible with transplacental transfer of diazoxide causing inhibition of release of insulin from the neonatal pancreas. Alopecia and hypertrichosis lanuginosa have occurred in several infants who were exposed in utero to diazoxide during the last 19 to 60 days of pregnancy. Abnormal hair growth was first noted at the age of one week and persisted when the infants were last seen at the ages of 5 months to one year.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use diazoxide with caution during breast-feeding. Limited data suggest diazoxide passes into breast milk in small amounts. One breast-feeding adult and infant pair noted minimal amounts of diazoxide in milk (range 0.7 mg/L to 3.1 mg/L) with maternal doses of 150 mg to 175 mg daily with no infant adverse effects. Because of potential adverse reactions, including hyperglycemia, consider infant blood glucose monitoring, especially during the newborn period.

In general, more serious reactions occur following IV administration than with oral administration, but this may reflect the different indications, dosage and condition of the patient. The incidence of adverse reactions were originally published when 300 mg IV bolus doses were routinely given. The most common adverse effects at this time were hypotension (7%), nausea/vomiting (4%), dizziness and weakness (2%). Using the present, lower dosages, these effects are thought to be less common and less severe. Presently, hypotension is uncommon in patients being treated with IV diazoxide alone; but is common when diazoxide is used in combination with diuretics or other drugs with hypotensive effects. Severe hypotension may necessitate treatment with sympathomimetic agents. Hypotension occurs occasionally in those receiving oral diazoxide for hypoglycemia. Other adverse effects observed as a result of the vasodilating action of IV diazoxide include warm sensations, flushing, pruritus, headache, diaphoresis, and weakness.Sodium retention and water retention can occur frequently in patients receiving multiple doses of diazoxide, resulting in edema, weight gain and congestive heart failure (especially in uremic patients).Myocardial and cerebral ischemia can occur in patients receiving IV diazoxide. Occasionally, thrombosis can occur as well. Myocardial ischemia may present as angina, ECG changes, atrial arrhythmias, or ventricular arrhythmias. Transient sinus tachycardia, sinus bradycardia, or palpitations have been observed following IV administration.     coma, diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperglycemia
    General Information
    Although in some cases the therapeutic intent of diazoxide is to raise blood glucose, excessive increases can occur. Hyperglycemia is a major adverse reaction of diazoxide. Hyperglycemia occurred in 17% of people treated with diazoxide extended-release tablets compared to 5% of the placebo group. Severe manifestations of hyperglycemia, including diabetic ketoacidosis and nonketotic hyperosmolar coma, have been reported in people receiving diazoxide and may develop quickly. Monitor for signs and symptoms of ketoacidosis, such as nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, generalized malaise, and shortness of breath. Advise individuals to monitor urine glucose and ketones and promptly report abnormal findings to a health care provider, especially under stress conditions. After initiating treatment for hyperphagia, regularly monitor fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and HbA1c. Monitor fasting glucose more frequently in people with risk factors for hyperglycemia, such as obesity, elevated FPG, HbA1c at the upper limit of normal or above, concomitant use of growth hormone, or concomitant use of systemic corticosteroids. If hyperglycemia occurs after initiating hyperphagia treatment, monitor fasting glucose as clinically indicated, and at least twice-weekly until returning to baseline. Consider monitoring ketones in people with worsening hyperglycemia. If hyperglycemia is treated with antihyperglycemic medication during diazoxide therapy, continue monitoring fasting glucose at least once a week for 8 weeks, then once every 2 weeks as clinically indicated. Consider consultation with a health care provider with expertise in the treatment of hyperglycemia and counsel individuals about lifestyle changes. Restoration of fluid and electrolytes with administration of insulin is usually effective for hyperglycemia management. Based on the severity of hyperglycemia, diazoxide therapy may require dosage interruption, reduction, or discontinuation in order to avoid progression to ketoacidosis. Due to the long half-life of diazoxide, monitor individuals for a prolonged period of time after discontinuation until blood glucose stabilizes. A reduction in dosage at the first appearance of hyperglycemia may avoid progression to ketoacidosis and/or hyperosmolar coma.

Description
  Text
  Diazoxide is a benzothiadiazine derivative structurally similar to thiazide diuretics, but with no diuretic action. It is used for the management of hypoglycemia secondary to hyperinsulinism and for the treatment of hyperphagia in people with Prader-Willi syndrome. Diazoxide produces a dose-related increase in blood glucose, primarily due to the inhibition of insulin release. The mechanism for hyperphagia is unknown. Major adverse reactions include hyperglycemia (including diabetic ketoacidosis) and edema. Diazoxide was approved by the FDA in January 1973.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Diazoxide causes a dose-related increase in blood glucose, primarily due to inhibition of insulin release, though it also has extrapancreatic effects. Insulin or tolbutamide will reverse the hyperglycemic effects of diazoxide. The mechanism of action in the treatment of hyperphagia is unknown. Diazoxide decreases sodium and water excretion secondary to increased proximal tubular resorption of sodium, which may lead to clinically significant edema. Chloride, uric acid, and para-aminohippuric acid excretion are also reduced during diazoxide therapy with no effect on glomerular filtration rate. Diazoxide may increase renin secretion and IgG serum concentration, and decrease cortisol secretion. Plasma free fatty acid concentrations are increased during diazoxide therapy. Diazoxide increases heart rate.

  Text
  Diazoxide is administered orally. Diazoxide is 90% to 93% protein bound; only unbound diazoxide is active. The volume of distribution is approximately 44.9 L; it crosses the blood-brain barrier. Diazoxide is primarily metabolized in the liver via CYP1A2 and to a minor extent by CYP3A4. The majority of unchanged drug and metabolites (85% to 92%) are excreted in the urine; 2% is excreted in feces. The oral suspension has a half life of 24 to 36 hours with long-term administration. The extended-release tablet has an estimated terminal half-life of 106 hours in individuals with Prader-Willi syndrome.
 
Pharmacodynamics:
Diazoxide increases blood glucose primarily via inhibition of insulin release; administration results in reduced fasting plasma insulin from baseline. Diazoxide also increases uric acid, renin secretion, and serum IgG concentrations, and decreases cortisol secretion.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP1A2, CYP3A4
 
Diazoxide is a CYP1A2 substrate and inhibitor. In a clinical study with fluvoxamine (a strong CYP1A2 inhibitor), coadministration with fluvoxamine at an inhibitory dose increased single dose diazoxide Cmax by 17.5% and AUC by 60%. Diazoxide is a CYP3A4 substrate; concomitant use of the extended-release tablet with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors increases diazoxide exposure. Concomitant use of diazoxide with itraconazole (a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor) may increase the AUC of diazoxide by 1.1- to 2-fold. Itraconazole did not appreciably affect the Cmax of diazoxide.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Diazoxide extended-release tablets are not recommended in people with hepatic impairment; specific guidelines for dosage adjustments of the immediate-release oral suspension are not available.
  Renal Impairment
  Diazoxide extended-release tablets are not recommended in people with renal impairment. Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments of the immediate-release oral suspension are not available; however, the elimination of diazoxide is reduced in people with renal impairment. Dosage adjustments may be necessary based upon clinical response.
