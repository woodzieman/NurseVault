Interactions
Trientine: (Major) In general, oral mineral supplements should not be given since they may block the oral absorption of trientine. However, iron deficiency may develop, especially in children and menstruating or pregnant women, or as a result of the low copper diet recommended for Wilson's disease. If necessary, iron may be given in short courses, but since iron and trientine each inhibit oral absorption of the other, 2 hours should elapse between administration of trientine and iron doses.

    Headline
    hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypophosphatemia, metabolic acidosis, proteinuria, renal tubular acidosis
    General Information
    Copper accumulation with copper histidinate can result in drug-induced kidney injury, including renal tubular toxicity and glomerular injury, which may lead to decreased kidney function or new-onset proteinuria. New-onset or worsening non-anion gap metabolic acidosis may indicate drug-related renal tubular acidosis, while increased beta-2 microglobulin and electrolyte abnormalities, including hypophosphatemia, hyponatremia, and hypokalemia, may be signs of proximal renal tubular toxicity. Monitor individuals starting or restarting copper histidinate for signs and symptoms of renal tubular toxicity, and provide supportive care with electrolyte repletion and supplementation as clinically indicated.

Description
  Text
  Copper histidinate is a copper replacement therapy indicated for the treatment of Menkes disease. It is the first treatment approved for Menkes disease in pediatric patients. Menkes disease is a rare X-linked recessive genetic disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the ATP7A gene, which encodes a copper-transporting ATPase required for the absorption and systemic distribution of dietary copper. The disorder primarily affects male infants, although females are typically carriers and may be affected in rare cases. Impaired intestinal copper absorption and defective copper transport to the brain and other tissues result in a profound deficiency of copper-dependent enzymes, leading to clinical manifestations that include seizures, hypoglycemia, prolonged jaundice, failure to thrive, growth retardation, thermoregulatory instability, developmental delays, and intellectual disability. In clinical studies, individuals who initiated copper histidinate treatment within 4 weeks of birth demonstrated a significant improvement in overall survival compared with untreated historical controls, corresponding to a 78% reduction in the risk of death; median survival was substantially longer in early-treated individuals (177.1 months) than in untreated controls (17.6 months), and nearly half of early-treated individuals survived beyond 6 years, whereas no untreated individuals survived past 6 years of age. Individuals who initiated treatment after 4 weeks of birth also experienced a significant survival benefit, with a 73% reduction in the risk of death; approximately 31% survived beyond 6 years, while no untreated individuals survived beyond this age. The most commonly reported adverse effects associated with copper histidinate administration included infections (such as pneumonia and viral infections), respiratory complications, and seizures. Copper histidinate was FDA-approved in January 2026.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Copper histidinate is a bioavailable copper replacement therapy that is administered subcutaneously to bypass the impaired gastrointestinal absorption observed in individuals with Menkes disease. Menkes disease is caused by mutations in the ATP7A gene, which helps transport copper throughout the body. Copper is needed to activate many enzymes that support normal body functions. Impaired copper transport results in reduced activity of copper-dependent enzymes, leading to progressive and potentially fatal abnormalities affecting the hair, brain, bones, liver, and blood vessels. Treatment with copper histidinate increases serum copper and ceruloplasmin concentrations.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Subcutaneous Route
    Description
    The absolute bioavailability of copper histidinate following subcutaneous injection has not been determined. Following a single subcutaneous 3-mg dose of copper histidinate (approximately twice the recommended dose for individuals 1 year and older) in healthy adult subjects, the median Tmax was 0.75 hours. The geometric mean (CV%) Cmax was 67 (36%) nanograms/mL, the geometric mean AUC from time 0 to 24 hours was 186 (21%) nanograms x hour/mL, and the geometric mean AUC from time 0 to infinity was 296 (15%) nanograms x hour/mL. At the recommended dosage, the mean (SD) serum copper concentration increased from a baseline of 30 (25) mcg/dL to 114 (38) mcg/dL at 12 months and gradually decreased over the 36-month treatment period, with a mean concentration of 63 (31) mcg/dL at 36 months. Mean serum ceruloplasmin concentrations were 12 (12) mg/dL at baseline, 33 (11) mg/dL at 12 months, and 20 (8) mg/dL at 36 months.
  Text
  Copper histidinate is administered subcutaneously. In vitro studies showed no binding of copper histidinate to human plasma proteins. Following a single subcutaneous dose of 3 mg (approximately twice the approved dosing for individuals 1 year and older) in healthy adult subjects, the mean (SD) apparent volume of distribution during the terminal elimination phase (Vz/F) was 1,034 (588) L. The metabolism of copper histidinate has not been well characterized, and elimination of elemental copper occurs primarily via biliary excretion. Serum copper histidinate concentration-time profiles demonstrated an initial decline followed by a terminal elimination phase, consistent with dissociation of copper from the copper histidinate complex to carrier proteins (ceruloplasmin and albumin), followed by incorporation of copper and histidine into endogenous pools. The mean serum clearance was 10.3 L/hour, and the mean terminal half-life was 75 hours in healthy adult subjects receiving a 3-mg dose.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, BCRP, MATE2K, OCT1, and OCT2
Copper histidinate is a weak inhibitor of CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, BCRP, MATE2-K, OCT1, and OCT2, and an inhibitor of BSEP and MATE1. Copper histidinate is not an inducer of CYP1A2, CYP2B6, or CYP3A. Copper histidinate is not a substrate of BCRP, P-gp, MATE1, MATE2-K, OAT1, OAT3, OATP1B1, OATP1B3, or OCT2. Copper histidinate is not an inhibitor of P-gp, OAT1, OAT3, OATP1B1, or OATP1B3.

Administration
  General Information

  Route Specific Administrations
    Administration Details
      Name
      Subcutaneous Administration
      Description
      Reconstitution
Remove 1 vial of copper histidinate from the refrigerator and allow it to stand at room temperature for 30 minutes.
Withdraw 1 mL of 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection into a 3-mL syringe and inject it down the inside wall of the vial to reconstitute.
Gently swirl the vial continuously until the powder is completely dissolved. Do not shake. Each vial yields a concentration of 2.9 mg/mL.
Visually inspect the reconstituted solution for particulate matter and discoloration; the solution should be blue. Discard if particles are present or the solution is discolored or cloudy.
Do not mix with other medications.
Storage: If the reconstituted vial is not used immediately, store the vial refrigerated at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) for up to 24 hours or at room temperature at 20 to 25 degrees C (68 to 77 degrees F) for up to 4 hours. Discard the vial if not used within 24 hours of refrigeration or within 4 hours at room temperature.
 
Subcutaneous Injection
If refrigerated, remove the vial from the refrigerator and allow it to stand at room temperature for 30 minutes prior to administration.
A caregiver may administer copper histidinate after appropriate training.
Slowly withdraw 0.5 mL of copper histidinate from the vial.
Administer subcutaneously into the abdominal area (at least 2 inches from the navel), buttocks, or the outer lateral aspect of the upper arm or thigh. Rotate injection sites with each injection; do not inject into areas where the skin is scarred, tender, bruised, red, or indurated.
Discard any unused portion after each use; do not administer more than 1 dose from a vial.
Missed dose: Administer as soon as possible. Administer the next scheduled dose at least 6 hours after the missed dose.
