Drug Class: Mitomycin is an antitumor antibiotic that acts as an alkylating agent and is derived from Streptomyces caespitosus.

Routes: intraocular, injection (IV/IM/SC), topical; Common adult doses: Mitomycin for injection: 20 mg/m2 IV.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): anemia, bone marrow suppression, eosinophilia, leukopenia, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ards), bronchospasm, cough, dyspnea, hemolysis, hemolytic anemia, hemolytic-uremic syndrome, pulmonary edema, confusion, drowsiness, headache, syncope, back pain, dysuria, flank pain, hematuria, increased urinary frequency, urinary urgency, hematemesis, asthenia, fatigue, malaise, blurred vision, cataracts

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Mitomycin is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity reactions or idiosyncratic reactions to the drug. Mitomycin is contraindicated for use by patients with thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, or an increase in risk of bleeding due to other causes. Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Mitomycin is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity reactions or idiosyncratic reactions to the drug.
 
Mitomycin is contraindicated for use by patients with thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, or an increase in risk of bleeding due to other causes. Mitomycin should be used cautiously in patients who have had previous myelosuppressive therapy such as chemotherapy or radiation therapy. Bone marrow suppression is the most common and severe of the toxic effects of mitomycin and may contribute to overwhelming infections in an already compromised patient. Patients with an active infection should be treated prior to receiving mitomycin. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with chemotherapy. Mitomycin therapy should be withheld in patients with severe bone marrow suppression including neutropenia/leukopenia (WBC = 60 mg. Blood product transfusion may exacerbate the symptoms associated with HUS. Patients whose cumulative dose of mitomycin is >= 60 mg should be monitored closely for unexplained anemia with fragmented cells on peripheral blood smear, thrombocytopenia, and decreased renal function.
 
Acute bronchospasm and shortness of breath have been reported following the administration of vinca alkaloids in patients who have received or are currently receiving mitomycin (see Drug Interactions). Cases of adult respiratory distress syndrome have been reported in patients receiving mitomycin in combination with other chemotherapy agents and maintained at FIO2 concentrations greater than 50% pre-surgery. Therefore, caution should be exercised using only enough oxygen to provide adequate arterial saturation. Careful attention should be paid to fluid balance and over-hydration should be avoided. Pulmonary fibrosis has been associated with mitomycin cumulative doses > 50—60 mg/m2. This reaction can be progressive and fatal despite cessation of mitomycin therapy; close monitoring of pulmonary symptoms is warranted in susceptible patients, particularly those with pre-existing pulmonary disease or respiratory insufficiency.
 
Mitomycin can cause fetal harm if administered during pregnancy (FDA pregnancy classification D). If mitomycin is used during pregnancy, or if the patient becomes pregnant while receiving this drug, the patient should be apprised of the potential hazard to the fetus or potential risk for loss of the pregnancy. Females of childbearing potential should be advised to avoid becoming pregnant. In 2 separate studies, HPβCD, the excipient for Mitozytrex, was fetotoxic in animal studies at doses about 1/30 and 1/6 the amount of HPβCD administered per recommended human dose of Mitozytrex™ on a mg/m2 basis. Infertility may occur in patients receiving mitomycin. In animal studies, at doses 1/5 to 4/5 the recommended human dose on a mg/m2 basis, significantly decreased sperm production, sperm count, and sperm motility resulting in reduced pregnancy rates and increased frequency of malformations, and inhibition of fertilization and implantation were noted in male and female animals, respectively. Prescribers should discuss potential reproductive options with patients prior to beginning treatment with mitomycin.
 
It is not known if mitomycin is excreted in human milk. Because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in the nursing infant, it is recommended that breast-feeding be discontinued during mitomycin therapy.
 
The safety and efficacy of mitomycin in children and infants have not been established.
 
Use care to avoid accidental exposure to mitomycin during preparation, handling, and administration. Use of protective gowns, gloves, and goggles is recommended. Avoid ocular exposure of mitomycin solutions.
 
Vaccination during chemotherapy or radiation therapy should be avoided because the antibody response is suboptimal. When chemotherapy is being planned, vaccination should precede the initiation of chemotherapy by >= 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    General Information

    Headline
    serious adverse effects
    General Information
    Due to the risk of serious adverse effects such as bone marrow suppression and hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), systemic administration of mitomycin requires an experienced clinician familiar with the use of cancer chemotherapeutic agents. Appropriate management of therapy and complications is only possible when adequate diagnostic and treatment facilities are readily available.
    Headline
    thrombocytopenia
    General Information
    Mitomycin solution for injection is contraindicated in patients with thrombocytopenia. Bone marrow suppression, notably thrombocytopenia and leukopenia, is the most common and severe adverse reaction associated with intravenous administration of mitomycin. Because of cumulative myelosuppression, a complete blood count with differential should be obtained repeatedly during intravenous therapy and for at least 8 weeks following therapy; dose reductions are based on nadir leukocyte and platelet counts of the previous cycle. Subsequent cycles of treatment should not begin until the leukocyte count is at least 4,000 cells/mm3 and the platelet count is at least 100,000 cells/mm3. Thrombocytopenia and neutropenia have also occurred in patients receiving pyelocalyceal instillation of mitomycin; there were no signs of gross extravasation via urinary tract perforation or impaired mucosa in these patients. Therefore, a complete blood count with differential should also be obtained prior to each dose of mitomycin for pyelocalyceal instillation. An interruption or discontinuation of therapy may be necessary if thrombocytopenia or neutropenia occur.
    Headline
    bleeding disorder
    General Information
    Mitomycin solution for injection is contraindicated in patients with a bleeding disorder or an increase in bleeding tendency, including coagulation disorders, due to the bleeding risk associated with thrombocytopenia which occurs commonly with treatment.
    Headline
    bladder perforation, rupture of the upper urinary tract
    General Information
    Mitomycin for pyelocalyceal solution and mitomycin for intravesical solution are both contraindicated in patients with a bladder perforation due to the risk of increased systemic absorption and an increased risk of myelosuppression; mitomycin for pyelocalyceal solution is additionally contraindicated in patients with a perforation or rupture of the upper urinary tract. Evaluate the bladder prior to intravesical or pyelocalyceal instillation of mitomycin and do not administer to patients with a perforated bladder or mucosal compromise until bladder integrity has been restored.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Avoid the use of mitomycin for pyelocalyceal solution and mitomycin for intravesical solution in patients with severe renal impairment (eGFR less than 30 mL/min); data are not available in patients with severe renal impairment or renal failure. A higher incidence of hematuria and urinary tract infections was observed in patients with moderate renal impairment (eGFR 30 to 59 mL/min) who received intravesical administration of mitomycin; monitor these patients for an increased incidence of adverse reactions. Systemic mitomycin should not be given to patients with a serum creatinine greater than 1.7 mg/dL. Monitor patients for evidence of nephrotoxicity.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Mitomycin may cause fetal harm if used during pregnancy, based on findings from animal studies and its mechanism of action. In animal reproduction studies, intravenous mitomycin caused teratogenicity.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with mitomycin. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. When given via intravesical or pyelocalyceal instillation, advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception during treatment and for 6 months after the last dose. For those that can cause pregnancy in others, contraception should be used during treatment and for 3 months after the last dose. Specific contraception recommendations for mitomycin injection and ophthalmic solution are not available.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while receiving mitomycin and for at least 1 week after the last dose of the ophthalmic, intravesical, or pyelocalyceal solution. There are no specific recommendations for the injectable mitomycin. There are no data on the presence of mitomycin in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. However, mitomycin has the potential to cause serious side effects in the breastfed child.

Interactions
Cholera Vaccine: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the live cholera vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to cholera bacteria after receiving the vaccine.
Dengue Tetravalent Vaccine, Live: (Contraindicated) Avoid administration of the live dengue virus vaccine with immunosuppressive drug therapy and prior to immune recovery following treatment with immunosuppressive drug therapy. When feasible, administer indicated live virus vaccines at least 4 weeks before planned immunosuppression or wait until at least 3 months after discontinuation. The time to restoration of immune competence may be longer in some patients. Patients with altered immunocompetence may be at increased risk for severe adverse reactions due to uninhibited growth of the attenuated live virus. Additionally, vaccines may be less effective if administered during a period of altered immunocompetence.
SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) vaccines: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the SARS-CoV-2 virus vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications.   Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The most common and serious adverse effect associated with mitomycin therapy is cumulative bone marrow suppression (64%) manifested by pancytopenia, thrombocytopenia, anemia, leukopenia, and neutropenia. Thrombocytopenia and/or leukopenia may occur anytime within 8 weeks after administration, usually within 3 weeks, with a duration of up to 4 weeks. Because hematologic recovery is slow following repeated mitomycin treatments, an 8-week interval is usually necessary following the second or third injection. Dosage adjustments are recommended for severe neutropenia or thrombocytopenia (see Dosage). Thrombocytopenia can be severe and long-lasting. Recovery after cessation of therapy was with in 10 weeks. About 25% of patients do not recover form leukopenic or thrombocytopenic episodes.Cutaneous toxicity (pruritus, phlebitis, vein pain) and mucous membrane toxicity (stomatitis, esophagitis, mucositis) (4%) can occur following administration of mitomycin. An injection site reaction consisting of cellulitis can be severe. Alopecia occurs frequently. Skin rash (unspecified) has been reported rarely. Nail discoloration with purple banding of the fingernails occurs on rare occasions. The most severe dermatologic reaction is tissue necrosis and subsequent desquamation that results if the drug is extravasated during injection. Extravasation may occur with or without an accompanying stinging or burning sensation and even if there is adequate blood return when the injection needle is aspirated. There have been reports of delayed erythema and/or skin ulceration (skin ulcer) occurring either at or distant from the injection site. Skin grafting has been required in some cases. In case reports, DMSO has been used to treat mitomycin extravasations. Cutaneous radiation recall reaction is possible. Local reactions, including toxic epidermal necrolysis, have been reported when mitomycin is administered intra-arterially into previously irradiated tissues. Paresthesias may occur, which are thought to be due to local damage to nerve endings in the dermis.Mitomycin-induced pulmonary toxicity, although rare, can be serious and fatal. This toxicity is manifested by hemoptysis, dyspnea, non-productive cough, interstitial pneumonitis, and radiographic indications of pulmonary infiltrates. Steroids have been used in the treatment of mitomycin-induced pulmonary toxicity, but the therapeutic value has not been determined. Acute shortness of breath and severe bronchospasm have been reported following the administration of vinca alkaloids in patients who had previously or simultaneously received mitomycin. The onset of this acute respiratory distress occurred within minutes to hours after the vinca alkaloid injection. Bronchodilators, steroids, and/or oxygen have produced symptomatic relief. Pre-treatment with bronchodilators may help to lessen the frequency and severity of the reaction; however, there is still a possibility of a life-threatening or fatal reaction. A few cases of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) have been reported in patients receiving mitomycin in combination with other chemotherapy agents and maintained at FIO2 concentrations > 50% perioperatively. Pulmonary fibrosis has been associated with mitomycin cumulative doses > 50—60 mg/m2. This reaction can be progressive and fatal despite cessation of mitomycin therapy; close monitoring of pulmonary symptoms is warranted in susceptible patients.Hemolytic-uremic syndrome (HUS), also described as microangiopathic hemolytic anemia with consumption or thrombotic microangiopathy, has been reported in patients receiving mitomycin. The symptoms of this syndrome include microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (hematocrit = 1.6). The syndrome may occur at any time during systemic therapy with mitomycin as a single agent or in combination with other cytotoxic drugs; however the exact incidence has not been defined. Of 83 patients studied, 72 developed the syndrome at total doses exceeding 60 mg of mitomycin. Microangiopathic hemolysis with fragmented red blood cells on peripheral blood smears has occurred in 98% of patients with HUS. Other complications of this syndrome include pulmonary edema (65%), neurologic abnormalities (16%), hypertension, activation of the fibrinolytic system, and consumption of clotting factors. Although the exact cause is unknown, mitomycin-induced endothelial injury is suspected to be the initial insult resulting in the other complications. Attempts to treat this syndrome have included corticosteroids, exchange transfusions, and usual therapeutic interventions for disseminated intravesicular coagulopathy. Hypertension seems to respond well to angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI) therapy in low doses. A high mortality rate (52%) has been associated with this syndrome. The use of combined prednisone, captopril, and heparin therapy with dialysis and vigorous blood component support has lead to survival in some patients.Acute side effects due to mitomycin (14%) include fever, anorexia, and nausea/vomiting. Malaise and asthenia/weakness have been reported as part of post-marketing surveillance. Lethargy or weakness can last for several days up to as long as 3 weeks. Delayed nausea and vomiting, similar to cisplatin, can occur. Anorexia can be prolonged. Other side effects that do not appear to be unequivocally related to mitomycin include headache, blurred vision, confusion, drowsiness, syncope, fatigue, edema, thrombophlebitis, hematemesis, diarrhea, and pain (unspecified). Of 1281 patients treated with mitomycin, 2% demonstrated a significant rise in serum creatinine. There appeared to be no correlation between total dose administered or duration of therapy and the degree of renal impairment.Myelosuppression has not been noted during bladder instillation of mitomycin; however, 6% (3 of 20) of patients in one series developed painful increased urinary frequency following intravesical treatment. Bladder fibrosis/contraction has been reported with intravesical administration of mitomycin, which in some cases has required cystectomy. Patients should be warned regarding the possibility of urine discoloration following bladder instillation and also systemic administration.Congestive heart failure, often treated effectively with diuretics and cardiac glycosides, has rarely been reported during mitomycin therapy. Almost all patients who experienced this side effect had received prior doxorubicin therapy. It has been suggested that cardiomyopathy occurs with greater frequency and at lower cumulative drug doses in patients receiving doxorubicin and mitomycin in combination.Secondary malignancy has been reported following mitomycin administration. The HPβCD solubilizing agent in Mitozytrex™ is also carcinogenic in animal models; the clinical relevance of this finding is unclear.Veno-occlusive disease (VOD) of the liver has been reported after high-dose mitomycin therapy during autologous bone marrow transplantation.Arterial thrombosis has been associated with intra-arterial administration of mitomycin.

    anemia, bone marrow suppression, eosinophilia, leukopenia, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia
    General Information
    Cumulative bone marrow suppression is the most common and most serious toxicity reported in patients treated with systemic mitomycin (64.4%). Thrombocytopenia and leukopenia may occur anytime in the first 8 weeks of therapy (average, 4 weeks). Recovery typically occurs within 10 weeks after discontinuation of therapy; approximately 25% of leukopenic or thrombocytopenic episodes did not recover. Myelosuppression has also occurred in patients receiving pyelocalyceal administration of mitomycin including anemia (14% to 38%; grade 3 or 4, 1.4%), lymphopenia (21%; grade 3, 2.9%), and thrombocytopenia (21%; grade 3, 2.8%). Gross extravasation of pyelocalyceal mitomycin via urinary tract perforation or impaired mucosa was not observed in the patients with grade 3 thrombocytopenia and neutropenia. Hematologic adverse effects have also been reported in patients with bladder cancer treated with mitomycin for intravesical solution including decreases from baseline in hemoglobin (17%; grade 3 or 4, 0.8%), lymphocytes (14%; grade 3 or 4, 0.4%), and neutrophils (10%; grade 3 or 4, 0.4%) and increases from baseline in eosinophils (eosinophilia) (15%).

    hemolysis, hemolytic anemia, hemolytic-uremic syndrome, pulmonary edema
    General Information
    Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) has been reported in individuals receiving systemic mitomycin therapy, consisting primarily of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (hematocrit, 25% or less), thrombocytopenia (platelets, 100,000 cells/mm3 or less), and irreversible renal failure (serum creatinine 1.6 mg/dL or more); the incidence of this syndrome has not been defined. Microangiopathic hemolysis with fragmented red blood cells on peripheral blood smears has occurred in 98% of individuals with HUS; less frequent complications include pulmonary edema (65%), neurologic abnormalities (16%), and high blood pressure. Of 83 individuals studied, 72 developed HUS at total doses exceeding 60 mg of mitomycin (approximately 87%); closely monitor individuals receiving doses higher than 60 mg for unexplained anemia with fragmented cells on peripheral blood smear, thrombocytopenia, and decreased renal function. Blood product transfusion may exacerbate the symptoms. HUS has a mortality rate of 52%.

    blurred vision, cataracts, endophthalmitis, hyphema, iritis, keratitis, macular edema, ocular hemorrhage, ocular hypotonia, papilledema, retinal detachment, retinal hemorrhage, visual impairment, wound dehiscence
    General Information
    The use of mitomycin topical solution in concentrations higher than 0.2 mg/mL or for longer than 2 minutes may result in corneal or scleral damage, including thinning or perforation. Direct contact with the corneal endothelium will result in cell death. The most frequent adverse reactions to mitomycin topical solution used in ocular surgery occur locally and include hypotony maculopathy, blebitis, endophthalmitis, vascular reactions, corneal reactions, and cataracts. Post-operative ocular hypotonia (hypotony) due to choroidal detachment, choroidal effusion, serous choroidal detachment, suprachoroidal hemorrhage, hypotony maculopathy, presence of supraciliochoroidal fluid, or hypoechogenic suprachoroidal effusion has also frequently been associated with the use of mitomycin topical solution. In phakic patients, the use of mitomycin topical solution has been associated with increased frequency of lenticular change and formation of cataracts. Other ophthalmic adverse reactions associated with the use of mitomycin topical solution include: bleb ulceration, chronic bleb leak, encapsulated or cystic bleb, bleb-related infection, wound dehiscence, conjunctival necrosis, thin-walled bleb, corneal endothelial damage, epithelial defect, anterior synechiae, superficial punctuate keratitis, Descemet's detachment, induced astigmatism, endophthalmitis, iritis, fibrin reaction, cataract development, cataract progression, capsule opacification, capsular constriction or capsulotomy rupture, posterior synechiae, retinal pigment epithelial tear, retinal detachment (serous and rhegmatogenous), scleritis, hyphema, central retinal vein occlusion, hemiretinal vein occlusion, retinal hemorrhage, ocular hemorrhage (i.e., vitreal hemorrhage and blood clot, subconjunctival hemorrhage, disk hemorrhage), macular edema, sclera thinning or ulceration, intraocular lens capture, papilledema, malignant glaucoma, lacrimal drainage system obstruction, ciliary block, corneal vascularization, cystic conjunctival degeneration, upper eyelid retraction, dislocated implants, and visual impairment including decreased visual acuity and severe vision loss. Description
  Text
  Mitomycin is an antitumor antibiotic that acts as an alkylating agent and is derived from Streptomyces caespitosus. It is indicated intravenously for the treatment of metastatic stomach or pancreatic cancer in proven combinations with other chemotherapeutic agents. An ophthalmic solution is indicated as a topical adjunct to ab externo glaucoma surgery; this formulation is not intended for intraocular administration. A pyelocalyceal solution is indicated for the treatment of patients with low-grade upper tract urothelial cancer (LG-UTUC); this formulation appears as a semisolid gel at room temperature and must be chilled to become a viscous liquid for administration. Finally, an intravesical solution is indicated for the treatment of adult patients with recurrent low-grade intermediate-risk non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (LG-IR-NMIBC); this formulation (a semisolid gel at room temperature and must be chilled to become a viscous liquid for administration) is for intravesical installation only and should not be administered by pyelocalyceal instillation or any other route. Mitomycin can be associated neutropenia and thrombocytopenia; an interruption of therapy or discontinuation of therapy may be necessary if myelosuppression occurs.

  Antineoplastic Activity
Mitomycin is an alkylating agent that inhibits DNA synthesis; at high concentrations, cellular RNA and protein synthesis are also suppressed. The guanine and cytosine content correlate with the degree of mitomycin-induced cross-linking.
 
Activity in Ocular Surgery 
In glaucoma surgery, mitomycin is applied topically for a short period during the surgery. It inhibits the proliferative phase of the wound-healing pathway through inhibition of fibroblast as well asendothelial cell growth and replication. It is approximately 100-fold more potent than fluorouracil after a single topical intraoperative application. These antifibrotic actions of the drug prevent scarring of the tissue when surgeons create a flap in the eye to relieve the excess fluid buildup, reducing intraocular pressure following the surgery.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Intravenous Route
    Description
    The Cmax of mitomycin after an intravenous injection of 30 mg was 2.4 mcg/mL; the Cmax after a 20 mg and 10 mg injection was 1.7 mcg/mL and 0.52 mcg/mL, respectively. After intravenous administration, mitomycin is rapidly cleared from the serum, with concentrations reduced by 50% in 17 minutes after a single bolus injection.
    Name
    Topical Route
    Description
    Ocular Administration
It is not known if mitomycin is systemically absorbed following topical ocular administration during surgery. The proposed dose of mitomycin used during glaucoma surgery (0.2 mg) is significantly lower than intravenous doses used for oncologic indications (up to 20 mg/m2); therefore, systemic concentrations after topical ocular administration would be expected to be multiple orders of magnitude lower than those achieved by IV administration. Mitomycin is cleared from ophthalmic tissue after intraoperative topical application and irrigation, as metabolism occurs in other affected tissues.
    Name
    Other Route(s)
    Description
    Pyelocalyceal Use
Plasma concentrations of mitomycin were variable after instillation of up to 60 mg of mitomycin into the pyelocalyceal system, ranging from 2.43 ng/mL to 12.8 ng/mL over the course of treatment. The mean Cmax of mitomycin was 6.24 ng/mL, which is estimated to be less than 1% of the expected Cmax after intravenous administration. After instillation into the pyelocalyceal system, mitomycin (Jelmyto) forms a semisolid gel which dissolves from normal kidney urine flow, releasing mitomycin for up to 4 to 6 hours; it is eliminated unchanged in the urine.
Intravesical Installation
Following intravesical installation of 75 mg of mitomycin (Zusduri), the Cmax of mitomycin was 2.3 nanogram/mL (0.2 to 8.9 nanogram/mL), which is less than 1% of the expected Cmax following intravenous administration. Following installation into the bladder, mitomycin (Zusduri) forms a semisolid gel which dissolves in the urine. Visible gel is present in the urine of treated patients for up to 24 hours (median, 5 hours); mitomycin is excreted unchanged in the urine. Systemically absorbed mitomycin is rapidly cleared from the serum and approximately 10% is excreted unchanged in the urine.

  Text
  Mitomycin can be administered intravenously, topically, or by pyelocalyceal instillation or intravesical instillation, depending on the dosage form and indication; one dosage form should not be substituted for another. While systemic clearance is primarily affected by hepatic metabolism, metabolism occurs in other tissue as well. The rate of clearance is inversely proportional to the Cmax due to saturation of the degradative pathways. Approximately 10% of a dose of mitomycin is excreted unchanged in the urine; this percentage increases proportionally with dose due to saturation of metabolic pathways at low doses.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: None.

  Route Specific Administrations
    Administration Details
      Name
      Intravenous Administration
      Description
      Mitomycin for injection is administered intravenously. Because the drug is extremely irritating to tissues, it should not be administered intramuscularly or subcutaneously.
Visually inspect parenteral products for particulate matter and discoloration prior to administration whenever solution and container permit. Mitomycin IV solutions are a blue-gray color.
Monitor the patient closely for signs and symptoms of extravasation; cellulitis, ulceration, and sloughing may result if extravasation occurs.
 
Reconstitution
5 mg vial: Add 10 mL Sterile Water for Injection.
20 mg vial: Add 40 mL Sterile Water for Injection.
40 mg vial: Add 80 mL Sterile Water for Injection.
Shake vial to dissolve the powder. If the powder for injection does not dissolve immediately, let stand at room temperature until completely dissolved.
Storage after reconstitution: The reconstituted solution is stable for 7 days at room temperature or 14 days under refrigeration.
 
Dilution
The solution may be further diluted in 0.9% Sodium Chloride for Injection (stable for 12 hours at room temperature) or Sodium Lactate Injection (stable for 24 hours at room temperature) to a final concentration of 20 to 40 mcg/mL.

    Ophthalmic Administration
    Description
    Reconstitution
The non-sterile circulating nurse should open the outer pack of the Mitosol Kit and affect the sterile transfer of all contents to the sterile field.
The sterile scrub technician should handle and open the sterile inner tray, and then assemble and dispense its contents as below.
Remove the vial and vial adapter from the blue foam pouch. Screw the clear plunger rod to the rubber plunger of the pre-filled syringe.
Rock the syringe cap sideways (do not twist) until it breaks free from the syringe collar.
Attach the threaded end of the connector to the syringe, and then firmly attach the smaller threaded end of the vial adapter to the safety connector.
Stand upright on a sturdy, flat surface and push on the vial lid until sealed and secure.
Inject the entire contents of the syringe (1 mL Sterile Water for Injection) into the vial of mitomycin for a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL; shake to dissolve.
DO NOT force the plunger; the syringe will not operate if the vial adapter and syringe connector are not properly connected. Forcing the plunger may result in syringe leakage and exposure to mitomycin.
If the product does not dissolve immediately, allow it to stand at room temperature until dissolution.
Storage after reconstitution: After reconstitution, mitomycin is stable for 1 hour at room temperature.
 
Preparing Sponges
Invert the mitomycin vial and syringe; draw the full volume of medication into the syringe.
Remove all sponges from the sponge tray, and return to the tray only those sponges to be used.
Unscrew the syringe with the safety connector from the vial and vial adaptor.
DO NOT remove the safety connector from the syringe.
Place the vial and vial adaptor in the chemotherapy waste disposal bag (yellow bag); set the bag aside, within the sterile field, for additional use.
Remove the sponge container from the sterile inner tray.
Screw both syringes into the sponge container: the TB syringe to one end, and the syringe with reconstituted mitomycin to the other.
Inject the medication into the sponge container, fully saturating the sponges with the entire reconstituted contents of the vial.
Leave reconstituted mitomycin undisturbed in the sponge container for 60 seconds. Do not force the syringe plunger. If any excess fluid remains, withdraw the plunger of the TB syringe to draw out excess fluid/air.
 
Administration
NOTE: Mitomycin for solution (for ophthalmic use) is for topical application to the surgical site of glaucoma filtration surgery only; it is not intended for intraocular administration. Intraocular administration may result in cell death leading to corneal infarction, retinal infarction, and ciliary body atrophy.
With both syringes connected, open the sponge container and offer the contents to the surgeon for placement.
The treatment area should approximate 10 mm x 6 mm +/- 2 mm.
Apply fully saturated sponges equally to the treatment area, in a single layer, with the use of surgical forceps.
Keep the sponges on the treatment area for 2 minutes, then remove them and copiously irrigate the surgical site.
Return the used sponges back into the sponge container for disposal. Close the container lid.
With both syringes still connected, remove the entire assembly from the surgical field and place it in the Chemotherapy Waste Bag provided.
The use of mitomycin ophthalmic solution in concentrations higher than 0.2 mg/mL or for use longer than 2 minutes may lead to unintended corneal and/or scleral damage including thinning or perforation. Direct contact with the corneal endothelium will result in cell death.

    Other Administration Route(s)
    Description
    Pyelocalyceal Administration (Jelmyto)
Mitomycin for pyelocalyceal solution is for pyelocalyceal use only. It is NOT for intravenous use, topical use, or oral administration.
Prior to every instillation, the patient should take sodium bicarbonate 1.3 g PO the evening prior to, the morning of, and 30 minutes prior to the instillation procedure (total, 3.9 g). If the patient is to be anesthetized, they should NOT take sodium bicarbonate within 30 minutes prior to treatment.
General anesthesia, local anesthesia, sedation, prophylactic antibiotics, and/or antihistamines may be used at the discretion of the treating urologist.
Consider holding diuretics one day prior to instillation until 4 hours post-instillation.
Intravesical administration of mitomycin may discolor the urine to a violet to blue color following the instillation procedure. Patients should avoid contact with urine for at least 6 hours post-instillation, should void sitting on a toilet, and should flush the toilet several times after use.
Mitomycin for pyelocalyceal solution must be prepared under chilled conditions. Once reconstituted, it will appear as a viscous liquid for instillation. If there is any difficulty pushing or withdrawing the solution during preparation, put the components back in the Chilling Block until it liquifies.
Preparation
The day prior to preparation, put the Chilling Block upside down in the freezer at -20 to -12 degrees Celsius (-4 to 10.4 degrees Fahrenheit) overnight.
Remove the Chilling Block from the freezer and disinfect it with 70% Isopropyl alcohol spray or equivalent. Allow the Chilling Block to air dry, and then place it upright inside the hood or isolator.
Wait 20 minutes.
Connect vial adaptors to all 3 vials. Connect a syringe adaptor to one of the 10 mL syringes and to the 20 mL syringe.
Place the 3 vials (2 vials mitomycin, 1 vial sterile hydrogel), the 10 mL syringe, and the 20 mL syringe into the Chilling Block for at least 10 minutes.
During this time, withdraw 2 mL of sterile water into the other 10 mL syringe and set aside for later use.
Slowly fill the chilled 20 mL syringe with 14 mL of sterile hydrogel. Recap the syringe and place it in the Chilling Block.
Slowly fill the chilled 10 mL syringe with 4 mL of sterile hydrogel. Discard the unused portion of sterile hydrogel.
Replace the needle on the 2 mL sterile water syringe with the Luer Lock connector.
Remove the syringe adaptor from the 4 mL sterile hydrogel syringe and connect it to the other side of the Luer Lock connector on the 2 mL sterile water syringe.
Gently mix the sterile water with the sterile hydrogel by pushing the plungers back and forth at least 25 times to create the "pre-wetting solution" (PWS).
Transfer the 6 mL PWS into one of the syringes. Replace the Luer Lock connector with a new syringe adaptor. Place the 6 mL PWS syringe in the Chilling Block.
Reconstitution
Remove both mitomycin vials from the Chilling Block; gently tap the bottom of each vial on the table to ensure all powder is at the bottom of the vials.
Remove the chilled 6 mL PWS syringe from the Chilling Block. Inject 3 mL PWS into each vial of mitomycin and discard the empty PWS syringe.
NOTE: To ensure accurate dosing, the contents of each vial must be the same.
Gently swirl each vial of mitomycin upright at least 15 times; ensure all powder and admixture remains at the bottom of the vial. DO NOT invert or shake.
Immediately remove the chilled 14 mL syringe of sterile hydrogel from the Chilling Block and inject 7 mL into each vial of mitomycin.
Gently swirl each vial of mitomycin upright at least 15 times; ensure all powder and admixture remains at the bottom of the vial. DO NOT invert or shake.
Recap and place the 20 mL syringe in the Chilling Block.
Recap and place both vials of mitomycin in the Chilling Block for 5 minutes; then remove the vials and vigorously swirl them upright at least 15 times, ensuring all admixture remains at the bottom of the vials. Return both vials to the Chilling Block. Repeat these steps every 5 minutes for 30 minutes (6 times). DO NOT invert or shake the vials.
Remove 1 vial of mitomycin from the Chilling Block. After vigorously swirling upright at least 15 times, slowly withdraw the entire contents of the vial (7 mL) into the chilled 20 mL syringe. If it is difficult to withdraw the admixture, place the contents back into the Chilling Block until it liquifies again. Inject the contents of this syringe into the remaining chilled vial of mitomycin, so that all the admixture is in 1 vial.
Recap the vial adaptor. Vigorously swirl the vial upright at least 15 minutes.
Reconstituted mitomycin for pyelocalyceal solution has reverse thermal properties with a gelation point of approximately 19 degrees Celsius (66 degrees Fahrenheit).
Storage after reconstitution: Instill mitomycin for pyelocalyceal solution as soon as possible after reconstitution. If immediate instillation is not possible, it may be stored at 20 to 25 degrees Celsius (68 to 77 degrees Fahrenheit) for up to 96 hours (4 days) protected from light; it will appear as a semisolid gel when stored under these conditions. The "Discard after" date and time is 8 hours from the completion of the preparation at room temperature; this should be documented on the admixture label and applied to the prepared vial.
Pyelocalyceal Instillation
Once chilled at -3 to 5 degrees Celsius (27 to 41 degrees Farenheit), mitomycin for pyelocalyceal solution will convert to a viscous liquid for instillation and is stable for up to 1 additional hour.
Reconstituted mitomycin for pyelocalyceal solution must be instilled within 1 hour after it is converted to a viscous liquid.
Instill mitomycin for pyelocalyceal solution as a chilled solution using a Uroject12 Lever, a Luer lock syringe, and a ureteral catheter with molded Luer lock connector.
 
Intravesical Administration (Zusduri)
Mitomycin for intravesical solution is for intravesical instillation only. Do not administer by pyelocalyceal instillation or by any other route.
Intravesical instillation of mitomycin (Zusduri) requires syringes and a urinary catheter with fixed Luer Lock connectors.
Intravesical administration of mitomycin may discolor the urine to a violet to blue color following the instillation procedure. Patients should avoid contact with urine for at least 24 hours post-instillation, void sitting on a toilet, wash hands and genital area with soap and water after each urination, and flush the toilet several times after use.
Mitomycin for intravesical solution must be prepared under chilled conditions; it has reverse thermal properties with a gelation point of approximately 19 degrees C (66 degrees F). Once reconstituted, it will appear as a semisolid gel at room temperature and as a viscous liquid when chilled. If there is any difficulty pushing or withdrawing the solution during preparation, put the components back in the Chilling Block until it liquifies.
Preparation
Supplies needed from facility (do not substitute any of these components):
Two empty 20 mL Luer lock syringes
Two 5 mL Luer lock syringes filled with 3 mL of Sterile Water for Injection
Three OnGuard2 CSTD vial adaptors
Four OnGuard2 CSTD syringe adaptors
Chilling Block or other means of chilling (device design may vary)
The day prior to preparation, put the Chilling Block in the freezer at -20 to -12 degrees C (-4 to 10.4 degrees F) overnight. Refer to the Chilling Block instructions for use for more information.
Just prior to use, remove the Chilling Block from the freezer and disinfect it per internal policies. Allow the Chilling Block to air dry, and then place it inside the hood or isolator.
Connect vial adaptors to all three vials; two vials of mitomycin (40 mg per vial) and 1 vial of sterile hydrogel (60 mL).
Connect syringe adaptors to both empty 20 mL syringes and both syringes filled with 3 mL of Sterile Water for Injection.
Place one mitomycin vial, the sterile hydrogel vial, and both empty 20 mL syringes into the Chilling Block for approximately 20 minutes.
Set the second mitomycin vial and both syringes filled with 3 mL of Sterile Water for Injection aside for later use.
Reconstitution
Reconstitute the first mitomycin (Zusduri) vial:
Slowly fill one of the empty, chilled 20 mL syringe with 14 mL of sterile hydrogel. Slowly fill the other empty, chilled 20 mL syringe with 13 mL of sterile hydrogel.
Place both 20 mL syringes and the sterile hydrogel vial back in the Chilling Block.
Remove a chilled mitomycin vial from the Chilling Block and inject 3 mL of Sterile Water for Injection into the vial.
Gently swirl the mitomycin vial upright at least 20 times; do not invert or shake the vial.
Inject the chilled 14 mL and 13 mL syringes of sterile hydrogel into the mitomycin vial.
Place both empty 20 mL syringes back in the Chilling Block for later use.
Vigorously swirl the mitomycin vial upright for about 2 minutes; do not invert or shake the vial.
Set the mitomycin vial aside for later use.
Reconstitute the second mitomycin (Zusduri) vial following the same process.
Combine the contents of the mitomycin admixture vials:
Slowly fill each empty, chilled 20 mL syringe with 14 mL of admixture from the second mitomycin vial (to total 28 mL).
NOTE: If there is any difficulty withdrawing the admixture, place components back in the Chilling Block until the admixture liquifies again.
Inject the two 14 mL admixture syringes into the first mitomycin vial. One vial of reconstituted mitomycin (Zusduri) results in a concentration of 80 mg per 60 mL.
Storage after reconstitution: Instill reconstituted mitomycin as soon as possible. If not used immediately, it may be stored under refrigeration and protected from light at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) for up to 7 days, or under refrigeration at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) for 6 days followed by no more than 24 hours at room temperature (20 to 25 degrees C, or 68 to 77 degrees F). Avoid excessive heat over 40 degrees C (104 degrees F).
Intravesical Instillation
Supplies needed (do not substitute these components):
One vial of reconstituted mitomycin (Zusduri) with a concentration of 80 mg per 60 mL.
Ice bath and plastic bag
Urinary catheter (12 Fr to 16 Fr with fixed Luer lock connector)
One catheter adapter (OnGuard2 CSTD Luer lock adaptor)
Four 20 mL polypropylene Luer lock syringes (BD Luer-Lok syringes)
One 5 mL Luer lok syringe filled with 3 mL of Sterile Water for Injection
Five OnGuard2 CSTD syringe adaptors
Place the reconstituted mitomycin vial in a plastic bag.
Place the plastic bag in the ice bath for approximately 20 minutes; ensure the vial is completely immersed in the ice bath.
Connect a syringe adaptor to the 3 mL syringe containing Sterile Water for Injection and set aside.
Connect syringe adaptors to the four 20 mL syringes; these are the administration syringes. Once adaptors are connected, do not disconnect.
Remove the reconstituted mitomycin from the ice bath ensuring the admixture is liquid. If needed, place back in the ice bath until the admixture liquefies.
Swirl the vial upright to ensure the mitomycin is uniformly mixed.
Slowly fill each of the four administration syringes with 14 mL of mitomycin admixture (a total of 56 mL); there may be foaming in the syringe while withdrawing the mitomycin.
Place the 4 administration syringes in the plastic bag and place the plastic bag in the ice bath; ensure the four syringes are completely immersed for at least 2 minutes.
Prior to instillation, ensure that all four administration syringes contain liquid mitomycin and keep all syringes chilled in the ice bath until instillation time.
Instillation:
Place the urinary catheter in the bladder and drain the urine.
Connect the catheter adaptor to the urinary catheter.
Connect an administration syringe to the urinary catheter.
Empty each administration syringe over approximately 30 seconds; reconstituted mitomycin is viscous, even in a chilled state. Disconnect the syringe adaptor from the catheter adaptor.
Immediately repeat these steps without delay for the remaining 3 administration syringes.
Following instillation of all 4 administration syringes, flush the urinary catheter with 3 mL of Sterile Water for Injection.
Wait 15 minutes before removing the urinary catheter from the patient.

  Text
  The suggested maximum tolerated dose (MTD) for mitomycin is dependent on type of mitomycin injection used, performance status, other chemotherapy agents or radiation given in combination, and disease state. Therefore, dosing may vary from protocol to protocol. If questions arise, clinicians should consult the appropriate references to verify the dose.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  CrCl less than 30 mL/min: Avoid the use of mitomycin for pyelocalyceal solution and mitomycin for intravesical solution.
SCr greater than 1.7 mg/dL: Do not administer mitomycin for injection.
