Drug Class: Corticotropin (ACTH) is a parenteral preparation of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH).

Routes: topical, injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 80 units/day subcutaneous or IM for most conditions; up to 120 units/day IM for multiple sclerosis exacerbations.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): acne vulgaris, ecchymosis, erythema, hyperhidrosis, impaired wound healing, injection site reaction, petechiae, rash, skin atrophy, esophageal ulceration, gi bleeding, gi perforation, pancreatitis, anaphylactic shock, dizziness, hypotension, pruritus, scarlatiniform exanthema, urticaria, wheezing, candidiasis, fever, infection, nasal congestion, cataracts, exophthalmos, ocular hypertension, ocular infection, atrial fibrillation, edema

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

        Infants and Children younger than 2 years
        Dosage Description
        75 units/m2/dose IM twice daily for 2 weeks is the FDA-approved regimen. The dose should then be tapered over a 2-week period to avoid adrenal insufficiency. The FDA-approved product label suggests the following tapering schedule: 30 units/m2/dose IM every morning for 3 days, 15 units/m2/dose IM every morning for 3 days, 10 units/m2/dose IM every morning for 3 days, and 10 units/m2/dose IM every other morning for 6 days. Various other non-FDA-approved regimens have been used off label. Low doses of 5 to 40 units/day IM for 1 to 6 weeks have been recommended by some neurologists, whereas others recommend larger doses of 40 to 160 units/day IM for 3 to 12 months. In 1 study, no major difference in efficacy was found between low doses for short periods and large doses for longer periods of time; however, hypertension was more common with the larger doses. In this study, the low-dose regimen was 20 units/day IM for 2 weeks. If the patient responded, the dose was tapered and discontinued over a 1-week period. If the patient did not respond, the dose was increased to 30 units/day IM for 4 weeks, then tapered and discontinued over a 1-week period. The high-dose regimen was 150 units/m2/day IM for 3 weeks; the dose was then tapered and discontinued over 9 weeks.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Certain dosage forms of ACTH injection should not be given by the intravenous route. Do not give H.P. Acthar repository Gel via intravenous administration.
 
The use of corticotropin is contraindicated in patients with previous hypersensitivity to corticotropin therapy and those with porcine protein hypersensitivity. Although true corticosteroid hypersensitivity is rare, patients who have demonstrated a prior hypersensitivity reaction to corticotropin, ACTH should not receive any form of corticotropin, ACTH. It is possible, though also rare, that such patients will display cross-hypersensitivity to other corticosteroids. It is advisable that patients who have a hypersensitivity reaction to any corticosteroid undergo skin testing, which, although not a conclusive predictor, may help to determine if hypersensitivity to another corticosteroid exists. Such patients should be carefully monitored during and following the administration of any corticosteroid.
 
Use this drug cautiously in patients with thromboembolic disease because corticotropin rarely has been reported to increase blood coagulability and to precipitate intravascular thrombosis, thrombophlebitis, and thromboembolism.
 
The use of corticotropin is contraindicated in patients with scleroderma, recent surgery, hypertension, and congestive heart failure. It also is contraindicated in the treatment of primary adrenal insufficiency, hypercortisolism, or any condition associated with these disorders. In addition, glucocorticoids can produce or aggravate Cushing's syndrome and therefore should be avoided in patients with Cushing's disease. Due to the sodium-retaining and potassium-wasting properties of the drug, corticotropin should be used with caution in patients with hypokalemia or hypernatremia.
 
Due to suppression of the immune system, corticotropin should not be used in patients with infection caused by bacteria, viruses, or fungi that are not controlled by antimicrobials, unless it is a life-threatening situation. The use of corticotropin is contraindicated in patients with a systemic fungal infection or ocular herpes infection. The use of corticotropin in patients with herpes simplex ocular infection can increase the risk of corneal perforation. Vaccines and immunizations should not be given during therapy with corticotropin because the drug inhibits antibody response and neurologic reactions may be aggravated. Those patients with latent tuberculosis or a positive tuberculin skin test should be observed closely during therapy with corticotropin.
 
Since corticotropin stimulates the production of endogenous corticosteroids, the drug should be used with caution in patients with GI disease, including diverticulitis, ulcerative colitis, or intestinal anastomosis (because of the possibility of perforation). The use of corticotropin is contraindicated in patients with peptic ulcer disease.
 
Corticotropin should be used with extreme caution in patients with psychosis, emotional instability, renal disease, diabetes mellitus, or seizure disorder because the drug can exacerbate these conditions. Diabetic patients may have an increased requirement for insulin or oral hypoglycemics. Patients with hypothyroidism or hepatic disease can have an exaggerated response to corticotropin or exogenous corticosteroids, so any corticosteroid should be used with caution in these patients.
 
Prolonged therapy with corticotropin causes insufficiency of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis due to suppression of corticotropin release from the pituitary. Patients on long-term therapy may require additional doses of corticosteroids when subjected to stress such as illness, infection, surgery, or trauma.
 
Although corticotropin is used to treat myasthenia gravis refractory to conventional anticholinesterase therapy, corticotropin should be used with extreme caution in these patients. During initiation of corticotropin, there is a marked decrease in muscle strength which usually requires respiratory support. Administration of corticotropin to patients with myasthenia gravis should be undertaken in an intensive care unit.
 
Prolonged therapy with corticotropin can cause atrophy of the bone protein matrix due to protein catabolism. This atrophy can cause osteoporosis, fractures, aseptic necrosis of femoral and humoral heads, and pathologic fractures of long bones. Corticotropin is contraindicated in patients with pre-existing osteoporosis. Use cautiously in elderly, debilitated, or postmenopausal patients. Also, due to retardation of bone growth, corticotropin should not be used for prolonged periods in children.
 
Corticotropin is classified pregnancy category C. Safe use of corticotropin during pregnancy has not been established. Corticotropin causes the release of endogenous corticosteroids, which have been suspected of causing fetal malformations. Corticotropin has been shown to cause fetal damage in animals. Corticotropin use should be avoided during pregnancy unless the potential therapeutic benefit justifies the added risk to the fetus.
 
It is not known whether corticotropin, ACTH is excreted in human milk. Because many drugs are excreted in human milk and due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in nursing infants, a decision should be made whether to discontinue breast-feeding or to discontinue the drug, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. The use of corticotropin is contraindicated in individuals with porcine protein hypersensitivity. Prolonged administration of corticotropin may increase the risk of hypersensitivity reactions; consider hypersensitivity if symptoms arise during therapy. Anaphylaxis has been reported with postmarketing use of corticotropin. Limited data suggest the potential for the development of antibodies to corticotropin with chronic administration along with a loss of both endogenous ACTH and corticotropin activity. Although true corticosteroid hypersensitivity is rare, individuals who have demonstrated a prior hypersensitivity reaction to corticotropin should not receive any form of corticotropin.     Headline
    heart failure, hypertension
    General Information
    Corticotropin is contraindicated in individuals with uncontrolled hypertension or congestive heart failure and should be used with caution in individuals any degree of hypertension or heart failure. Corticotropin should be used cautiously in individuals with other types of cardiac disease due to its propensity to cause water retention and hypertension and to lower serum potassium concentrations. Individuals may require potassium supplementation while receiving corticotropin.
    Headline
    decreased gastrointestinal wall integrity, diverticulitis, GI abscess, intestinal anastomosis, peptic ulcer disease
    General Information
    Since corticotropin stimulates the production of endogenous corticosteroids, the drug should be used with caution in individuals with GI disease, including in individuals where there is decreased gastrointestinal wall integrity or a possibility of impending GI perforation, GI abscess or other pyogenic infections, diverticulitis, or fresh intestinal anastomosis. The use of corticotropin is contraindicated in individuals with peptic ulcer disease.
    Headline
    scleroderma, surgery
    General Information
    The use of corticotropin is contraindicated in individuals with scleroderma or in individuals who have had recent surgery.
    Headline
    adrenocortical hyperfunction, primary adrenal insufficiency
    General Information
    Corticotropin is contraindicated in individuals with primary adrenal insufficiency, adrenocortical hyperfunction, or any condition associated with these disorders. Prolonged therapy with corticotropin causes hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) suppression due to inhibition of corticotropin release from the pituitary. Individuals on long-term therapy may require additional doses of corticosteroids when subjected to stress such as illness, infection, surgery, or trauma. Abrupt discontinuation of corticotropin, especially after prolonged therapy, can lead to adrenal insufficiency. Individuals should be monitored for symptoms of adrenal insufficiency after corticotropin therapy is discontinued; these symptoms include fatigue, anorexia, weakness, hyperpigmentation, weight loss, hypotension, and abdominal pain. Individuals and caregivers of pediatric individuals receiving corticotropin for the treatment of infantile spasms should be informed of the possibility of adrenal insufficiency when the drug is discontinued and instructed to monitor for these symptoms. Adrenal insufficiency can be minimized by tapering of the corticotropin dose prior to discontinuation. Glucocorticoids can also cause or aggravate Cushing syndrome and therefore should be avoided in individuals with Cushing disease.
    Headline
    mood disorder, myasthenia gravis, psychotic disorder, seizures
    General Information
    Corticotropin should be used with extreme caution in individuals with certain chronic or comorbid conditions because the drug may exacerbate these conditions. These include existing emotional instability or psychotic tendencies, such as in individuals with a mood disorder or psychotic disorder, neurologic diseases such as myasthenia gravis, or seizures.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism
    General Information
    Corticotropin should be used with caution in individuals with certain endocrine conditions such as diabetes mellitus and hypothyroidism. Corticotropin may exacerbate diabetes mellitus; diabetic individuals may have an increased requirement for insulin or oral hypoglycemics. Monitor individuals for hyperglycemia during and for a period of time after corticotropin therapy. Individuals with hypothyroidism can have an exaggerated response to corticotropin or exogenous corticosteroids; any corticosteroid should be used with caution in these individuals.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Due to the sodium-retaining, fluid-retaining, and potassium-wasting properties of the drug, corticotropin should be used with caution in individuals with renal impairment, renal failure, or other individuals who may be sensitive to fluid/electrolyte disturbances.
    Headline
    increased risk for osteoporosis, osteoporosis
    General Information
    Prolonged therapy with corticotropin can cause atrophy of the bone protein matrix due to protein catabolism. This atrophy can cause osteoporosis, fractures, aseptic necrosis of femoral and humoral heads, and pathologic fractures of long bones. Corticotropin is contraindicated in individuals with pre-existing osteoporosis. Special consideration should be given to individuals at increased risk for osteoporosis (e.g., postmenopausal females).
    Headline
    hepatic cirrhosis
    General Information
    Corticotropin, ACTH should be used with caution in individuals with hepatic cirrhosis, as there is an enhanced effect.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Corticotropin, ACTH may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy through stimulation of endogenous corticosteroid production. Use of systemic corticosteroids during pregnancy has been associated with intrauterine growth restriction, decreased birth weight, and preterm birth; however, the underlying maternal condition may also contribute to these outcomes. Hypoadrenalism has been reported in infants born to people who received high-dose and/or prolonged corticosteroid therapy during pregnancy. Corticotropin, ACTH has not been adequately evaluated in animal studies to characterize the potential risk of use during pregnancy. Adrenocortical disease is uncommon during pregnancy, as most cases are diagnosed before pregnancy; however, trimester-specific normal cortisol responses to ACTH stimulation have been established. Because adrenal disease can be associated with significant morbidity, prompt and accurate diagnosis is important. Use of cosyntropin to confirm suspected adrenal insufficiency during pregnancy has been described in the literature.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero should be closely monitored for signs of hypoadrenalism, such as poor feeding, irritability, weakness, and vomiting. If present, manage accordingly.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use corticotropin, ACTH with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of corticotropin, ACTH in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. However, due to its large molecular weight and short half-life, corticotropin, ACTH is unlikely to be present in human milk in clinically relevant amounts. Absorption by a breastfed child is also unlikely, as corticotropin would be expected to undergo degradation in the child's gastrointestinal tract. Animal data suggest that administration of corticotropin to a lactating patient may increase cortisol concentrations in human milk. Alternative therapies to consider may include prednisone or methylprednisolone.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Adverse effects associated with short-term use of corticotropin usually are not harmful, however, long term administration may result in detrimental effects. Administration of corticotropin can produce skin atrophy and thinning, acne vulgaris, diaphoresis, facial erythema, petechiae, ecchymosis, and easy bruising. The skin hyperpigmentation and androgenic effects (e.g., hirsutism, amenorrhea, and acne) are greater for corticotropin than for corticosteroids. An injection site reaction may occur also and consists of transient local induration, pain, and abscesses. Necrotizing vasculitis has been reported during corticotropin therapy.Corticotropin therapy may produce a hypersensitivity reaction even in patients who have not received the drug previously. The reactions usually manifest as skin reactions (pruritus, scarlatiniform exanthema, urticaria), dizziness, nausea/vomiting, and mild fever. In some cases, anaphylactic shock, wheezing, circulatory failure, and death have occurred. These hypersensitivity reactions may be caused by an impurity in the preparation or the drug itself. Prolonged administration increases the risk of hypersensitivity reactions.Posterior subcapsular cataracts (especially in children), exophthalmos, or ocular hypertension can be produced by corticotropin therapy, which can result in glaucoma or damage to the optic nerve. In some cases, children receiving corticotropin have developed pseudotumor cerebri (increased intracranial pressure) which may cause papilledema. Pseudotumor cerebri occurred most frequently following a dosage reduction or immediately after discontinuation of corticotropin therapy. Oculomotor or abducens nerve paralysis, visual loss, and headache may also occur.Corticotropin can produce hypernatremia, hypocalcemia, hypophosphatemia, and/or hypokalemia as a result of its mineralocorticoid activity. Edema, hypokalemia, hypertension, and hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis usually result from sodium retention. Congestive heart failure can also develop in some patients.Corticotropin therapy can produce mental status changes such as euphoria, emotional lability, depression, insomnia, personality changes, and frank psychosis. The drug can aggravate emotional instability or psychotic tendencies. Other neurologic effects include vertigo, dizziness, seizures or convulsions, and EEG changes.Chronic administration of exogenous corticotropin can result in suppression of corticotropin release from the pituitary, leading to hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) suppression. The duration and extent of HPA suppression is highly variable and depends on the dose, frequency and time of administration, and duration of therapy. Unlike the corticosteroids which cause adrenocortical atrophy, corticotropin causes adrenal hyperplasia. Various endocrine disorders, such as hypercorticism (Cushing's syndrome), menstrual irregularity, and hyperglycemia, can also occur with prolonged therapy. In addition, a non-HPA withdrawal syndrome can occur following abrupt discontinuance of corticosteroid therapy, and it apparently is unrelated to adrenocortical insufficiency. This syndrome includes symptoms such as anorexia, lethargy, nausea/vomiting, headache, fever, arthralgia, myalgia, desquamation, weight loss, and hypotension. These effects are believed to be due to the sudden change in glucocorticoid concentration rather than to low corticosteroid levels.Glucocorticoids are responsible for protein metabolism, and prolonged therapy can result in various musculoskeletal manifestations, including: myopathy (myalgia, muscle wasting, muscle weakness), impaired wound healing, bone matrix atrophy (osteoporosis), bone fractures such as vertebral compression fractures or fractures of long bones, and avascular necrosis of femoral or humoral heads. These effects are more likely to occur in older or debilitated patients. Glucocorticoids interact with calcium metabolism at many sites, including: decreasing the synthesis by osteoblasts of the principle proteins of bone matrix, malabsorption of calcium in both the nephron and the gut, and reduction of sex hormone concentrations. Although all of these actions probably contribute to glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis, the actions on osteoblasts is most important. Glucocorticoids do not modify vitamin D metabolism. Postmenopausal women, in particular, should be monitored for signs of osteoporosis during corticosteroid therapy. Because of retardation of bone growth, children receiving prolonged corticosteroid therapy may have growth inhibition. Intra-articular injections of corticosteroids can cause Charcot-like arthropathy and post-injection flare. Atrophy at the site of injection has been reported following administration of soluble glucocorticoids. Tendon rupture has also been reported.Corticotropin can cause GI effects such as nausea/vomiting, abdominal distension, ulcerative esophagitis, and pancreatitis. The manufacturers state that corticotropin should not be used in patients with peptic ulcer or a history of peptic ulcer, however, in a published review of 93 studies of corticosteroid use, the incidence of peptic ulcer disease was not found to be higher in steroid recipients compared to control groups. While most of these studies did not utilize endoscopy, it is unlikely that corticosteroids or corticotropin contribute to the development of peptic ulcer disease. Dyspepsia also occurs with corticotropin, but at a lesser frequency than with corticosteroids.

  Corticotropin (ACTH) is a parenteral preparation of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). Corticotropin is a hormone naturally secreted by cells in the anterior lobe of the pituitary gland; corticotropin for commercial use is a highly purified sterile product extracted from the pituitary glands of pigs. In some situations, cosyntropin, a synthetic polypeptide containing the same first 24-amino acid sequence as natural corticotropin and with less antigenicity, has replaced corticotropin, primarily as an aid in the diagnosis of adrenocortical insufficiency. Corticotropin is indicated for numerous autoimmune, allergic, and inflammatory conditions; however, the corticotropin repository injection is most commonly used in adult patients for multiple sclerosis ; other indications are usually treated with prednisone, methylprednisolone or more commonly used systemic corticosteroids. In pediatric patients, corticotropin is most commonly used for the treatment of infantile spasms infants and children less than 2 years of age. Due to its derivation, the drug is contraindicated in patients with porcine protein hypersensitivity.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Corticotropin and endogenous ACTH stimulates steroidogenesis and the release of cortisol (hydrocortisone), corticosterone, and weak androgens from the adrenal cortex. The physiologic and pharmacologic effects of corticotropin are due primarily to the glucocorticoid cortisol, which also has some mineralocorticoid activity. Prolonged administration of large doses of corticotropin induces hyperplasia and hypertrophy of the adrenal cortex and continuous high output of cortisol, corticosterone and weak androgens. The release of endogenous ACTH is under the influence of the nervous system via the regulatory hormone released from the hypothalamus and by a negative corticosteroid feedback mechanism. Elevated plasma cortisol suppresses ACTH release. The antiinflammatory actions of corticosteroids are thought to involve phospholipase A2 inhibitory proteins, collectively called lipocortins; lipocortins control the biosynthesis of potent mediators of inflammation such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes by inhibiting the release of the precursor molecule arachidonic acid. Other effects of corticotropin include extra-adrenal effects such as binding to melanocortin receptors.
 
Although oral glucocorticoids do not depend on adrenal function for effectiveness, have a more predictable profile, and have easily regulated doses for patient-specific therapy, corticotropin therapy is preferred by some clinicians in certain conditions.
 
The mechanism of action of corticotropin for the treatment of infantile spasms is unknown.

Pharmacokinetics
  Special Populations

    Hepatic Impairment

    There is an enhanced effect of corticotropin (ACTH) in patients with cirrhosis of the liver.
  Text
  Corticotropin (ACTH) is administered subcutaneously and intramuscularly. Corticotropin is available as a repository corticotropin injection (RCI). ACTH rapidly disappears from the circulation following its intravenous administration; in humans, the plasma half-life is about 15 minutes. The pharmacokinetics of the repository corticotropin injection have not been adequately characterized. The maximal effects of a trophic hormone on a target organ are achieved when optimal amounts of hormone are acting continuously. Thus, a fixed dose of the repository corticotropin injection will demonstrate a linear increase in adrenocortical secretion with increasing duration of an infusion.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: None known
