Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General dosing and discontinuation recommendations
The risks and benefits of ongoing benzodiazepine use should be assessed every 3 months or as clinically indicated.
Those who are candidates for discontinuation should be assessed for risks of withdrawal prior to stopping the medication; individuals who are likely to be physically dependent on the benzodiazepine should be tapered off the medication rather than having an abrupt discontinuation. While most tapers can be managed outpatient, patients at imminent risk of harm related to continued use (e.g., drug interactions, falls, or overdose), those with concomitant physical or mental health conditions that may complicate the safe tapering, and those with current or anticipated severe or complicated withdrawals may require inpatient care.
Tapering strategies should be patient-specific and may be adjusted as needed to address withdrawal symptoms. Initial dose reductions of 5% are recommended, with an assessment of tolerability made. In general, the pace of benzodiazepine tapering should include dose reductions of 5% to 10% every 2 to 4 weeks and should not exceed 25% every 2 weeks.
Individuals at risk of physical dependence, such as those taking a higher dose for more than a year, may require a conservative taper of 5% to 10% every 6 to 8 weeks or slower, depending on symptoms. Those with lower risks of physical dependence or who tolerate the initial dose reduction may be able to reduce the dose by 10% to 25% every 2 to 4 weeks.
If withdrawal symptoms occur and interfere with the taper (e.g., sleep difficulty or anxiety), the taper may be paused or further slowed. Adjunctive medications may also be considered.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Oxazepam is contraindicated in any patient with a known oxazepam hypersensitivity or known allergies to any component of the formulation. Some tablet preparations of oxazepam contain tartrazine dye (FD&C yellow 5) that can cause allergic reactions or bronchial asthma in patients with tartrazine dye hypersensitivity. This reaction occurs more frequently in patients who are allergic to aspirin. Patients with a benzodiazepine hypersensitivity to other benzodiazepines may experience cross-sensitivity to oxazepam.
 
Oxazepam is not indicated for use in patients with psychosis. Oxazepam may be beneficial for patients with major depression; in contrast, other benzodiazepines have been reported to worsen depression. Oxazepam should be administered cautiously and in minimal quantities, if at all, to patients with suicidal ideation. Oxazepam should be used cautiously in patients with bipolar disorder because mania and hypomania have been reported in conjunction with the use of benzodiazepines in depressive disorders.
 
Due to CNS depression, patients should be cautioned against driving or operating machinery until they know how oxazepam may affect them. Some patients may experience excessive sedation and impaired ability to perform tasks. Increased CNS effects may be seen with concurrent use of oxazepam and other CNS depressant agents (see Drug Interactions), and in patients with acute ethanol intoxication or organic brain syndromes (i.e., dementia). Although oxazepam is indicated for acute ethanol withdrawal, patients with ethanol intoxication who have also consumed oxazepam have an increased risk of respiratory depression and coma. Ethanol should be avoided during treatment with oxazepam. Oxazepam should be used with extreme caution in patients with respiratory depression, shock, pulmonary disease such as severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or sleep apnea because the drug can exacerbate ventilatory failure. Anterograde amnesia has been reported to occur with benzodiazepines.
 
Oxazepam can cause physical and psychological dependence, and should be used with extreme caution in patients with known, suspected, or a history of substance abuse or benzodiazepine dependence. Abrupt discontinuation of oxazepam after prolonged use can cause seizures in susceptible patients. Patients with a history of a seizure disorder or who are taking other drugs that lower the seizure threshold (e.g., bupropion, tricyclic antidepressants, phenothiazines) should not be withdrawn abruptly from benzodiazepines due to the risk of precipitating a seizure; status epilepticus has also been reported. The severity of withdrawal symptoms may be associated with higher doses. Abrupt discontinuation of benzodiazepine therapy has been reported to cause withdrawal symptoms such as irritability, nervousness, and insomnia. Benzodiazepine withdrawal is more likely to occur following abrupt cessation after excessive or prolonged doses, but it can occur following the discontinuance of therapeutic doses administered for as few as 1—2 weeks. Benzodiazepine withdrawal also can be more intense if the benzodiazepine involved possesses a relatively short duration of action such as alprazolam or oxazepam. Oxazepam should be withdrawn cautiously and slowly, using a very gradual dosage-tapering schedule. During withdrawal, the greatest risk of seizure appears to be during the first 24 to 72 hours.
 
Oxazepam should be used with extreme caution in patients with myasthenia gravis because the drug can exacerbate this condition.
 
The administration of oxazepam can exacerbate acute intermittent porphyria, so the drug should be used with caution in patients with this condition.
 
Oxazepam is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category D because it can cause harm to the fetus when administered to pregnant women. Positive evidence of human fetal risk exists based on investigational, marketing, or human studies with other benzodiazepines. Because the use of oxazepam in pregnancy is rarely a matter of urgency, its use during this period should almost always be avoided. The patient should be apprised of the potential risk to the fetus if she should become pregnant during oxazepam therapy. It should be anticipated that neonates may experience withdrawal symptoms and ventilatory depression if the mother has been using benzodiazepines late in pregnancy. Neonatal flaccidity has been reported in an infant whose mother was using benzodiazepines. Oxazepam has no established use in labor or obstetric delivery.
 
Oxazepam might be distributed into breast milk, causing sedation, feeding difficulties, and weight loss in the nursing infant. The use of oxazepam during breast-feeding is generally not recommended.
 
The elimination of oxazepam has not been shown to be significantly impaired in hepatic disease; however, cautious use is suggested as the drug undergoes extensive metabolism. Begin dosage at the low end of the dosage range in patients with liver dysfunction and adjust according to individual patient response and tolerance. Particular caution should be observed in patients with alcoholism.
 
Oxazepam's elimination has not been shown to be significantly impaired in renal disease, but should be used with caution in patients with severe renal impairment or renal failure. Begin dosage at the low end of the dosage range in patients with kidney dysfunction and adjust according to individual patient response and tolerance.
 
The clearance and/or elimination of many drugs are reduced in the elderly. Delayed elimination in the elderly can either intensify or prolong the actions of adverse reactions of the drug. Benzodiazepine use has also been associated with falls in the elderly. Hypotension or orthostatic hypotension have been reported rarely with oxazepam and may contribute to falls or cardiac complications. Use oxazepam cautiously in patients with cardiac disease, cardiac arrhythmias, cerebrovascular disease, stroke or those who cannot tolerate shifts in blood pressure. The impairment of cognitive and motor function may be more marked in this patient group and lower initial dosage is recommended together with close monitoring (see Dosage). Federal OBRA regulations recommend maximum dosage limits for anxiolytic and hypnotic use in the nursing home resident (see Dosage).
 
Patients with late stage Parkinson's disease may experience worsening of their psychosis or impaired cognition with administration of benzodiazepines. Benzodiazepines may also cause incoordination or paradoxical reactions that may worsen symptoms of Parkinson's disease.
 
The safe and effective use of oxazepam in infants and children less than 6 years old has not been established and an absolute dose for children between 6—12 years has not been established. Children are generally more sensitive to the CNS effects of the benzodiazepines.
 
Many benzodiazepines are contraindicated in patients with acute closed-angle glaucoma. However, the manufacturers of oxazepam do not contraindicate use of the drug in any type of glaucoma. The mechanistic rational for this contraindication has been questioned, as benzodiazepines do not have antimuscarinic activity and do not raise intraocular pressure. Benzodiazepines may be used in patients with open-angle glaucoma who are receiving appropriate therapy.

    General Information
    Benzodiazepines may worsen depression. Appropriate precautions (e.g., limiting the total prescription size and increased monitoring for suicidal ideation) should be considered in patients with depression.
    Headline
    coadministration with certain medications, pulmonary disease, respiratory insufficiency
    General Information
    Deaths have been reported in people with severe pulmonary disease after initiation of benzodiazepines; closely monitor patients with impaired respiratory function (respiratory insufficiency). If signs and symptoms of respiratory depression, hypoventilation, or apnea occur, discontinue oxazepam. Oxazepam coadministration with certain medications, such as opioids, may result in serious adverse effects, including profound sedation, CNS depression, respiratory depression, coma, and death. The concomitant use of these medications has demonstrated an increased risk of drug-related mortality compared to the use of opioids alone. The combined use of opioids and benzodiazepines should be reserved for patients in whom alternative treatment options are inadequate. If the decision is made to prescribe oxazepam concomitantly with opioids, the lowest effective dose should be prescribed for the shortest duration of use, and patients should be followed closely for signs and symptoms of respiratory depression and sedation. Advise patients and caregivers about the risks of respiratory depression and sedation when oxazepam is used with opioids.  Due to the risks associated with concomitant use, the ASAM clinical practice guidelines suggest re-evaluation of the risks and benefits of continued benzodiazepine prescribing in patients taking opioids at least every 3 months or with every related clinical encounter or prescription renewal, whichever is more frequent.
    Headline
    abrupt discontinuation, increased risk for benzodiazepine withdrawal, substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    The use of benzodiazepines, including oxazepam, exposes users to serious adverse events, including risks of abuse, misuse, and addiction, which can lead to overdose or death. Abuse and misuse of benzodiazepines commonly involve concomitant use of other medications, alcohol, and/or illicit substances, which can increase the frequency of serious adverse outcomes. Before prescribing and throughout treatment, assess each patient's risk for abuse, misuse, and addiction using a standardized screening tool. Counsel patients about the risks and proper use of oxazepam and prescribe the lowest effective dose to reduce excess exposure and advise patients on proper disposal of unused medication. Minimize the concomitant use of CNS depressants or other substances associated with abuse, misuse, and addiction (e.g., opioid analgesics, stimulants). If a substance abuse disorder is suspected, evaluate the patient and institute or refer them for early treatment. Chronic, continued use of benzodiazepines may also lead to physical benzodiazepine dependence. The risk of dependence increases with longer treatment duration and higher daily doses and may lead to an increased risk for benzodiazepine withdrawal upon discontinuation. Abrupt discontinuation or rapid dose reduction can precipitate acute withdrawal reactions, which can be life-threatening. Patients with a history of a seizure disorder should not be withdrawn abruptly from benzodiazepines due to the risk of precipitating seizures; status epilepticus has also been reported. Clinicians should be aware that the use of flumazenil may increase the risk of seizures, particularly in long-term users of benzodiazepines. To reduce the risk of withdrawal reactions, use a gradual, patient-specific taper to reduce the dose or discontinue oxazepam. In some cases, patients using benzodiazepines have developed a protracted withdrawal syndrome with symptoms lasting weeks to more than 12 months.

    Oxazepam has the potential to impair judgment, thinking, or motor skills. Individuals should use caution when driving or operating machinery or participating in other activities requiring coordination and concentration until they know how oxazepam may affect them. Alcohol may further impair cognitive and motor skills and can increase the risk of serious CNS or respiratory depression and alcohol should be avoided during oxazepam treatment.

    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Oxazepam should be used cautiously in geriatric adults since they are more sensitive to the effects of benzodiazepines; lower initial doses and slower titration are recommended. Because oxazepam can cause drowsiness and a decreased level of consciousness, there is a higher risk of falls, particularly in the elderly, with the potential for subsequent severe injuries.The Beers Criteria state that benzodiazepines are potentially inappropriate medications in geriatric adults with a history of delirium, dementia, or a history of falls or fractures; benzodiazepines increase the risk of cognitive impairment, delirium, falls, fractures, and motor vehicle accidents in older adults and avoidance is generally recommended, with some exceptions for severe generalized anxiety disorder and other approved indications. If a benzodiazepine must be used, consider reducing the use of other CNS-active medications and implement other strategies to reduce fall risk. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates anxiolytic and sedative/hypnotic use in long-term care facilities. When a drug is used to induce sleep, treat a sleep disorder, manage behavior, stabilize mood, or treat a psychiatric disorder, tapering as outlined in the OBRA guidelines should be attempted unless clinically contraindicated. Dosages and durations of treatment used should align with prescribing labels, published literature recommendations, and expert guidelines.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Published data from observational studies of benzodiazepine use during pregnancy have not established a drug-associated risk of major birth defects. Early studies suggested an increased risk of congenital malformations with diazepam and chlordiazepoxide use. However, more recent case-control and cohort studies of benzodiazepine use during pregnancy, adjusted for confounding exposures to other substances, have not confirmed these findings. Benzodiazepines cross the placenta. Neonates exposed to benzodiazepines during late pregnancy, including during labor and delivery, may experience symptoms of sedation. Those with prolonged exposure during late pregnancy may experience withdrawal after delivery. The incidence, time to onset, and duration of sedation and withdrawal are multifactorial (e.g., duration of use, drug lipophilicity, placental disposition, degree of accumulation in neonatal tissues). Individuals who become pregnant or intend to become pregnant while taking oxazepam should be advised to discuss the possibility of discontinuing the drug with their physician. If a benzodiazepine is required during pregnancy, avoid first trimester administration if possible, consider short-acting agents, limit treatment to the shortest possible duration and lowest effective dose, and discontinue the drug well before delivery. There is a pregnancy registry that monitors pregnancy outcomes in people exposed to oxazepam or other psychiatric medications during pregnancy. Healthcare providers are encouraged to register patients by calling the National Pregnancy Registry for Psychiatric Medications at 1-866-961-2388 or visiting online at https://womensmentalhealth.org/research/pregnancyregistry/.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Monitor neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero for symptoms of withdrawal, such as hyperreflexia, irritability, restlessness, tremors, inconsolable crying, and feeding difficulties. In neonates exposed during late pregnancy, including during labor and delivery, monitor for sedation, including respiratory depression, lethargy, and hypotonia. If symptoms are present, manage accordingly.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use oxazepam with caution during breast-feeding. Oxazepam is present in human milk at low concentrations. It also has a short half-life compared to many other benzodiazepines, making it unlikely to cause adverse effects in a breastfed child. If used, monitor exposed children for excessive sedation, slowed breathing rate, poor feeding, and inadequate weight gain. Withdrawal may occur in those with long-term exposure to the drug. This may occur with cessation of breast-feeding or cessation of the medication in the breast-feeding person. In one case report, the concentration of oxazepam in breast milk was about one-tenth to one-third of that in the patient's plasma during the use of oxazepam 30 mg/day. The estimated infant exposure was less than 0.001% of the administered dose. In a retrospective cohort study of breast-feeding during use of a benzodiazepine, sedation was not reported in any infant exposed to oxazepam through breast milk; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the small percentage of lactating individuals enrolled.

    General Information
    Prolonged use of benzodiazepines can produce physiological dependence with or without psychological dependence. The risk of psychological dependence increases in those with a history of alcohol or substance abuse. Abuse and misuse of benzodiazepines commonly involve concomitant use of other medications, alcohol, and/or illicit substances, which is associated with an increased frequency of serious adverse outcomes, including respiratory depression, overdose, and death. Avoid or minimize concomitant use of CNS depressants or other medications associated with addiction or abuse. Abrupt discontinuation or rapid dosage reduction of benzodiazepines after continued use may precipitate acute withdrawal reactions, which can be life-threatening. Withdrawal symptoms may range from mild dysphoria and insomnia to a major withdrawal syndrome including abdominal and muscle cramps, vomiting, sweating, tremors, and seizures. In some cases, benzodiazepine users have developed a protracted withdrawal syndrome with withdrawal symptoms lasting weeks to more than 12 months. Use a gradual, patient-specific dosage taper to lower the risk of withdrawal reactions when reducing the dose or discontinuing therapy. If significant withdrawal symptoms occur, the rate of reduction may need to be slowed or paused. Adjunctive medications may also be considered to help mitigate withdrawal effects.

Description
  Text
  Oxazepam is an oral benzodiazepine used for the management of symptoms associated with anxiety disorders and for short-term amelioration of the symptoms of anxiety or anxiety associated with depression. In general, benzodiazepines should be prescribed for short periods only due to the potential for dependence and cognitive impairment, and the availability of safer alternatives. When treating anxiety disorders such as panic disorder, benzodiazepines are generally reserved for the temporary management of anxiety symptoms as an adjunct to first-line pharmacotherapies (e.g., SSRI or SNRI) or for treatment-resistant cases. The ASAM guidelines recommend re-evaluating the risks and benefits of ongoing benzodiazepine use at least every 3 months; for patients who are candidates for discontinuation, an individualized taper strategy should be used that considers dose, duration, and frequency of use. Oxazepam is often a preferred benzodiazepine in treating geriatric adults or individuals with liver disease, due to its relatively short half-life and lack of active metabolites. 68581 If possible, avoid coadministration of benzodiazepines and opioids due to the potential for profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death; patients receiving the combination should be monitored for respiratory depression and sedation. Oxazepam was approved by the FDA in 1965.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: Benzodiazepines act at the level of the limbic, thalamic, and hypothalamic regions of the CNS and can produce any level of CNS depression required including sedation, hypnosis, skeletal muscle relaxation, and anticonvulsant activity. Recent evidence indicates that benzodiazepines exert their effects through enhancement of the gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-benzodiazepine receptor complex. GABA is an inhibitory neurotransmitter that exerts its effects at specific receptor subtypes designated GABA-A and GABA-B. GABA-A is the primary receptor subtype in the CNS and is thought to be involved in the actions of anxiolytics and sedatives.Specific benzodiazepine receptor subtypes are thought to be coupled to GABA-A receptors. Three types of BNZ receptors are located in the CNS and other tissues; the BNZ1 receptors are located in the cerebellum and cerebral cortex, the BNZ2 receptors in the cerebral cortex and spinal cord, and the BNZ3 receptors in peripheral tissues. Activation of the BNZ1 receptor is thought to mediate sleep while the BNZ2 receptor affects muscle relaxation, anticonvulsant activity, motor coordination, and memory. Benzodiazepines bind nonspecifically to BNZ1 and BNZ2 which ultimately enhances the effects of GABA. Unlike barbiturates which augment GABA responses by increasing the length of time that chloride channels are open, benzodiazepines enhance the effects of GABA by increasing GABA affinity for the GABA receptor. Binding of GABA to the site opens the chloride channel resulting in a hyperpolarized cell membrane that prevents further excitation of the cell.Benzodiazepines alleviate insomnia by decreasing the latency to sleep and increasing sleep continuity and total sleep time through their effects on GABA.
