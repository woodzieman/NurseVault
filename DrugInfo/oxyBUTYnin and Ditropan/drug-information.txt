Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  All formulations of oxybutynin are contraindicated in patients with known hypersensitivity to oxybutynin; topical and transdermal formulations are also contraindicated in patients with known skin hypersensitivity to oxybutynin or other components of the products.
 
Due to its anticholinergic effects, oxybutynin should be used with caution in some patient populations. Oxybutynin is contraindicated in patients with urinary retention. Anticholinergics may precipitate urinary retention in patients with preexisting urinary tract obstruction or prostatic hypertrophy. In addition, oxybutynin should be given with caution to patients with renal impairment.
 
Oxybutynin is contraindicated in patients with uncontrolled narrow or closed-angle glaucoma. Oxybutynin may increase intraocular pressure and aqueous outflow resistance in patients with closed-angle glaucoma. The anticholinergic effects of oxybutynin may make the eyes dry, and this can cause irritation for wearers of contact lenses. The effects of oxybutynin may be additive to other anticholinergic medications.
 
Topical gels are typically flammable, therefore exposure to fire, flame, and tobacco smoking should be avoided until the gel has dried.
 
Transfer of topical gel oxybutynin may occur from person to person when vigorous skin-to-skin contact is made with the application site. Patients should be advised to cover the site of application if exposure is expected.
 
Because oxybutynin has a direct antispasmodic effect on smooth muscle that can delay gastric emptying, it is contraindicated for use in patients with gastric retention (e.g., gastroparesis, GI obstruction, pyloric stenosis). Oxybutynin is not recommended for use in the setting of ileus, toxic megacolon, or severe ulcerative colitis; oxybutynin can precipitate a paralytic ileus. Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) may be aggravated by the use of anticholinergics. The controlled-release formulation should be used cautiously in patients with pre-existing gastrointestinal narrowing; reports of obstructive symptoms in patients with known esophageal stricture in association with the ingestion of other drugs in nondeformable controlled-release formulations have been reported.
 
The anticholinergic action of oxybutynin may exacerbate the clinical symptoms of patients with myasthenia gravis or autonomic neuropathy.
 
According to the updated Beers criteria (2003) for inappropriate medication use in older adults, the immediate-release form of oxybutynin is not recommended for use in the elderly; however, the extended-release formulation of oxybutynin is considered to have less adverse effects in the elderly. When needed, oxybutynin should be used cautiously in geriatric patients. Clinical trials have reported that overall, anticholinergic side effects of oxybutynin are similar in young adults and in those over the age of 65 years. However, some elderly adults may be more susceptible to anticholinergic side effects of the drug.
 
Cardiac effects of antimuscarinic drugs are often unpredictable. Patients with cardiac disease, such as coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, cardiac arrhythmias, tachycardia, and hypertension can be negatively affected by the antimuscarinic action of oxybutynin.
 
Oxybutynin should be given with caution to patients who have hepatic disease. Oxybutynin is extensively metabolized in the liver to both active and inactive metabolites and dosage adjustments may be needed in these patients.
 
Patients with hyperthyroidism should be treated with caution because oxybutynin can aggravate this condition.
 
Although oxybutynin is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category B, safe use during pregnancy has not been established. Use during pregnancy should be determined by whether the benefits outweigh the risks.
 
It is not known if oxybutynin is excreted in breast milk. Because infants are usually more sensitive to anticholinergic effects of drugs, they may be at risk for adverse effects if exposed to oxybutynin. In addition, lactation suppression has been reported during post-marketing use of immediate-release oxybutynin. The frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established. A decision to discontinue oxybutynin or discontinue breast-feeding should be made, taking into account the importance of the medication to the lactating mother.
 
Insensitivity to heat (fever, diaphoresis) may occur when anticholinergics are administered in the presence of high environmental temperatures. Patients should avoid dehydration, undue sunlight (UV) exposure, or exposure to extremes of heat to avoid heat stroke.
 
Anticholinergic medications may cause blurred vision, drowsiness, or dizziness, especially with initial use. Patients should use caution when driving or operating machinery until they know how oxybutynin affects them. Ethanol intoxication can magnify sedative effects and is best avoided.
 
Oxybutynin should be used with caution in young children, who may be more susceptible to adverse anticholinergic effects than older children or adults. Oxybutynin is approved for use in children with overactivity of the bladder detrusor muscle. Safety and efficacy of the extended-release product (Ditropan XL) have been established in children >= 6 years of age. Do not use the extended-release product in pediatric patients who cannot swallow the tablet whole without chewing, dividing, or crushing.
 
The anticholinergic action of oxybutynin may exacerbate the clinical symptoms of patients with dementia. Therefore, oxybutynin should be used cautiously in this patient population. Close monitoring is advisable if treatment is necessary.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. There is little evidence in the literature regarding cross-reactivity among the antimuscarinics for overactive bladder (OAB) if an allergic response has previously been noted, unless the medications share chemical similarities, like chemical structure or similar metabolites. Some publications recommend avoidance of other agents in the class if a serious allergic response has occurred.

    bladder obstruction, prostatic hypertrophy, urinary retention
    General Information
    Oxybutynin use is contraindicated in people with urinary retention. Older adults and people with clinically significant bladder obstruction or incomplete emptying, such as those with benign prostatic hypertrophy (BPH), may have increased risk for urinary retention. Monitor oxybutynin recipients for symptoms of urinary retention and consider discontinuation of treatment if urinary retention occurs.
    Headline
    gastroesophageal reflux disease, gastroparesis, GI obstruction, hiatal hernia, ulcerative colitis
    General Information
    Oxybutynin is contraindicated for use in people with gastric retention (e.g., gastroparesis). Oxybutynin should be used with caution in people with gastrointestinal (GI) obstructive disorders (GI obstruction) due to the risk of gastric retention. Like other anticholinergic drugs, oxybutynin may decrease GI motility and should be used with caution in people with conditions associated with decreased GI motility, such as ulcerative colitis or intestinal atony. Oxybutynin should also be used with caution in people with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), hiatal hernia, or in patients concurrently taking drugs that can exacerbate esophagitis. Caution is advised when administering extended-release oxybutynin tablets to patients with pre-existing severe gastrointestinal narrowing (pathologic or iatrogenic). There have been rare reports of obstructive symptoms in patients with known esophageal stricture in association with the ingestion of other nondeformable controlled-release drug formulations.

Extended-release oxybutynin should be used with caution in people with autonomic neuropathy due to the risk of aggravation of symptoms of decreased gastrointestinal motility.

    Parkinson disease
    General Information
    Use oxybutynin with caution in people with Parkinson disease because the anticholinergic and central nervous system effects may aggravate symptoms. Close monitoring is advisable if treatment with an anticholinergic agent such as oxybutynin is necessary.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Oxybutynin recipients should be advised to avoid driving or operating machinery or activities requiring coordination and concentration until they know how oxybutynin will affect them. Anticholinergic drugs, including oxybutynin, are associated with anticholinergic central nervous system (CNS) effects, including confusion, dizziness, somnolence, and blurred vision. Monitor for signs of anticholinergic CNS effects, particularly after initiating oxybutynin treatment or with a dosage increase. Consider a dosage reduction or discontinuation of oxybutynin if a patient experiences adverse CNS effects.

    General Information
    Use oral oxybutynin (immediate and extended-release) and the 10% oxybutynin topical gel with caution in people with hepatic failure/impairment and in patients with renal impairment or renal failure. Extended-release oxybutynin and the 10% topical gel have not been studied in patients with hepatic impairment. Oxybutynin is not significantly eliminated by the kidney (less than 0.1% unchanged), but has not been specifically studied in patients with renal impairment.

The Beers Criteria state that antimuscarinics with strong anticholinergic properties, such as oxybutynin, are considered potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) in geriatric adults with dementia/cognitive impairment (adverse CNS effects) or delirium/high risk of delirium (new-onset or worsening delirium).

    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are limited data on the use of oxybutynin during pregnancy to evaluate for a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. Animal reproduction studies have shown no definite evidence of fetal harm. Data from a retrospective claims-based cohort study (n = 138,271) suggests that people exposed to anticholinergics for overactive bladder during pregnancy (n = 479) have a higher risk of pregnancy complications, such as preterm delivery or low birth weight compared to those who did not receive a prescription for these medications (RR 1.65; 95% CI 1.4 to 1.95; p less than 0.01). However, the risk of congenital malformations was not significantly different between the two groups (RR 1.24; 95% CI 0.85 to 1.8; p = 0.26). Oxybutynin was the most common medication used (84%), followed by solifenacin (7%), and tolterodine (5%), with 95% of exposures in the first trimester, 30% in the second trimester, and 19% in the third trimester. Based on dispensed prescriptions, the median length of exposure was 60 days (IQR 30 to 90 days).
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use oxybutynin with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of oxybutynin in human milk, its effects on a breast-fed child, or its effects on milk production. Lactation suppression has been reported with postmarketing use. Long-term use of oxybutynin may reduce milk production or milk letdown. If used long-term, monitor for signs of decreased lactation.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Most adverse reactions associated with oxybutynin are secondary to the anticholinergic and antispasmolytic activity of the drug. An atropine-like anticholinergic effect of oxybutynin can produce xerostomia, regardless of formulation used: an incidence of 71.4% with immediate-release tablets, 29—61% with extended-release tablets, 4.1—9.6% with the transdermal patch, and 29% with the topical gel. Other gastrointestinal (GI) side effects related to the antimuscarinic actions of oxybutynin on GI smooth muscle may be troublesome for patients. The most common GI-related side effects reported during studies with oxybutynin immediate-release tablets (5—20 mg/day) include constipation (15.1%), nausea/vomiting (11.6%), and dyspepsia (6%). Other GI-related side effects reported by 1 to 1% of patients reported abdominal pain, nausea, and flatulence. In patients applying the topical gel formulation, constipation (1.3%) was reported. Viral gastroenteritis (2.1%) was reported among patients using oxybutynin topical gel during clinical studies.
 
Severe allergic reactions, including urticaria, occur with antimuscarinic drugs and can develop in patients who are susceptible to such reactions. Oxybutynin should be discontinued if such reactions occur. Dermatologic effects reported in 1 to < 5% of patients receiving orally administered oxybutynin in clinical studies included xerosis and pruritus. Rash (unspecified) has been reported during post-marketing use; however, the frequency is unknown. Decreased sweating (hypohidrosis) from the suppression of sweat gland activity has also been reported during post-marketing use of oral oxybutynin. In some cases, fever may be indicative of a rapid rise in body temperature and is of concern in areas where the environmental temperature is high; heat stroke could be precipitated by oxybutynin.
 
The most common adverse effects associated with the use of topical oxybutynin formulations (i.e., the transdermal patch and topical gel) are localized, application site reactions. The most common adverse reaction to the oxybutynin transdermal patch is application site pruritus (14—16.8%) followed by erythema (5.6—8.3%). During clinical evaluation, severe application site reactions were reported by 5—6.4% of patients treated with the transdermal patch and was the leading cause of discontinuation. Vesicular rash (3.2%), macules (2.5%), and rash (unspecified) (3.3%) were also reported. The most common local adverse reactions to oxybutynin topical gel were application site reactions (5.4%) and included application site pruritus (2.1%), dermatitis (1.8%), papules, anesthesia, erythema, skin irritation, and pain. General pruritis (1.3%) was also noted by investigators to be reasonably associated with the use of the topical gel. The most common adverse reaction leading to drug discontinuation was application site reaction.
 
Cough, nasal dryness, pharyngitis, nasopharyngitis, or rhinitis may occur due to drying of respiratory secretions or other anticholinergic actions on the nasal mucosa. Infectious adverse events reported in 1 to < 5% of patients receiving immediate-release oxybutynin in clinical studies included upper respiratory tract infection, bronchitis, and fungal infection. Other respiratory disorders reported during use of oral oxybutynin formulations include pharyngolaryngeal pain, dry throat, sinus congestion, sinusitis, hoarseness, asthma, and nasal congestion. Rhinitis (2—6%) was reported during use of oxybutynin extended-release tablets (5—30 mg/day) in open label studies. During evaluation of oxybutynin topical gel, upper respiratory tract infection (5.4%) and nasopharyngitis (2.8%) were listed among common adverse events.
 
Central nervous system (CNS) reactions including dizziness (16.6%), drowsiness (14.0%), headache (7.5%), and flushing (1 to < 5%) were reported among patients receiving immediate-release oxybutynin. In addition, psychiatric effects reported during clinical study include insomnia (5.5%) and nervousness (6.5%). Among patients receiving extended-release oxybutynin, drowsiness (2—12%), headache (6—10%), and dizziness (4—6%) were reported. Psychiatric effects reported by 1 to < 5% of patients in this group include insomnia, depression, and nervousness. Dysgeusia and confusion were reported by 1 to < 5% of patients receiving oral oxybutynin formulations. Falls have been reported during clinical trials and post-marketing surveillance of both oral formulations; however, it is unknown if these events occurred as a result of other effects such as dizziness or unrelated causes. Memory impairment was reported during post-marketing surveillance of immediate-release oxybutynin. In addition, psychotic disorder (unspecified), agitation, hallucinations, and seizures were reported during post-marketing surveillance of both oral formulations; the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established. Greater than 1% of patients being treated with oxybutynin transdermal patch reported fatigue, somnolence, headache, and flushing. Dizziness was reported during post-marketing surveillance; the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established. However, dizziness has been reported among (2.8%) of patients receiving treatment with the topical gel. In this study group, fatigue (2.1%) and headache (1.5%) were also reported with two patients reporting severe headaches. Some conditions can be aggravated by alcohol intake or by concomitant use of CNS-active drugs. CNS symptoms may be dose-related; therefore, a dosage reduction or discontinuation of treatment may be necessary in some patients.
 
Musculoskeletal adverse effects including back pain, arthralgia, extremity pain, asthenia, flank pain, pain (unspecified), and fatigue occurred in 1 to < 5% of patients receiving immediate-release oxybutynin in clinical studies. Patients receiving extended-release oxybutynin in clinical studies reported asthenia (3—7%) and pain (unspecified) (4—7%). In this study group, back pain, arthralgia, and extremity pain, occurred in 1 to < 5% of patients.
 
Urinary hesitancy (8.5%), urinary tract infection (6.5%), and urinary retention (6%) have been reported with immediate-release oxybutynin. Cystitis, dysuria, and increased urinary frequency (i.e., pollakiuria) have also been reported in 1 to < 5% of patients in clinical studies. At a similar incidence, urinary retention and/or hesitancy, cystitis, and dysuria have been reported with extended-release formulations. Urinary tract infection (5%) has also been reported in this study group. Impotence (erectile dysfunction) has been reported during post-marketing surveillance of both oral formulations. Urinary hesitancy and/or urinary retention or impotence can occur from the antimuscarinic activity of oxybutynin. In clinical studies with the oxybutynin transdermal patch, dysuria (2.4%) has been reported. Urinary tract infections (6.9%) have also been reported among study patients using oxybutynin topical gel. Of note, the elderly may be more sensitive to the genitourinary effects of oxybutynin.
 
Palpitations, sinus tachycardia, fluid retention, edema, peripheral edema have all been reported in 1 to < 5% of patients receiving therapeutic doses of immediate-release oxybutynin. Likewise, palpitations, hypertension, peripheral edema, and chest pain (unspecified) have been reported with extended-release oxybutynin at the same incidence rate. QT prolongation was reported during post-marketing surveillance of immediate-release oxybutynin. Tachycardia has been reported during post-marketing surveillance of both oral formulations; however, the frequency is unknown.
 
Ocular hypertension can precipitate undiagnosed glaucoma. Ocular pain should be reported and evaluated. Blurred vision (9.6%) reported among patients receiving immediate-release oxybutynin is usually transient, but continued visual impairment is possible. Keratoconjunctivitis sicca and ocular irritation have been reported in 1 to < 5% of patients receiving immediate-release oxybutynin in clinical studies. Cycloplegia and mydriasis have been reported during post-marketing use; however, the frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established. Blurred vision (1—8%) and xerophthalmia (3—6%) have been reported during use of extended-release oxybutynin in clinical studies. Abnormal vision (2.5%) has also been reported from oxybutynin transdermal patch users.
 
Increased blood glucose (e.g., hyperglycemia) has been reported in 1 to < 5% of patients receiving immediate-release oxybutynin in clinical studies.
 
Lactation suppression has been reported during post-marketing use of immediate-release oxybutynin. The frequency is unknown and causality to the drug has not been established.

Description
  Text
  Oxybutynin is an oral and transdermal, synthetic muscarinic antagonist indicated for the treatment of overactive bladder (OAB) with symptoms of urge urinary incontinence, urinary urgency, and frequency. Oral, topical gel, and transdermal patch formulations are available for adult use. Immediate-release oral formulations are also indicated for the treatment of pediatric patients 6 years of age and older with symptoms of detrusor overactivity associated with a neurological condition (neurogenic bladder). Per guidelines for idiopathic OAB, either bladder-specific antimuscarinics or beta-3 receptor agonists may be used as initially a monotherapy or in combination with behavioral interventions depending on the patient's needs and treatment goals. When using a bladder-specific antimuscarinic, use of extended-release oral formulations (e.g., oxybutynin XL) is preferred to immediate-release products as these formulations may limit side effects such as dry mouth; transdermal therapy (i.e., oxybutynin gels or patches) may also be offered. Intolerance or side effects may be mitigated with dose reduction or selection of a different bladder-specific antimuscarinic agent. Clinicians may consider combination therapy with an antimuscarinic and beta-3 agonists for individuals refractory to monotherapy with either therapy alone. Oxybutynin was initially FDA-approved in 1975; a transdermal oxybutynin patch for use in women was granted FDA approval for nonprescription use in 2013.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Oxybutynin is a tertiary amine ester that exerts antimuscarinic (atropine-like) and antispasmodic (papaverine-like) actions on smooth muscle. Antimuscarinic activity resides predominately in the R-isomer and a metabolite, desethyloxybutynin, has pharmacological activity similar to that of oxybutynin. Oxybutynin exerts a direct antispasmodic effect on smooth muscle and inhibits the muscarinic action of acetylcholine on smooth muscle. The effects on the bladder detrusor muscle are mediated via M3 receptors. Oxybutynin exhibits only one-fifth of the anticholinergic activity of atropine on the rabbit detrusor muscle, but 4 to 10 times the antispasmodic activity. No blocking effects occur at skeletal neuromuscular junctions or autonomic ganglia (antinicotinic effects). Clinically, oxybutynin relaxes bladder smooth muscle. In patients with conditions characterized by involuntary bladder contractions, cystometric studies have demonstrated that oxybutynin increases bladder (vesical) capacity, diminishes the frequency of uninhibited contractions of the detrusor muscle, and delays the initial desire to void. Oxybutynin thus decreases urgency and the frequency of both incontinent episodes and voluntary urination.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Following oral administration of immediate-release oxybutynin, absorption from the gut is rapid, with peak concentrations occurring at 1 hour. Antispasmodic activity occurs within 30 to 60 minutes of administration and can last for 6 to 10 hours. Administration of an oral extended-release formulation (e.g., Ditropan XL), results in a Cmax at 4 to 6 hours and consistent 24-hour plasma-concentrations at steady-state. Coadministration of oxybutynin solution with food results in a slight delay in absorption and a 25% increase in bioavailability.
    Name
    Topical Route
    Description
    Topical 10% Gel: Oxybutynin is transported across intact skin and into the systemic circulation by passive diffusion across the stratum corneum. Steady-state concentrations are achieved within 3 to 7 days of continuous daily dosing. Absorption of oxybutynin topical gel is similar after application to the abdomen, upper arm/shoulders or thighs. Transdermal administration bypasses the first-pass gastrointestinal and hepatic metabolism, reducing the formation of the N-desethyloxybutynin metabolite. Only small amounts of CYP3A4 are found in the skin, limiting pre-systemic metabolism during transdermal absorption. The AUC ratio of N-desethyloxybutynin metabolite to parent compound following multiple transdermal applications is approximately 1:1 for the topical gel.
Transdermal System: The Oxytrol transdermal patch delivers 3.9 mg/day of oxybutynin through the skin by passive diffusion across the stratum corneum. Absorption of oxybutynin is bioequivalent after application of the patch to the abdomen, buttocks, or hips. Plasma concentrations increase for approximately 24 to 48 hours after patch application; steady-state concentrations are maintained for up to 96 hours. Transdermal administration of oxybutynin bypasses enteric and hepatic first-pass metabolism, reducing the formation of the N-desethyloxybutynin metabolite. Since the skin contains only small amounts of CYP3A4, limited pre-systemic metabolism occurs with transdermal administration. The AUC ratio of the N-desethyloxybutynin metabolite to parent compound following multiple applications of the transdermal system was 1.3:1.

  Text
  Oxybutynin is administered orally and topically (transdermal patch or gel). Oxybutynin is administered as a racemic mixture of R-and S-enantiomers. Oxybutynin is widely distributed in body tissues following systemic absorption, with an estimated volume of distribution of 193 L following intravenous administration of 5 mg of oxybutynin chloride. Oxybutynin has an affinity for anticholinergic receptors in the urinary tract, salivary glands, and CNS. Both enantiomers of oxybutynin are highly bound (greater than 99%) to plasma proteins; the active metabolite of oxybutynin (N-desethyloxybutynin) is also highly bound (greater than 97%) to plasma proteins. The major binding protein is alpha-1 acid glycoprotein. Oxybutynin is extensively metabolized in the liver by the cytochrome P450 enzyme system, particularly CYP3A4. Metabolites include N-desethyloxybutynin, which is pharmacologically active and phenylcyclohexylglycolic acid, which is inactive. The elimination half-life (half-life) of immediate-release oxybutynin is 2 to 5 hours. Less than 0.1% of the administered dose is excreted in the urine as unchanged oxybutynin or desethyloxybutynin.  
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4
Oxybutynin is extensively metabolized, primarily by CYP3A4. When immediate-release dosage forms were administered with a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor (ketoconazole), mean oxybutynin plasma concentrations were approximately 3 to 4-fold higher. When extended-release oral oxybutynin was administered with the strong CYP3A4 inhibitor, mean mean oxybutynin plasma concentrations were approximately 2-fold higher. Other inhibitors of the CYP3A4 may also alter oxybutynin mean pharmacokinetic parameters, such as Cmax or AUC. No dosage adjustments are recommended, but use with caution.
