Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Isoflurane is contraindicated in patients with a suspected genetic predisposition to or a history of malignant hyperthermia. Isoflurane is a halogenated anesthetic and is also contraindicated in patients with a sensitivity to isoflurane or other halogenated anesthetics hypersensitivity. Patients who have been previously treated with halothane anesthesia may be at increased risk of sensitivity hepatitis when receiving isoflurane.
 
Isoflurane is relatively contraindicated in patients with head trauma, intracranial mass, preexisting increased intracranial pressure, or space-occupying intracranial lesions because it can increase intracranial pressure.
 
Use of isoflurane in patients with myasthenia gravis can cause an increase in muscular weakness due to the neuromuscular blocking effects of anesthetics.
 
Although rare, hyperkalemia development with isoflurane use may occur, especially in patients with latent as well as overt neuromuscular disease such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy (see Adverse Reactions). If a patient develops hyperkalemia, evaluation for latent neuromuscular disease is recommended.
 
To avoid myocardial ischemia in patients with coronary artery disease, maintenance of normal hemodynamics is important regardless of the anesthetics employed. Isoflurane can produce coronary vasodilation at the arteriolar level in selected animal models; isoflurane has been shown to divert blood from collateral dependent myocardium to normally perfused areas in an animal model. Isoflurane is probably a coronary dilator in humans, but data are absent to support the coronary arteriolar dilation property of isoflurane being associated with coronary steal or myocardial ischemia in patients with coronary artery disease.
 
Isoflurane has been observed to react with desiccated carbon dioxide absorbents in anesthetic circle systems to form carbon monoxide and subsequently cause carboxyhemoglobinemia. Absorbents should be changed prior to isoflurane use, and inflow rates of less than 2—3 L/min will help prevent absorbents from drying out.
 
Safe and effective use of isoflurane has not been established in patients less than 18 years of age such as infants and children. Isoflurane's MAC is higher in children than in adults and is highest in very young children and decreases with increasing age (see Pharmacokinetics). Use isoflurane with caution in elderly patients because they often have significantly decreased MACs and are more susceptible to anesthetic-induced hypotension and circulatory depression.
 
Patients should be counseled not attempt driving or operating machinery for at least 24 hours after receiving isoflurane. Patients should have a caregiver present to drive them home after outpatient procedures where they received isoflurane. Patients may experience impaired mental cognition for 2—3 days after receiving isoflurane.
 
Use of isoflurane with citrate-anticoagulated blood product transfusions can result in an additive neuromuscular blockade.
 
Isoflurane has been reported to cause prolongation of the QT interval, which constitutes a risk for ventricular tachycardia, including torsade de pointes. This should be taken into consideration when contemplating the use of isoflurane in patients with a existing QT prolongation or in patients receiving drugs that may prolong the QT interval (see Drug Interactions).
 
Isoflurane is a FDA pregnancy risk category C drug. Animal studies have indicated possible fetotoxicity, although no adequate, well-controlled studies have been conducted in humans. Use isoflurane during pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus. During labor and obstetric delivery, isoflurane can produce uterine relaxation, which can delay delivery and increase postpartum hemorrhage. Adequate data have not been developed to establish application of isoflurane in obstetrical anesthesia. Some retrospective data indicate that chronic accidental exposure to inhalational anesthetics, as can occur in operating room personnel, may increase the incidence of spontaneous fetal abortions, teratogenesis, and fetal stillbirths.
 
It is not known whether isoflurane is excreted in milk, so use isoflurane with caution in women who are breast-feeding.

    General Information
    Isoflurane is contraindicated in individuals in whom general anesthesia is contraindicated.
    Headline
    increased intracranial pressure, increased risk for increased intracranial pressure
    General Information
    In individuals with increased intracranial pressure or increased risk for increased intracranial pressure, administer isoflurane in conjunction with intracranial pressure-reducing strategies, as clinically appropriate. Isoflurane markedly increases cerebral blood flow at deeper levels of anesthesia to produce a transient increase in intracranial pressure.
    Headline
    bradycardia, cardiomyopathy, congenital long QT syndrome, coronary artery disease, females, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, QT prolongation
    General Information
    Avoid use of isoflurane in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk. Monitor QT interval when administering isoflurane to susceptible individuals. Additionally, monitor for signs of inadequate myocardial perfusion (e.g., ECG, blood pressure) during isoflurane administration. Consider additional cardiac monitoring in individuals with known coronary artery disease, as clinically necessary. Isoflurane can cause dose-dependent coronary vasodilation and has been shown to divert blood from collateral-dependent myocardium to normally perfused areas in an animal model (coronary steal). The extent to which coronary steal occurs in individuals with steal-prone coronary anatomy is unclear.
    Headline
    hypotension, hypovolemia
    General Information
    Particular care must be taken when selecting the isoflurane dosage for individuals with hypovolemia, hypotension, or otherwise hemodynamically compromised (e.g., due to concomitant medications). Isoflurane causes a dose-dependent reduction in systemic vascular resistance and blood pressure.
    Headline
    neuromuscular disease
    General Information
    Inhaled anesthetics have been associated with rare increases in serum potassium concentrations that have resulted in cardiac arrhythmias and death in pediatric populations during the postoperative period. Individuals with latent or overt neuromuscular disease, particularly Duchenne muscular dystrophy, appear to be most vulnerable. In many, but not all, cases, succinylcholine was administered concurrently.
    Headline
    malignant hyperthermia
    General Information
    Isoflurane is contraindicated in individuals with known or suspected genetic susceptibility to malignant hyperthermia. Isoflurane can induce malignant hyperthermia in individuals with known or suspected susceptibility based on genetic factors or family history, including those with certain inherited ryanodine receptor (RYR1) or dihydropyridine receptor (CACNA1S) variants. Multiple pathogenic variants of RYR1 and CACNA1S genes have been associated with malignant hyperthermia susceptibility in individuals receiving volatile anesthetic agents, including isoflurane. Assess variant pathogenicity based on prior clinical experience, functional studies, prevalence information, or other evidence. Concomitant use of succinylcholine may further increase the risk of malignant hyperthermia.
    Headline
    hepatic disease, history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use
    General Information
    Isoflurane is contraindicated in individuals with a history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use, specifically a history of confirmed hepatitis due to a halogenated inhalational anesthetic or a history of unexplained moderate to severe hepatic dysfunction (e.g., jaundice associated with fever and/or eosinophilia) after anesthesia with isoflurane or other inhalational halogenated anesthetics. As with other halogenated anesthetic agents, isoflurane may cause sensitivity hepatitis in individuals who have been sensitized by previous exposure to halogenated anesthetics. Exercise clinical judgment when isoflurane is used in individuals with underlying hepatic disease or under treatment with drugs known to cause hepatic dysfunction; repeated anesthetics within a short period of time may result in increased effects in individuals with underlying hepatic conditions or additive effects in individuals treated with drugs known to cause hepatic dysfunction. Evaluate the need for repeated exposure in each individual and adjust the dose of isoflurane based on signs and symptoms of adequate depth of anesthesia if repeated exposure in a short period of time is clinically indicated.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Advise individuals to avoid driving or operating machinery or participating in activities requiring coordination and concentration for at least 24 hours after administration of a general anesthetic. The ability to perform such tasks may be impaired after receiving anesthetic agents.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, infants, neonates
    General Information
    During the induction of anesthesia, saliva flow and tracheobronchial secretion can increase and may cause laryngospasm, particularly in children and adolescents. Emergence from anesthesia in children may elicit a brief state of agitation that may hinder cooperation. Repeated or lengthy use of general anesthetic and sedation drugs during surgeries or procedures in neonates, infants, and children younger than 3 years, including in utero exposure during the third trimester, may have negative effects on brain development. Consider the benefits of appropriate anesthesia in young children against the potential risks, especially for procedures that may last more than 3 hours or if multiple procedures are required during the first 3 years of life. It may be appropriate to delay certain procedures if doing so will not jeopardize the health of the child. No specific anesthetic or sedation drug has been shown to be safer than another. Human studies suggest that a single short exposure to a general anesthetic in young pediatric individuals is unlikely to have negative effects on behavior and learning; however, further research is needed to fully characterize how anesthetic exposure affects brain development. With the exception of neonates, isoflurane minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) decreases with increasing age.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    The minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) of isoflurane decreases with increasing age. Adjust the dose of isoflurane accordingly in geriatric adults. Avoid use of isoflurane in people who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including geriatric age. Monitor QT interval when administering isoflurane to susceptible individuals.
    Headline
    pregnancy, pregnancy termination
    General Information
    There are no adequate and well-controlled studies on the use of isoflurane during human pregnancy. Adequate data are not available to establish its application in obstetrical anesthesia. Increased post-implantation loss and decreased live births were observed in pregnant mice exposed to isoflurane at concentrations of 0%, 0.075%, or 0.3% for 2 hours per day on gestation days 6 through 15. Repeated or lengthy use of general anesthetic and sedation drugs during the third trimester of pregnancy may have negative effects on fetal brain development. Consider the benefits of anesthesia in pregnant patients and the potential risks, especially for procedures that may last more than 3 hours or if multiple procedures are required prior to delivery. It may be appropriate to delay certain procedures. No specific medications for anesthesia or sedation have been shown to be safer than others. Human studies suggest that a single short exposure to a general anesthetic in young pediatric patients is unlikely to have negative effects on behavior and learning; however, further research is needed to fully characterize the effects of anesthetic exposure on brain development. Isoflurane has been associated with increased blood loss, comparable to that seen with halothane, in patients undergoing pregnancy termination. Chronic exposure to inhalational anesthetics, as can occur in operating room personnel, may increase the risk for miscarriage.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Isoflurane is compatible with breast-feeding. Although there is no published experience with isoflurane in breast-feeding patients, inhalational anesthetics have a short serum-half life and poor oral bioavailability. Clinically significant exposure in the breastfed child is not expected. No waiting period or discarding of milk is required; breast-feeding can be resumed once the patient has sufficiently recovered from general anesthesia.
    Headline
    maternal Venezuelan heritage
    General Information
    Consider alternatives to isoflurane, such as intravenous anesthetics or regional anesthesia, when clinically appropriate and available, in individuals with maternal Venezuelan heritage, particularly those known or suspected to carry the rare mitochondrial genetic variant MT-ND4 m.11232T>C. Unexpected catastrophic outcomes, including severe neurologic adverse outcomes and death, have been reported in adults and pediatric individuals of maternal Venezuelan heritage following routine general anesthesia with volatile anesthetics. Although reported cases have involved sevoflurane, this genetic variant raises concerns regarding all volatile anesthetics, including isoflurane. Care teams and individuals are encouraged to report adverse events or side effects related to the use of these products to the FDA's MedWatch Safety Information and Adverse Event Reporting Program.

    General Information
    Malignant hyperthermia can be precipitated by many drugs used in anesthetic practice, including halogenated anesthetics. Description
  Text
  Isoflurane is an inhalational halogenated anesthetic indicated for induction and maintenance of general anesthesia. Adequate data have not been developed to establish its application in obstetrical anesthesia. Cases of postoperative hepatic dysfunction or hepatitis, including fatal hepatic necrosis and hepatic failure, have been reported with isoflurane. As with other halogenated anesthetic agents, isoflurane may cause hypersensitivity hepatitis in patients who have been sensitized by previous exposure to halogenated anesthetics. Repeated or lengthy use of general anesthetic and sedation drugs during surgeries or procedures in pediatric patients younger than 3 years, including in utero exposure during the third trimester, may have negative effects on brain development. No specific anesthetic or sedation drug has been shown to be safer than another.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The exact mechanism by which inhalational anesthetics function is not known. There appears to be a correlation between anesthetic potency and lipid solubility (Meyer-Overton theory), suggesting that these anesthetics likely affect the lipid matrix of nerve cell membranes in the brain. Furthermore, NMR and electron spin resonance studies indicate that anesthetics cause a local disordering of the lipid membrane matrix, possibly decreasing the number of molecules that alternate simultaneously between the gel and crystalline states, and thereby altering membrane function.

  Special Populations
    Name
    Pediatrics
    Description
    The MAC of isoflurane decreases with age. The MAC of isoflurane when mixed in oxygen is 1.6% in neonates, 1.87% in infants 1—6 months of age, 1.8% in infants 6—12 months of age, 1.6% in children 1—3 years of age, and 1.6% in children 3—5 years of age.
  Text
  Isoflurane is administered by inhalation and is readily absorbed via the pulmonary capillary system. Isoflurane's blood-to-gas partition coefficient is 1.43, and the oil-to-gas partition coefficient is 90.8. Isoflurane undergoes minimal biotransformation in man; a small amount (0.17%) of a given dose can be recovered from urine as metabolites. Approximately 95% is eliminated unchanged by exhalation. In adults with a mean age of 44 +/- 7 years, the minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) of isoflurane is 1.15% when mixed in oxygen and is 0.5% in 70% nitrous oxide.
