Drug Class: Promethazine and dextromethorphan are used together in an oral liquid preparation to relieve cough and upper respiratory symptoms associated with allergy or the common cold.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: Do not exceed 30 mL/day PO (i.e., not to exceed promethazine 37.5 mg in combination with dextromethorphan 90 mg per 24 hours).

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): dysuria, ileus, impotence (erectile dysfunction), mydriasis, urinary retention, xerophthalmia, agitation, anxiety, confusion, delirium, depression, dizziness, drowsiness, fatigue, hallucinations, headache, impaired cognition, insomnia, nightmares, palpitations, restlessness, seizures, akathisia, diplopia, dystonic reaction, euphoria, excitability, pseudoparkinsonism, tinnitus, torticollis

In an Overdose: Excessive doses or overdosage of dextromethorphan may result in nausea and vomiting.Dextromethorphan; promethazine combinations may cause dizziness, drowsiness, fatigue, and sedation. Dextromethorphan; promethazine is not recommended in children aged < 2 years.Treatment of excessive dosage or overdosage with dextromethorphan; promethazine is generally symptomatic and supportive.

  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  NOTE: This monograph discusses the contraindications/precautions of dextromethorphan; promethazine combination products. Clinicians may wish to consult the individual monographs for more information about each agent.
 
Promethazine is contraindicated for use in patients with known phenothiazine hypersensitivity. Cross-sensitivity may occur. This contraindication includes patients who have experienced agranulocytosis, bone marrow suppression, blood dyscrasias, or jaundice due to phenothiazine therapy.
 
Dextromethorphan-promethazine should be avoided, if possible, in patients with open-angle glaucoma or closed-angle glaucoma because of the anticholinergic effects of promethazine. An H1-antagonist with less anticholinergic effects should be substituted. An increase in intraocular pressure may occur from the anticholinergic actions of promethazine, precipitating an acute attack of glaucoma. Elderly patients are more susceptible to the anticholinergic effects of promethazine, including possible precipitation of undiagnosed glaucoma. Other ocular effects resulting from the anticholinergic effects of promethazine include dry eyes or blurred vision. This may be of significance in the elderly and wearers of contact lenses. Because of anticholinergic actions, dextromethorphan; promethazine combinations should also be used cautiously in patients with bladder obstruction pelvic tumors, prostatic hypertrophy, renal disease, renal impairment or renal failure, urethral stricture, or urinary retention. Promethazine should also be used with caution in patients with GI disease including GI obstruction (e.g., stenosing peptic ulcer, pyloroduodenal obstruction) or ileus due to the anticholinergic effects of promethazine on the gastrointestinal tract. The elderly are more susceptible to these effects of drugs and should be carefully monitored for adverse reactions.
 
Patients with head trauma or with increased intracranial pressure should be given promethazine with caution, because promethazine can make it difficult to evaluate neurologic parameters.
 
Sedative drugs or CNS depressants including dextromethorphan; promethazine should be avoided in patients with a history of sleep apnea.
 
Dextromethorphan; promethazine should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease. Promethazine and dextromethorphan are extensively metabolized and drug elimination may be reduced in patients with significant hepatic impairment. Dosage adjustments may be required.
 
Any patient receiving dextromethorphan; promethazine should be warned about the possibility of sedation and to use caution when driving or operating machinery until the effects of the drug are known.
 
Phenothiazine derivatives lower the seizure threshold through their effect on GABA; therefore, dextromethorphan; promethazine should be avoided, if possible, in patients with a seizure disorder (seizures) or those receiving anticonvulsants.
 
Combination products containing dextromethorphan; promethazine should be avoided where possible in patients with asthma or chronic cough due to either chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (i.e., chronic bronchitis or emphysema) or tobacco smoking. The anticholinergic activity of H1-antagonists may result in thickened bronchial secretions in the respiratory tract thereby aggravating acute asthma, status asthmaticus, or COPD. Although H1-antagonists should be avoided during acute bronchospasm or asthmatic attack, these anticholinergic effects do not preclude the use of H1-antagonists in all asthmatic or COPD patients. Because promethazine exhibits a significant amount of anticholinergic activity, dextromethorphan-promethazine should be avoided in those who have experienced a worsening in respiratory status due to H1-antagonist therapy.
 
The quinidine-like local anesthetic and anticholinergic effects of antihistamines are responsible for adverse cardiac effects such as tachycardia, ECG changes, hypotension, and arrhythmias. Although these cardiovascular effects are uncommon, H1-antagonists such as promethazine should be used conservatively in patients with cardiac disease or cardiac arrhythmias.
 
Phenothiazines like promethazine may cause skin hyperpigmentation or photosensitivity. Hyperpigmentation generally is restricted to areas of the body exposed to sunlight. Photosensitivity can result, and patients should be warned either avoid sunlight (UV) exposure or to use effective sunscreens (SPF 15+) on exposed areas of the body. Withdrawal of the drug can reverse the effects. Contact dermatitis is also possible in predisposed individuals if they come in contact with liquid dosage forms of phenothiazines.
 
Use dextromethorphan; promethazine with caution in patients with diabetes mellitus; an increase in blood glucose has been reported in patients receiving promethazine.
 
Dextromethorphan; promethazine products are contraindicated for use in children < 2 years of age, infants, and neonates due to the risk for fatal promethazine-associated respiratory depression. Seizures and/or paradoxical CNS stimulation may also occur in this age group. If dextromethorphan; promethazine is used in children 2 years or older, the lowest possible effective dose should be given, and the concomitant use of other respiratory depressants should be avoided. There have been a number of cases of respiratory depression, sleep apnea, and SIDS in children receiving phenothiazine antihistamines at various weight-based dosages. The mechanism of this reaction is not yet known; therefore, dextromethorphan; promethazine should be used with extreme caution, if at all, in children with a family history of SIDS or sleep apnea. Promethazine-associated extrapyramidal symptoms that may occur in children receiving dextromethorphan; promethazine may be confused with signs of undiagnosed primary disease; thus the drug should be avoided in children whose signs or symptoms suggest the possibility of Reye's syndrome, hepatic encephalopathy, or other hepatic disease. In pediatric patients who are acutely ill associated with dehydration, there is an increased susceptibility to promethazine-associated dystonic reactions with the use of dextromethorphan; promethazine. In January 2007, the CDC warned caregivers and healthcare providers of the risk for serious injury or fatal overdose from the administration of cough and cold products to children and infants less than 2 years of age. This warning followed an investigation of the deaths of three (3) infants less than 6 months of age that were attributed to the inadvertent inappropriate use of these products. The symptoms preceding these deaths have not been clearly defined, and there is a lack of conclusive data describing the exact cause of death. The report estimated that 1519 children less than 2 years of age were treated in emergency departments during 2004—2005 for adverse events related to cough and cold medications. In October 2007, the FDA Nonprescription Drug Advisory Committee and the Pediatric Advisory Committee recommended that nonprescription cough and cold products containing pseudoephedrine, dextromethorphan, chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, brompheniramine, phenylephrine, clemastine, or guaifenesin not be used in children less than 6 years of age. In January 2008, the FDA issued a Public Health Advisory recommending that OTC cough and cold products not be used in infants and children less than 2 years. An official ruling regarding the use of these products in children greater than 2 years has not yet been announced. The FDA recommends that if parents and caregivers use cough and cold products in children greater than 2 years, labels should be read carefully, caution should be used when administering multiple products, and only measuring devices specifically designed for use with medications should be used. While some combination cough/cold products containing these ingredients are available by prescription only and are not necessarily under scrutiny by the FDA, clinicians should thoroughly assess each patient's use of similar products, both prescription and nonprescription, to avoid duplication of therapy and the potential for inadvertent overdose.
 
Dextromethorphan and promethazine are classified as pregnancy risk category C drugs, as monotherapy or in combination. One controlled human study found the use of dextromethorphan alone to be relatively safe for use during pregnancy, including during the first trimester. However, the safety of combination products containing dextromethorphan during pregnancy has not been established. Promethazine, if taken within two weeks of obstetric delivery, may inhibit platelet aggregation in the newborn. However, according to one manufacturer, limited data suggest that use of promethazine during labor and delivery does not have an appreciable effect on the duration of labor or delivery and does not increase the risk of need for intervention in the newborn. The effect on later growth and development of the newborn is unknown. Because there are no adequate studies in pregnant women, dextromethorphan; promethazine should be considered during pregnancy only when the benefits of therapy outweigh the potential risks to the fetus. Diagnostic pregnancy tests based on immunological reactions between HCG and anti-HCG may result in false-negative or false-positive interpretations in patients receiving promethazine.
 
It is not known whether promethazine or dextromethorphan is excreted in human milk. In general, H1-antagonists such as promethazine are not recommended for use during breast-feeding because they can induce a paradoxical CNS stimulation in neonates or seizures in premature infants. Antihistamines like promethazine may also cause transient EEG changes in the neonate. Inhibition of lactation may also occur. Alternative methods of feeding should be used if promethazine therapy is necessary.
 
Dextromethorphan; promethazine combinations contain the phenothiazine antihistamine promethazine. Phenothiazines can lower the seizure threshold. Because of a potential increased risk of seizures, phenothiazines should not be used during intrathecal radiographic contrast administration. This drug combination should be discontinued 48 hours before the myelography and should not be resumed until at least 24 hours after the procedure.
 
Dextromethorphan should be used cautiously, if at all, in patients receiving MAOI therapy; dextromethorphan is usually contraindicated in patients receiving traditional non-selective inhibitors of MAO (e.g., isocarboxazid, tranylcypromine, phenelzine) (see Drug Interactions).

    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    The Beers Criteria identifies first-generation antihistamines as potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) for geriatric adults, recommending their avoidance due to their high anticholinergic properties, decreased clearance in older age, the development of tolerance when used as sleep aids, and an increased risk of anticholinergic effects and toxicity compared to younger individuals. These medications should particularly be avoided in patients with dementia or cognitive impairment (due to adverse CNS effects), those at high risk for delirium (which can worsen or trigger new-onset delirium), and men with lower urinary tract symptoms or benign prostatic hyperplasia (due to risks of urinary retention or hesitancy). Since older adults tend to have reduced therapeutic requirements for drugs that cause sedation/respiratory depression, the promethazine dosage should generally be reduced for these patients.

    pregnancy
    General Information
    The safety and efficacy of combination products containing promethazine; dextromethorphan during pregnancy has not been established; use during pregnancy only when the benefits of therapy outweigh the potential risks to the fetus. Few studies have addressed the safety of using cough medications during pregnancy. Nonpharmacologic symptomatic treatment is generally preferred as a first-line measure in the pregnant patient. Pregnant patients should see their health care professional for diagnosis and treatment recommendations. Dextromethorphan acts as a low-affinity antagonist to the glutamate receptor subtype N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) in the CNS, and there has been some concern about its safe use during pregnancy. Dextromethorphan exhibited adverse developmental effects in avian embryos; however, the avian study data have limited applicability to human gestation. However, due to the small sample size, an increased risk of rare malformations could not be ruled out.  Consider alternatives.     Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Caution is recommended during use of promethazine; dextromethorphan during breast-feeding. Because of the potential for serious adverse reactions (sedation, respiratory depression, movement disorders, paradoxical reactions) in breastfed infants from promethazine, use is not recommended during breast-feeding as safer alternatives are available for the treatment of cold, allergy and congestion.  It is likely that some transfer of promethazine into human milk occurs. Promethazine may also lower basal prolactin secretion and may interfere with the establishment of lactation. Occasional doses or short-term use of promethazine may be possible during breast-feeding. Consider alternatives to promethazine; dextromethorphan. Some experts consider dextromethorphan alone, at usual adult doses for cough and for limited duration of use for this purpose, to be compatible with breast-feeding and unlikely to be harmful to the breastfed infant. Based on dextromethorphan's relatively low molecular weight, some transfer into breast milk is expected. One study estimated infant exposure via breast milk to be less than 1% of the usual maternal dose. If the use of dextromethorphan is necessary during lactation, it is best to chose products that are alcohol-free. Guidelines recommend loratadine or cetirizine at the lowest dose as preferred antihistamines during breast-feeding because they are safer with only low levels found in breast milk.

Description
  Text
  Promethazine and dextromethorphan are used together in an oral liquid preparation to relieve cough and upper respiratory symptoms associated with allergy or the common cold. Dextromethorphan is a centrally acting, non-narcotic antitussive agent. Promethazine is a phenothiazine-structure sedating antihistamine (H-1 blocker) that dries the nasal and sinus passages. There is a boxed warning against use in children and infants less than 2 years of age; postmarketing cases of respiratory depression, including fatalities, have been reported with use of promethazine in young pediatric patients.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The cumulative actions of dextromethorphan in combination with promethazine result in the relief of cough and associated symptoms of allergy or the common cold.
Promethazine: The predominant action of promethazine is antagonism of H1-receptors. Although promethazine is classified as a phenothiazine, its ability to antagonize dopamine is approximately one-tenth that of chlorpromazine. Like other sedating antihistamines, promethazine does not prevent the release of histamine, but competes with free histamine for binding at H1-receptor sites. Histamine receptors in the GI tract, uterus, large blood vessels, and bronchial muscle are blocked. In addition to its antihistaminic action, it provides clinically useful sedative and antiemetic effects. Sedation is significant at concentrations achieved from therapeutic promethazine dosages. Mild antitussive activity has been attributed to promethazine, but this effect probably results from anticholinergic and sedative actions.
Dextromethorphan: Dextromethorphan is a non-competitive antagonist of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors in the brain and spinal cord. It is the d-isomer of levorphanol but has none of the analgesic, respiratory depressive, or sedative effects associated with opiate agonists when used in usual antitussive dosages. As an antitussive, dextromethorphan acts centrally on the cough center in the medulla to raise the threshold for coughing by decreasing the excitability of the cough center. Dextromethorphan is about equal to codeine in depressing the cough reflex. In therapeutic dosage dextromethorphan also does not inhibit ciliary activity. Naloxone, an opiate-antagonist, does not block the antitussive effects of dextromethorphan.

  Text
  Promethazine; dextromethorphan combinations are administered orally.
Promethazine: Promethazine is highly protein-bound (80% to 93%). It is widely distributed in body tissues and fluids. Promethazine is metabolized by the liver to a variety of compounds; the sulfoxides of promethazine and N-demethylpromethazine are the predominant metabolites appearing in the urine. The elimination half-life is 10 to 14 hours.
Dextromethorphan: Dextromethorphan is approximately 60% to 70% protein bound. Dextromethorphan is primarily metabolized by the CYP2D6 isoenzyme. Dextromethorphan is metabolized primarily by liver enzymes undergoing O-demethylation, N-demethylation, and partial conjugation with glucuronic acid and sulfate. In humans, (+)-3-hydroxy-Nmethyl-morphinan, (+)-3-hydroxymorphinan, and traces of un-metabolized drug were found in urine after oral administration.
 
Affected Cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2D6
Dextromethorphan is metabolized via CYP2D6; potent inhibitors of this enzyme can increase dextromethorphan exposure.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available. Use promethazine with caution in patients with hepatic dysfunction.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
