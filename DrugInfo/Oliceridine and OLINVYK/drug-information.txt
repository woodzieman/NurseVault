Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Reserve opioid analgesics for use in individuals for whom alternative treatment options are ineffective, not tolerated, or would be otherwise inadequate to provide sufficient management of pain.
Individualize dosing; consider severity of pain, clinical response, prior analgesic treatment experience, and risk factors for addiction, abuse, and misuse. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration consistent with individual treatment goals. Reserve titration to higher doses for individuals in whom lower doses are insufficient and in whom expected benefits of using a higher dose opioid clearly outweigh substantial risks.
Monitor individuals closely for respiratory depression, which may occur at any time during therapy. Consider this risk when selecting an initial dosage and when adjusting the dose. Continually reevaluate individuals to assess the maintenance of pain control and the relative incidence of adverse reactions, as well as to monitor for the development of addiction, abuse, or misuse. If the level of pain increases after dosage stabilization, attempt to identify the source of increased pain before increasing the opioid dosage. If unacceptable opioid-related adverse reactions are observed, consider reducing the dosage. Adjust the dosage to obtain an appropriate balance between management of pain and opioid-related adverse reactions.
Many acute pain conditions (e.g., pain occurring with surgical procedures or acute musculoskeletal injuries) require no more than a few days of an opioid. Clinical guidelines on opioid prescribing for some acute pain conditions are available.
There is substantial interpatient variability in the relative potency of different opioid drugs and products. When adjusting oliceridine dosages or converting from another opioid agonist, it is better to underestimate daily dose requirements and give rescue doses than to risk an adverse reaction. If an adverse event occurs, the next dose may be reduced. It is estimated that oliceridine 1 mg IV is approximately equipotent to morphine 5 mg IV. As individuals differ in their response to opioids, use this comparison only as a guide. Refer to the Opioid Agonists Drug Class Overview for approximate equianalgesic doses.
If the 27 mg cumulative daily oliceridine dose is reached and analgesia is still required, use an alternative analgesic regimen until oliceridine can be resumed the next day.
Avoid abrupt discontinuation or rapid dosage reduction in an individual who is physically dependent on opioid analgesics. When discontinuing oliceridine therapy in individuals who are opioid-dependent, taper the dose gradually while monitoring carefully for signs and symptoms of withdrawal.

    Headline
    brain tumor, coma, head trauma, increased intracranial pressure, loss of consciousness
    General Information
    Avoid opioid use in individuals with loss of consciousness or coma; opioids may obscure the clinical course in an individual with a head trauma injury. Monitor individuals who may be susceptible to the intracranial effect of carbon dioxide retention (e.g., those with evidence of increased intracranial pressure or brain tumor) for signs of sedation and respiratory depression, particularly when initiating opioid therapy. Opioids may reduce respiratory drive and the resultant carbon dioxide retention can further increase intracranial pressure.
    Headline
    seizures
    General Information
    Opioids may increase seizure frequency in individuals with seizure disorders and may increase seizure risk in other clinical settings associated with seizures. Regularly evaluate individuals with a history of seizure disorders for worsened seizure control during opioid therapy.
    Headline
    hypovolemia, shock
    General Information
    Avoid the use of opioids in individuals with circulatory shock. Opioids may cause vasodilation that can further reduce cardiac output and blood pressure. Monitor individuals whose ability to maintain blood pressure has been compromised by hypovolemia or concurrent administration of certain CNS depressant medications (e.g., phenothiazines, general anesthetics) for hypotension after opioid treatment initiation or dosage titration due to increased risk for severe hypotension.
    Headline
    asthma exacerbation, cachexia, central sleep apnea, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coadministration with certain medications, cor pulmonale, hypercapnia, hypoxia, respiratory depression, respiratory insufficiency, severe asthma
    General Information
    Oliceridine is contraindicated in individuals with significant respiratory depression and those with asthma exacerbation or severe asthma in an unmonitored setting or in the absence of resuscitative equipment. Avoid opioid coadministration with certain medications, specifically other CNS depressants when possible, as this significantly increases the risk for profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, and death. Reserve concomitant prescribing of these medications for use in individuals for whom alternative treatment options are inadequate; if concurrent use is necessary, use the lowest effective dosages and minimum treatment durations needed. Monitor individuals closely for signs or symptoms of respiratory depression and sedation. Careful monitoring is required, particularly when CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducers and/or CYP2D6 inhibitors are used concomitantly. Concurrent use of a CYP3A4 inhibitor or discontinuation of a concurrently used CYP3A4 inducer may result in an increase in plasma oliceridine concentrations, which may increase the risk for respiratory depression. Concurrent use of a CYP3A4 inducer or discontinuation of CYP3A4 and/or CYP2D6 inhibitor may result in a decrease in plasma oliceridine concentrations, which may be associated with reduced efficacy and/or opioid withdrawal. Individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cor pulmonale, respiratory insufficiency, hypoxia, hypercapnia, or preexisting respiratory depression are at increased risk of decreased respiratory drive even at recommended doses. Individuals with advanced age, cachexia, or debilitation are also at an increased risk for opioid-induced respiratory depression. Monitor such individuals closely, particularly when initiating and titrating the opioid; consider the use of non-opioid analgesics in these individuals. Carbon dioxide retention from opioid-induced respiratory depression may also worsen opioid sedating effects. Consider decreasing the opioid dosage in individuals with central sleep apnea (CSA). Opioids increase the risk of CSA and sleep-related hypoxemia in a dose-dependent fashion.
    Headline
    acute pancreatitis, biliary tract disease, GI obstruction
    General Information
    Oliceridine is contraindicated in individuals with known or suspected GI obstruction, including paralytic ileus. Opioids may cause spasm of the sphincter of Oddi or increases in serum amylase. Monitor individuals with biliary tract disease, including acute pancreatitis, for worsening symptoms.
    Headline
    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Individuals with mild or moderate hepatic impairment may require less frequent oliceridine dosing. When using oliceridine in individuals with severe hepatic impairment or hepatic failure, consider reducing the initial dose, and administer subsequent doses only after a careful review of the individual's severity of pain and overall clinical status.
    Headline
    major psychiatric disorder, substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    Opioids expose users to the risks of addiction, abuse, and misuse. Although the risk of addiction in any individual is unknown, it can occur in individuals appropriately prescribed an opioid. Addiction can occur at recommended dosages and if the medication is misused or abused. The risk of opioid-related overdose or overdose-related death is increased with higher doses, and this risk persists over the course of therapy. The potential for these risks should not prevent the proper management of pain in any given individual. Assess an individual's risk for opioid addiction, abuse, or misuse before prescribing an opioid, and monitor for the development of these behaviors or conditions. Risks are increased in individuals with a personal or family history of substance abuse disorder (including alcohol or drug abuse or addiction) or major psychiatric disorder (e.g., major depression). Individuals at increased risk may be prescribed opioids, but use in such individuals necessitates intensive counseling about the risks and proper use of the opioid along with frequent reevaluation for signs of addiction, abuse, and misuse. Opioids are sought for nonmedical use and are subject to diversion from legitimate prescribed use. Consider these risks when prescribing or dispensing opioids. Strategies to reduce these risks include prescribing the medication in the smallest appropriate quantity.

Opioids can impair the mental or physical abilities needed to perform potentially hazardous activities.

    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Geriatric adults may have increased sensitivity to opioids. Use cautious dose selection for opioids in geriatric adults, usually starting at the low end of the dosing range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function and concomitant disease or other medication therapy. Increase the opioid dosage slowly in geriatric adults and frequently reevaluate for signs of CNS and respiratory depression. According to the Beers Criteria, opioid agonists are considered potentially inappropriate medications in geriatric adults with a history of falls or fractures and should be avoided in these populations, except in the setting of severe acute pain, since opioids can produce ataxia, impaired psychomotor function, syncope, and additional falls. If an opioid must be used, consider reducing the use of other CNS-active medications that increase the risk of falls and fractures and implement strategies to reduce fall risk. In individuals receiving palliative care, the balance of benefits and harms of medication management may differ from those of the general population of older adults.
    Headline
    labor, obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on oliceridine use during pregnancy are insufficient to define the risk of major birth defects or miscarriage. Opioids cross the placenta. In animal reproductive studies, oliceridine reduced live litter size at birth and increased postnatal pup mortality between birth and postnatal day 4 when administered intravenously to rats from organogenesis through weaning at clinically relevant plasma exposures. Oliceridine did not affect embryo-fetal development in rats and rabbits when administered intravenously during organogenesis at exposures 7 and 8 times the estimated exposure at the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) based on AUC, respectively. Opioids can prolong labor and obstetric delivery by temporarily reducing the strength, duration, and frequency of uterine contractions. This effect is not consistent and may be offset by an increased rate of cervical dilatation, which may shorten labor. In addition, neonates exposed to opioids in utero may present with respiratory depression at birth. Because of this, an opioid antagonist (e.g., naloxone) must be available for reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression in the neonate at delivery. Guidelines recommend early universal screening of pregnant individuals for opioid use and opioid use disorder at the first prenatal visit. Obtain a thorough history of substance use and review the Prescription Drug Monitoring Program to determine if individuals have received prior prescriptions for opioids or other high-risk drugs such as benzodiazepines. Discuss the risks and benefits of opioid use during pregnancy, including the risk of becoming physiologically dependent on opioids, the possibility for neonatal withdrawal, and how long-term opioid use may affect care during a future pregnancy. In individuals undergoing uncomplicated normal spontaneous vaginal birth, consider opioid therapy only if expected benefits for both pain and function are anticipated to outweigh risks. If opioids are used, use in combination with nonpharmacologic therapy and non-opioid pharmacologic therapy, as appropriate. Use immediate-release opioids instead of extended-release or long-acting opioids; order the lowest effective dosage and prescribe no greater quantity of opioids than needed for the expected duration of such pain severe enough to require opioids. For individuals using opioids for chronic pain, consider strategies to avoid or minimize the use of opioids, including alternative pain therapies (i.e., nonpharmacologic) and non-opioid pharmacologic treatments. Opioid agonist pharmacotherapy (e.g., methadone or buprenorphine) is preferable to medically supervised withdrawal in pregnant individuals with opioid use disorder.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with opioids. Long-term use of opioids may influence the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, leading to hormonal changes that may manifest as a libido decrease, impotence, erectile dysfunction, amenorrhea, or infertility. The causal role of opioids in the clinical manifestations of hypogonadism is unknown. It is not known whether the effects on fertility are reversible.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero may present with respiratory depression and psycho-physiologic effects at birth. Monitor exposed newborns for signs of excess sedation and respiratory depression. If necessary, administer an opioid antagonist (e.g., naloxone) to reverse the effects of opioid-induced respiratory depression. Long-term use of opioids during pregnancy can result in physical dependence in the exposed infant. Monitor newborns for symptoms of withdrawal and manage accordingly. Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome may present as irritability, hyperactivity, abnormal sleep pattern, high-pitched cry, tremor, vomiting, diarrhea, and failure to gain weight. It can be life-threatening if not recognized and treated. Onset, duration, and severity of opioid withdrawal may vary based on the specific opioid used, duration of use, timing and amount of last maternal use, and rate of elimination by the infant.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use oliceridine with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of oliceridine in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Monitor children exposed to opioids through breast milk for excess sedation and respiratory depression. Withdrawal symptoms may occur in the breast-fed child when maternal administration of the opioid is stopped or when breast-feeding is discontinued, particularly with chronic use. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition.

    Headline
    flushing, hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, QT prolongation, sinus tachycardia, syncope
    General Information
    Oliceridine may cause severe hypotension, including orthostatic hypotension and syncope in ambulatory individuals. Hypotension, sinus tachycardia, increased blood pressure, and flushing were reported in more than 1% to less than 5% of oliceridine-treated subjects during clinical trials; hot flush was reported in more than 1% to 7% of subjects. The effect of oliceridine on cardiac physiology was studied in single and multiple-dose thorough QT studies. The multiple-dose study was conducted with a maximum daily cumulative dose of 27 mg. In both studies, there was mild QT prolongation. In the multiple-dose study, the maximum mean placebo-corrected, baseline-adjusted change in the heart rate-corrected QT interval, using an individualized correction method (QTcI), was 11.7 milliseconds (2-sided 90% CI 14.7 milliseconds) at 9 hours. The effect on QT prolongation at total cumulative daily doses more than 27 mg has not been studied in a thorough QT study. Total cumulative daily doses exceeding 27 mg/day may increase the risk for QTc interval prolongation; therefore, do not exceed an oliceridine cumulative total daily dose of 27 mg/day.

    Headline
    amenorrhea, gonadal suppression, impotence (erectile dysfunction), infertility, libido decrease
    General Information
    Opioids may interfere with the endocrine system by inhibiting the secretion of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), cortisol, and luteinizing hormone (LH), and by stimulating secretion of prolactin, growth hormone (GH), insulin, and glucagon. Chronic use of opioids may influence the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, leading to hormonal changes that may manifest as hypogonadism (gonadal suppression). Although the exact causal role of opioids in the clinical manifestations of hypogonadism is unknown, patients could experience libido decrease, impotence (erectile dysfunction), amenorrhea, or infertility. Other various medical, physical, lifestyle, and psychological stressors may influence gonadal hormone concentrations; these stressors have not been adequately controlled for in clinical studies with opioids. Patients presenting with signs or symptoms of androgen deficiency should undergo laboratory evaluation.

    General Information
    Pharmacologic tolerance can develop during chronic opioid therapy. Tolerance is the need for increasing opioid doses to maintain initial pain relief. Typically, tolerance presents as a decrease in the duration of analgesia and can be managed by increasing the opioid dose or frequency.     neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome, physiological dependence, psychological dependence, withdrawal
    General Information
    Oliceridine is a controlled substance with a high potential for abuse and psychological dependence. Abuse and addiction are separate and distinct from physiological dependence and tolerance, which can develop during chronic opioid therapy. In postmarketing studies, addiction, abuse, misuse, and fatal and non-fatal opioid overdose were observed in subjects with long-term opioid use. In a prospective, 12-month observational cohort study in individuals initiating long-term use of Schedule II opioid analgesics, 1% to 6% of subjects across 2 cohorts met the criteria for addiction. Approximately 9% and 22% of subjects newly met the criteria for prescription opioid abuse and misuse, respectively. In a retrospective, observational cohort study in individuals with new long-term use of Schedule II opioid analgesics, the 5-year cumulative incidence estimates for opioid-involved overdose or opioid overdose-related death ranged from approximately 1.5% to 4% across study sites, counting only the first event during follow-up. Approximately 17% of first opioid overdoses observed over the entire study period (5 to 11 years) were fatal. Higher baseline opioid dose was the strongest and most consistent predictor of opioid-involved overdose or opioid overdose-related death. Tolerance is characterized by a reduced response to a drug after repeated administration. Physiological dependence develops as a result of repeated drug use. It manifests with a withdrawal syndrome after abrupt discontinuation or significant dose reduction, or administration of an agent with opioid antagonist activity (e.g., naloxone). Withdrawal is characterized by restlessness, lacrimation, rhinorrhea, yawning, perspiration, chills, myalgia, and mydriasis. Other symptoms include irritability, anxiety, backache, joint pain, weakness, abdominal cramps, insomnia, nausea, anorexia, vomiting, diarrhea, or increased blood pressure, respiratory rate, or heart rate. Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) may occur in neonates and infants exposed to opioids in utero. Withdrawal symptoms may include irritability, hyperactivity, abnormal sleep pattern, high-pitched cry, tremor, vomiting, diarrhea, and failure to gain weight. Onset, duration, and severity of opioid withdrawal may vary based on the specific opioid used, duration of use, timing and amount of last maternal use, and rate of elimination by the infant. Exposed newborns should be monitored closely. NOWS can be life-threatening if not recognized and treated. Treatment should be managed by clinicians experienced with neonatal withdrawal.

    Serotonin syndrome has been reported in patients taking opioids at recommended doses.     General Information
    Cases of opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH) have been reported, both with short-term and longer-term use of opioids. OIH occurs when an opioid paradoxically causes an increase in pain or an increase in sensitivity to pain. Symptoms of OIH include increased levels of pain upon opioid dosage increase, decreased levels of pain upon opioid dosage decrease, or pain from ordinarily non-painful stimuli (allodynia). These symptoms may suggest OIH only if there is no evidence of underlying disease progression, opioid tolerance, opioid withdrawal, or addictive behavior. Data suggests a strong biologic plausibility between opioids and OIH and allodynia. If OIH is suspected, carefully consider appropriately decreasing the dose of the current opioid analgesic or opioid rotation (safely switching to a different opioid).

Description
  Text
  Oliceridine is an intravenous opioid agonist indicated for the treatment of severe acute pain in adults, where the pain is severe enough to require an intravenous opioid and for whom alternative treatments are inadequate. Oliceridine is indicated for use in hospitals or other controlled clinical settings, such as during inpatient and outpatient procedures. It is not indicated for at-home use. Use of oliceridine beyond 48 hours has not been evaluated in controlled clinical trials. Safety and efficacy were established by comparing oliceridine to both morphine and placebo in randomized, controlled trials of patients undergoing bunionectomy (n = 389) or abdominoplasty (n = 401). Oliceridine demonstrated a statistically greater reduction in pain intensity than placebo and was non-inferior to morphine in both populations. It has a favorable safety and tolerability profile for respiratory and gastrointestinal adverse effects compared to morphine.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Oliceridine is a full opioid agonist and is relatively selective for the mu-opioid receptor. It is a mu-opioid receptor ligand that preferentially engages the G protein pathway, which is thought to be the principal pathway leading to the analgesic effects of opioids. Oliceridine has minimal to no recruitment of beta-arrestin signaling compared to conventional opioids, which is believed to reduce its opioid-related adverse event profile.

  Text
  Oliceridine is administered intravenously. Mean steady-state Vd ranges from 90 to 120 L, indicating extensive tissue distribution. Plasma protein binding is 77%. Oliceridine is primarily metabolized by CYP2D6 and CYP3A4, with minor contributions from CYP2C9 and CYP2C19 into inactive metabolites. Metabolic clearance occurs via oxidation with subsequent glucuronidation. Other biotransformation pathways include N-dealkylation, glucuronidation, and dehydrogenation. The majority of metabolites (70%) and a small amount of parent drug (0.97% to 6.75% of the dose) are excreted in the urine, with the remainder of metabolites eliminated in the feces. The half-life of oliceridine is 1.3 to 3 hours.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, CYP3A4
Oliceridine is primarily metabolized by CYP2D6 and CYP3A4, with minor contributions from CYP2C9 and CYP2C19. Only the inhibition of CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 significantly affects drug metabolism. Oliceridicine does not inhibit any P450 isoenzymes at clinically relevant concentrations. In vitro data indicate oliceridine is not an inhibitor of any of the major drug transporters (e.g., BCRP, MDR1).
