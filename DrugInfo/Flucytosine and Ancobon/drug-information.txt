Drug Class: Flucytosine is an oral antifungal agent.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 150 mg/kg/day PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): agranulocytosis, anemia, aplastic anemia, bone marrow suppression, eosinophilia, fatigue, fever, leukopenia, pancytopenia, thrombocytopenia, weakness, colitis, gi bleeding, peptic ulcer, azotemia, crystalluria, renal failure (unspecified), elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatic necrosis, hepatotoxicity, hyperbilirubinemia, jaundice, photosensitivity, pruritus, rash (unspecified), toxic epidermal necrolysis, urticaria, ataxia, confusion, drowsiness

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Flucytosine is renally eliminated and must be given with extreme caution to patients with renal impairment. Flucytosine serum concentrations can accumulate to toxic levels in patients with renal impairment.

        Neonates†
        Dosage Description
        50 to 150 mg/kg/day PO divided every 6 hours in combination with amphotericin B (conventional or lipid formulation) is the most commonly reported dosage range; adjust dosage based on serum flucytosine concentrations. Rarely, up to 200 mg/kg/day PO has been used; however, based on pharmacokinetic data, that dosage is likely to be excessive for most neonates. Although most cases in the literature were administered flucytosine in 4 divided doses per day, pharmacokinetic data suggest that a longer interval (i.e., 50 to 100 mg/kg/day given every 24 hours) may be sufficient in neonates due to a longer elimination half-life, immature renal function, and the time-dependent pharmacodynamic activity of flucytosine. The addition of flucytosine to amphotericin B therapy is maybe used as part of salvage therapy.

        Heading
        Neonates
        Dosage Description
        50 to 150 mg/kg/day PO divided every 6 hours in combination with amphotericin B (conventional or lipid formulation) is the most commonly reported dosage range ; adjust dosage based on serum flucytosine concentrations. Rarely, up to 200 mg/kg/day PO has been used ; however, based on pharmacokinetic data, that dosage is likely to be excessive for most neonates. Although most cases in the literature were administered flucytosine in 4 divided doses per day, pharmacokinetic data suggest that a longer interval (i.e., 50 to 100 mg/kg/day given every 24 hours) may be sufficient in neonates due to a longer elimination half-life, immature renal function, and the time-dependent pharmacodynamic activity of flucytosine. Treatment should continue until all signs and symptoms and CSF and radiologic abnormalities have resolved. Intraventricular devices should be removed. The addition of flucytosine to amphotericin B therapy is not routinely recommended for neonatal candidiasis by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA). Treatment outcomes of combination therapy with amphotericin B and flucytosine for candidiasis in neonates have been conflicting. An early study revealed improvement in 15 patients (n = 7 neonates) on combination therapy. In a study of extremely low birth weight neonates with candidiasis (total n = 320; with positive culture for Candida in CSF, n = 27), clearance of CSF was longer in neonates who received combination therapy compared with amphotericin B alone.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Flucytosine is renally eliminated and must be given with extreme caution to patients with renal impairment. Flucytosine serum concentrations can accumulate to toxic levels in patients with renal impairment. In addition, adverse effects occur more frequently in azotemic patients. Frequent serum concentrations of flucytosine should be determined in patients with renal disease to ensure that levels remain within the therapeutic range of 50—100 mcg/ml. Thoroughly review prescribing information before administering flucytosine.Flucytosine must be given with extreme caution to patients with bone marrow depression. Patients at risk include those with hematologic disease, receiving radiation therapy or drugs which depress bone marrow, or have a history of treatment with such drugs or radiation. Bone marrow toxicity can be irreversible and may lead to death in immunosuppressed patients. The risk/benefit ratio should be considered when flucytosine is to be administered to patients with bone marrow suppression or a history of a hematological disease because they are most susceptible to flucytosine's myelosuppressive effects. Frequent monitoring of hepatic function and of the hematopoietic system is indicated during flucytosine therapy.Flucytosine should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease because it can cause hepatitis or jaundice, worsening any previously existing hepatic dysfunction.Patients who have previously received either radiation therapy or cytotoxic drug therapy also may be at an increased risk for flucytosine-associated bone marrow suppression because their bone marrow may still be recovering from the previous myelosuppression.Flucytosine should be used with caution in patients with dental disease. Flucytosine can cause myelosuppression and there may be an increased risk of infection. Dental work should be performed prior to initiating flucytosine therapy or deferred until blood counts return to normal.Intramuscular injections should not be administered to patients with platelet counts < 50,000/mm3 who are receiving flucytosine. IM injections may cause bleeding, bruising, or hematomas due to thrombocytopenia secondary to flucytosine-induced bone marrow suppression.Flucytosine should be used cautiously in patients with electrolyte imbalance (especially hypokalemia). Flucytosine has been associated with hypokalemia. Close monitoring is necessary during flucytosine therapy.Flucytosine is classified as pregnancy category C. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of flucytosine in pregnant women. Flucytosine has shown teratogenic effects in rats, so it should not be used during pregnancy or in females with the potential to become pregnant during therapy.It is not known if flucytosine is excreted in human milk. Because many drugs are excreted in human milk and because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in nursing infants, a decision should be made whether to discontinue breast-feeding or to discontinue flucytosine, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother.The safe and effective use of flucytosine has not been systematically studied in children or infants. A small number of neonates were treated with flucytosine, with and without amphotericin B, for systemic candidiasis. No unexpected adverse reactions occurred. However, hypokalemia and acidemia were reported in one patient who received flucytosine with amphotericin B, and anemia was observed in another patient who received flucytosine alone. Transient thrombocytopenia was noted in two other patients, one of whom also received amphotericin B.Flucytosine may cause photosensitivity so patients should use caution with sunlight (UV) exposure.

    dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase deficiency
    General Information
    Flucytosine is contraindicated in individuals with known complete dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase deficiency.     Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Use flucytosine with extreme caution in individuals with renal impairment or renal failure. Flucytosine is primarily excreted by the kidneys, and renal impairment may lead to drug accumulation. Monitor renal function and flucytosine serum concentrations closely during therapy; adjust the flucytosine dose in individuals with renal insufficiency to prevent progressive accumulation of the drug.
    Headline
    bone marrow suppression
    General Information
    Use flucytosine with extreme caution in individuals with bone marrow suppression. Flucytosine may cause bone marrow toxicity, which may be irreversible and lead to death in immunosuppressed individuals. Individuals with underlying hematologic disease, those receiving radiation therapy or other medications that suppress bone marrow, or those with a history of treatment with such medications or radiation may be at increased risk for bone marrow suppression. Frequent monitoring of hematopoietic function is indicated during flucytosine therapy.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Use flucytosine during pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant people. In animal studies, flucytosine was shown to be teratogenic in rats at doses ranging from 0.051 to 0.89 times the recommended human dose (RHD) during the first trimester (days 7 to 13 of gestation). Teratogenic effects observed in the offspring included vertebral fusions, cleft lip and palate, and micrognathia. Flucytosine was not teratogenic in rabbits receiving doses of 0.243 times RHD, nor were there any statistically significant increases in adverse events observed in mice receiving doses of 0.236 times RHD.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking flucytosine, due to the risk of serious adverse reactions in the breastfed child. There are no data on the presence of flucytosine or its metabolites in human milk, their effects on the breastfed child, or their effects on milk production. Based on the drug's low protein binding, small molecular weight, and high oral bioavailability, a moderate amount of drug is expected to be transferred to milk.

  General Information
  Flucytosine toxicity involves the rapidly proliferating tissues such as the bone marrow and the lining of the GI tract. Description
  Text
  Flucytosine is an oral antifungal agent. It is a fluorinated pyrimidine that is structurally similar to both fluorouracil and floxuridine. Flucytosine is also known as 5-FC. It was approved by the FDA in 1971 and until recently was used mainly in combination with amphotericin B in the treatment of cryptococcal meningitis. Since the release of fluconazole in 1990, flucytosine use has declined significantly.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Flucytosine penetrates fungal cells, where it is deaminated to fluorouracil by the fungal enzyme cytosine deaminase. Mammalian cells do not convert flucytosine to fluorouracil. Acting as an antimetabolite, fluorouracil competes with uracil, interfering with pyrimidine metabolism and eventually disrupting both RNA and protein synthesis. Flucytosine may also be converted to fluorodeoxyuridylic acid, which inhibits the enzyme thymidylate synthase and disrupts DNA synthesis. Although flucytosine is metabolized to 5-fluorouracil, flucytosine itself does not possess antineoplastic activity.
 
Susceptible fungi readily deaminate flucytosine to its active component, 5-fluorouracil. Resistance develops rapidly, however, if flucytosine is used as a single agent. The mechanism of resistance can be loss of the permease necessary for cytosine transport or decreased activity of uridine monophosphate pyrophosphorylase or cytosine deaminase. Fungi that usually are susceptible include: Candida species, Cryptococcus neoformans, and Candida glabrata.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Flucytosine is rapidly and almost completely absorbed with a bioavailability between 78% to 89%. Peak concentrations are achieved approximately 1 to 2 hours after administration. After a single 2 g dose in normal adults, peak serum concentrations were 30 to 40 mcg/mL; however, mean concentrations of 70 to 80 mcg/mL were measured in adults receiving flucytosine 150 mg/kg/day in combination with amphotericin for 6 weeks.

    Pediatrics
    Description
    Neonates
The clearance of flucytosine is prolonged and highly variable in neonatal patients due to immature renal function. In a pharmacokinetic study in 13 neonates (mean gestational age, 27.4 weeks [range, 24 to 40 weeks]; mean weight, 1.2 kg [range, 580 to 3,320 g]), the median elimination half-life of flucytosine was 7.4 hours, which is approximately twice that of adults; however, clearance was found to be significantly variable with half-lives ranging from 3.4 to 34.1 hours. Median volume of distribution was 1.1 L/kg (range, 0.4 to 2 L/kg), and the time to peak serum concentration was 2.5 +/- 1.3 hours. In this study, neonates received flucytosine 50 to 100 mg/kg/day PO once daily and amphotericin B IV (usual dose = 0.5 mg/kg/day infused over 4 to 6 hours).
  Text
  Flucytosine is administered orally. It is a small molecule with limited protein binding (2% to 4%); therefore, it has a large Vd (approaching that of total body water) and is widely distributed throughout the body. Flucytosine penetrates the blood-brain barrier, achieving therapeutic concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid. Flucytosine is not metabolized. While fungal cells convert flucytosine to fluorouracil intracellularly, only trace amounts of fluorouracil can be detected in the serum. Elimination is primarily renal. Over 90% of a dose is excreted by glomerular filtration as unchanged drug. The elimination half-life is a function of creatinine clearance; in most adults with normal renal function, it is around 2.5 to 4 hours.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: none

      Extemporaneous Compounding-Oral
      Description
      Extemporaneous preparation of 10 mg/mL flucytosine oral suspension
Empty the contents of four (4) flucytosine 250-mg capsules into a glass mortar. Add 10 mL of vehicle to the powder and triturate well.
Vehicle options include Cherry syrup or a 1:1 mixture of Ora-Plus and Ora-Sweet (or Ora-Sweet SF)
Transfer the contents to a calibrated amber bottle. Rinse the mortar with additional vehicle and pour the contents into the bottle. Repeat rinsing mortar with the vehicle to ensure complete transfer of flucytosine powder into the bottle.
Add enough vehicle to the bottle to bring the total volume of the suspension to 100 mL.
Storage: The suspension is stable for 60 days when stored at room temperature or under refrigeration. Protect from light.
 
Extemporaneous preparation of 50 mg/mL flucytosine oral suspension
Empty the contents of six (6) flucytosine 500-mg capsules into a glass mortar. Add 15 mL of vehicle to the powder and triturate well.
Vehicle options include a 1:1 mixture of Ora-Plus and Ora-Sweet SF or Ora-Plus and strawberry syrup (prepared by combining 3,200 mL Simple Syrup NF with 600 mL strawberry fountain syrup)
Transfer the contents to a calibrated amber bottle. Rinse the mortar with another 15 mL of vehicle and pour the contents into the bottle. Repeat rinsing mortar with the vehicle to ensure the complete transfer of flucytosine powder into the bottle.
Add enough vehicle to the bottle to bring the total volume of the suspension to 60 mL.
Storage: The suspension is stable for 90 days when stored at room temperature or under refrigeration. Protect from light.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  Adults
Initial dosage adjustment:
CrCl more than 40 mL/minute: No dosage adjustment needed.
CrCl 20 to 40 mL/minute: Extend initial dosing interval to every 12 hours. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on serum flucytosine concentrations.
CrCl 10 to 19 mL/minute: Extend initial dosing interval to every 24 hours. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on serum flucytosine concentrations.
CrCl less than 10 mL/minute: Extend initial dosing interval to every 48 hours. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on serum flucytosine concentrations.
 
Pediatric patients
The following initial dosage adjustments are based on the usual recommended dose in children of 25 to 37.5 mg/kg/dose PO every 6 hours:
CrCl 30 to 50 mL/minute/1.73 m2: Extend initial dosing interval to every 8 hours. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on serum flucytosine concentrations.
CrCl 10 to 29 mL/minute/1.73 m2: Extend initial dosing interval to every 12 hours. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on serum flucytosine concentrations.
CrCl less than 10 mL/minute/1.73 m2: Extend initial dosing interval to every 24 hours. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on serum flucytosine concentrations.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
Adults
25 to 50 mg/kg PO every 48 to 72 hours, given immediately after dialysis. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on serum flucytosine concentrations.
 
Pediatric patients
25 to 37.5 mg/kg/dose PO every 24 hours. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on flucytosine serum concentrations.
 
Peritoneal dialysis
Adults
500 mg to 1 g PO every 24 hours. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on flucytosine serum concentrations.
 
Pediatric patients
25 to 37.5 mg/kg/dose PO every 24 hours. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on flucytosine serum concentrations.
 
Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT)
Adults
Extend initial dosing interval to every 12 to 24 hours. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on flucytosine serum concentrations.
 
Pediatric patients
25 to 37.5 mg/kg/dose PO every 8 hours. Adjust dosage, if necessary, based on flucytosine serum concentrations.
