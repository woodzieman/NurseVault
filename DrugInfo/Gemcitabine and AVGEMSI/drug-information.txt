Drug Class: Gemcitabine is a deoxycytidine antimetabolite nucleoside inhibitor, closely related to cytarabine.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 1,250 mg/m2 IV once weekly; number of weekly doses and length of cycle is indication-specific.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): drowsiness, fatigue, headache, pharyngitis, asthenia, chills, diaphoresis, fever, insomnia, malaise, myalgia, rhinitis, azotemia, hematuria, hemolytic uremic syndrome, proteinuria, renal failure (unspecified), elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatic failure, hepatotoxicity, hyperbilirubinemia, sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, veno-occlusive disease, colitis, enterocolitis, renal tubular necrosis, vasculitis, anemia, hemolysis, lymphopenia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Gemcitabine should be used cautiously in patients who have had previous myelosuppressive chemotherapy.           1,000 mg/m2 IV over 30 minutes on days 1, 8, and 15 every 28 days for 6 cycles. In a phase 3 trial, 368 patients with previously untreated pancreatic cancer were randomized to receive gemcitabine or observation after complete surgical resection. Disease-free survival (13.4 months vs. 6.9 months), the primary endpoint, and overall survival (22.8 months vs. 20.2 months) were significantly longer in the gemcitabine arm. In another phase 3 trial, 442 patients with surgically resected pancreatic cancer were randomized to receive gemcitabine before and after fluorouracil-based chemoradiation therapy (CRT) or fluorouracil before and after fluorouracil-based CRT. Beginning 3 to 8 weeks after surgery, gemcitabine patients received 1,000 mg/m2 IV over 30 minutes on days 1, 8, and 15, followed in 1 to 2 weeks by 5.5 weeks of fluorouracil-based CRT, which was followed in 3 to 5 weeks by gemcitabine 1,000 mg/m2 IV over 30 minutes on days 1, 8, and 15, every 28 days for 3 additional cycles. The prospective primary endpoints of the study were overall survival (OS) and OS in patients with tumors of the pancreatic head. No significant difference was observed in OS for the total treatment population, however patients with tumors confined to the pancreatic head (n = 330) did experience a significantly longer OS in the gemcitabine arm (20.6 months vs. 16.9 months). Grade 4 hematologic toxicities were significantly greater in patients treated with gemcitabine (14% vs. 2%).

          1,000 mg/m2 IV over 100 minutes (10 mg/m2/minute) on day 1 in combination with oxaliplatin (100 mg/m2 IV on day 2), repeated every 2 weeks until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Gemcitabine should be used cautiously in patients who have had previous myelosuppressive chemotherapy. Patients treated with gemcitabine should be monitored for hematologic toxicity with a complete blood count, including differential and platelet count prior to each dose or on day 8 of combination therapy and prior to the next cycle of combination therapy. Gemcitabine dosage modification is recommended in patients experiencing significant bone marrow suppression with neutropenia (nadir = 1500/mm3 and platelet count >= 100,000/mm3 prior to the next dose or cycle of gemcitabine. Patients with an active infection should be treated before administering gemcitabine. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with chemotherapy. Gemcitabine doses should be reduced or discontinued in patients who develop such infections.
 
Myelosuppressive effects of gemcitabine can increase the risk of infection or bleeding; therefore, dental work should be delayed until blood counts have returned to normal. Patients, especially those with dental disease, should be instructed in proper oral hygiene, including caution in use of regular toothbrushes, dental floss, and toothpicks.
 
Although gemcitabine-induced thrombocytopenia is transient, intramuscular injections should not be administered to patients with platelet counts = 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Reactions include anaphylaxis.
    Headline
    radiation therapy
    General Information
    Gemcitabine is not recommended for use in combination with radiation therapy due to the risk of excessive toxicity. In a clinical trial of patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) receiving concurrent (given together or 7 days or less apart) gemcitabine and thoracic radiation therapy, life-threatening mucositis, esophagitis, and pneumonitis were reported. Excessive toxicity has not been observed when gemcitabine is administered more than 7 days before or after radiation (nonconcurrent); however, radiation recall has occurred in patients who have received gemcitabine after prior radiation.
    Headline
    alcohol use disorder, hepatic cirrhosis, hepatitis, liver metastases
    General Information
    Use gemcitabine with caution in patients with concurrent liver metastases, or a history of hepatitis, alcohol use disorder, or hepatic cirrhosis. Treatment with gemcitabine can cause drug-induced liver injury and administration in patients with these conditions may exacerbate the underlying hepatic insufficiency.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Pregnancy should be avoided by females of reproductive potential during gemcitabine treatment and for at least 6 months after the last dose. Although there are no adequately controlled studies in pregnant animals or humans, gemcitabine can cause fetal harm or death when administered during pregnancy based on its mechanism of action and animal studies. Women who are pregnant or who become pregnant while receiving gemcitabine should be apprised of the potential hazard to the fetus. Two case reports described gemcitabine administration to pregnant women in the second trimester or later. In one case, a woman with lung cancer was administered gemcitabine and carboplatin at 25 weeks gestation. The baby was delivered at 28 weeks by elective cesarean. The baby had multiple complications from prematurity, but the authors speculated that chronic lung disease and excessive lung secretions may have been due to gemcitabine or the malignancy. At 8 months of age, the infant was successfully weaned from oxygen therapy and had age-appropriate neurodevelopment. The other case report described a pregnant woman with lung cancer who received gemcitabine in combination with cisplatin (weeks 19 to 25), in addition to cisplatin and docetaxel (weeks 9 to 18); the infant was delivered by cesarean at 33 weeks. An extensive workup of the infant failed to find any abnormalities and the infant was developing normally at 10 months of age. In animal studies, gemcitabine was embryotoxic in mice at doses approximately 0.005 times the recommended human dose on a mg/m2 basis, causing fetal malformations such as cleft palate and incomplete ossification. It was fetotoxic in rabbits at doses approximately 0.002 times the recommended human dose on a mg/m2 basis, resulting in fetal malformations such as fused pulmonary artery and absence of the gall bladder; embryotoxicity also occurred, including decreased fetal viability, reduced live litter sizes, and developmental delays.
    Headline
    contraception requirements, infertility, male-mediated teratogenicity, pregnancy testing, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel patients about the reproductive risk and contraception requirements during gemcitabine treatment. Gemcitabine can be teratogenic if taken by the mother during pregnancy.  Females of reproductive potential should avoid pregnancy and use effective contraception during treatment with gemcitabine and for 6 months after the last dose; males with female partners of reproductive potential should use effective contraception during and for at least 3 months after treatment with gemcitabine due to a risk of male-mediated teratogenicity. Females of reproductive potential should undergo pregnancy testing prior to initiation of gemcitabine. Women who become pregnant while receiving gemcitabine should be apprised of the potential hazard to the fetus. Based on animal studies, gemcitabine may cause infertility in males of reproductive potential. It is not known whether these effects on fertility are reversible.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in nursing infants from gemcitabine, advise women to discontinue breast-feeding during treatment and for at least 1 week after the final dose. It is not known whether gemcitabine is present in human milk, although many drugs are excreted in human milk.
    Headline
    bladder perforation
    General Information
    Gemcitabine for intravesical administration (Inlexzo) is contraindicated in patients with a bladder perforation. Evaluate the bladder before intravesical administration and do not administer to patients with a perforated bladder or mucosal compromise until bladder integrity has been restored due to the risk of systemic exposure and severe adverse reactions.
    Headline
    magnetic resonance imaging
    General Information
    A patient with an intravesicular gemcitabine system indwelling in the bladder can be scanned in a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) system only under the following conditions due to potential safety hazards or severe adverse reactions. Static magnetic field of 1.5-Tesla and 3-Tesla, only; maximum special gradient magnetic field of 5,000 Guass/cm or less; and maximum magnetic resonance system reported, whole body averaged specific absorption rate of 1-W/kg for 60 minutes of continuous scanning (i.e., per pulse sequence or back-to-back sequences without breaks) in the Normal Operating Mode of operation for the MR system. Under these conditions, an intravesicular gemcitabine system is expected to produce a maximum temperature rise of 2 degrees Celsius after 15 minutes of continuous scanning. In nonclinical testing, the image artifact caused by an intravesicular gemcitabine system extends approximately 2 mm from the device using a gradient echo pulse sequence and a 3-Tesla MR system.

Interactions
Cholera Vaccine: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the live cholera vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to cholera bacteria after receiving the vaccine.
Decitabine; Cedazuridine: (Major) Avoid the concomitant use of decitabine; cedazuridine with drugs that are metabolized by the enzyme cytidine deaminase (CDA), such as gemcitabine; the cytotoxicity of gemcitabine may be increased. Cedazuridine is a CDA inhibitor. CDA is one of the enzymes responsible for the metabolism of gemcitabine to form inactive metabolites.
Dengue Tetravalent Vaccine, Live: (Contraindicated) Avoid administration of the live dengue virus vaccine with immunosuppressive drug therapy and prior to immune recovery following treatment with immunosuppressive drug therapy. When feasible, administer indicated live virus vaccines at least 4 weeks before planned immunosuppression or wait until at least 3 months after discontinuation. The time to restoration of immune competence may be longer in some patients. Patients with altered immunocompetence may be at increased risk for severe adverse reactions due to uninhibited growth of the attenuated live virus. Additionally, vaccines may be less effective if administered during a period of altered immunocompetence.
Doravirine; Islatravir: (Major) Avoid concomitant use of islatravir and gemcitabine. Concurrent use may decrease islatravir exposure which may result in loss of therapeutic effect and development of resistance. Concomitant use may interfere with the conversion of islatravir to its active form, islatravir-triphosphate (ISL-TP). Islatravir is activated via cellular kinases; gemcitabine is a deoxycytidine kinase (dCK) substrate and may compete for this activation pathway.
Lithium: (Moderate) Moderate to significant dietary sodium changes, or changes in sodium and fluid intake, may affect lithium excretion. Systemic sodium chloride administration may result in increased lithium excretion and therefore, decreased serum lithium concentrations. In addition, high fluid intake may increase lithium excretion. For patients receiving sodium-containing intravenous fluids, symptom control and lithium concentrations should be carefully monitored. It is recommended that patients taking lithium maintain consistent dietary sodium consumption and adequate fluid intake during the initial stabilization period and throughout lithium treatment. Supplemental oral sodium and fluid should be only be administered under careful medical supervision.
SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) vaccines: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the SARS-CoV-2 virus vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to SARS-CoV-2 virus after receiving the vaccine.
SARS-CoV-2 Virus (COVID-19) mRNA Vaccine: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the SARS-CoV-2 virus vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to SARS-CoV-2 virus after receiving the vaccine.
SARS-CoV-2 Virus (COVID-19) Recombinant Spike Protein Nanoparticle Vaccine: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the SARS-CoV-2 virus vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to SARS-CoV-2 virus after receiving the vaccine.
Sodium Iodide I-131: (Moderate) Monitor for bone marrow suppression during concomitant use of sodium iodide I-131 and antineoplastic agents. Concomitant use of bone marrow depressants may enhance the depression of the hematopoietic system caused by the use of large doses of sodium iodide I-131.
Tolvaptan: (Moderate) Coadministration of tolvaptan and hypertonic saline (e.g., 3% NaCl injection solution) is not recommended. The use of hypertonic sodium chloride in combination with tolvaptan may result in a too rapid correction of hyponatremia and increase the risk of osmotic demyelination (i.e., central pontine myelinolysis).
Tuberculin Purified Protein Derivative, PPD: (Moderate) Immunosuppressives may decrease the immunological response to tuberculin purified protein derivative, PPD. This suppressed reactivity can persist for up to 6 weeks after treatment discontinuation. Consider deferring the skin test until completion of the immunosuppressive therapy.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Myelosuppression, which may include pancytopenia, is the dose-limiting toxicity of gemcitabine when given as a single agent. Four possibly treatment-related deaths in patients with myelosuppression were reported in the non-small cell lung cancer trials where gemcitabine was given in combination with cisplatin; no deaths were reported in the cisplatin alone arm. In another study comparing gemcitabine plus cisplatin with cisplatin and etoposide, one death (1.5%) was reported in the gemcitabine/cisplatin arm due to febrile neutropenia associated with renal failure, which as possibly treatment related. No treatment-related deaths occurred in the cisplatin/etoposide arm. During single agent gemcitabine trials, leukopenia and neutropenia occurred in 62% and 63% of patients, respectively. In trials of gemcitabine plus cisplatin, leukopenia and neutropenia were reported in 82—86% and 79—81% of patients, respectively. Anemia has been reported in 68% of patients receiving single-agent gemcitabine therapy; 8% of patients experienced grade 3/4 anemia. Thrombocytopenia occurs in 24—85% of patients treated with gemcitabine alone or in combination, although, Grade 3 or 4 thrombocytopenia occurs in <= 10% of patients treated with gemcitabine alone. The incidence of severe (grade 3/4) leukopenia and neutropenia was 29—64% when gemcitabine was given in combination with cisplatin. In the studies comparing gemcitabine/cisplatin to etoposide/cisplatin in non-small cell lung cancer, the overall incidence of Grade 4 neutropenia on the gemcitabine/cisplatin arm was less than on the etoposide/cisplatin arm; the incidence of sepsis was the same on both arms (2%). Grade 3 anemia and Grade 3/4 thrombocytopenia was more common in patients treated with gemcitabine/cisplatin versus etoposide/cisplatin. During trials of gemcitabine plus cisplatin anemia occurred in 89% of patients with 25% of patients experiencing grade 3/4 anemia. Grade 3 and 4 thrombocytopenia occurred in 50% of patients treated with gemcitabine in combination with cisplatin. When gemcitabine was given in combination with paclitaxel during clinical trials leukopenia and neutropenia were reported in 21% and 69% of patients, respectively. Grade 3/4 neutropenia was reported in 48% of patients treated with the gemcitabine and paclitaxel combination. Clinical trial experience with gemcitabine and paclitaxel demonstrated an anemia incidence of 69% with 7% of patients experiencing grade3 or 4 toxicity. Less than 6% of patients treated with gemcitabine and paclitaxel experienced Grade 3 or 4 thrombocytopenia. During the clinical trial of gemcitabine and carboplatin in ovarian cancer, the overall incidence of leukopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia were 86%, 88%, 81%, and 88%, respectively. Red blood cell transfusions were required in 29% of patients and platelet transfusions in 3% of patients. Myelosuppression in ovarian cancer patients was managed with hematopoietic growth factors and chemotherapy dosage adjustments. Bleeding occurs in about 17% of all patients; however severe bleeding events are rare. Clinically, it appears that gemcitabine myelosuppression is cumulative and affects platelets more than other cell lines. In addition, elderly patients seem to require greater dose reductions for myelosuppression than other patient groups.Infections have been reported in 16% and 28% of patients receiving gemcitabine alone or in combination with other agents, respectively. Sepsis has been seen in 4% of patients treated for non-small cell lung cancer and in < 1% of patients treated for pancreatic cancer. Febrile neutropenia was reported in 5% of patients treated with gemcitabine and paclitaxel combination therapy.The overall incidence of fever was 41% in patients receiving gemcitabine as a single agent. Fever, not related to infection, can develop within 6—12 hours of the first dose of gemcitabine. The fever responds to acetaminophen and usually occurs only with the first dose. A flu-like syndrome has been observed with gemcitabine therapy. Symptoms including fever, asthenia, anorexia, headache, cough, chills, and myalgia were commonly reported. Fever and asthenia were also frequently reported as isolated symptoms. Insomnia, rhinitis, diaphoresis, and malaise were reported infrequently.A maculopapular rash, including pruritus (13%) and erythema, can appear within 48—72 hours of starting therapy with gemcitabine in 30% of patients. A skin rash (unspecified) including desquamation was reported in 11% of patients receiving gemcitabine in combination with paclitaxel. Mild alopecia, usually minimal, was reported by 15% of patients receiving single-agent gemcitabine. Anaphylactoid reactions have been reported rarely with gemcitabine. Injection site reaction including extravasation has been reported in 4% of patients during single-agent gemcitabine trials. There were no reports of tissue necrosis; gemcitabine is not a vesicant. Cellulitis and non-serious injection site reactions without extravasation have been reported rarely. During post marketing experience, severe skin reactions including desquamation and bullous skin reactions have been reported rarely. Vascular toxicity reported rarely with gemcitabine includes clinical signs of vasculitis. Gangrene has also been reported very rarely during post-marketing experience.Mild to moderate nausea/vomiting is seen in 69% of patients treated with gemcitabine. Severe nausea and vomiting occurred in < 15% of patients. Constipation (11—23%), diarrhea (14—20%), and stomatitis (11—20%) have also been reported during clinical trials of gemcitabine as a single agent and in combination with other agents. Other noted adverse effects during gemcitabine therapy include drowsiness (5%), edema (13%), peripheral edema (20%), generalized edema (< 1%), and mild paresthesias (10%).During single-agent gemcitabine clinical trials for pancreatic cancer, elevated hepatic enzymes have been observed in about 70% of patients; hyperbilirubinemia was reported in 13% of patients. However, there was no evidence of increasing hepatic toxicity with either longer duration of exposure to gemcitabine or with greater cumulative dose. Serious hepatic toxicity, including hepatic failure and death, has been reported very rarely in patients receiving gemcitabine alone or in combination with other hepatotoxic drugs. A case report describes 2 patients who died after developing colitis (necrotizing enterocolitis) following their first cycle of gemcitabine chemotherapy for ovarian cancer.Hemolytic-uremic syndrome (HUS) or renal failure (unspecified) have been reported in patients following one or more doses of gemcitabine. Of the 6 patients who developed HUS during clinical trials (0.25%), 4 patients developed HUS while on gemcitabine therapy and 2 patients immediately post-therapy. The diagnosis of HUS should be considered if the patient develops anemia with evidence of microangiopathic hemolysis, elevation of bilirubin or LDH, reticulocytosis, severe thrombocytopenia, and/or renal failure (elevation of serum creatinine or BUN); gemcitabine therapy should be discontinued immediately. Renal failure leading to death or requiring dialysis, despite discontinuation of therapy, has been rarely reported. The majority of renal failure cases leading to death were due to HUS. Hematuria (15—35%) and low-grade proteinuria (12—45%) have been observed during gemcitabine therapy. No explanation exists for these effects, although gemcitabine-induced thrombocytopenia is well-described. Elevated BUN (azotemia) and serum creatinine were reported in 16% and 8% of patients, respectively, receiving gemcitabine as a single agent.In clinical trials dyspnea, unrelated to the underlying disease has been reported in 23% of patients receiving gemcitabine therapy. Dyspnea was occasionally accompanied by bronchospasm (< 2%). Pulmonary adverse reactions including pulmonary edema, pulmonary fibrosis, interstitial pneumonitis, or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), sometimes severe, have been reported following 1 or more doses of gemcitabine. Some patients reported respiratory symptoms up to 2 weeks after completing gemcitabine therapy. If such reactions develop, gemcitabine therapy should be discontinued. Early use of supportive care measures may help ameliorate these conditions. Respiratory failure and death occurred very rarely despite discontinuation of therapy in some patients. A case of fatal pulmonary veno-occlusive disease (VOD) possibly related to gemcitabine has been reported. The etiology of these reactions is unknown.A radiation recall reaction occurring in 2 patterns has been reported with concurrent and non-concurrent use of gemcitabine and radiation therapy. If gemcitabine is administered more than 7 days before or after radiation therapy, a typical radiation recall reaction is noted. Data suggest that gemcitabine may be given after the acute effects of radiation have resolved or at least 1 week following completion of radiation therapy. Clinical trials have demonstrated that gemcitabine has radiosensitizing activity. When gemcitabine and radiation are given concurrently, the toxicity seen is dependent upon the dose of gemcitabine and radiation, the frequency of administration of gemcitabine, the radiotherapy planning technique, the target tissue and the target volume. In a single clinical trial of non-small cell lung cancer patients, gemcitabine 1000 mg/m2 weekly was given concurrently with thoracic radiation for 6 weeks. Severe and potentially life-threatening mucositis, especially esophagitis and pneumonitis was reported, especially in those patients receiving large volumes of radiation (median treatment volumes 4795 cm3). Other studies suggest that lower doses of gemcitabine with concurrent radiation therapy have predictable and less severe toxicity. An optimum dosage regiment has not been determined.During single-agent clinical trials, 2% of patients discontinued therapy with gemcitabine due to cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular accident (stroke), arrhythmia, and hypertension; the incidence of these reactions is very rare. Arrhythmias were predominantly supraventricular in nature when they occurred. Many of these patients had a prior history of cardiovascular disease. During clinical trials of non-small cell lung cancer patients treated with gemcitabine plus cisplatin, cardiac dysrhythmias of grade 3 or greater were reported in 7 (3%) patients compared to 1 (< 1%) Grade 3 dysrhythmias reported with cisplatin alone. Hypomagnesemia and hypokalemia were associated with one Grade 4 arrhythmia in the gemcitabine/cisplatin arm.

Description
  Text
  Gemcitabine is a deoxycytidine antimetabolite nucleoside inhibitor, closely related to cytarabine. Although cytarabine and gemcitabine are similar in structure and metabolic pathways, they differ greatly in the spectrum of antitumor activity. While cytarabine is used almost exclusively for acute leukemias, gemcitabine is active against a variety of solid tumors. It is indicated as monotherapy for locally advanced or metastatic pancreatic cancer, in combination with cisplatin for locally advanced or metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), in combination with paclitaxel for metastatic breast cancer, and in combination with carboplatin for advanced ovarian cancer. Gemcitabine intravesical system is indicated for the treatment of Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG)-unresponsive, non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) with carcinoma in situ with or without papillary tumors; this formulation is for intravesical instillation only and should not be administered by any other route. Increased toxicities including clinically significant hypotension, severe flu-like symptoms, myelosuppression, and asthenia are observed with infusion times greater than 60 minutes or dosing more frequently than once weekly.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Gemcitabine kills cells undergoing DNA synthesis and blocks the progression of cells through the G1/S-phase boundary. It is metabolized by nucleoside kinases to diphosphate (dFdCDP) and triphosphate (dFdCTP) nucleosides. Gemcitabine diphosphate inhibits ribonucleotide reductase, an enzyme responsible for catalyzing reactions that generate deoxynucleoside triphosphates for DNA synthesis, resulting in reductions in deoxynucleotide concentrations including dCTP. Gemcitabine triphosphate competes with dCTP for incorporation into DNA. The reduction in the intracellular concentration of dCTP by the action of the diphosphate enhances the incorporation of gemcitabine triphosphate into DNA (self-potentiation). After the gemcitabine nucleotide is incorporated into DNA, only one additional nucleotide is added to the growing DNA strands, which results in the initiation of apoptosis.

    Length of infusion:
The half-life of gemcitabine for short infusions (less than 70 minutes) ranged from 42 to 94 minutes, and for long infusions (70 to 285 minutes) ranged from 245 to 638 minutes, depending on age and gender; this likely reflects a greatly increased volume of distribution with longer infusions.   Text
  Gemcitabine can be administered intravenously or by intravesical instillation. Protein binding is negligible. The pharmacokinetics of gemcitabine are linear and are best described by a 2-compartment model. The exact sites of distribution of gemcitabine are unknown, but the distribution is affected by the duration of the infusion and gender. The volume of distribution increases with infusion length (less than 70 minutes, 50 L/m2; 70 to 285 minutes, 370 L/m2). Gemcitabine is metabolized by nucleoside kinases to diphosphate (dFdCDP) and triphosphate (dFdCTP) nucleosides. Within 1 week, 92% to 98% of a dose is recovered, almost entirely in the urine; gemcitabine (less than 10%) and inactive metabolite, 2', 2'-difluorodeoxyuridine (dFdU), account for 99% of the excreted dose. The active metabolite, gemcitabine triphosphate, can be extracted from peripheral blood mononuclear cells; the terminal half-life for this metabolite from mononuclear cells ranges from 1.7 to 19.4 hours.

Patient Counseling (Gemcitabine intravesical system)
Instruct patients to drink approximately 1,500 mL of fluids per day during therapy with gemcitabine intravesical system to ensure adequate urine production for drug release.
Instruct patients not to empty the bladder immediately prior to the insertion procedure; the presence of urine in the bladder can facilitate deployment of the system. Patients can resume micturition after the procedure.
During the indwelling period of approximately 3 weeks for gemcitabine intravesical system, advise both male and female patients to void urine while sitting on a toilet, to avoid urine contact with skin, to wash hands with soap and water and to wash their genital area with water after each urination, and to flush the toilet after use.
Gemcitabine intravesical system contains a metal wire. MRI may be performed safely with gemcitabine intravesical system only under very specific conditions; refer to the Instructions for Use provided with this medication. Instruct patients to carry the MRI Safety Information Card and show healthcare providers in case of need for MRI scans.

Insertion of gemcitabine intravesical system
Gather supplies required for gemcitabine intravesical system insertion: 1 sterile urinary catheter, 1 sterile stylet, and 1 sterile gemcitabine intravesical system, gloves, and either two 10 mL prefilled water-based lubricant syringes or two empty 10 mL syringes and water-based lubricant.
Put on gloves.
Prepare 2 syringes with sterile water-based lubricant; lubricant is to be used to lubricate the tip of the urinary catheter and to facilitate the insertion of the gemcitabine intravesical system.
Open the gemcitabine intravesical system foil pouch and remove the white inner pouch.
Open the white pouches containing gemcitabine intravesical system, the urinary catheter, and stylet and transfer contents onto a sterile work surface; do not remove the plastic sleeves. Do not use if the packaging is damaged.
Put on sterile gloves.
Remove the plastic sleeve from the gemcitabine intravesical system.
Lubricate the tip of the urinary catheter (without stylet). Insert the urinary catheter by hand and advance until urine return; do not empty the bladder. Use depth markings to maintain coude tip orientation and insertion depth position throughout the procedure.
Inject 2 to 3 mL of lubricant from the first syringe into the end of the urinary catheter with the urinary catheter placed in the bladder.
Insert either end of the gemcitabine intravesical system into the urinary catheter and advance fully until inserted.
Inject 2 to 3 mL of lubricant from the second syringe into the urinary catheter to help advance the gemcitabine intravesical system further, with the urinary catheter placed into the bladder.
Slowly insert the stylet into the urinary catheter until the stylet hub is flush with end of the urinary catheter, ensuring the gemcitabine intravesical system exits the urinary catheter and enters the bladder.
Remove the urinary catheter and stylet together as a single unit; do not remove the urinary catheter and stylet individually. The gemcitabine intravesical system should remain inside the bladder.
Following gemcitabine intravesical system insertion, complete the MRI Safety Information Card and provide it to the patient. Advise patients to carry the card and show healthcare providers in case of need for MRI scans.
Removal of gemcitabine intravesical system
Use a water-based lubricant to lubricate the cystoscope
Insert the cystoscope into the bladder to locate the gemcitabine intravesical system.
Introduce non-cutting, grasping forceps through the working channel of the cystoscope. Do not use cutting forceps.
Grasp the gemcitabine intravesical system over the tubing and metal wire. Do not grasp on or near the ends, which could result in exposing the metal wire, causing damage to surrounding tissue.
Remove the cystoscope and forceps out of the urethra together to remove the gemcitabine intravesical system under direct vision. Do not remove the gemcitabine intravesical system through the working channel of the cystoscope; doing so may damage the system and/or the cystoscope.
Following removal, inspect the gemcitabine intravesical system to confirm it is unbroken and intact.
