        Dosage Description
        1000 mg PO as a single dose 30 minutes before travel and repeated every 4 hours as needed with a maximum dose of 2—4 g/day has been used. Approximately half of the clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy of ginger in the treatment of motion sickness; while the others have not. The variation in findings may be due to the lack of standard preparations of ginger used in clinical trials.         Single dose of at least 500 mg to 1000 mg PO prior to surgery; maximum dose of 2—4 g/day. Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Ginger has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration as a pharmaceutical. However, it is 'Generally Recognized As Safe' and appears on the FDA's GRAS list. Products containing ginger are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Consumers should also be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.Ginger is a cholagogic agent that promotes the flow of bile into the intestine, especially through contraction of the gallbladder. Therefore, ginger is relatively contraindicated for use in those with active gallbladder disease or cholelithiasis.Ginger inhibits thromboxane synthetase and is a prostacyclin agonist. A randomized double-blind study in healthy males reported no differences in bleeding time, platelet count, whole blood platelet aggregometry and thromboelastrography before, 3 hours, and 24 hours after ingestion of 2 grams of dried ginger compared to placebo. As this was a small trial of healthy males, use caution when bleeding is a concern (i.e. anticoagulant therapy and thrombolytic therapy). Theoretically the positive inotropic effects of some gingerols could interfere with some treatments for cardiac disease; patients with cardiac arrhythmias or other heart problems should not exceed normal dietary intakes of ginger unless they have consulted with their health care professional.Because excessive amounts of ginger may affect glycemic control and result in hypoglycemia; use caution in individuals with diabetes mellitus. More frequent blood glucose monitoring may be needed if ginger is taken.The use of ginger during pregnancy is controversial. While the German Commission E does not recommend the use of ginger during pregnancy, it lists no specific contraindications related to pregnancy. Possible mutagenic and antimutagenic effects associated with ginger in vitro have not been evaluated in human studies. Controlled trials to date during human pregnancy have not indicated that ginger causes harm to the mother or fetus or other severe toxicity; the dose of ginger ingested during these trials (i.e., no more than 1 g of fresh root) has generally been below the amounts of ginger used as a spice in various foods. Females should be advised not to exceed normal dietary intakes of ginger. Sufficient data regarding the safety of ginger therapeutically in human pregnancy do not exist and long-term use is not recommended. The risks versus benefits to the mother and fetus should be considered before administration of ginger for therapeutic purposes.Because insufficient data are available, women who are breast-feeding their infants should be advised not to exceed normal dietary intakes of ginger.Elderly patients are recommended to consult a health-care professional prior to using a dietary supplement such as ginger. Ginger is not recommended for use in children < 12 years old as safety and effectiveness have not been established.

    Advise patients to discontinue ginger supplements 2 weeks prior to a planned procedure (e.g., surgery). Ginger may increase the risk of bleeding due to its antiplatelet effects.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) states that ginger can be considered as a first-line nonpharmacologic option for nausea/vomiting during pregnancy. While the German Commission E does not recommend the use of ginger during pregnancy, it lists no specific contraindications related to pregnancy. Controlled trials to date during human pregnancy have not indicated that ginger causes harm to the pregnant individual or fetus or other severe toxicity; the dose of ginger ingested during these trials (i.e., no more than 1 gram of fresh root) has generally been below the amounts of ginger used as a spice in various foods. Pregnant individuals should be advised not to exceed normal dietary intakes of ginger.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Ginger is compatible with breast-feeding. Ginger is "generally recognized as safe" (GRAS) by the FDA. There are no data on the presence of ginger or its metabolites in human milk or their effects on milk production. Small studies have not identified adverse effects on the breast-fed child.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Ginger is usually well-tolerated. However, side effects from herbal products are not always reported. There are no reports of severe toxicity related to ginger in humans. Large doses of ginger have been reported to cause pyrosis (heartburn). Large doses have the potential to cause cardiac arrhythmias, bleeding, and CNS depression, however there have been no reports in the literature. In one controlled trial for nausea and emesis during pregnancy, abdominal discomfort (dyspepsia), pyrosis (heartburn) and diarrhea were reported side effects in the ginger group, and headache was reported in both the ginger and placebo groups equally. The ginger was effective at relieving symptoms without apparent adverse effect on pregnancy outcome; no teratogenic effects were noted. However, there is not sufficient data regarding the safety of the routine use of ginger in human pregnancy.Allergic reactions to ginger are infrequent, but theoretically may occur as contact dermatitis or rash (unspecified) with topical or systemic exposure, respectively.Additional adverse reactions related to ginger that have been reported to the FDA Special Nutritional Adverse Event Monitoring System (SNAEMS) have included one case report of headache, lethargy, abnormal urine discoloration, asthenia/weakness, and hypotension from the ingestion of single-ingredient ginger root capsules. There is no certainty that a reported ADR to SNAEMS can be attributed to a particular product or ingredient; the available information may not be complete enough to make this determination.

Description
  Text
  Ginger is a dietary supplement derived from the underground stem (rhizome) of the perennial plant Zingiber officinale Roscoe. Usually grown in warmer climates, ginger is often referred to by its country of origin. Jamaican ginger, African ginger, and Cochin ginger (China) contain different concentrations of the active ingredient(s). Ginger is chemically classified as an essential oil with pungent principles. The dried rhizome contains approximately 1 to 3% volatile oil which is the source of ginger's characteristic aroma; an oleoresin contains the pungent properties. Ginger has been in use as a digestive aid, spice, and flavoring agent for over 20 centuries. Primarily used as a spice or a dietary supplement, ginger has been used for the prevention of motion sickness. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) guidelines state that ginger may be considered as a first-line nonpharmacologic option for nausea/vomiting during pregnancy; use of ginger may reduce nausea in pregnant women but is not shown to be effective at reducing vomiting episodes. The German Commission E lists anorexia as an indication for a fixed combination of Ginger root, Gentian root, and Wormwood. Reports of neurotoxicity associated with Jamaican ginger in the 1930s were due to an adulterant used in the extract. Ginger is listed in the USP and is Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) by the FDA.
 
NOTE: In the U.S., herbal products and dietary supplements are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for dietary supplements as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since supplements are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for these products and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Ginger posesses antiemetic, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and cardiotonic properties. Ginger is a cholagogic agent that promotes the flow of bile into the intestine, especially through contraction of the gallbladder. Pharmacologically active components of the oleoresin include gingerols, a class of structurally similar cardioactive compounds, and shogaols. In vitro, gingerols produce concentration-dependent inotropic effects through activation of sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium adenosine triphosphatase (SR Ca2+ATPase).
 
•Antiemetic Actions: Ginger's mechanism of action for the prevention of motion sickness and nausea is not clear. In animal studies, ginger produced antispasmodic effects and exhibited anticholinergic and antihistaminic action. Ginger increases salivation and stimulates intestinal motility through increased tone and peristalsis of the intestines in humans. However, the results of a double-blind randomized crossover trial of healthy volunteers demonstrated no measurable effect on gastric emptying. Galanolactone, a diterpenoid constituent of ginger, demonstrated activity as a 5HT3 antagonist in vitro. Receptors for 5HT3 are located on intestinal vagal nerve terminals and in the chemoreceptor trigger zone.
 
•Anti-inflammatory Actions: Ginger inhibits thromboxane synthetase and is a prostacyclin agonist. Prostaglandins induce vasodilation and increase tissue permeability, which, in turn, promote the influx of fluids and leukocytes. Ultimately, the classic symptoms of inflammation result: swelling, redness, warmth, and pain. It is believed that at least one of the mechanisms by which ginger produces its antiinflammatory effects is through the inhibition of prostaglandin and leukotriene biosynthesis. Through this dual inhibition of eicosanoid biosynthesis, ginger decreases capillary permeability, thus reducing swelling and the influx of inflammatory mediators.
 
•Analgesic Actions: The analgesic property of ginger is thought to be mainly a result of peripheral actions but direct effects on the CNS are possible. By inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis, ginger decreases the perception of pain.
 
•Antiplatlet Activity: Several pungent constituents of ginger, Zingiber officinale are reported to inhibit arachidonic acid (AA) induced platelet activation in humans. The constituent (8)-paradol is the most potent inhibitor of COX-1 and exhibits the greatest anti-platelet activity versus other gingerol analogues. The mechanism of ginger-associated platelet inhibition may be related to decreased COX-1/Thomboxane synthase enzymatic activity.

Pharmacokinetics
  Text
  Ginger is administered orally; products are usually dried or baked and powdered fresh rhizome. The pharmacokinetic properties of ginger have not been studied in human subjects. Many in vitro studies have used injectable solutions of ginger.

    Description
    Ginger is available in a variety of forms including the fresh herb, comminuted rhizome, dry extracts for teas, candied, tablets, capsules, powder and liquid. Capsules or tablets are considered the preferred dosage forms.
Liquid sources, such as ginger ale, usually contain low concentrations of ginger; a large volume must be consumed to promote clinical effects.
Candied or crystallized ginger is prepared by boiling the fresh rhizome in a syrupy solution. The product is marketed in Asian food stores in slices sprinkled with granulated sugar. Pieces 1 inch square and 1/4 inch thick weighing approximately 4 grams are equivalent to about one 500 mg capsule of ginger.
