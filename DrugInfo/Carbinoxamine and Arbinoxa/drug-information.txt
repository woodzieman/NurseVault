Drug Class: Carbinoxamine is an oral sedating antihistamine (H1-blocker) of the ethanolamine class that is similar in structure and effect to diphenhydramine.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 32 mg/day PO for the 4 mg tablet, the oral solution, or the extended release oral solution; 24 mg/day PO for the 6 mg tablet.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Carbinoxamine should not be used patients with sulfite hypersensitivity or in patients with hypersensitivity to carbinoxamine or other antihistamines, especially structurally related ethanolamine-type H1-blockers, such as diphenhydramine, clemastine, and tripelennamine.
 
The effects of carbinoxamine may be additive to other anticholinergic medications.
 
Carbinoxamine can precipitate or aggravate urinary retention via its anticholinergic effects. Therefore, carbinoxamine should not be used in patients with bladder obstruction and symptomatic prostatic hypertrophy, and should be used cautiously in patients with urinary retention.
 
Carbinoxamine is not recommended in patients with GI obstruction because the drug further decreases GI motility and can cause paralytic ileus. In general, peptic ulcer disease may be aggravated by the use of anticholinergic medications.
 
The anticholinergic activity of carbinoxamine may result in thickened bronchial secretions in the respiratory tract thereby aggravating an acute asthmatic attack or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and impairing expectoration. Although antihistamines should be avoided during an acute asthmatic attack (i.e., status asthmaticus), the anticholinergic effects of these drugs do not preclude their use in all asthmatic or COPD (e.g., emphysema or chronic bronchitis) patients, particularly if the above respiratory symptom is not a primary component of the illness. Antihistamines can usually be used safely in asthmatic patients who experience severe perennial allergic rhinitis without exacerbating asthma.
 
Carbinoxamine should not be used in patients who have closed-angle or open-angle glaucoma. The anticholinergic effect, resulting in increased intraocular pressure, may precipitate an attack of closed-angle glaucoma and the anticholinergic mydriatic effects may also cause a slight increase in intraocular pressure where open-angle glaucoma therapy may need to be adjusted.
 
The anticholinergic effects of carbinoxamine may make the eyes dry. This can cause an increased lens awareness or blurred vision for those who wear contact lenses. The use of lubricating drops may be necessary.
 
Anticholinergic effects also warrant caution when carbinoxamine is used in patients who have hyperthyroidism, cardiac disease, or hypertension.
 
Although not specifically reported with carbinoxamine, antihistamines have been found to cause photosensitization. Protective measures (i.e., using sunscreen and protective clothing) against sunlight (UV) exposure should be encouraged until the patients' tolerance to carbinoxamine is determined.
 
Safe and effective use of carbinoxamine has not been established in infants and children < 2 years of age. Manufacturers who are currently marketing unapproved carbinoxamine products must submit an application to the FDA showing safety and efficacy to continue distribution and sales. The recent FDA action originated from cumulative reports of deaths among young children (< 2 years) receiving carbinoxamine-containing products, although an exact association to the drug has not been confirmed. Neonates have an increased susceptibility to the anticholinergic side effects, such as central nervous system (CNS) excitation and an increased tendency toward convulsions.
 
The manufacturer states that this product is contraindicated in patients who are on MAOI therapy and for 14 days after stopping MAOI therapy.
 
Carbinoxamine is contraindicated in women who are breast-feeding because it is distributed into breast milk and can, therefore, be ingested by the nursing infant; also, the anticholinergic effects of carbinoxamine may inhibit lactation.
 
Carbinoxamine is considered FDA pregnancy risk category C. Safe use in pregnancy has not been established. Although there are no adequate human studies of the effects of this drug on the fetus, animal reproduction studies have shown adverse fetal effects. Therefore, in making the decision to administer this drug during pregnancy, the potential risks to the fetus must be weighed against the potential benefits to the mother. Avoidance of cough-cold combination drugs, especially those containing ethanol, is often recommended during pregnancy.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Carbinoxamine is an ethanolamine class first-generation antihistamine. Allergies to one antihistamine can sometimes lead to cross-reactivity with other chemically-related antihistamines. Additionally, some carbinoxamine products contain sodium metabisulfite, a sulfite that may cause anaphylaxis including life-threatening or less severe asthmatic episodes in susceptible individuals.
    Headline
    asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
    General Information
    Per the prescription product labels, carbinoxamine should not be used for the treatment of asthma. The anticholinergic activity may result in thickened bronchial secretions in the respiratory tract thereby aggravating an acute asthmatic attack and impairing expectoration. Carbinoxamine and other first-generation antihistamines do not usually aggravate stable asthma and may be used adjunctively to treat allergies that may trigger asthma. While carbinoxamine is not available at this time in nonprescription (OTC) products, the FDA generally recommends to use sedating antihistamines such as carbinoxamine with caution in people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), including emphysema or chronic bronchitis, as the anticholinergic activity of first-generation H1-antagonists may result in thickened bronchial secretions in the respiratory tract, thereby aggravating COPD.
    Headline
    bladder obstruction, prostatic hypertrophy
    General Information
    Carbinoxamine can precipitate or aggravate urinary retention via its anticholinergic effects. Therefore, carbinoxamine should be used with considerable caution in people with symptomatic prostatic hypertrophy and bladder neck obstruction (bladder obstruction).
    Headline
    gastric outlet obstruction
    General Information
    Use carbinoxamine with considerable caution in people with gastric outlet obstruction (stenosing peptic ulcer or pyloroduodenal obstruction). Carbinoxamine has anticholinergic effects that may aggravate these conditions.

    General Information
    Due to the anticholinergic actions, carbinoxamine should be used with considerable caution in people with closed-angle glaucoma. Also, use carbinoxamine with caution in other patients with increased intraocular pressure.

    General Information
    While first-generation antihistamines do not usually aggravate hyperthyroidism, cardiovascular disease, or hypertension, the manufacturer prescription labels continue to recommend caution with carbinoxamine use in individuals with these conditions.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Carbinoxamine can cause drowsiness. Advise individuals receiving carbinoxamine to avoid driving or operating machinery and activities requiring coordination and concentration until the effects of the drug are known. Additive drowsiness may be seen during coadministration with alcohol or other CNS depressants; the prescription product labels recommend to avoid alcoholic beverages while using carbinoxamine.
    Headline
    children, infants, neonates
    General Information
    Carbinoxamine use is contraindicated in neonates, infants, and children younger than 2 years. Deaths have been reported in children younger than 2 years who were taking carbinoxamine-containing drug products. Neonates have an increased susceptibility to anticholinergic side effects, such as CNS excitation, which may lead to convulsions. In other children, carbinoxamine may diminish mental alertness. In very young children, particularly, it may produce paradoxical excitation. Additionally, due to the risk for serious adverse reactions, the FDA recommends against administration of cough and cold products to neonates, infants, and children younger than 2 years. When administering such medications to older pediatric patients, they advise caregivers to read product labels carefully, use caution when administering multiple products to avoid duplication of ingredients, and use only measuring devices specifically designed for use with medications. Care teams should thoroughly assess the use of similar products, both prescription and nonprescription, to avoid duplication of therapy and the potential for inadvertent overdose. The Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) list includes carbinoxamine as a potentially inappropriate medication for use in neonates, infants, and children younger than 2 years and use should be avoided. If avoidance is not possible, use carbinoxamine with caution and appropriate monitoring.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Carbinoxamine is more likely to cause dizziness, sedation, and hypotension in geriatric adults (approximately 60 years or older) and may also cause confusion and oversedation in the elderly. Therefore, lower initial dosages are recommended for the geriatric population; observe closely.   The Beers Criteria identifies systemic first-generation antihistamines as potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) for geriatric adults, recommending their avoidance due to their high anticholinergic properties, decreased clearance in older age, and an increased risk of anticholinergic effects and toxicity compared to younger individuals. These medications should particularly be avoided in individuals with dementia or cognitive impairment (due to adverse CNS effects), those at high risk for delirium (which can worsen or trigger new-onset delirium), and men with lower urinary tract symptoms or benign prostatic hyperplasia (due to risks of urinary retention or hesitancy).
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Published data over decades of use of various antihistamines, including carbinoxamine, have not identified a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. However, animal reproduction studies have not been conducted with carbinoxamine. It is also not known whether carbinoxamine can cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy and should be given to a pregnant person only if clearly needed.  Thus, alternatives to carbinoxamine are usually preferred in the pregnant individual. Loratadine and oral cetirizine are acceptable antihistamine alternatives based on their excellent safety data and recommendation in multiple guidelines for use during pregnancy. Non-pharmacologic methods (e.g., fluids, rest) are recommended to be tried first for symptomatic relief of cold symptoms or allergies during pregnancy. Pregnant patients should see their health care professional for a proper diagnosis and for treatment recommendations.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Breast-feeding is not recommended during treatment with carbinoxamine.  Some products specifically contraindicate the use of carbinoxamine during breast-feeding. However, the breast-feeding contraindication was removed from the extended-release suspension. Based on the physical properties of carbinoxamine, it is likely that carbinoxamine is present in breastmilk. There are published reports of drowsiness and irritability in infants exposed to antihistamines via breast milk. There are post-marketing reports of deaths in children under 2 years of age exposed to carbinoxamine by oral administration. There are no available data on the effects on milk production.  Side effects of sedating first-generation antihistamines in breastfed infants such as irritability, disturbed sleeping patterns, drowsiness, hyperexcitability, and excessive crying have been reported in a number of cases. Antihistamines can lower basal prolactin secretion and may interfere with the establishment of lactation. Consider treatment alternatives. Guidelines recommend loratadine or cetirizine at the lowest dose as preferred antihistamines in breast-feeding individuals because they are safer with only low levels found in breast milk.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  CNS depression, manifested as drowsiness and/or dizziness, can occur during therapy with ethanolamine H1-antagonists such as carbinoxamine. Sedation is a frequent adverse reaction to most H1-blockers. There is considerable individual patient response to sedative effects, so patients should be warned of the possible impairment of mental acuity. These side effects may disappear after a few days of medication. Other possible CNS effects include headache and muscular weakness.
 
Carbinoxamine and other H1-antagonists can cause a paradoxical CNS stimulation; however, this is more likely to occur with high doses, in children or in the elderly. Symptoms may include ataxia, excitability, hallucinations, insomnia, irritability, palpitations, psychosis, restlessness, and, in severe cases, seizures.
 
Although not specifically reported with carbinoxamine, antihistamines have been found to cause photosensitivity. Protective measures (i.e., using sunscreen and protective clothing) against sunlight (UV) exposure should be encouraged until the tolerance to carbinoxamine is determined.
 
Carbinoxamine possesses a significant degree of anticholinergic activity. Anticholinergic effects that can occur during therapy include thickening of bronchial secretions, confusion, dysuria or urinary retention, insomnia, nervousness, xerophthalmia, xerostomia, and temporary visual impairment or blurred vision. The anticholinergic effect may result in increased intraocular pressure in susceptible patients. Geriatric patients are more susceptible to these adverse reactions, since endogenous cholinergic activity declines with age.
 
H1-antagonists like carbinoxamine can cause adverse GI effects including abdominal pain, constipation, dyspepsia, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea and anorexia.
 
Although there have not been any reports with carbinoxamine, adverse cardiovascular responses can be associated with the anticholinergic properties or the quinidine-like anesthetic effects of H1-antagonists. These responses include sinus tachycardia, palpitations, and cardiac arrhythmias. Alpha-adrenergic blockade can lead to hypotension. Hypertension may also occur, but is usually not clinically significant.

Description
  Text
  Carbinoxamine is an oral sedating antihistamine (H1-blocker) of the ethanolamine class that is similar in structure and effect to diphenhydramine. Carbinoxamine is used to relieve seasonal allergic rhinitis and to treat other allergic conditions. Carbinoxamine is used in many combination prescription and non-prescription (OTC) allergy and cold products. Carbinoxamine has been available since before 1938, prior to the modern FDA drug-approval process. As part of an FDA initiative to ensure that all prescription and non-prescription medications are FDA-approved, manufacturers of non-approved carbinoxamine products (single and multi-ingredient) must submit an application showing safety and efficacy to continue distribution and sales. Due to deaths reported in very young children and infants, the drug is contraindicated in pediatric patients 2 years of age and younger.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Carbinoxamine is a competitive antagonist at histamine type-1 receptors (H1-receptors). Like other antihistamines it does not prevent the cellular release of histamine, as do cromolyn or nedocromil, but rather competes with free histamine for binding at H1-receptor sites. Carbinoxamine competitively antagonizes the effects of histamine on H1-receptors in the GI tract, uterus, large blood vessels, and bronchial smooth muscle. Blockade of H1-receptors suppresses the edema, flare, and pruritus that result from histaminic activity.
 
The sedating H1-blockers also possess anticholinergic properties in varying degrees. The anticholinergic activity of ethanolamine derivatives, such as carbinoxamine, is more potent than other antihistamines. This anticholinergic action appears to be due to a central antimuscarinic effect, although exact mechanisms are unknown. Sedative effects from carbinoxamine result from its ability to cross the blood brain barrier, where it inhibits histamine N-methyltransferase and blocks central histaminergic receptors. Chronic administration may lead to some degree of tolerance, but this can be beneficial due to a reduction in sedative effects.

Like other antihistamines, the distribution of carbinoxamine has not been fully determined, although there is a tendency among most antihistamines to show highest concentrations in the lung.
