Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Recombinant factor VIIa (NovoSeven®) should not be administered to patients with bovine protein hypersensitivity, hamster protein hypersensitivity or murine protein hypersensitivity. NovoSeven® contains trace amounts of proteins derived from the manufacturing and purification processes such as mouse IgG (maximum of 1.2 ng/mg), bovine IgG (maximum of 30 ng/mg) and protein from hamster kidney cells and calf serum media (maximum of 19 ng/mg).Patients with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), advanced arteriosclerosis, crush injury (trauma), sepsis, or concomitant treatment with activated or nonactivated prothrombin complex concentrates (aPCCs/PCCs) may have an increased risk of developing thrombotic events during treatment with recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) due to circulating tissue factor (TF) or predisposing coagulopathy. The actual risk of thrombotic events with rFVIIa treatment in patients with hemophilia and inhibitors is not known, but is considered to be low. In patients without hemophilia and receiving rFVIIa, the risk of arterial and venous thromboembolic events is not known, but may be associated with an increased risk of thromboembolic events. One clinical study in elderly non-hemophilia intracerebral hemorrhage patients reported an increased risk of arterial thromboembolic events including myocardial ischemia, myocardial infarction, and cerebral ischemia and/or infarction. In addition, since the approval of rFVIIa in 1999, 220 reports describing 246 thromboembolic events (arterial, venous, and those involving devices) have been reported to the FDA in patients receiving rFVIIa (data collected through November 1, 2005). It should be noted that approximately 87% of these reports have been in patients receiving rFVIIa for off-label indications. Patients receiving rFVIIa should be monitored for signs or symptoms of activation of the coagulation system or thrombosis. When there is laboratory confirmation of intravascular coagulation in the presence of clinical thromboembolic disease, the dosage of rFVIIa should be reduced or the treatment stopped based upon the patient's symptoms. Due to limited clinical studies that clearly address the effect of post-hemostatic dosing, use caution during prolonged treatment with recombinant factor VIIa.There are no adequate, well-controlled studies of recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) in pregnant women. Recombinant factor VIIa is considered a FDA category C agent. The use of rFVIIa during pregnancy should be limited to situations where the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus. Factor VII concentrates have been given to pregnant women with factor VII deficiency during the peripartum and postpartum periods to minimize bleeding.It is not known if recombinant factor VIIa is excreted in breast milk. Because many drugs are excreted in breast milk and the potential for serious adverse reaction in nursing infants, a decision should be made whether to discontinue breast-feeding or to discontinue the drug considering the importance of the drug to the mother.In some cases, laboratory tests such as activated thromboplastin time (aPTT) may not correlate with clinical response of recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) treatment. A shortened prothrombin time (PT) and aPTT should be expected. Normalization of these parameters may not occur since the activity of rFVIIa is limited to the site of hemorrhage.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Certain recombinant factor VIIa (i.e., Sevenfact) is produced by recombinant DNA technology using genetically engineered rabbits and is contraindicated in individuals with known allergy to rabbits or rabbit proteins. Individuals with known IgE-based hypersensitivity to casein may be at higher risk of hypersensitivity reactions from certain recombinant factor VIIa (i.e., Sevenfact). Individuals with a known hypersensitivity to bovine, hamster, or mouse proteins may be at higher risk of hypersensitivity reactions with other certain recombinant factor VIIa (i.e., NovoSeven RT) as it contains trace amounts of proteins derived from the manufacturing and purification processes such as mouse IgG (maximum of 1.2 nanograms/mg), bovine IgG (maximum of 30 nanograms/mg), and protein from BHK-cells and media (maximum of 19 nanograms/mg).
    Headline
    General Information
    General Information
    Laboratory coagulation parameters [e.g., prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), plasma factor VII clotting activity (FVII:C)] do not correlate with clinical response to recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) treatment. Assays of PT/INR, aPTT, and FVII:C may give different results with different reagents.
    Headline
    cardiovascular disease, crush injury, geriatric, sepsis, thromboembolism, thrombophilia
    General Information
    Serious arterial and venous thrombotic events may occur with recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa). Individuals with a history of congenital or acquired hemophilia receiving concomitant treatment with activated or non-activated prothrombin complex (aPCC/PCC) or other hemostatic agents, cardiovascular disease (including atherosclerosis, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease), crush injury, sepsis, thrombophilia, or thromboembolism may have an increased risk of thromboembolic events with use of rFVIIa. Older and geriatric adults, particularly with acquired hemophilia and receiving other hemostatic agents, may also have an increased risk of thromboembolic events with rFVIIa. Consider the presence of age-related comorbidities and risks associated with thrombotic and thromboembolic events when administering rFVIIa to adults older than 50 years. Monitor for signs and symptoms of activation of the coagulation system or thrombosis. There is limited information about the safety of rFVIIa in individuals with a history of arterial or venous thromboembolic disease because these individuals were excluded from clinical trials.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate and well-controlled studies on the use of recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) during pregnancy to determine if there is a drug-related risk. Treatment of rats and rabbits has been associated with mortality at doses up to 6 and 5 mg/kg, respectively; no evidence of teratogenicity was observed. Factor VII concentrates have been given to pregnant people with factor VII deficiency during the peripartum and postpartum periods to minimize bleeding.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of rFVIIa in human milk, its effect on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Because rFVIIa is a large protein molecule, transfer into breast milk is expected to be minimal. Any small amount present in milk would likely be destroyed in the child's gastrointestinal tract, resulting in negligible systemic absorption. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition.

Interactions
Anti-inhibitor Coagulant Complex: (Major) Avoid concomitant use of recombinant factor VIIa and activated prothrombin complex concentrates (aPCCs) due to the increased risk for thrombotic events.
Concizumab: (Moderate) Monitor for thromboembolic events during concomitant use of concizumab and bypassing agents, such as recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa). Use the lowest-approved dose of rFVIIa when needed for the treatment of breakthrough bleeding events. Additive and sometimes synergistic increases in thrombin peak, as quantified in the thrombin generation assay, have been observed during concomitant use, which may increase the risk for adverse clotting events.
Factor XIII A-Subunit, Recombinant: (Major) Concomitant use of recombinant factor XIII A-subunit with factor VIIa, recombinant may result in thrombosis. However, no clinical or nonclinical human studies have been performed with recombinant factor XIII A-subunit and recombinant factor VIIa at recommended human doses. Simultaneous use of factor VIIa, recombinant and recombinant factor XIII A-subunit should be avoided due to the potential for thrombosis.
Factor XIII Concentrate: (Major) The risk of potential interaction between factor VIIa, recombinant and coagulation factor concentrates has not been adequately evaluated. Simultaneous use of factor VIIa, recombinant and factor XIII concentrate should be avoided due to the potential for thrombosis.
Fitusiran: (Major) Avoid the concomitant use of fitusiran and recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) for bleeding prophylaxis; when starting fitusiran, discontinue rFVIIa within 7 days of initiation. For the management of breakthrough bleeding, reduce the dose of rFVIIa to 45 mcg/kg or less, not to be repeated in less than 2 hours. Additive and sometimes synergistic increases in thrombin peak, as quantified by thrombin generation assay, have been observed during concomitant use which may increase the risk for adverse clotting events.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) is well tolerated in patients with hemophilia A or B with inhibitors during clinical trials. The following adverse reactions were reported in >= 5% of patients: fever (16%), non-specific bleeding (15%), hemarthrosis (14%), decreased serum fibrinogen levels (10%) and hypertension (9%). Other adverse events occurring in 1% of patients include arthrosis, coagulation disorder, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), edema, increased fibrinolysis, headache, hypotension, injection site reaction, pain, pneumonia, decreased prothrombin levels, pruritus, purpura, rash (unspecified), abnormal renal function, sinus bradycardia, and nausea/vomiting. Serious adverse events that were possible, probable, or unknown in relation to rFVIIa were seen in 14 (4.7%) patients during clinical trials. Of these patients, 6 died due to either worsening of chronic renal failure, anesthesia complications during proctoscopy, renal failure complicating a retroperitoneal bleed, ruptured abscess leading to sepsis and DIC, pneumonia, or splenic hematoma and GI bleeding.Anaphylactoid reactions have been reported in patients treated with recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) and are more common in patients being treated for factor VII deficiency. It does not appear that antibodies against rFVIIa are formed in patients with hemophilia A, hemophilia B, or hemophilic patients with acquired inhibitors. Factor VII deficient patients may represent a group of patients who are at increased risk for the development of antibodies against rFVIIa. Antibody formation has been reported in a patient with factor VII deficiency who received an overdose of rFVIIa.In clinical trials of recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa), thrombosis was reported in 2 patients. In vitro data suggest that risk of hypercoagulability (as determined by the formation of free thrombin) is low with rFVIIa treatment. Since the approval of rFVIIa in 1999, 220 reports describing 246 thromboembolic events in patients receiving rFVIIa have been reported to the FDA (data collected through November 1, 2005). Approximately 87% of these reports are in patients receiving rFVIIa for off-label indications. The types of events reported include 129 (52%) arterial thromboembolic events (stroke, acute myocardial infarction, other arterial thromboses), 100 (41%) venous thromboembolic events (pulmonary embolism, other venous thromboembolism), and 15 (6%) events involving clotted devices. Furthermore, death has been reported in 67 patients during this time period; the probable cause of death in 43 of the 67 (64%) patients is thrombosis. Additional information on the adverse event profile of rFVIIa and the incidence of thrombotic events in particular is being collected through a post-market surveillance program. In addition, this program is designed to expand the base of experience regarding the use of rFVIIa (NovoSeven®). Adverse reactions reported include high D-dimer levels and consumptive coagulopathy; events related to thromboembolism including myocardial infarction, myocardial ischemia, cerebral infarction and/or ischemia, thrombophlebitis, arterial thrombosis, deep vein thrombosis and related pulmonary embolism; and isolated cases of hypersensitivity reactions including anaphylactoid reactions. All prescribers can obtain information regarding contribution of patient data to this program by calling 1—877—362—7355.Peripheral continuous infusion of recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) has been associated with phlebitis. Previously, addition of heparin or low-molecular weight heparin (2—5 ml) directly to the concentrate was recommended; however, the addition of heparin to the concentrate leads to an approximately 20—30% loss of factor VIIa activity. Furthermore, the addition of LMWH can cause aggregates to form. Therefore, the use of heparin or low-molecular weight heparin is not recommended. Most commonly, normal saline is infused via a Y-site at a rate of 10—20 ml/hr to prevent local phlebitis. When administered into a central vein, coadministration of normal saline is not necessary.

    Headline
    antibody formation
    General Information
    Antibody formation may occur with the use of recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa). Perform testing for neutralizing antibodies if adequate hemostasis is not achieved, factor VIIa activity fails to reach the expected concentration, or PT is not corrected. In particular, monitor factor VII deficient patents for prothrombin time (PT) and factor VII coagulant activity before and after administration of rFVIIa. IgG antibody formation against rFVIIa and FVII occurred in 1 of 75 patients with congenital factor VII deficiency during clinical trials. It does not appear that antibodies against rFVIIa are formed in patients with hemophilia A, hemophilia B, or hemophilic patients with acquired inhibitors. Factor VII deficient patients may represent a group of patients who are at increased risk for the development of antibodies against rFVIIa. Antibody formation has been reported in a patient with factor VII deficiency who received an overdose of rFVIIa. Antibody formation had also been reported in patients previously treated with human plasma and/or plasma-derived factor VII. In some cases, the antibody showed an inhibitory effect in vitro.

    angina, increased intracranial pressure, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, stroke, thromboembolism, thrombosis
    General Information
    Serious arterial and venous thrombotic events (thrombosis, thromboembolism) may occur with recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa). Discontinue or dose reduce rFVII, depending on the individual's condition, when there is laboratory confirmation of intravascular coagulation or the presence of clinical thrombosis. During clinical trials for NovoSeven RT, thrombotic adverse reactions occurred in 4% of subjects with acquired hemophilia. In subjects with acquired hemophilia, cerebrovascular accident/stroke (0.7%) was reported in addition to the thromboembolic events [cerebral artery occlusion, cerebral ischemia, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, and deep vein thrombosis (DVT)]. Increased intracranial pressure (intracranial hypertension) was reported in 1.5% of subjects with congenital factor VII deficiency. Serious reactions reported in subjects with hemophilia A or B with inhibitors include thrombosis, deep thrombophlebitis, pulmonary embolism, cerebrovascular disorder, and angina pectoris; internal jugular thrombosis was reported in 2% of subjects in the perioperative setting. DVT (0.7%) was also reported from data in subjects with Glanzmann's thrombasthenia.

    infusion-related reactions, phlebitis
    General Information
    Infusion-related reactions, including infusion site discomfort (15%), hematoma (7.5%), and other reactions (6.5%), have occurred with recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) administration. One subject developed an infusion reaction immediately after administration of the first dose; the reaction lasted 45 minutes. Symptoms included flushing, chest tightness, shakiness, transient tachycardia, and mild hypotension. Symptoms resolved without intervention and did not recur with subsequent administration. Phlebitis may occur with peripheral continuous infusion; phlebitis occurred in 1.3% of patients with congenital factor VII deficiency receiving rfVIIa. Most commonly, normal saline is infused via a Y-site at a rate of 10 to 20 mL/hour to prevent local phlebitis. When administered into a central vein, coadministration of normal saline is not necessary.

Description
  Text
  Recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) is a glycoprotein similar to human plasma-derived factor VII, a vitamin K-dependent clotting factor. Recombinant FVIIa consists of 406 amino acid residues with a molecular weight of 50 kD. Originally developed for the treatment of patients with hemophilia A or B with inhibitors, rFVIIa is now also used for on-demand treatment and perioperative management of bleeding in patients with acquired hemophilia, factor VII deficiency, and Glanzmann's thrombasthenia refractory to platelet transfusions. Before initiating immune tolerance induction (ITI) in patients with hemophilia A and inhibitors, rFVIIa is preferred first-line for the treatment of bleeding to avoid exposure to FVIII contained in activated prothrombin complex concentrates (aPCC) that may trigger anamnesis. Recombinant FVIIa has also been used off-label in the treatment of von Willebrand's disease and to normalize prolonged prothrombin time in patients with excessive warfarin dosage. One of the limitations of recombinant FVIIa treatment is the short dosing interval required to provide therapeutic concentrations. Continuous infusion, compared to intermittent infusion, has been reported to decrease the amount of rFVIIa required by up to 50%. However, studies have utilized a broad range of dosing with variable efficacy; a clear correlation between doses used and hemostatic efficacy is not always present. Continuous infusion of rFVIIa is only approved for use in perioperative management during major surgery. Although there is the potential to induce thrombotic events, rFVIIa has been associated with very few adverse events in both hemophilia and non-hemophilia settings.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) functions as native activated factor VII (FVIIa) in the plasma. Recombinant factor VIIa may exert its effects through tissue factor (TF) dependent or independent mechanisms or, most likely, a combination of these mechanisms. TF is normally located in the deeper layers of the vessel wall without any direct contact with the circulation. Low concentrations of FVIIa are constitutively present in serum accounting for about 1% (4 mg/mL) of the total amount of factor VII protein. At the site of tissue injury, TF and FVIIa form complexes and initiate the extrinsic coagulation cascade by activating factor IX and factor X. Factor Xa, in combination with other factors, then converts prothrombin to thrombin leading to the formation of a hemostatic plug and local hemostasis by conversion of fibrinogen into fibrin. At concentrations obtained therapeutically, rFVIIa activates enough factor IX and X on the surface of activated platelets to restore the formation of thrombin by platelets. This would allow each platelet to produce more thrombin leading to a stable fibrin network even in the absence of an optimal initial platelet plug. Localization of rFVIIa on activated platelets would tend to restrict the activity of rFVIIa to the sites of injury. In addition, the platelet surface activity of rFVIIa could explain its effect in patients with thrombocytopenia and platelet function defects.
 
Administration of rFVIIa reduces the prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) in patients with factor VII deficiency and hemophilia patients. In patients with hemophilia A/B with inhibitors, the PT shortened to about a 7-second plateau at a FVII:C concentration of approximately 5 Units/mL. For FVII:C concentrations more than 5 Units/mL, there is not further change in PT. Administration of rFVIIa shortens the prolonged aPTT in hemophilia A/B patients with inhibitors; however, normalization is usually not observed in doses shown to induce clinical improvement. These changes do not result in a hypercoagulable state or thrombotic events since the activity of rFVIIa seems to be restricted to the site of injury. The amount of thrombin and factor Xa generated in the presence of rFVIIa is not higher than that in normal plasma. The onset of generation of factor Xa and thrombin is earlier in the presence of rFVIIa than without rFVIIa.

Maximum Dosage Limits
  Text
  Specific maximum dosage information is not available. Individualize dosage based on the indication, location and extent of bleeding, need for urgent hemostasis, and known patient responsiveness to factor VIIa-containing bypassing agents during prior bleeding events. Cumulative daily doses more than 900 mcg/kg, which may be associated with a greater risk of thromboembolic complications, have not been studied.
