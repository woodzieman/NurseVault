Drug Class: Cabergoline is a synthetic ergoline-derived dopamine agonist that is indicated for the treatment of hyperprolactinemic disorders, either idiopathic or due to pituitary adenomas.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: The usual maximum for hyperprolactinemia is 1 mg PO twice per week, but doses of 3 mg/week have been reported. Max off-label dose reported for Cushing's syndrome: 7 mg/week PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): dental pain, throat irritation, weight gain, weight loss, dizziness, drowsiness, headache, insomnia, paresthesias, vertigo, asthenia, edema, fatigue, hot flashes, malaise, hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, palpitations, peripheral edema, syncope, cough, pleural effusion, pulmonary fibrosis, retroperitoneal fibrosis, cardiac valvulopathy, fluid retention, heart failure, pericarditis, anxiety, confusion

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Cabergoline is contraindicated in patients with a hypersensitivity to cabergoline or a known ergot alkaloid hypersensitivity because of the possibility of an allergic reaction including anaphylaxis. Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Cabergoline is contraindicated in patients with a hypersensitivity to cabergoline or a known ergot alkaloid hypersensitivity because of the possibility of an allergic reaction including anaphylaxis.
 
Cabergoline is extensively metabolized in the liver, primarily through hydrolysis. The AUC and peak plasma concentrations of the drug are increased in the presence of severe hepatic impairment (see Pharmacokinetics). Although quantitative guidelines are not available, a dosage reduction should be considered in those with significant hepatic disease; careful patient monitoring is advised.
 
Dizziness and drowsiness are frequently reported side effects of cabergoline. Therefore, patients should be cautioned about engaging in tasks requiring mental alertness such as driving or operating machinery until they know how the drug will affect their cognition.
 
Cabergoline has minimal affinity for adrenergic receptors; however, it has been associated with orthostatic hypotension in some instances. Initiating treatment with dosages greater than 1 mg PO are most likely to produce this effect. Cabergoline should be used cautiously in those with a history of hypotension or those receiving medications known to cause hypotension.
 
Like other ergot derivatives, cabergoline is contraindicated in patients with uncontrolled hypertension. The drug should be discontinued immediately if hypertension, severe headaches, or other adverse CNS signs appear.
 
Cabergoline is contraindicated for use by patients with a history of pulmonary, pericardial, cardiac valvular, or retroperitoneal fibrotic disorders. Cabergoline should not be used in patients with a history of or current signs/clinical symptoms of respiratory or cardiac disorders linked to fibrotic tissue such as pulmonary fibrosis. One case of constrictive pericarditis with the subsequent development of pleuropulmonary inflammatory-fibrotic syndrome has occurred in conjunction with cabergoline administration. Similar to other ergot derivatives, cabergoline can cause fibrotic changes, inflammatory fibrosis, pleural effusion, and valvulopathy with chronic administration. According to the manufacturer, discontinuation of cabergoline has resulted in an improvement in the signs and symptoms of pleural effusion and pulmonary fibrosis.
 
Cautious use of cabergoline is recommended for patients who have hemodynamically significant valvular disease or who have been exposed to other medications associated with valvulopathy. A cardiovascular evaluation and echocardiography consideration are recommended before cabergoline initiation to assess for valvular disease. The benefit of treatment with cabergoline should be carefully assessed against the risks in those with a history of valvular heart disease, and appropriate radiographic and laboratory monitoring is advisable if treatment is necessary. Periodic assessment of cardiac status and consideration of echocardiography are needed for patients who receive long-term treatment regardless of baseline cardiac status.
 
Cabergoline interferes with lactation as a result of central inhibition of prolactin secretion. Therefore, cabergoline should not be used during breast-feeding. In addition, cabergoline is not indicated for postpartum lactation inhibition because the use of other dopamine agonists for this purpose has resulted in hypertension, stroke, and seizures.
 
Dopamine agonists are sometimes used as alternatives to anticholinergics for the treatment of extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) associated with antipsychotics. However, dopamine agonists can exacerbate the symptoms of schizophrenia such as hallucinations. Cabergoline should be used cautiously in patients with psychosis or psychotic disorders. In addition, cabergoline has caused hallucinations and confusion in Parkinson's patients receiving the drug. Therefore, it should be used with extreme caution in parkinsonian patients who exhibit signs of dementia. Hallucinations can occur during cabergoline monotherapy or when the drug is used concomitantly with levodopa.
 
Depression has been reported in 1—5% of patients receiving cabergoline. Patients with depressive disorders should be closely monitored during treatment with cabergoline.
 
Cabergoline is classified as pregnancy category B. Reproductive studies in rabbits have shown no teratogenic or embryotoxic effects. No increases in miscarriage rates, congenital malformations, birthweights or postnatal development were noted during a review of over 200 pregnancies in cabergoline-exposed women when compared to the expected rate in the general population. Dopamine agonists, such as cabergoline, should generally not be used in patients with pregnancy-induced hypertension (eclampsia, preeclampsia, and postpartum hypertension). Because there are no well-controlled studies in pregnant women, cabergoline should be used during pregnancy only if clearly needed. Instruct patients to notify their physician if they suspect they are pregnant, become pregnant, or intend to become pregnant during therapy. Conduct a pregnancy test if there is any suspicion of pregnancy.
 
The safety and efficacy of cabergoline in children have not been established.
 
In general, cabergoline dosing should be more cautious in the elderly, starting at the lower end of the dose range to account for differences in renal, hepatic, or cardiac systems as well as concomitant disease states and medications.
 
Dostinx tablets contain lactose; patients with lactase deficiency should take appropriate precautions with use.

    Headline
    valvular heart disease
    General Information
    Cabergoline is contraindicated in individuals with a history of valvular heart disease, as suggested by anatomical evidence of valvulopathy of any valve, determined by pre-treatment evaluation including echocardiographic demonstration of valve leaflet thickening, valve restriction, or mixed valve restriction stenosis. Postmarketing cases of cardiac valvulopathy have been reported in individuals receiving cabergoline. These cases have generally occurred during the use of high doses of the drug (i.e., more than 2mg/day). Cases of cardiac valvulopathy have also been reported in individuals receiving lower doses of cabergoline for the treatment of hyperprolactinemic disorders. Cautious use of cabergoline is recommended in individuals who have been exposed to other medications associated with valvulopathy. All individuals should undergo a cardiovascular evaluation, including a echocardiogram, before cabergoline initiation to assess for valvular disease. If valvular disease is present, cabergoline treatment should not be initiated. Clinical and diagnostic monitoring (e.g., chest x-ray, CT scan, echocardiogram) should be conducted periodically in all individuals receiving cabergoline to evaluate the risk of cardiac valvulopathy. Echocardiographic monitoring should be conducted every 6 to 12 months or as needed by the presence of edema, new cardiac murmur, dyspnea, congestive heart failure, or other signs and symptoms of valvulopathy. Cabergoline should be discontinued if an echocardiogram demonstrates new valvular regurgitation, valvular restriction, or valve leaflet thickening.
    Headline
    pericardial fibrosis, pleural fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, retroperitoneal fibrosis
    General Information
    Cabergoline is contraindicated for use in individuals with a history of pulmonary, pericardial, or retroperitoneal fibrotic disorders (e.g., pulmonary fibrosis, pleural fibrosis, pericardial fibrosis, or retroperitoneal fibrosis). Do not use cabergoline in individuals with a history of or current signs/clinical symptoms of respiratory, cardiac, or other disorders linked to fibrotic tissue. One case of constrictive pericarditis with subsequent pleuropulmonary inflammatory-fibrotic syndrome has occurred with cabergoline administration. Similar to other ergot derivatives, cabergoline can cause fibrotic changes, inflammatory fibrosis, pleural effusion, and valvulopathy. Therefore, individuals receiving cabergoline should be monitored for the potential development of fibrotic disorders. Clinical and diagnostic monitoring (e.g., erythrocyte sedimentation rate, chest x-ray, serum creatinine) should be considered at baseline and as needed during treatment. Potential signs and symptoms of fibrotic disorders, including dyspnea, chronic cough, chest pain, renal insufficiency, ureteral/abdominal vascular obstruction, abdominal masses or tenderness, and cardiac failure, should be promptly evaluated. Discontinuation of cabergoline may result in improvement in the symptoms of pleural effusion and pulmonary fibrosis.
    Headline
    hypertension
    General Information
    Like other ergot derivatives, cabergoline is contraindicated in individuals with uncontrolled hypertension. Ergot-derived dopamine agonists have been associated with hypertension, strokes, and seizures.
    Headline
    impulse control disorder
    General Information
    Use cabergoline with caution in people with impulse control disorder and warn about the development of new or increased impulse control symptoms/compulsive behaviors. Some individuals receiving dopamine agonists, including cabergoline, have reported pathological gambling, increased libido, and hypersexuality. Generally, these impulse control symptoms are reversible after the dose is reduced, or the drug is discontinued. Instruct individuals to report such changes while receiving cabergoline. Dose reduction or discontinuation should be considered in those who experience these effects.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    The use of cabergoline is not recommended in people with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure. Individuals with moderate hepatic impairment (and Child-Pugh class B) require increased monitoring for adverse reactions. The recommendations for individuals with mild hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A) are the same as those with normal hepatic function.
    Headline
    eclampsia, preeclampsia, pregnancy
    General Information
    Avoid the use of cabergoline during pregnancy. The risks of cabergoline increase in individuals with pregnancy-induced hypertension (e.g., preeclampsia, eclampsia, or postpartum hypertension). Published case reports have not reported a clear association with cabergoline and major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse fetal outcomes when cabergoline was used during early pregnancy. However, these case reports cannot definitely establish the absence of cabergoline-associated risk. If conception occurs during therapy, discontinue cabergoline if the risks to the mother or fetus outweigh the maternal benefits.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel individuals about the reproductive risk associated with cabergoline. There is an association of use of dopamine agonists with hypertension, seizures, myocardial infarction, and stroke during pregnancy and in the period after birth. Instruct individuals to notify their physician if they suspect they are pregnant, become pregnant, or intend to become pregnant during therapy. Conduct pregnancy testing on people who may become pregnant if there is any suspicion of pregnancy. If conception occurs during therapy, discontinue cabergoline if the maternal and fetal risks outweigh the benefits of treatment.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Cabergoline is not recommended during breast-feeding or in people who are planning to breastfeed after birth. A portion of the medication may be transferred into the breast milk, and cabergoline may inhibit lactation due to the central prolactin-lowering effect. One source states that in patients with hyperprolactinemia, it is possible to carefully administer cabergoline doses to lower the prolactin to safe ranges, but high enough to retain lactation. If cabergoline treatment is medically necessary, the breastfed infant should be monitored for possible ergot-related side effects. Cabergoline has been used off-label to suppress lactation. The use of other dopamine agonists (e.g., bromocriptine) for lactation inhibition has resulted in serious adverse effects including hypertension, stroke, and seizures. The cabergoline prescription label states to avoid use of cabergoline for the inhibition or suppression of physiologic lactation because of the risk of these serious adverse reactions.
    Headline
    postpartum
    General Information
    Avoid the use of cabergoline during the postpartum period because of the risk of serious adverse reactions. These risks of cabergoline are especially notable in individuals with postpartum hypertension. Use of bromocriptine, another dopamine agonist, in postpartum individuals has been associated with cases of hypertension, stroke, myocardial infarction, seizures, and death.

  Most adverse reactions observed with cabergoline are mild to moderate in severity and may be minimized by administration of the drug with food and by following the recommended dose titration. Tolerance to most side effects develops with chronic administration. Among the most frequently reported gastrointestinal (GI) side effects during clinical trials were nausea/vomiting (27%/2%), constipation (10%), and abdominal pain (5%). In particular, GI-related side effects were less frequent and less severe in the cabergoline group. Tolerance to most side effects develops with chronic administration. Side effects occurring in 1—5% of patients receiving cabergoline include dysmenorrhea, mastalgia, hot flashes, and visual impairment (unspecified).
 
Peptic ulcer or duodenal ulcer occurred rarely among patients with Parkinson's disease who received doses of cabergoline up to 11.5 mg daily.
 
Although cabergoline has only minimal affinity for adrenergic receptors, clinical trial data indicate that orthostatic hypotension has been a frequently reported side effect in some studies. Most instances of hypotension are asymptomatic and do not require discontinuation of the drug. Initial doses higher than 1 mg may produce orthostatic hypotension. Among 168 patients who got cabergoline 0.125 mg—1 mg PO twice weekly, 4% had postural hypotension. In another study of 221 patients, 3 had syncope, and  3 had hypotension. Caution is recommended if cabergoline is used concomitantly with drugs that may lower blood pressure (see Drug Interactions).
 
One case of constrictive pericarditis with the subsequent development of pleuropulmonary inflammatory-fibrotic syndrome has occurred in conjunction with cabergoline administration. Similar to other ergot derivatives, cabergoline has been associated with pleuropulmonary changes, pleural effusion, and pulmonary fibrosis with chronic administration. Pleural effusion and pulmonary fibrosis occurred rarely among patients with Parkinson's disease who received doses up to 11.5 mg daily. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) has been found to be abnormally increased in association with pleural effusion/fibrosis. Chest x-ray examination is recommended in cases of unexplained ESR increases to abnormal values. Serum creatinine measurements can also be used to help in the diagnosis of fibrotic disorder. After diagnosis of pleural effusion or pulmonary fibrosis, cabergoline discontinuation has been reported to result in improvement of signs and symptoms. Do not use cabergoline in patients with a history of or current signs and or clinical symptoms of respiratory or cardiac disorders linked to fibrotic tissue (see Contraindications). Instruct patients to report shortness of breath, persistent cough, difficulty breathing when lying down, or extremity swelling.
 
Cardiac valvulopathy may occur, especially with cabergoline daily doses of at least 2 mg given long-term for the treatment of Parkinson's disease. Rarely, cardiac valvulopathy has been reported with short-term treatment (< 6 months) or in patients receiving lower doses for the treatment of hyperprolactinemia. Therefore, use the lowest effective dose for the treatment of hyperprolactinemia and periodically reassess the need for continuing therapy. Among 221 patients who received cabergoline, 2 had dependent edema, 2 had peripheral edema, 2 had periorbital edema, and 2 had palpitations. Peripheral edema was a common adverse event among patients with Parkinson's disease who received doses up to 11.5 mg daily; heart failure occurred rarely. The risk for valvular heart disease was assessed in a prevalence study involving 155 patients taking dopamine agonists for Parkinson's disease and 90 control subjects without the disease. Echocardiography was performed to assess the severity of regurgitation of the mitral, aortic, and tricuspid valves and degree of deformity of the mitral-valve apparatus. Of the patients taking dopamine agonists, 49 were receiving cabergoline. Moderate to severe regurgitation (grade 3—4) occurred significantly more frequently in the ergot dopamine agonist group (pergolide [23.4%] and cabergoline [28.6%]) compared to the non-ergot dopamine agonist group (pramipexole [0%] and ropinirole [0%]), or the control group (5.6%). In a separate population-based cohort study of 11,417 patients receiving various antiparkinsonian medications, the risk of newly diagnosed cardiac valve regurgitation was greater with cabergoline, as well as pergolide, compared to other dopamine agonists. Cautious use of cabergoline is recommended for patients who have hemodynamically significant valvular disease or who have been exposed to other medications associated with valvulopathy. Perform a cardiovascular evaluation, and consider echocardiography to assess for valvular disease before cabergoline initiation. Periodic reassessment of cardiac status and consideration of echocardiography is advised for patients receiving long-term treatment. Evaluate any patient taking cabergoline who develops signs or symptoms of cardiac disease such as dyspnea, edema, congestive heart failure, or a new cardiac murmur for possible valvulopathy.
 
Commonly observed adverse effects among patients with Parkinson's disease who received cabergoline doses up to 11.5 mg daily were confusion, dyskinesia, and hallucinations. Side effects occurring in 1—5% of patients included anxiety and depression. Further, during post-marketing surveillance, reports of aggression and psychotic disorder have been received. Some patients had prior adverse reactions to dopamine agonists.
 
Pathological gambling, libido increase, and hypersexuality have been reported in patients treated with dopamine agonists including cabergoline. Pathological gambling, increased libido, and hypersexuality have been generally reversible upon dose reduction or treatment discontinuation.

    General Information
    Cabergoline is contraindicated in people with a history of pleural, pulmonary, or retroperitoneal fibrosis. Signs and symptoms of progressive fibrosis include pleuro-pulmonary disease (e.g., dyspnea, shortness of breath, persistent cough, and chest pain) and renal impairment or ureteral/abdominal vascular obstruction (e.g., pain in the loin/flank, lower limb edema, abdominal masses or tenderness that may indicate retroperitoneal fibrosis). Monitor signs and symptoms of progressive fibrosis during cabergoline treatment. Consider clinical and diagnostic monitoring for pleural, pulmonary, and retroperitoneal fibrosis, such as with erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), serum creatinine measurements, chest-x-ray, and other investigations at baseline and as necessary during cabergoline treatment. If pleural, pericardial, retroperitoneal, or pulmonary fibrosis occur, discontinue cabergoline.

    Cabergoline is contraindicated in the presence of valvular disease or pericardial fibrosis (constrictive pericarditis). Cases of valvular and pericardial fibrosis have often manifested as heart failure. Following cabergoline treatment initiation, monitor for the development of valvulopathy with a cardiac echocardiogram at intervals of 6 to 12 months or as clinically indicated with new onset edema or fluid retention, cardiac murmur, dyspnea, or heart failure. During cabergoline treatment, monitor for chest pain and signs and symptoms of heart failure and if heart failure occurs, valvular fibrosis and pericarditis should be excluded. The risk for valvular heart disease was assessed in a placebo-controlled prevalence study of subjects taking dopamine agonists for Parkinson's disease (PD, n = 155) and controls without the disease (n = 90). Of the subjects taking dopamine agonists, 49 were receiving cabergoline. Echocardiography was performed to assess the severity of regurgitation of the mitral, aortic, and tricuspid valves and degree of deformity of the mitral valve apparatus. Moderate to severe regurgitation (grade 3 to 4) occurred significantly more frequently in the ergot dopamine agonist group (pergolide 23.4%, cabergoline 28.6%) than the non-ergot dopamine agonist group (pramipexole 0%, ropinirole 0%), or the control group (5.6%). In a separate population-based cohort study of subjects receiving various antiparkinsonian medications (n = 11,417), the risk of newly diagnosed cardiac valve regurgitation (CVR) was greater with cabergoline and pergolide than other dopamine agonists. Postmarketing cases of cardiac valvulopathy have been reported in people who received cabergoline. These cases have generally occurred during administration of high doses of greater than 2 mg/day for the treatment of PD. Cabergoline is not approved for the treatment of PD. Cases of cardiac valvulopathy have also been reported in people who received lower dosages of cabergoline for the treatment of hyperprolactinemic disorders. In a 12-year, multi-country retrospective cohort study, the use of cabergoline for PD was associated with an increased risk of cardiac valvular regurgitation (CVR). Compared to non-ergot derived dopamine agonists and levodopa, CVR with cabergoline use had an incidence rate per 10,000 person years of 68 (95% CI: 37, 115) vs. 10 (95% CI: 5, 19) for non-ergot dopamine agonists and 11 (95% CI: 7,17) for levodopa. Before initiating cabergoline, perform a cardiovascular evaluation, including an echocardiogram, to evaluate for valvular disease. Consider clinical and diagnostic monitoring such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), serum creatinine measurements, chest-x- ray, and other investigations and cardiac imaging at baseline and as necessary while people are treated with cabergoline. Discontinue cabergoline if the individual has a new diagnosis of valvular regurgitation, valvular restriction, valve leaflet thickening, or pericarditis. Use cabergoline in people treated with other drugs associated with valvulopathy only if the potential benefit of cabergoline outweighs the risk.

Description
  Text
  Cabergoline is a synthetic ergoline-derived dopamine agonist that is indicated for the treatment of hyperprolactinemic disorders, either idiopathic or due to pituitary adenomas. Results from comparative studies of the 2 medications indicate that cabergoline is more effective than bromocriptine in suppressing prolactin secretion, and it has a more favorable tolerability profile. In addition, the unique dosing strategy of cabergoline is a potential advantage of the drug. Whereas bromocriptine requires multiple daily dosages for effectiveness, cabergoline has a long half-life and is administered once to twice weekly for hyperprolactinemia. Cabergoline is also used off-label in the treatment of Cushing's disease in selected patients; guidelines strongly recommend cabergoline or pasireotide in patients with mild disease if residual tumor is present and there is a potential for tumor shrinkage. Although there is evidence for the efficacy of cabergoline as an off-label treatment for restless legs syndrome (RLS), guidelines strongly recommend against use due to the risk of cardiac valvulopathy and the availability of safer alternatives for RLS. Individuals should undergo a cardiovascular evaluation, including echocardiogram, to assess the potential presence of valvular disease before deciding to initiate cabergoline. Clinical and diagnostic monitoring should be conducted in treated patients periodically to assess the risk of cardiac valvulopathy.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Cabergoline is an ergot derivative and dopamine receptor agonist with a high affinity for dopamine D2 receptors. Results of in vitro studies demonstrate that cabergoline exerts a direct inhibitory effect on the secretion of prolactin by rat pituitary lactotrophs. Cabergoline decreased serum prolactin concentrations in reserpinized rats. Receptor-binding studies indicate that cabergoline has low affinity for dopamine D1, alpha 1- and alpha 2-adrenergic, and 5-HT1- and 5-HT2-serotonin receptors.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    The Tmax for cabergoline is reached in 2 to 3 hours following single oral doses of 0.5 to 1.5 mg (1.5 times the maximum recommended dose) in healthy subjects. Following dosing of cabergoline between 0.5 to 7 mg (7 times the maximum recommended dose), plasma concentrations appeared to be dose-proportional. The absolute bioavailability of cabergoline is unknown. High-fat foods did not alter pharmacokinetics.

    Males aged 20 to 34 years were shown to have had higher Cmax than females aged 20 to 27 years, while males aged 66 to 75 years had lower Cmax compared to females aged 66 to 74 years. The clinical significance of the findings is unknown.
  Text
  Cabergoline is administered orally. Cabergoline is moderately bound (40% to 42%) to human plasma proteins in a concentration-independent manner. A significant fraction of the administered oral dose undergoes a first-pass effect. Cabergoline is extensively metabolized, predominately via hydrolysis of the acylurea or urea moiety, which abolishes the prolactin-lowering effect of cabergoline. Major metabolites identified do not contribute to the therapeutic effect of the drug. The elimination half-life is approximately 63 to 69 hours. Following oral administration of radioactive cabergoline to 5 healthy volunteers, approximately 22% and 60% of the dose was excreted within 20 days in the urine and feces, respectively. Less than 4% of the dose was excreted unchanged in the urine. Nonrenal and renal clearances for cabergoline are about 3.2 L/minute and 0.08 L/minute, respectively. Urinary excretion in hyperprolactinemic individuals was similar.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: none

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Use is not recommended in people with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh Class C) or hepatic failure. No dosage adjustments necessary for people with mild hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh Class A).
