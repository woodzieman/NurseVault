Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Fibrinogen concentrate is contraindicated in patients who have manifested severe immediate hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis, to fibrinogen concentrate or to the product components. Fibrinogen concentrate may be inappropriate for patients with albumin hypersensitivity. Each vial of fibrinogen concentrate contains 400—700 mg of human albumin. Hypersensitivity reactions such as hives, generalized urticaria, tightness of the chest, wheezing, hypotension, and anaphylaxis may occur (see Adverse Reactions).
 
Administer fibrinogen concentrate cautiously in patients with a history of thromboembolic disease. Life-threatening thromboses have been reported in patients receiving fibrinogen concentrate. Based on the mechanism of action of fibrinogen concentrate, patients predisposed to thromboembolic events may be at increased risk of adverse clot development after fibrinogen administration (see Mechanism of Action). In addition, thromboses may develop in patients with congenital fibrinogen deficiency (afibrinogenemia and hypofibrinogenemia) with or without the use of fibrinogen replacement therapy (see Adverse Reactions). Patients with dysfibrinogenemia (presence of abnormal or dysfunctional fibrinogen) are also at risk for clotting complications. Two mechanisms have been proposed for the abnormal thrombus formation in dysfibrinogenemic patients. The first mechanism involves a fibrin binding site mutation causing thrombin to adhere to fibrin with low-affinity. As a result of the weak binding, thrombin is released by the fibrin clot back into circulation and is available for further thrombus formation. The second proposed mechanism involves mutations that impair profibrinolytic function. The impaired fibrinolytic function may be a result of defective plasmin binding to fibrin or of fibrin resistance to the digestive action of plasmin. Fibrinogen concentrate is not indication for acute bleeding in patients with dysfibrinogenemia. Weigh the benefits of fibrinogen concentrate in patients with thromboembolic disease against the risk of complications.
 
Fibrinogen concentrate is made from human plasma and may contain agents known to cause infection. The risk of transmitting infectious agents has been reduced by screening plasma donors for exposure to certain viruses, testing for the presence of certain viruses, and inactivating and/or reducing viruses. Donated plasma is processed by the manufacturer to inactivate and/or remove certain viruses; however, an unknown infectious agent may be present in the final product. Report all infections thought to have been transmitted by fibrinogen concentrate to CSL Behring at 1—866—915—6958.
 
The effectiveness of fibrinogen concentrate is dependent upon the presence of thrombin and activated factor XIII (see Mechanism of Action). In patients with hepatic disease, all clotting factors except factor VIII are decreased. It is reasonable, based on the mechanism of action of fibrinogen concentrate, that patients with hepatic insufficiency may have a decreased response to treatment with fibrinogen concentrate.
 
Fibrinogen concentrate has been studied in four children under 16 years of age. Limited data suggest that children may eliminate fibrinogen concentrate faster than older patients (>16 years). Monitor plasma fibrinogen concentrations closely when administering fibrinogen concentrate to children (see Pharmacokinetics).
 
Safety and efficacy of fibrinogen concentrate have not been established in the elderly. Data are lacking in regard to responses in the elderly as compared with younger patients.
 
Fibrinogen concentrate is classified in FDA pregnancy risk category C. Fibrinogen concentrate should be used with caution during pregnancy because its effects on the fetus and reproduction capacity are not known. Animal studies have not been done to determine the effects of fibrinogen concentrate during pregnancy. Fibrinogen concentrate should be used during pregnancy only if clearly needed.
 
The use of fibrinogen concentrate in lactating mothers has not been studied. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternal drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: Data are very limited; data are based on the results of a single RiaSTAP dose administered to 14 patients.
 
According to the manufacturer, the most common adverse reactions associated with fibrinogen concentrate are generalized reactions such as chills, fever, headache, and nausea/vomiting. In a study of five afibrinogenemic and hypofibrinogenemic patients, two patients reported reactions determined to be possibly related to infusions of fibrinogen concentrate. The first patient developed a fever (37.6 degrees C) and dyspnea at the end of a fibrinogen infusion. The symptoms subsided without any countermeasures after 1 hour (dyspnea) and 6 hours (fever). The second patient experienced dizziness associated with hypotension (110/70 mmHg). After 15 minutes, blood pressure normalized and the dizziness resolved without treatment. Both patients received a fibrinogen concentrate product different from RiaSTAP at dosages of 69—71 mg/kg and rates of 3.3—5.2 ml/minute.
 
Thrombosis with or without embolization may occur spontaneously in patients with congenital fibrinogen deficiency regardless of the use of fibrinogen concentrate. Data are limited to case reports, and the incidence of spontaneous thrombosis in this population is unknown. Thrombus development is theorized to begin with damage to the vascular endothelium by recurrent hemorrhages stimulating the release of thrombin. In the absence of fibrinogen, thrombin is not bound by fibrin clots but rather remains in circulation for a longer duration. The abundance of circulating thrombin causes platelet activation and aggregation to occur, resulting in the formation of a thrombus that is free of fibrin deposits. It is reasonable, based on the mechanism of action, that thrombosis may occur after administration of fibrinogen concentrate (see Mechanism of Action). Thromboembolic episodes are among the most serious adverse reactions that have been reported after the use of fibrinogen concentrate. Life-threatening thromboses associated with fibrinogen concentrate products different for RiaSTAP have been reported in the literature. In a study of congenital fibrinogen deficient patients, one patient developed a deep vein thrombosis and non-fatal pulmonary embolism during treatment with a fibrinogen substitute. The patient developed this thromboembolism after a surgical procedure despite prophylactic heparin. In a separate study, three ischemic cerebrovascular incidences and one myocardial infarction were observed. All four were postoperative events occurring a median of 7.5 days after fibrinogen concentrate administration, and in all four events, the patients experienced perioperative hemorrhages with hypotension requiring transfusion (range, 13—36 transfusions). The dose of fibrinogen concentrate received by these four patients ranged from 2 to 4 grams, and the peak plasma fibrinogen concentration ranged from 139 to 205 mg/dl. Postmarketing reports of thromboembolic complications in patients receiving fibrinogen concentrate include myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, deep vein thrombosis, and arterial thrombosis. Weigh the benefits of fibrinogen concentrate therapy against the risk of thrombosis. Monitor all patients receiving fibrinogen concentrate for signs and symptoms of thrombosis (e.g., unexplained pleuritic chest and/or leg pain or edema, hemoptysis, dyspnea, tachypnea, or unexplained neurologic symptoms). Also, instruct patients to immediately report any signs or symptoms of thrombotic events.
 
Hypersensitivity or anaphylactoid reactions may occur with the use of fibrinogen concentrate. Allergic reactions are among the most serious adverse reactions that have been reported after fibrinogen concentrate treatment. An anaphylactic reaction was reported in a patient who received 86 prophylactic infusions of a fibrinogen product different from RiaSTAP (approximate dose, 77 mg/kg). The patient experienced the reaction (hypotension, cyanosis, abdominal pain, and back pain) after administration of the 56th dose. It was not reported if corrective measures were employed; all infusions before and after this reaction were well tolerated. If symptoms of allergic reactions or early signs of hypersensitivity reactions including rash (unspecified), generalized urticaria, chest tightness, wheezing, dyspnea, hypotension, or anaphylaxis occur, immediately discontinue administration; do not readminister fibrinogen concentrate to a patient who has a severe immediate hypersensitivity reaction (see Contraindications).

    cardiac arrest, chest pain (unspecified), disseminated intravascular coagulation, hepatitis, myocardial infarction, phlebitis, pulmonary embolism, stroke, thromboembolism, thrombosis
    General Information
    Thrombotic events including myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, deep vein thrombosis, and arterial thrombosis have been reported with fibrinogen concentrate use. In a randomized controlled trial (n = 735) comparing fibrinogen concentrate to cryoprecipitate, 32 subjects (8.6%) in the fibrinogen concentrate group experienced 37 thromboembolism adverse events compared to 45 subjects (12.4%) in the cryoprecipitate group experienced 50 thromboembolism adverse events. Thromboembolic adverse events in fibrinogen concentrate-treated subjects included cerebrovascular accident (stroke) (n = 17), intestinal ischemia (n = 4), deep vein thrombosis (n = 3), myocardial infarction (n = 3), peripheral ischemia (n = 2), pulmonary embolism (n = 3), transient ischemic attack (n = 1), cardiac arrest (n = 1), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) (n = 1), ischemic hepatitis (n = 1), and thrombophlebitis (n =1). A single case each of digital foot ischemia, portal vein thrombosis after splenectomy, and peripheral phlebitis of the upper limbs was also reported in fibrinogen concentrate clinical trials. Additionally, chest pain (unspecified), chest discomfort, and venous thrombosis of the limb were reported as a single event in fibrinogen concentrate-treated subjects in another clinical trial. Thrombosis (i.e., deep vein thrombosis, portal vein thrombosis) and increased fibrin D-dimer were also reported in 4% of fibrinogen concentrate-treated subjects in another clinical trial. Weigh the benefits of fibrinogen concentrate administration against the risks of thrombosis. Monitor individuals receiving fibrinogen concentrate for signs or symptoms of thrombosis. Thrombosis may occur spontaneously in individuals with acquired or congenital fibrinogen deficiency with or without the use of fibrinogen replacement therapy. Treatment with human fibrinogen concentrate in congenital fibrinogen deficiency has been associated with risk of thrombosis at target fibrinogen concentrations less than 150 mg/dL. The risk of thrombosis may be greater when the target fibrinogen plasma concentration is 150 mg/dL.

    anaphylactoid reactions, cough, dyspnea, erythema, pruritus, rash, wheezing
    General Information
    Anaphylactoid reactions are among the most serious adverse reactions reported with fibrinogen concentrate use. If early signs of hypersensitivity (e.g., hives, urticaria, chest tightness, wheezing, hypotension, anaphylaxis) occur, discontinue administration immediately and institute appropriate medical treatment. A single subject developed a mild skin reaction (pruritus and erythema) during clinical trials and was treated with diphenhydramine and hydrocortisone. The subject received 2 more fibrinogen concentrate infusions and was treated prophylactically with diphenhydramine and hydrocortisone with no further drug reactions. Hypersensitivity reactions including facial swelling were reported in 7% of fibrinogen concentrate-treated subjects in clinical trials. Wheezing (n = 1), cough (n = 1), and dyspnea (n = 2) were also reported in fibrinogen concentrate-treated subjects in clinical trials (n = 22). Anaphylaxis, dyspnea, and rash have been reported with postmarketing use of fibrinogen concentrate.

Description
  Text
  Fibrinogen concentrate is a human blood coagulation factor indicated for the treatment of acute bleeding in individuals with congenital fibrinogen deficiency, including afibrinogenemia and hypofibrinogenemia, and for fibrinogen supplementation in bleeding individuals with acquired fibrinogen deficiency. Fibrinogen concentrate is not indicated for dysfibrinogenemia. Prior to the availability of fibrinogen concentrate, correction of fibrinogen deficiencies occurred via administration of cryoprecipitate or fresh frozen plasma (FFP). Fibrinogen concentrate maintains several advantages over cryoprecipitate and FFP including purification during the production process, dose standardization to target fibrinogen concentrations, and minimization of the infusion volume. Spontaneous thrombosis may occur in individuals with acquired or congenital fibrinogen deficiency with or without the use of fibrinogen replacement therapy; thrombotic events have been reported in individuals receiving fibrinogen concentrate. Fibrinogen concentrate was FDA-approved in 2009.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Fibrinogen concentrate aids clot formation in individuals with congenital fibrinogen deficiency by replacing the low or missing coagulation factor. Fibrinogen (factor I) is the final substrate in the clot forming process known as the coagulation cascade. The ultimate goal of the multi-step coagulation cascade is to convert fibrinogen into an insoluble fibrin clot. This conversion is accomplished through the interaction of fibrinogen with 2 enzymes, thrombin and activated factor XIII. Initially, fibrinogen binds with circulating thrombin, which causes the release of fibrinopeptide A (FPA) and B (FPB). The release of FPA occurs rapidly and results in the formation of a soluble fibrin monomer (fibrin I). FPB is released at a much slower rate and results in the formation of fibrin II, which is capable of polymerization. Next, in the presence of calcium, activated factor XIII causes the aggregated fibrin II polymers to cross-link. The cross-linked fibrin polymers are the end result of the coagulation cascade and provide elasticity and strength to the hemostatic platelet plug. Soluble fibrinogen also mediates platelet aggregation by binding to the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor on the platelet surface activated after blood vessel injury. This interaction acts as a bridge between platelets, facilitating their aggregation and formation of the primary platelet plug. The end product of the coagulation cascade is cross-linked fibrin that stabilizes and reinforces the primary platelet plug and results in secondary hemostasis to stop bleeding. Fibrinogen is vital to clot strength and elasticity, which can be assessed via maximum clot firmness (MCF).
