Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  The Sotradecol brand of sodium tetradecyl sulfate contains benzyl alcohol and, therefore, should not be used in patients with benzyl alcohol hypersensitivity. Potentially fatal allergic reactions have associated with the use of sodium tetradecyl sulfate, including anaphylaxis. Sodium tetradecyl sulfate should not be used in patients who have experienced previous hypersensitivity reactions to the drug. As a precaution, it is recommended that 0.5 ml of sodium tetradecyl sulfate be injected into a varicosity, followed by observation of the patient for several hours before administration of a second or larger dose. During administration, there should be immediate access to appropriate treatments should an anaphylactic reaction occur. In extreme emergencies administer epinephrine injection and antihistamines as necessary.
 
Injections of sodium tetradecyl sulfate into a varicose vein should be slow and consist of a small amount (not to exceed 2 ml for superficial varicosities) of the solution. The physician should also be aware that tissue necrosis is likely to result from extravasation; this injection is for intravenous use only and should not be delivered via intramuscular administration or subcutaneous administration. The presence of deep ulcerations at previous injection sites indicates that further sessions of sclerotherapy should be postponed until the ulcers have adequately healed.
 
Sodium tetradecyl sulfate injection is contraindicated in patients with acute superficial thrombophlebitis; valvular or deep vein incompetence; huge superficial veins with wide open communications to deeper veins; phlebitis migrans; acute cellulitis or other acute infection such as tuberculosis or sepsis; or varicosities caused by abdominal and pelvic tumors unless the tumor has been removed. Other contraindications include patients who are bedridden. Sodium tetradecyl sulfate also is contraindicated in patients with uncontrolled diseases as uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, uncontrolled thyroid disease (e.g., toxic hyperthyroidism or thyrotoxicosis), neoplastic disease, blood dyscrasias (hematological disease) and acute pulmonary disease (e.g., acute bronchospasm) or serious skin disease. Sodium tetradecyl sulfate should only be administered by physicians who are familiar with an acceptable injection technique. Because of the danger of thrombosis extension into the deep venous system, a thorough pre-treatment evaluation for valvular competency should be performed. In particular, deep venous patency must be determined by angiography and/or the Perthes test before sclerotherapy is undertaken. Venous sclerotherapy should not be performed if tests, such as the Trendelenburg and Perthes, and angiography show significant valvular or deep venous incompetence. Use extreme caution if using in patients with underlying arterial disease, such as marked peripheral arteriosclerosis or thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger's Disease). Thromboembolic disease (including deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism) can occur up to 4 weeks after treatment with sodium tetradecyl sulfate. Patients should be encouraged to wear elastic compression stockings to decrease the incidence of such events.
 
Sodium tetradecyl sulfate should not be used in patients with GI perforation. Use caution in patients with GI bleeding since they may aspirate during the endoscopic administration of the drug unless the patient's airway is protected. Perforation, ulceration, and variceal or esophageal stricture are possible complications of sclerotherapy for variceal bleeding (see Adverse Reactions).
 
Sodium tetradecyl sulfate is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. Adequate or well-controlled investigations in humans or animals have not been performed. Only use during pregnancy when clearly needed; the potential benefit should justify the potential fetal risk.
 
Exercise caution in administering sodium tetradecyl sulfate to a woman who is breast-feeding. It is not known if sodium tetradecyl sulfate is excreted in human milk.
 
Safety and efficacy in children have not been established. The Sotradecol injection contains benzyl alcohol; benzyl alcohol has been associated with 'gasping syndrome' in neonates and premature infants.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  An injection site reaction consisting of pain, pruritus, or ulceration may occur with sodium tetradecyl sulfate. If extravasation of the injection occurs, sloughing and tissue necrosis may result. After treatment of varicose veins, a permanent skin discoloration, usually small in size and hardly noticeable, but potentially cosmetically objectionable, may remain along the path of the sclerosed vein segment. Mild systemic reactions that have been reported with sodium tetradecyl sulfate include headache and nausea/vomiting.Allergic-type reactions, including urticaria, wheezing or dyspnea, allergic rhinitis, anaphylactoid reactions, and potentially fatal anaphylactic shock have been reported with sodium tetradecyl sulfate. Six deaths have been reported with the use of sodium tetradecyl sulfate, two due to fatal anaphylactic shock; one of these patients had a history of asthma. Another death, due to a fatal pulmonary embolism, was reported in a female who was taking an unspecified antiovulatory agent (see Drug Interactions) at the time of treatment. Another death occurred in a premenopausal female NOT taking oral contraceptives. Thromboembolism (including deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism) can occur up to 4 weeks after injection of sodium tetradecyl sulfate. Patients should be encouraged to wear elastic compression stockings to decrease the risk of such events.Complications such as chest pain (unspecified), temporary dysphagia, fever, and small pleural effusions may occur during the use of sodium tetradecyl sulfate during sclerotherapy of esophageal varices. Some adverse events reported may be related to the use of endoscopy or the sclerotherapy procedure versus the use of the drug per se. Sclerotherapy may result in esophageal ulcers or GI perforation. Esophageal ulceration results in recurrent bleeding in roughly 20% of patients by some estimates. Although frequency and severity varies widely in the literature, esophageal stricture formation leading to dysphagia has been seen in approximately 15% of patients. Bacterial peritonitis, esophageal perforation, mediastinitis, brain abscess, spinal cord paralysis, and pericarditis are uncommon but serious complications. Actively bleeding patients are at increased risk for aspiration pneumonia which can be avoided by protecting the airway with endotracheal intubation.

Description
  Text
  Sodium tetradecyl sulfate is an injectable sclerosing agent indicated for the treatment of small uncomplicated varicose veins of the lower extremities that show simple dilation with competent valves. The benefit-to-risk ratio should be considered in selected patients who are great surgical risks. Sodium tetradecyl sulfate should only be administered by a health care professional experienced in venous anatomy and the diagnosis and treatment of conditions affecting the venous system and familiar with proper injection technique. Severe adverse local effects, including tissue necrosis, may occur after extravasation.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Sodium tetradecyl sulfate injection is a sclerosing agent. Intravenous injection causes intima inflammation and thrombus formation. This usually occludes the injected vein. Subsequent formation of fibrous tissue results in partial or complete vein obliteration that may or may not be permanent. Sclerosant-induced venous thrombosis may not be the principal hemostatic mechanism in variceal sclerotherapy. A key factor in the ability of sodium tetradecyl sulfate, as well as other sclerosing agents, to stop active hemorrhage and initiate hemostasis might be attributed to the esophageal and vascular smooth muscle spasm which is induced by the sclerosing agent. During active bleeding, the sclerosant injected into the esophageal varices may dissipate rapidly since the varices have a much higher blood-flow rate and no functioning valves. There is no difference in the rate of hemostasis when sclerosants are intentionally injected around the varices compared to intravascular injection, further supporting the theory that acute venous thrombosis is not the primary hemostatic mechanism. The mechanical compression effect of submucosal edema, created by the injection of sclerosing agents, may also be responsible for acute hemostasis.
