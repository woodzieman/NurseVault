Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  This drug does not treat viral infection (e.g., common cold). Prescribing gatifloxacin in the absence of a proven or strongly suspected bacterial infection or a prophylactic indication is unlikely to provide benefit to the patient and increases the risk of the development of drug-resistant bacteria. Patients should be told to complete the full course of treatment, even if they feel better earlier.
 
Gatifloxacin is contraindicated in patients with a history of quinolone hypersensitivity. Serious and occasionally fatal hypersensitivity reactions have been reported in patients receiving therapy with quinolones, often following the first dose. Gatifloxacin should be discontinued if an allergic reaction or any other sign of hypersensitivity appears (see Adverse Reactions). Serious acute hypersensitivity reactions require immediate treatment.
 
Gatifloxacin oral suspension should be used with caution in patients with phenylketonuria because the oral suspension contains 168 mg of phenylalanine per 5 ml of reconstituted suspension. Other gatifloxacin products do not contain phenylalanine.
 
The efficacy of gatifloxacin for the treatment of syphilis is not known. Antimicrobial agents used in high doses for short periods of time to treat gonorrhea may mask or delay the symptoms of incubating syphilis. All patients with gonorrhea should have a serologic test for syphilis at the time of diagnosis. Patients treated with gatifloxacin should have a follow-up serologic test for syphilis 3 months after treatment for gonorrhea.
 
Gatifloxacin and other quinolones have been associated with an increased risk of tendonitis and tendon rupture requiring surgical repair or resulting in prolonged disability. Discontinue gatifloxacin at the first sign of tendon inflammation or tendon pain as these are symptoms that may precede rupture of the tendon. Tendon rupture typically involves the Achilles, hand, or shoulder tendons and can occur during therapy or up to a few months after therapy has been stopped. The risk of tendon rupture is further increased in those over age 60, those receiving concomitant corticosteroid therapy, and in organ transplant recipients (including kidney, heart, and lung transplants). Other reasons for tendon ruptures include physical activity or exercise, kidney failure, or tendon problems in the past. If patients experience tendon inflammation or pain, they should rest and refrain from exercise until the diagnosis of tendonitis or tendon rupture has been confidently excluded.
 
Seizures have been reported in patients receiving quinolones, including gatifloxacin. Increased intracranial pressure, and toxic psychosis have also been reported in patients receiving quinolones. Quinolones may cause central nervous system stimulation that may lead to tremors, restlessness, lightheadedness, confusion, hallucinations, paranoia, depression, nightmares, and insomnia. These reactions may occur following the first dose. If these reactions occur in patients receiving gatifloxacin, the drug should be discontinued and appropriate treatment measures instituted. Patients should be warned to avoid driving or operating machinery until they know how gatifloxacin affects them. As with other quinolones, gatifloxacin should be used with caution in patients with a known or suspected CNS disorder (e.g., seizure disorder, severe cerebrovascular disease), or in the presence of other risk factors (e.g., certain drug therapy, renal dysfunction) that may predispose patients to seizures or lower seizure threshold.
 
Almost all antibacterial agents have been associated with pseudomembranous colitis (antibiotic-associated colitis) which may range in severity from mild to life-threatening. In the colon, overgrowth of Clostridia may exist when normal flora is altered subsequent to antibacterial administration. The toxin produced by Clostridium difficile is a primary cause of pseudomembranous colitis. It is known that systemic use of antibiotics predisposes patients to development of pseudomembranous colitis. Consideration should be given to the diagnosis of pseudomembranous colitis in patients presenting with diarrhea following antibacterial administration. Systemic antibiotics should be prescribed with caution to patients with inflammatory bowel disease such as ulcerative colitis or other GI disease. If diarrhea develops during therapy, the drug should be discontinued. Following diagnosis of pseudomembranous colitis, therapeutic measures should be instituted. In milder cases, the colitis may respond to discontinuation of the offending agent. In moderate to severe cases, fluids and electrolytes, protein supplementation, and treatment with an antibacterial effective against Clostridium difficile may be warranted. Products inhibiting peristalsis are contraindicated in this clinical situation. Practitioners should be aware that antibiotic-associated colitis has been observed to occur over two months or more following discontinuation of systemic antibiotic therapy; a careful medical history should be taken. Prolonged ophthalmic use of gatifloxacin may also result in overgrowth of non susceptible organisms, including fungi. If superinfection occurs, discontinue use and institute alternative therapy. When clinically necessary, the patient should be examined with the aid of magnification, such as slit lamp biomicroscopy and, where appropriate, fluorescein staining.
 
Gatifloxacin should be used cautiously in elderly patients since they are more likely to have decreased renal function and an increased risk of toxic reactions. Therefore, care should be taken in dose selection and renal function monitored. Post-marketing reports have included disturbances of glucose homeostasis in elderly patients treated with gatifloxacin. Elderly patients who may have unrecognized diabetes, age-related renal function impairment, underlying medical problems, and/or are taking concomitant glucose-altering medications may be at particular risk for serious dysglycemias.
 
Gatifloxacin should be used cautiously in patients with renal impairment or renal disease. Gatifloxacin is excreted by the kidney and the risk of gatifloxacin-induced adverse reactions, such as blood glucose changes, may be greater in these patients. Careful clinical observation and appropriate laboratory studies should be performed prior to and during gatifloxacin therapy. Dosage reduction is necessary in patients with creatinine clearance 450 msec. Post-marketing studies show an association between increases in gatifloxacin dose and increases in QTc interval; therefore the recommended dose and the recommended IV infusion rate should not be exceeded. Gatifloxacin should be used with caution when given concurrently with drugs that prolong the QT interval such as cisapride, erythromycin, pimozide, antipsychotics, and tricyclic antidepressants. Gatifloxacin should also be used with caution in patients with ongoing pro-arrhythmic conditions (e.g., hypokalemia, significant bradycardia, congestive heart failure, myocardial ischemia, and atrial fibrillation). QT prolongation may increase the risk of developing ventricular arrhythmias including torsade de pointes; rare cases of torsade de pointes have been reported, primarily in elderly patients receiving concomitant medications known to cause QT prolongation. No cardiovascular morbidity or deaths have been reported due to gatifloxacin-associated QT prolongation.
 
Gatifloxacin has not been studied in patients with severe hepatic disease. Therefore gatifloxacin should be used cautiously in these patients.
 
Gatifloxacin is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. Animal data suggest that there is no embryolethal or teratogenic effects, however, fetotoxic effects were observed. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women. Therefore, gatifloxacin should be used during pregnancy only when the potential benefit outweighs the potential risk to the fetus.
 
The safe and effective use of systemic gatifloxacin has not been established in children and adolescents below the age of 18 years. Systemic fluoroquinolones, including gatifloxacin, cause arthropathy and osteochondrosis in juvenile animals of several species. Evidence supporting sustained injury to developing joints in humans is lacking at this time; however, the possibility of rare occurrences has not been excluded. One retrospective study compared the rate of tendon or joint disorders in > 7,000 children 20,000 children who received azithromycin. The incidence of potential tendon or joint disorders was found to be approximately 2% in both the fluoroquinolone and azithromycin groups, and verified disorders was reported in < 1% in both groups. The authors state that this incidence is likely to reflect the background incidence of these disorders in children. Due to concerns of increasing bacterial resistance and the possibility of rare joint injury, the American Academy of Pediatrics Committee on Infectious Diseases recommends reserving the use of systemic fluoroquinolones for infections caused by multidrug-resistant pathogens for which there is no safe and effective alternative and for instances when parenteral therapy is not feasible and no other effective oral agent is available. Topical ocular administration of gatifloxacin has not been associated with arthropathy. The safe and effective use of ophthalmic gatifloxacin has not been established in infants less than 1 year old.
 
There are no data on the pharmacokinetics of gatifloxacin in lactating women. It is not known whether this drug is excreted in human milk. Gatifloxacin has a low molecular weight and is expected to be excreted in human breast milk. Fluoroquinolones have been associated with arthropathy in juvenile animals of several species. The manufacturer states that because of the potential for serious adverse effects from gatifloxacin in nursing infants, a decision should be made to discontinue breast-feeding or discontinue administration of the drug, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother. Two other fluoroquinolones, ciprofloxacin and ofloxacin, are considered to be compatible with breast-feeding by the American Academy of Pediatrics.
 
Phototoxicity reactions have been observed in patients who were exposed to direct sunlight or tanning booths while receiving some quinolones. The potential for gatifloxacin to cause photosensitivity was compared to that of ciprofloxacin and lomefloxacin in a placebo controlled study. Based on the results of the study, gatifloxacin had a lower potential for producing delayed photosensitivity skin reactions than ciprofloxacin or lomefloxacin (manufacturer product literature). Nonetheless, patients should avoid excessive sunlight (UV) exposure and therapy should be discontinued if phototoxicity occurs.
 
Quinolones should be used cautiously in patients with myasthenia gravis. Quinolones may exacerbate the signs of myasthenia gravis and lead to life threatening weakness of the respiratory muscles.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  During clinical trials of gatifloxacin, the majority of adverse reactions were described as mild in nature. Approximately 3% of patients discontinued gatifloxacin therapy due to adverse reactions. The most frequently reported (>= 3%) adverse reactions classified as possibly, probably, or definitely related to gatifloxacin therapy included diarrhea, dizziness, headache, nausea/vomiting, and vaginitis. Adverse reactions were similar for both oral and intravenous gatifloxacin. Local injection site reactions (redness at injection site) were noted in 5% of patients. Other adverse reactions considered clinically relevant and reported in >= 0.1% to 450 msec. Increased intracranial pressure, and toxic psychosis have been reported in patients receiving quinolones. Quinolones may also cause central nervous system stimulation that may lead to tremors, restlessness, lightheadedness, confusion, hallucinations, paranoia, depression, nightmares, and insomnia. These reactions may occur following the first dose. If these reactions occur in patients receiving gatifloxacin, the drug should be discontinued and appropriate treatment measures instituted. As with other quinolones, gatifloxacin should be used with caution in patients with a known or suspected CNS disorder (e.g., seizure disorder, severe cerebrovascular disease), or in the presence of other risk factors (e.g., certain drug therapy, renal dysfunction) that may predispose patients to seizures or lower seizure threshold.Disturbances in glucose homeostasis, including symptomatic hyper- and hypoglycemia, have been reported with gatifloxacin, usually in diabetic patients. The manufacturer reports that studies in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus controlled on oral hypoglycemic agents show that gatifloxacin causes disturbances in glucose homeostasis including an increase in serum insulin and decrease in serum glucose. Increases in fasting serum glucose were also observed, usually after the third day of therapy, continuing throughout treatment, and returning to baseline by 28 days after stopping gatifloxacin in most patients. Post-marketing reports have included both hypo- and hyperglycemic events and pancreatitis. These events include hyperosmolar non-ketotic hyperglycemic coma, diabetic ketoacidosis, hypoglycemic coma, seizures, and mental status changes (including loss of consciousness). Hypoglycemic episodes, in some cases severe, have occurred in diabetic patients being treated with either sulfonylurea or non-sulfonylurea oral antidiabetic agents. These events usually occurred on the first day of therapy and usually within 3 days after starting therapy. In a nested case-control study comparing gatifloxacin with levofloxacin in hospitalized patients, gatifloxacin was associated with a greater risk of causing hypoglycemia. This risk remained even after adjusting for other hypoglycemic risk factors (e.g., concomitant hypoglycemic drugs, renal failure, sepsis syndrome). Another retrospective study of inpatients reported no difference in the rates of glucose abnormalities between patients receiving gatifloxacin or levofloxacin. Hyperglycemic episodes, in some cases severe and associated with hyperosmolar non-ketotic hyperglycemic coma, have also been reported in diabetic patients, mostly between 4 and 10 days following initiation of gatifloxacin. Some of the hyperglycemic and hypoglycemic episodes were life-threatening and many required hospitalization. Most of these events were reversible when appropriately managed, however, a few resulted in fatalities. Underlying medical problems and concomitant medications may have contributed to the glucose abnormality. Hyperglycemia, including hyperosmolar non-ketotic hyperglycemic coma, was reported in patients not previously diagnosed with diabetes mellitus. If signs and symptoms of either hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia occur in any patient being treated with gatifloxacin, appropriate therapy must be initiated and gatifloxacin discontinued.Fluoroquinolones have been associated with an increased risk of tendonitis and tendon rupture requiring surgical repair or resulting in prolonged disability. A significant number of cases have been reported, including cases in the US, in patients ranging from 33 to 85 years old. Discontinue gatifloxacin at the first sign of tendon inflammation or tendon pain as these are symptoms that may precede rupture of the tendon. Ruptures have occurred unilaterally and bilaterally, and have involved the Achilles tendon and shoulder joint and hand tendons. The risk of tendon rupture is further increased in those over age 60, those receiving concomitant steroid therapy, and in kidney, heart, and lung transplant recipients. Other reasons for tendon ruptures include physical activity or exercise, kidney failure, tendon problems in the past.Treatment with an antibacterial agent, including gatifloxacin, can alter the normal flora of the colon and allow overgrowth of Clostridium difficile. Pseudomembranous colitis may develop from a toxin produced by Clostridium difficile. Therefore, this diagnosis should be considered in any patient who presents with diarrhea after administration of gatifloxacin.Serious and occasionally fatal hypersensitivity (anaphylactoid or anaphylactic) reactions have been reported in patients receiving therapy with quinolones, often following the first dose. Some reactions have been accompanied by cardiovascular collapse, hypotension, shock, seizures, loss of consciousness, paresthesias (e.g., tingling), angioedema (including tongue edema/swelling, laryngeal edema/swelling, throat edema/swelling, or facial edema/swelling, etc.), airway obstruction (including bronchospasm, shortness of breath, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), dyspnea, urticaria/hives, pruritus, and other serious skin reactions. Only a few patients had a history of prior hypersensitivity reaction to quinolones. Allergic reactions of varying severity, including anaphylactic shock and anaphylactoid reactions, have occurred in patients receiving gatifloxacin. Gatifloxacin should be discontinued if an allergic reaction or any other sign of hypersensitivity appears. Serious acute hypersensitivity reactions require immediate treatment.Serious and sometimes fatal adverse reactions of uncertain etiology have been reported during quinolone therapy. Serious adverse reactions are extremely rare and generally occur following multiple doses. Clinical manifestations may include one or more of the following: fever, rash (unspecified), severe dermatologic reactions (e.g., toxic epidermal necrolysis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, etc.); arthralgia, myalgia, serum sickness, vasculitis; allergic pneumonitis; interstitial nephritis, acute renal failure (unspecified) or insufficiency; hepatitis, jaundice, acute hepatic necrosis/hepatic failure; tendon pain, inflammation or rupture; anemia (including hemolytic anemia and aplastic anemia), thrombocytopenia, including thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), leukopenia, agranulocytosis, pancytopenia, and/or other hematologic abnormalities. Gatifloxacin should be discontinued immediately at the first appearance of any such reaction and appropriate intervention should be instituted. Cases of gatifloxacin-induced hepatic failure and hepatitis have been reported in the literature. The potential for gatifloxacin to cause photosensitivity was compared to that of ciprofloxacin and lomefloxacin in a placebo controlled study. Based on the results of the study, gatifloxacin had a lower potential for producing delayed photosensitivity skin reactions than ciprofloxacin or lomefloxacin.The most frequently reported adverse reactions after ophthalmic administration occurred in approximately 5—10% of patients and included ocular irritation, increased secretion of tears (lacrimation), corneal inflammation (ocular inflammation), keratitis, and papillary conjunctivitis. Other reported adverse reactions occurring in 1—4% of patients included chemosis, conjunctival hemorrhage (ocular hemorrhage), xerophthalmia, ocular discharge, ocular pain, blepharitis, headache, red eye, visual impairment (reduced visual acuity), and taste disturbance (dysgeusia).

Description
  Text
  Gatifloxacin is an ophthalmic fluoroquinolone antibiotic. It belongs to a group of quinolones known as 8-methoxy fluoroquinolones. Gatifloxacin is effective against both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. Compared to other fluoroquinolones, gatifloxacin and other 8-methoxyfluoroquinolones have markedly improved activity against penicillin-susceptible and -resistant pneumococci. Gatifloxacin ophthalmic solution is indicated for the treatment of bacterial conjunctivitis in adults and pediatric patients 1 year and older.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Gatifloxacin is bactericidal via inhibition of DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II), an enzyme that is involved in the replication, transcription, and repair of bacterial DNA, as well as topoisomerase IV, an enzyme that helps separate the daughter DNA molecules. In gram-negative bacteria, the primary target is the DNA gyrase A subunit, while the primary target in gram-positive bacteria is generally topoisomerase IV.
