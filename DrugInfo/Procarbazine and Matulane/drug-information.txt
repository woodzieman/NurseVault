Drug Class: Procarbazine is an oral, cell cycle-phase specific antineoplastic agent used in the treatment of Hodgkin lymphoma, non-Hodgkin's lymphomas, brain tumors, and lung cancers.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 6 mg/kg/day PO as a single agent or 100 mg/m2/day PO in combination with other chemotherapy agents.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): anemia, eosinophilia, hemolytic anemia, infection, leukopenia, pancytopenia, thrombocytopenia, hematemesis, ataxia, coma, confusion, dizziness, drowsiness, headache, hyporeflexia, nystagmus, paresthesias, peripheral neuropathy, seizures, tremor, edema, hypotension, sinus tachycardia, syncope, cough, hoarseness, pleural effusion, pneumonitis, azoospermia, gynecomastia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Procarbazine should only be given by or under the supervision of a physician experienced in the use of potent antineoplastic drugs.         Dosage not established. The CAMP regimen (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, methotrexate, procarbazine) has been studied in randomized clinical studies. Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Procarbazine should only be given by or under the supervision of a physician experienced in the use of potent antineoplastic drugs. Adequate clinical and laboratory facilities should be available to patients for proper monitoring of treatment. Procarbazine is contraindicated in patients with severe bone marrow suppression (inadequate marrow reserve), as demonstrated by bone marrow aspiration. Consider the possibility of an inadequate marrow reserve in a patient with leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, or anemia. Patients with preexisting bone marrow suppression, including neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, should be allowed to recover their counts prior to procarbazine administration. Procarbazine therapy should not be given to patients with neutrophil counts 40 mg/dl and/or serum creatinine > 2 mg/dl) or hepatic function (e.g., jaundice, serum bilirubin > 3 mg/dl). Renal and hepatic function should be evaluated prior to therapy; urinalysis, transaminase, alkaline phosphatase, and blood urea nitrogen/creatinine should be repeated at least weekly during therapy.
 
Procarbazine has weak monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitor activity and can affect blood pressure. Do not use procarbazine along with MAOI therapy (see Drug Interactions). Cautious use of procarbazine is warranted in patients with a pheochromocytoma. Pheochromocytomas secrete pressor substances that may alter blood pressure during therapy. Patients with cardiac disease, coronary artery disease (e.g., angina), or cardiac arrhythmias may be aggravated by decreases in blood pressure. In addition, patients who have undergone sympathectomy may be more sensitive to the hypotensive effects. Similarly, patients with cerebrovascular disease may aggravated cerebral ischemia as a result of reduced blood pressure. Patients with thyroid disease, specifically hyperthyroidism, may have increased sensitivity to the pressor amines.
 
Patients should be advised to discontinue tobacco smoking during procarbazine therapy. Tobacco use during procarbazine therapy has been associated with an increased risk of secondary lung cancer. Results of a case-controlled study in patients with Hodgkin's disease receiving MOPP combination chemotherapy suggest an increased risk of lung cancer in a dose-dependent fashion, which is multiplied with exposure to tobacco smoke.
 
Patients with G6PD deficiency are at increased risk for developing hemolysis during procarbazine therapy.
 
Procarbazine can change the pattern of epileptiform seizures in patients with a seizure disorder. Parkinson's disease may be aggravated by procarbazine therapy. Patients with paranoid schizophrenia or other hyperexcitable personality states (e.g., mania, bipolar disorder) should use caution because the MAO inhibition by procarbazine may cause excessive CNS stimulation or swing manic-depressive patients into manic episodes.
 
Procarbazine is a known teratogen and should not be used during pregnancy (FDA pregnancy risk category D). Females should avoid becoming pregnant during procarbazine therapy. Both male and female patients should use barrier contraception when either is taking procarbazine because of the drug's effects on germ cell lines. Both male and female patients desiring to conceive should be counseled regarding the possibility of infertility and should discuss alternatives for adequate contraception during and following procarbazine therapy.
 
Although it is unknown whether procarbazine is excreted into breast milk, its use during breast-feeding is generally not recommended due to its carcinogenic and mutagenic effects.
 
Procarbazine treatment should be individualized for use in children. Undue toxicity (e.g., tremors, coma, and seizures) has occurred in some cases. Very close clinical monitoring is required.
 
Vaccination during chemotherapy or radiation therapy should be avoided because the antibody response is suboptimal. When chemotherapy is being planned, vaccination should precede the initiation of chemotherapy by >= 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    bone marrow suppression, serious adverse effects
    General Information
    Procarbazine may cause serious adverse effects. Administration should only be given by or under the supervision of a physician experienced in the use of potent antineoplastic drugs and requires a specialized care setting with adequate clinical and laboratory facilities available for proper monitoring. Procarbazine is contraindicated in patients with severe bone marrow suppression (inadequate marrow reserve), as demonstrated by bone marrow aspiration. Consider the possibility of an inadequate marrow reserve in a patient with leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, or anemia. It is recommended to delay the start of procarbazine treatment for 1 month or longer in patients who have had previous myelosuppressive therapy such as radiation therapy or chemotherapy.
    Headline
    ethanol ingestion, hepatic failure, renal failure, renal impairment, tyramine ingestion
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid ethanol ingestion with procarbazine therapy since there may be a disulfiram-like reaction. Advise patients to avoid tyramine ingestion (e.g., foods such as wine, yogurt, ripe cheese, bananas) due to some monoamine oxidase inhibitory activity of procarbazine. Use procarbazine with caution in patients with preexisting hepatic impairment/hepatic failure and renal impairment/renal failure; drug accumulation in these patients may result in increased toxicity. Evaluate renal and hepatic function at baseline and monitor at least weekly during therapy.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Procarbazine may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy. Advise patients to avoid becoming pregnant while taking procarbazine. If used during pregnancy or pregnancy occurs during procarbazine treatment, discuss the potential hazards to the fetus with the pregnant individual. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of procarbazine use during pregnancy. However, there have been case reports of teratogenicity, including malformations in offspring, when procarbazine was administered in combination with other chemotherapy agents during pregnancy. Teratogenicity was reported in rats when pregnant animals received procarbazine at doses that were 4 to 13 times higher than the recommended human dose of 6 mg/kg/day. Neurogenic tumors were observed in the offspring of rats following procarbazine 125 mg/kg IV on day 22 of gestation.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking procarbazine. There are no data on the presence of procarbazine in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. However, procarbazine has the potential to cause serious side effects, including tumorigenicity, in the breastfed child.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with procarbazine. Azoospermia and infertility effects have been reported in patients with Hodgkin disease treated with procarbazine in combination with other chemotherapy agents. It is unclear to what extent procarbazine was involved in the male germ cell damage.

Caffeine may produce hypertension or hypertensive crisis or induce cardiac arrhythmias if administered to patients taking drugs with strong MAOI properties.   Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The major cytotoxic effects of procarbazine are most evident in the more rapidly proliferating tissues such as the lining of the GI tract and the bone marrow. Bone marrow suppression, resulting in pancytopenia with leukopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia, can occur. The nadir for platelets is around week 4. Anemia and neutropenia recover around week 4 to 6. As with other hydrazine derivatives, hemolysis may occur with the appearance of Heinz-Ehrlich inclusion bodies in erythrocytes. Bleeding symptoms including petechiae, purpura, epistaxis, and hemoptysis have also been reported. Myelosuppression has not been reported following high dose intravenous or continuous infusion administration of procarbazine.Procarbazine has potent immunosuppressive effects. Procarbazine-induced immunosuppression has been used therapeutically for the treatment of autoimmune disorders and in stem cell transplant preparative regimens.Adverse oral and GI symptoms associated with procarbazine toxicity include nausea/vomiting, diarrhea, anorexia, and stomatitis. Nausea and vomiting are the most common adverse reactions and may be lessened by titrating the dose to the desired level and administering the daily dosage amount in divided doses. Concurrent ingestion of alcohol during procarbazine therapy may result in a disulfiram-like reaction (including severe nausea and vomiting). Severe diarrhea or ulcerative stomatitis may occur during procarbazine therapy leading to hemorrhagic enteritis/GI bleeding or GI perforation, and possibly leading to death; procarbazine therapy should be discontinued until these reactions resolve. Other GI adverse reactions include hepatic dysfunction (e.g., elevated hepatic enzymes, jaundice), hematemesis, melena, dysphagia, abdominal pain, constipation, and xerostomia (dry mouth). Following intravenous administration of single high-dose (i.e., 2 g/m2) bolus infusions or 5-day continuous infusions, patients experienced severe nausea and vomiting in addition to neurotoxicity. Following small intermittent intravenous dosing, the toxicity is similar to oral administration without offering additional clinical benefit.Many adverse CNS effects have occurred in patients during procarbazine therapy including paresthesias, peripheral neuropathy, nystagmus, depression, mania, acute exogenous psychosis, hallucinations, headache, dizziness, nervousness or restlessness, apprehension, nightmares, insomnia, ataxia, tremor, coma, confusion, delirium, and seizures. Procarbazine therapy should be discontinued if neuropathy, paresthesia, or confusion develops. CNS effects can occur in as many as 30% of patients. Patients should be counseled in advance regarding these effects. Following intravenous administration, neurotoxicity is more pronounced and is dose-limiting. After single high-dose intravenous bolus infusions (2 g/m2) or 5-day continuous infusions of procarbazine, patients experienced severe nausea, vomiting, confusion, and coma lasting several days.Cardiac adverse effects can occur with procarbazine therapy. Reported cardiac adverse reactions include hypotension, sinus tachycardia, and syncope. Severe hypertension or hypertensive crisis is a serious but rare reaction probably related to the MAO inhibitor activity of procarbazine. Orthostatic hypotension has also been reported.Pulmonary adverse reactions during procarbazine therapy include pneumonitis, pleural effusion, and cough. Development of interstitial pneumonitis usually necessitates discontinuation of the drug. Procarbazine-induced fever can occur and, in at least one case (in an 8-year-old child), fulminant hyperpyrexia occurred, accompanied by tremors, cyanosis, dyspnea, palpitations, and severe emesis.Procarbazine causes gonadal suppression and toxicity including amenorrhea and anovulation in women, azoospermia in men, and infertility in both men and women. Although some of these effects may be reversible, over 90% of males treated with MOPP chemotherapy experience irreversible azoospermia and 50% of women have permanent drug-induced ovarian failure. In women who regain normal ovarian function following MOPP chemotherapy, there seems to be no impairment in fertility or increased incidence of birth defects. Gynecomastia in prepubertal and early pubertal boys has been reported.Procarbazine is a known carcinogen and can cause secondary malignancy, specifically acute leukemia and lung cancer, in patients with Hodgkin's disease treated with procarbazine in combination with other chemotherapy and/or radiation. The risk of secondary lung cancer appears to be multiplied by tobacco use. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) considers there is sufficient evidence for human carcinogenicity of procarbazine when it is given in intensive regimens that include other antineoplastic agents but that there is inadequate evidence of carcinogenicity in humans when procarbazine is given alone.Constitutional symptoms, such as fever, chills, diaphoresis, fatigue, flushing, myalgia, and arthralgia, can occur with procarbazine therapy. Other adverse reactions reported during procarbazine therapy include infection, herpes infection, dermatitis, pruritus, alopecia, skin hyperpigmentation, rash (unspecified), eosinophilia, urticaria, generalized allergic reactions (anaphylactoid reactions), weakness, edema, and slurred speech (dysarthria).Ocular toxicity including retinal hemorrhage, papilledema, photophobia, diplopia, and inability to focus have been reported during procarbazine therapy.Teratogenesis has been reported in infants whose mothers received procarbazine with other chemotherapy agents during pregnancy. In addition, tumors were reported in offspring of animals who were exposed to procarbazine during pregnancy.

Description
  Text
  Procarbazine is an oral, cell cycle-phase specific antineoplastic agent used in the treatment of Hodgkin lymphoma, non-Hodgkin's lymphomas, brain tumors, and lung cancers. It has been described as a nonclassic alkylating agent and is not cross-resistant with the other alkylating agents. Today, it is used almost exclusively for Hodgkin lymphoma. Procarbazine originally was synthesized as a monoamine oxidase inhibitor, and this activity, as well as a disulfiram-like reaction, are part of its unusual adverse effect profile. Procarbazine was approved by the FDA in 1969.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: Procarbazine must be activated by chemical decomposition or by microsomal oxidation via the hepatic cytochrome P-450 enzyme system to exert its cytotoxic effects. Several active metabolites can form including species capable of binding to DNA and free radicals. Procarbazine's exact mechanism(s) of action is not clear, but the drug appears to have several sites of action in the cell. Hydrogen peroxide and formaldehyde are generated from the procarbazine activation and are thought to interact with DNA, resulting in cytotoxicity. Procarbazine and its metabolites are capable of causing chromatid and single DNA strand breaks in animal models. The number of breaks depends on the dose and time elapsed after treatment. Procarbazine also inhibits DNA, RNA, and protein synthesis. Procarbazine may inhibit the incorporation of thymidine, deoxycytidine, formate, adenine, and 4-amino-5-imidazolecarboxamide into DNA, prevent the utilization of orotic acid in RNA synthesis, and prevent the utilization of leucine in the synthesis of proteins. However, the major mechanism of procarbazine cytotoxicity may be methylation of DNA due to the high levels of O6-methylguanine, a known mutagenic and carcinogenic adduct, reported in animal models. The drug appears to be most active during the G2 phase. Resistance to procarbazine develops rapidly after exposure to the drug. One mechanism of resistance to procarbazine is O6-alkylguanine-DNA alkyltransferase (AGAT)-mediated repair of O6-methylguanine. Other mechanisms of drug resistance are possible.Procarbazine exhibits weak monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitor activity; it appears the risk for serious drug interactions is not as great for procarbazine as for traditional MAOIs like phenelzine or tranylcypromine. When MAO is blocked, octopamine (a false neurotransmitter) accumulates diluting the usual potent neurotransmitters. This resulting sympathetic blockade lowers blood pressure, thus producing an antihypertensive action. Conversely, inhibition of MAO in the GI tract and liver can cause systemic absorption of large amounts of tyramine, which can result in hypertension because of excessive release of norepinephrine.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Procarbazine is rapidly and completely absorbed across the GI tract following oral administration. Procarbazine undergoes significant first-pass metabolism via the cytochrome P450 system or mitochondrial monoamine oxidase enzymatic conversion to form azoprocarbazine, which is then further metabolized intracellularly to cytotoxic metabolites. Following oral administration, equilibration between the plasma and CSF occurs within 15—30 minutes.
    Name
    Intravenous Route
    Description
    Procarbazine is not given intravenously. Intravenous administration of procarbazine results in significantly less clinical activity and a different spectrum of toxicity, demonstrating the importance of the first-pass metabolism for activation of procarbazine. In addition, procarbazine is not stable in aqueous solution and breaks down, albeit slowly, in the presence of light to the biologically inactive hydrazone.
  Text
  Procarbazine is given orally. The plasma half-life of procarbazine is about 7 minutes indicating rapid and extensive hepatic metabolism. Data in humans show that the methylazoxy isomer is the major plasma metabolite present after a single 250 mg oral dose of procarbazine. The plasma concentration of this metabolite peaks at approximately 90 minutes and seems to have an initial plasma half-life of 60 minutes. Other metabolites present at lesser serum concentrations than the methylazoxy isomer are azoprocarbazine and the benzylazoxy isomer. Procarbazine may induce its own metabolism as evidenced by a significant increase in azoprocarbazine concentrations following day 14 of dosing. It is unclear if procarbazine enzyme induction results in clinically significant drug interactions with other drugs. Procarbazine metabolites are eliminated renally; N-isopropylterephthalamic acid is the major urinary metabolite, which is inactive. Approximately 70% of a procarbazine dose is excreted in the urine within 24 hours. There is minimal fecal excretion (4—12% over 96 hours).

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Although specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; reduced doses are recommended to avoid excessive toxicity.
  Renal Impairment
  Although specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available; reduced doses are recommended to avoid excessive toxicity in patients with a BUN > 40 mg/dl and/or serum creatinine > 2 mg/dl.
