Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Echinacea has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. Products containing echinacea are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Consumers should also be informed that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.Allergic reactions to echinacea, though infrequent, may be severe. Patients with a history of atopy or eczema may have a greater likelihood of developing severe or life-threatening reactions to echinacea. Allergic reactions to echinacea may also occur in subjects with known allergies to other plants of the Asteraceae family (formerly Compositae). Members include ragweed, chrysanthemums, marigolds, daisies, many other herbs (e.g., chamomile, feverfew or milk thistle), and the pyrethrins.Due to a lack of scientific data and an unknown potential for side effects, echinacea is not recommended for use in infants or young children without the approval and observation of a health care professional. Data from one controlled study suggest that echinacea treatment is ineffective in children.Due to a lack of scientific data and an unknown potential for side effects, echinacea should be used with caution in the elderly due to limited study in this patient population.If an adult patient decides to self-treat cold or influenza symptoms with echinacea, it is recommended that use not exceed 2 weeks without consultation of a health care professional. A potential problem with self-treatment could be delay in detection of a more serious infection or respiratory problem. Self-treatment should not occur with illnesses associated with persistent fever > 101 degrees F, discolored or bloody sputum, concurrent sinus or otic infection symptoms, pleuritic pain, or shortness of breath. The German Commission E recommends that any use of echinacea be limited to <= 8 weeks duration.The German Commission E does not list pregnancy as a contraindication to echinacea use. However, it is unknown what effect echinacea would have on a developing human fetus. A case-control study suggests that short-term use is not associated with malformations or other adverse fetal effects, but the data are still quite limited. Echinacea should be used cautiously during pregnancy. Women who are pregnant are advised to take echinacea only under the advice of a qualified health care professional.Echinacea should be used with caution in breast-feeding. It is unknown if echinacea is excreted in human breast milk. Women who are lactating are advised to take echinacea only under the advice of a qualified health care professional.Limited in vitro data suggests that echinacea may inhibit fertility in couples trying to conceive, use with caution in patients experiencing problems with infertility (see Adverse Reactions).Echinacea should be used with caution by persons with severe systemic illnesses such as pulmonary disease, neoplastic disease, or immunosuppression. Echinacea could potentially influence the progression of such diseases or interfere with the treatment of these conditions. There is concern that prolonged use of echinacea may suppress the immune response. The German Commission E considers the use of echinacea contraindicated in patients with chronic, serious, progressive systemic illnesses, such as acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, tuberculosis, multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and other collagen-vascular or autoimmune disease. The theory is that echinacea, through its immunostimulant properties, could exacerbate autoimmune diseases. Strong clinical evidence to support this theoretical precaution in patients with autoimmune or immunosuppressive disease is lacking; however, there are case reports suggestive of a possible need for precaution (see Adverse Reactions for details). Because the use of echinacea in combination with immunosuppressive therapy would seem counterproductive, caution is also warranted in patients undergoing corticosteroid therapy, including those patients with asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).Echinacea is recommended to be discontinued prior to surgery, especially in those who require perioperative immunosuppressive treatment, such as those awaiting organ transplant. It may be wise to discontinue this herb 2—3 weeks prior to an elective procedure.The German Commission E cautions against the use of echinacea in persons with diabetes mellitus. Parenteral administration of echinacea has apparently resulted in alterations in blood sugar control and other parameters in these patients. Patients with diabetes mellitus should also be cautioned against using topical echinacea on skin ulcerations or poorly healing wounds without the advice and supervision of a qualified health care professional.There is no experience with echinacea use in the presence of renal disease (including renal impairment or renal failure) or hepatic disease. Echinacea should be used with caution in these populations, particularly in the presence of pre-existing hepatic dysfunction.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: Some echinacea preparations, including Esberitox(TM), are a combination of several herbs, and each individual component may need to be evaluated for the presence of adverse reactions. Only adverse reactions pertaining to echinacea are discussed in this monograph.Adverse reactions to herbal products are not always documented. Clinical trials to date have been of short duration and have noted that adverse events occurring with oral administration of echinacea have consisted primarily of gastrointestinal disturbances, including dyspepsia and nausea/vomiting. Description
  Text
  NOTE: In the US, nutraceuticals are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for nutraceuticals as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since nutraceuticals are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for nutraceuticals and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products. Monographs on nutraceuticals are included in CP when reliable clinical data are available. The information presented below is condensed from the best clinical data we could find.
 
Echinacea is a name commonly used to describe herbs derived from the aerial and root portions of several different Echinacea spp., including Echinacea purpurea, Echinacea pallida, and Echinacea angustifolia. Echinacea purpurea, or purple coneflower, has been cultivated in the US for hundreds of years; the Plains Indian tribes used Echinacea purpurea root as a poultice for wounds, insect stings, and snakebites. In the US, echinacea was commonly used as an anti-infective prior to the discovery of antibiotics. In past studies, the poor botanical identification of Echinacea spp. has led some to believe that all Echinacea spp. are interchangeable. However, earlier pharmacologic studies of E. angustifolia have been now been identified as studies of E. pallida, and thus information supporting the clinical use of E. angustifolia is lacking. Clinical data does not support prophylactic use of echinacea in adults; however, in children, echinacea may prevent subsequent upper respiratory tract infections. In one study, the use of a common non-alcoholic extract available in Germany (Echinacin® Saft) reduced the risk of subsequent infection by 28% (P=0.01, 95% CI 8—44%) over a 4-month period. Clinical studies in adults are conflicting; some studies demonstrate efficacy in the alleviation of symptoms and in shortening the duration of upper respiratory infections, while others demonstrate no effect over placebo or a control group. Recently, a clinical trial in experimental rhinovirus infection found that 300 mg three times daily of Echinacea angustifolia is not effective in alleviating the symptoms or shortening the duration of infection. Data on the use of echinacea in children for the treatment of upper respiratory infections are negative. One randomized study in children 2—11 years of age has found Echinacea purpurea to be ineffective in shortening the duration or reducing the severity of upper respiratory infection vs. placebo; the incidence of rash in the treatment group was higher than with placebo. One reason for the conflicting findings in echinacea studies is that many of the studies have lacked statistical power or consistent methodology, including lack of comparable doses or preparations of echinacea. The great variability in herb composition among available studies makes comparisons of products, and thus the study results, difficult. However, a 2005 Cochrane's review indicates that aerial parts of Echinacea purpurea may be effective in providing symptomatic relief and shortening the duration of upper respiratory infections in adults if initiated early in the infection. The review also indicates that while other preparations of echinacea may be effective, evidence of such efficacy is lacking. Similarly, Germany's Commission E has approved only 2 Echinacea spp. oral formulations for clinical use. Echinacea purpurea (fresh plant juice from the aerial portions of the herb) and Echinacea pallida (hydroalcoholic extracts of the root) are recommended for oral use for supportive therapy of colds and influenza-like infections of the respiratory tract. Clinical data suggest that oral hydroalcoholic extracts of the root of Echinacea purpurea will likely also be approved for use in the German Commission E monographs in the future. Topical preparations of Echinacea purpurea are recommended by the Commission E for the external treatment of poorly healing wounds and chronic ulcerations. Esberitox N(TM) is a popular commercial brand of echinacea in Germany, and contains a blend of E. purpurea, E. pallida, and other active herbs. Many oral preparations of echinacea are currently available in the US, including many non-standardized products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The exact identity of all of the active ingredients in echinacea is still being investigated, and a clear mechanism of action has yet to be identified. Heteroxylan, a high molecular weight polysaccharide, and arabinogalactan are known to be active components. Echinacea acts as an immunostimulant by several mechanisms: stimulation of phagocytosis, stimulation of release of cytokines, and inhibition of the activity of hyaluronidase. In vitro studies have shown that heteroxylan activates phagocytes, and arabinogalactan causes the proliferation of T-lymphocytes and promotes the release of tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-1, and interferon-beta 2 from macrophages. Collectively, these properties of echinacea may increase the body's overall level of resistance to infection. It appears that echinacea has no direct bactericidal or bacteriostatic properties.

Pharmacokinetics
  Text
  Echinacea can be administered orally, parenterally, or topically. Precise pharmacokinetic data are not currently available for this herb. Products summarized under the name "echinacea" can be chemically different preparations due to the 3 different Echinacea spp. used in the manufacture of these products. The use of different parts of the plants (e.g., roots vs. aerial portions) and the use of different extraction methods (e.g., pressed juice, alcohol extraction) adds to the difficulty in analyzing products and pharmacokinetic data derived from this herb. Well-designed and controlled studies will be required to determine which product formulations are most effective; such controls will also be needed to derive pharmacokinetic data. Adding to the confusion is the fact that some commercial products have been found to be adulterated with plant extracts that were fraudulently substituted for echinacea.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4, CYP1A2
Echinacea appears to induce drug metabolism mediated by CYP3A4 and may have variable effects on CYP1A2. Drug interactions are most likely relevant when administered with substances with a narrow therapeutic index or low oral bioavailability. Published data are limited; more study is needed.
