Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Isoproterenol is contraindicated in cardiac arrhythmias associated with tachycardia (atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation) because the drug has cardiac-stimulant effects and can exacerbate tachyarrhythmias. One exception to this contraindication is the acute use of isoproterenol for the treatment of torsade de pointes in patients without history of ischemic heart disease until overdrive pacing is available; the American Heart Association (AHA) classifies this use as 'Class Indeterminate', indicating no official recommendation until further evidence is available. Isoproterenol is also contraindicated in patients with tachycardia or AV block due to digitalis toxicity because of the drug's cardiac-stimulant effects, which pose an increased risk of exacerbating the digitalis-induced arrhythmia.
 
Isoproterenol is contraindicated in patients with known sympathomimetic hypersensitivity because they are at risk of developing subsequent allergic reactions including anaphylaxis.
 
Some formulations of isoproterenol contain sulfites; patients with sulfite hypersensitivity should use isoproterenol preparations only after checking with their pharmacist and/or physician because they are at risk of developing immediate hypersensitivity reactions.
 
Isoproterenol stimulates insulin production, increases glycogenolysis in the liver, and can complicate the management of diabetes mellitus.
 
Isoproterenol should be administered with extreme caution to patients with severe and/or uncontrolled hypertension.
 
Because thyroid disease patients with hyperthyroidism can be more sensitive to sympathomimetics, caution must be used when administering this drug to this patient population to avoid thyrotoxic effects.
 
Isoproterenol is contraindicated in patients with angina and should be used with care in patients with severe coronary artery disease (cardiac disease) so as to maintain the appropriate balance of myocardial oxygen supply and demand.
 
Patients with pheochromocytoma should receive isoproterenol or any adrenergic agent with extreme caution, if at all, given the risk for hypertensive crisis or emergency associated with their condition.
 
Insufficient numbers of elderly patients have been studied to determine whether they respond differently to isoproterenol. Some data suggest that elderly healthy or hypertensive patients are less responsive to beta-adrenergic stimulation than are younger subjects. However, dose selection for elderly patients should be cautious, usually starting at the low end of the adult dosing range and should be individualized to desired clinical goals and patient response.
 
Isoproterenol is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. The drug should only be used during pregnancy when clearly needed as the effects of isoproterenol on the fetus have not been studied.
 
Isoproterenol should be administered with caution, if at all, to breast-feeding women as it is not known whether the drug is excreted into breast milk.
 
The safety and efficacy of isoproterenol in children have not been established. Intravenous infusions of isoproterenol in refractory asthmatic children at rates of 0.05—2.7 ug/kg/min have caused clinical deterioration, myocardial necrosis, congestive heart failure and death. The risks of cardiac toxicity appear to be increased by some factors including acidosis (respiratory acidosis or metabolic acidosis), hypoxemia, concomitant corticosteroid therapy, or the coadministration of methylxanthines (e.g., aminophylline or theophylline, see Drug Interactions) that are especially likely to be present in these patients. If intravenous isoproterenol is used in children with refractory asthma, patient monitoring must include continuous assessment of vital signs, frequent electrocardiography, and daily measurements of cardiac enzymes, including CPK-MB.
 
In general, sympathomimetics should be avoided in patients treated with MAOI therapy (see Drug Interactions).

    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    In a few individuals, presumably with organic disease of the AV node and its branches, isoproterenol has been reported to paradoxically worsen heart block or precipitate Adams-Stokes attacks during normal sinus rhythm or transient heart block.
    Headline
    atopy
    General Information
    Some formulations of isoproterenol may contain sodium metabisulfite; a sulfite, which may cause allergic-type reactions including anaphylactic symptoms and life-threatening or less severe asthmatic episodes, particularly in individuals with a history of allergies (atopy). However, the presence of metabisulfite in isoproterenol formulations does not preclude its use for treatment in emergency situations, even if the individual is sulfite-sensitive. Alternatives to isoproterenol in a life-threatening situation may not be effective.
    Headline
    angina, arteriosclerosis, cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, digitalis toxicity-induced AV block, tachycardia
    General Information
    Isoproterenol is contraindicated in individuals with angina, tachycardia, including ventricular arrhythmias requiring inotropic therapy, and digitalis toxicity-induced AV block or tachycardia. Isoproterenol may induce cardiac arrhythmias and myocardial ischemia, especially in individuals with coronary artery disease or cardiomyopathy. Doses sufficient to increase the heart rate to more than 130 beats per minute may increase the likelihood of inducing ventricular arrhythmias and will also tend to increase cardiac work and oxygen requirements, adversely affecting individuals with heart failure or a heart with a significant degree of arteriosclerosis. Additionally, isoproterenol is usually not recommended as an initial agent in treating cardiogenic shock after myocardial infarction; however, isoproterenol may produce beneficial hemodynamic and metabolic effects when a low arterial pressure has been elevated by other means. Isoproterenol is recommended to increase the heart rate and abolish post ectopic pauses in individuals with recurrent torsade de pointes associated with acquired QT prolongation and bradycardia that cannot be suppressed with intravenous magnesium.
    Headline
    asthma, children, hypoxemia, metabolic acidosis, respiratory acidosis
    General Information
    If intravenous isoproterenol is used in children with refractory asthma, monitor vital signs continually, electrocardiography frequently, and cardiac enzymes, including CPK-MB, daily. Clinical deterioration, myocardial necrosis, heart failure, and death have occurred in refractory asthmatic children receiving isoproterenol. The risks of cardiac toxicity appear to be increased by some factors (e.g., respiratory acidosis, metabolic acidosis, hypoxemia, coadministration of methylxanthines or corticosteroid therapy) likely to be present in such individuals.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Some data suggest that geriatric adults are less responsive to beta-adrenergic stimulation than younger individuals. In general, initiate isoproterenol at the low end of the dosing range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function and concomitant diseases or other drug therapy in geriatric adults.
    Headline
    labor, obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    Prolonged experience with the use of isoproterenol during pregnancy over several decades has not identified a drug associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. However, isoproterenol use during labor or obstetric delivery is associated with risks. Isoproterenol inhibits spontaneous or oxytocin-induced contractions of the human uterus and may delay the second stage of labor. Avoid isoproterenol use during the second stage of labor and in obstetrics when the patient's blood pressure exceeds 130/80 mmHg. Although isoproterenol may improve hypotension associated with shock, it may also result in uterine vasoconstriction, decreased uterine blood flow, uterine atony with hemorrhage, and fetal anoxia. Hypotension associated with shock is a medical emergency that can be fatal if left untreated; do not withhold life-sustaining therapy for the pregnant individual due to potential concerns about the effects of isoproterenol on the fetus. Delaying treatment may increase the risk of morbidity and mortality for both the pregnant individual and the fetus.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use isoproterenol with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of isoproterenol in human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effects on milk production. However, due to its short half-life, isoproterenol exposure is expected to be very low in the breast-fed child. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breast-fed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition.

  General Information
  CNS effects, such as nervousness, restlessness, insomnia, anxiety, diaphoresis, weakness, dizziness, mild tremor, lightheadedness, and headache, can occur during therapy with isoproterenol but generally require treatment only if prolonged.As an extension of its beta-adrenergic agonist effects (including sinus tachycardia, positive inotropic effects, hypotension), isoproterenol can cause cardiac arrhythmias (or arrhythmia exacerbation), especially in doses elevating the heart rate above 130 beats/min. Description
  Text
  Isoproterenol is a parenteral nonselective beta-adrenergic agonist indicated for mild or transient episodes of heart block that do not require electric shock or pacemaker therapy, serious episodes of heart block and Adams-Stokes attacks (except when caused by ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation), cardiac arrest until electric shock or pacemaker therapy is available, bronchospasm occurring during anesthesia, and as an adjunct treatment of hypovolemic and septic shock, low cardiac output states, congestive heart failure, and cardiogenic shock. It is a synthetic sympathomimetic amine that is structurally related to epinephrine but acts almost exclusively on beta receptors. In general, use is limited to patients refractory to other inotropic agents. Although not a first-line agent, isoproterenol may be used for bradyarrhythmias unresponsive to or inappropriate for treatment with atropine or while awaiting the availability of a pacemaker. In patients with sinus node dysfunction or second- or third-degree atrioventricular block associated with symptoms of hemodynamic compromise who are at a low likelihood of coronary ischemia, isoproterenol may be considered to increase heart rate, improve atrioventricular conduction, increase ventricular rate, and improve symptoms.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Isoproterenol is a potent agonist of both beta1- and beta2-adrenergic receptors, with minimal to no effects on alpha-adrenergic receptors at therapeutic doses. Isoproterenol stimulates beta-receptors throughout the body except for the sweat glands and facial arteries. Intracellularly, the actions of isoproterenol are mediated by cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), the production of which is augmented by beta stimulation. Consequently, isoproterenol stimulates myocardial performance primarily via stimulation of beta1-receptors on the myocardium and conducting system of the heart. The stimulation of these receptors results in increased inotropic and chronotropic effects. Systolic blood pressure is usually elevated due to increased inotropy, although diastolic blood pressure is decreased secondary to isoproterenol-induced vasodilation. As a result, pulse pressure is increased. Isoproterenol indirectly causes coronary artery vasodilation. Despite the increase in myocardial oxygen supply that can occur secondary to coronary vasodilation, the positive inotropic and chronotropic effects on the heart result in increased oxygen consumption, particularly relative to cardiac work, increased myocardial excitability, and automaticity. These latter cardiac effects of isoproterenol markedly increase the potential for developing dysrhythmias.
 
The major therapeutic effects of systemic isoproterenol include bronchial smooth muscle relaxation, cardiac stimulation, vasodilation in skeletal or GI muscle, and stimulation of glycogenolysis in the liver and other calorigenic mechanisms such as the release of free fatty acids. Isoproterenol also induces the release of insulin, which offsets the hyperglycemia that follows glycogenolysis. This activity is somewhat unique among other adrenergic compounds, particularly epinephrine. Isoproterenol's effects on smooth muscle are varied and determined by relative receptor density and hormonal effects. Isoproterenol exerts its relaxant effect on bronchial smooth muscle primarily via stimulation of beta2-receptors. Beta2 stimulation also prevents mast cell secretion of histamine and other autocoids, thus antagonizing its effect on end organs and reversing bronchoconstriction and edema.

    Pediatrics
    Description
    Infants and Children
The pharmacokinetics of isoproterenol were described in a study of infants and children (n = 19) with reactive airway disease or postoperative cardiac dysrhythmias who received the drug intravenously. Vd was 216 +/- 57 mg/kg. The steady-state plasma concentration (normalized to a rate of 0.05 mcg/kg/minute) was 1.9 +/- 0.3 ng/mL. The average plasma half-life was 4.2 +/- 1.5 minutes. The average clearance rate was 42.5 +/- 5 mg/kg/minute; postoperative cardiac patients had a lower clearance rate compared to airway disease patients (33.2 +/- 4.9 mg/kg/minute vs. 48.4 +/- 7.3 mg/kg/minute). This lower clearance rate may be due to diminished cardiac function and perfusion to the liver and kidneys or nonlinear pharmacokinetics.
  Text
  Isoproterenol is administered intravenously. Isoproterenol is rapidly metabolized in the liver and other tissues by catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) to the 3-O-methyl isoproterenol metabolite, which is subsequently conjugated with sulfate. It is a relatively poor substrate for monoamine oxidase (MAO). Excretion occurs via the urine. Half-life is 2.5 to 5 minutes.
