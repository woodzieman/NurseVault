Drug Class: Sodium oxybate is the sodium salt of gamma hydroxybutyric acid (GHB), a naturally occurring central nervous system transmitter with sedative and anesthetic properties.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 9 grams/night PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): complex sleep-related behaviors, dizziness, drowsiness, headache, memory impairment, obtundation, paresthesias, sleep paralysis, somnambulism, tremor, agitation, anxiety, confusion, depression, emotional lability, hallucinations, impaired cognition, irritability, paranoia, psychosis, suicidal ideation, weight loss, arthralgia, cataplexy, muscle cramps, blurred vision, edema, hyperhidrosis, hypertension, nocturia

In an Overdose: In clinical trials with sodium oxybate, two overdoses were reported. In the second case, death was reported following a multiple drug overdose consisting of sodium oxybate and numerous other drugs.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Sodium oxybate should be avoided in any patient with known hypersensitivity to the drug. Sodium oxybate is contraindicated in patients with succinic semialdehyde dehydrogenase deficiency.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Sodium oxybate should be avoided in any patient with known hypersensitivity to the drug.
 
Sodium oxybate is contraindicated in patients with succinic semialdehyde dehydrogenase deficiency. This rare disorder is an inborn error of metabolism variably characterized by mental retardation, hypotonia, and ataxia.
 
Sodium oxybate has the potential to impair cognitive and motor skills. For at least 6 hours after ingesting sodium oxybate, patients must refrain from engaging in hazardous occupations or activities requiring complete mental alertness (e.g., driving or operating machinery, or flying an airplane). Sleepwalking (somnambulism) may occur after use and may result in injury or the unintended performance of hazardous tasks. Patients should remain in bed during and after a dosage. Warn patients to avoid the use of alcoholic beverages in conjunction with this drug. Those receiving sodium oxybate must avoid ethanol intoxication or ingestion or use of other CNS depressants; in particular, sodium oxybate is contraindicated in patients being treated with sedative hypnotic agents (see Drug Interactions). Because of the potential for drug interactions, patients should be advised to inform their health care professional of their sodium oxybate prescription prior to surgery or other medical procedures.
 
Sodium oxybate should be avoided in patients who are in a coma. Use sodium oxybate with caution in patients with pre-existing depression, CNS depression, dementia, psychosis, confusion, or other neuropsychiatric problems. Sodium oxybate is GHB, a known central nervous system depressant and drug of abuse. GHB has been associated with some important central nervous system (CNS) adverse events (including death). Even at recommended doses of sodium oxybate, the use of this drug has been associated with confusion, depression (with rare reports of suicidal ideation) and other neuropsychiatric events (including psychosis, paranoia, hallucinations and agitation). The emergence of depression, confusion, or other neurologic or psychiatric problems during treatment requires careful and immediate evaluation. Patients at risk for attempted suicide should be monitored especially closely.
 
Use sodium oxybate with caution in patients with pre-existing significant pulmonary disease that places them at risk for respiratory depression, respiratory insufficiency or hypoxemia. For example, sleep apnea may worsen during sodium oxybate treatment. Sodium oxybate is GHB, a known central nervous system depressant and drug of abuse. GHB has been associated with some important respiratory adverse events (including death). Even at recommended doses of sodium oxybate, the use of this drug has been associated with respiratory depression, which occurred in clinical trials. Almost all of the patients with narcolepsy who received sodium oxybate at bedtime during clinical trials were receiving CNS stimulants; whether this affected respiration during the night is unknown.
 
There is limited experience with sodium oxybate in the elderly. Monitor elderly patients closely for impaired motor function and/or cognitive function when this drug is used.
 
Sodium oxybate has the potential to induce seizures. It has been speculated that this effect may be mediated through the action of sodium oxybate at GABA receptors. Although convulsant effects occur primarily at high dosages, sodium oxybate should be used cautiously in those patients with a history of seizure disorder or with conditions that lower the seizure threshold.
 
Urinary incontinence or fecal incontinence may worsen with the use of sodium oxybate (see Adverse reactions). In patients who develop incontinence for the first time during sodium oxybate therapy, underlying etiologies (including worsening sleep apnea or nocturnal seizures) may need to be ruled out before ascribing the incontinence to the use of the drug.
 
Sodium oxybate should be used cautiously in those with hepatic disease, since the drug is extensively metabolized in the liver. Patients with compromised liver function have an increased elimination half-life and systemic exposure to the drug. The initial dose should be reduced by one-half in patients with hepatic disease and titrated slowly (see Dosage).
 
Various cardiac effects have developed in those receiving sodium oxybate including bradycardia, hypertension, and tachycardia. Although these symptoms are most likely to occur following the administration of high dosages of the drug, sodium oxybate should be used cautiously in those with pre-existing cardiac disease, hypertension or cardiac arrhythmias. The daily sodium intake from the use of sodium oxybate ranges from 0.5 g (for a 3 g dose) to 1.6 g (for a 9 g dose). This increase in dietary sodium intake should be considered in patients with heart failure, hypertension, or renal disease (including renal impairment or renal failure). Because less than 5% of a sodium oxybate dose is eliminated via the kidney, no dosage adjustment is recommended for patients with renal impairment.
 
Prolonged use of sodium oxybate may produce drug dependence, tolerance, and subsequent withdrawal. Sodium oxybate should be avoided, if possible, in those with a history of substance abuse, including patients with active alcoholism. Clinical studies suggest a potential cross-tolerance with alcohol. Sodium oxybate should not be used in patients receiving sedative-hypnotic agents.
 
Abrupt discontinuation of sodium oxybate is not recommended, unless required by the patient's medical condition. Otherwise discontinuation should usually occur via a gradual reduction in dosage. Patients should be carefully observed for symptoms of an abstinence syndrome including anxiety, psychosis, insomnia, lethargy, muscle cramps, nausea, tachycardia, tremor and sweating during drug discontinuation.
 
The FDA has not established the safety and efficacy of sodium oxybate for use in children and adolescents with narcolepsy who are < 16 years of age. Sodium oxybate may affect endocrine function with chronic use, including growth hormone secretion. The effects on a child in whom growth and development are incomplete are uncertain. European data indicate that sodium oxybate has been used successfully in children in single-dose use for the purpose of anesthesia induction.
 
Sodium oxybate is listed as FDA pregnancy risk category B. Teratogenic effects have not been reported in animal studies; but no data are available in human gestation. The drug crosses the placenta in humans. Sodium oxybate should be used during pregnancy only if the benefits outweigh the potential risks to the fetus; the subsequent effects of maternal use of sodium oxybate (GHB) on later infant growth, development or maturation are not known. The use of sodium oxybate in eclampsia should be avoided due to the potential of the drug to cause seizures. According to one authority, parenteral sodium oxybate was used in obstetric anesthesia in the early 1960s; the drug decreases cerebral blood flow, glucose metabolism, and oxygen consumption, and was believed to be indicated in instances of fetal hypoxia. Uterine contractions diminish within 20 minutes after such use. Placental transfer of the drug is rapid, but umbilical vein levels have been noted to be no more than 25% of maternal concentrations. Neonates were noted to be sleepy, with slightly lower Apgar scores; blood levels of the drug are undetectable in the neonate within 30 minutes post-delivery. However, sodium oxybate has not been formally studied in labor or obstetric delivery.
 
It is unknown whether sodium oxybate is excreted in breast milk. The manufacturer recommends caution when sodium oxybate is administered to a nursing woman. Many medical experts would consider use of this drug contraindicated during breast-feeding, due to the potential and unknown risk for CNS or respiratory depression in the nursing infant.
 
Studies have demonstrated that sodium oxybate increases the release of growth hormone. Prolactin and cortisol secretion may also be enhanced. Electrolyte imbalances including hypernatremia and hypokalemia have been reported. Although the clinical significance of these findings is unknown, sodium oxybate should be used cautiously in those with a pre-existing hormonal (e.g., hyperprolactinemia) or electrolyte imbalance.

    General Information

    Headline
    depression, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Depression, and suicidal ideation and behavior, can occur in individuals treated with sodium oxybate, and behavioral changes were reported in both pediatric and adult populations treated with sodium oxybate during clinical evaluations. Some events, such as confusion, generally increased in incidence as the dosage increased. Carefully monitor for the emergence or increase in the occurrence of a behavioral or psychiatric event in adult and pediatric patients taking sodium oxybate. The emergence of depression requires careful and immediate evaluation. Individuals with a previous history of a depressive illness and/or suicide attempt should be monitored carefully for the emergence of depressive symptoms while taking sodium oxybate.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, CNS depression, driving or operating machinery, ethanol ingestion
    General Information
    Sodium oxybate causes CNS depression. Obtundation and clinically significant respiratory depression have occurred in individuals treated with sodium oxybate at recommended doses; many of the individuals in clinical trials for narcolepsy were also receiving CNS stimulants. Coadministration with alcohol and sedative-hypnotics is contraindicated; warn individuals against ethanol ingestion and do not use in individuals with active alcohol use disorder. Coadministration with other CNS depressants may increase the risk of respiratory depression, hypotension, profound sedation, syncope, and death. If concurrent use is required, consider dose reduction or discontinuation of one or more of the CNS depressants. Consider sodium oxybate dosing interruption if the short-term use of an opioid (e.g., during or after surgery) is required. Sodium oxybate may cause individuals to fall asleep abruptly without first feeling drowsy and individuals will often fall asleep within 5 to 15 minutes of dosing. Individuals should remain in bed during and after a dose and must refrain from engaging in hazardous occupations or activities requiring coordination and concentration (e.g., driving or operating machinery) for at least 6 hours after a dose.
    Headline
    substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    Sodium oxybate is the sodium salt of gamma hydroxybutyrate (GHB), an illicit drug of abuse. Abuse or misuse of GHB, either alone or in combination with other CNS depressants, has been associated with adverse central nervous system reactions including seizure, respiratory depression, decreased levels of consciousness, coma, and death. The rapid onset of sedation and amnestic properties of sodium oxybate can be dangerous for voluntary and involuntary users. Carefully evaluate individuals for a history of substance abuse disorder and follow individuals closely, observing them for signs of misuse and abuse of GHB (e.g., increase in the size or frequency of dosing, drug-seeking behavior, feigned cataplexy).
    Headline
    pulmonary disease, respiratory insufficiency, sleep apnea
    General Information
    Sodium oxybate may impair respiratory drive, especially in individuals with compromised respiratory function or preexisting respiratory insufficiency. Central apneas and clinically relevant desaturation events have been observed in both pediatric and adult individuals. Use sodium oxybate with caution in individuals with pulmonary disease, including sleep apnea, that places them at risk for respiratory depression or hypoxemia. Sleep-related breathing disorders tend to be more prevalent with obesity, in men, in postmenopausal women not on hormone replacement therapy, and among individuals with narcolepsy.
    Headline
    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use sodium oxybate cautiously in individuals with hepatic failure, since the drug is extensively metabolized in the liver. Individuals with compromised liver function have an increased elimination half-life and systemic exposure to the drug. A dosage adjustment is recommended in individuals with hepatic impairment.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Clinical narcolepsy studies did not include sufficient numbers of geriatric subjects age 65 years of age and older to determine whether they respond differently to sodium oxybate than younger adults; use caution with initial dosage and dosage titrations. In other controlled trials of immediate-release sodium oxybate, 39 (5%) of 874 patients were 65 years or older. Discontinuations of treatment due to adverse reactions were increased in geriatric adults compared to younger adults (20.5% vs. 18.9%) and the frequency of headaches was markedly increased in the older adults. The most common adverse reactions were similar in both age categories.
    Headline
    cardiac disease, heart failure, hypertension, renal failure, renal impairment, sodium restriction
    General Information
    Sodium oxybate has a high sodium content. Consider the amount of daily sodium intake in individuals sensitive to salt or with sodium restriction (e.g., those with heart failure, cardiac disease, hypertension, renal impairment, or renal failure). The sodium content per total nightly dose of sodium oxybate is 820 mg for 4.5 grams; 1,100 mg for 6 grams; 1,400 mg for 7.5 grams; and 1,640 mg for 9 grams.
    Headline
    labor, obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate data on the use of sodium oxybate during human pregnancy to evaluate for a drug-associated risk of major birth defects. Animal studies produced no clear evidence of developmental toxicity; however, increased stillbirths and decreased postnatal viability and growth were seen at clinically relevant doses. Oral sodium oxybate has not been studied in labor or obstetric delivery. When used in obstetric anesthesia, parenteral sodium oxybate decreased the rates of uterine contractions 20 minutes after injection. Newborns exhibited stable cardiovascular and respiratory status but were markedly sleepy, causing a slight decrease in Apgar scores. Placental transfer of gamma-hydroxybutyrate (GHB) is rapid; it has been detected in newborns at delivery after intravenous administration to the pregnant person. Subsequent effects on later growth, development, and maturation in humans are unknown.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use sodium oxybate with caution during breast-feeding. Gamma hydroxybutyrate (GHB) is present in human milk after oral administration of sodium oxybate. There is insufficient information on the risk to a breastfed child or the effect on milk production. An alternate medication (or alternative form of feeding) may be preferred, especially while feeding a newborn or premature infant. If administration cannot be avoided, avoid breast-feeding for 4 to 5 hours after each dose to minimize infant exposure. Monitor the child for sedation, respiratory depression, agitation, and vomiting.

The most frequent reasons for discontinuation (>1%) were nausea (2%), dizziness (2%), and vomiting (1%).

In the 717 patients, there were two suicides and one attempted suicide recorded in patients with a previous history of depression.

Sodium oxybate (aka GHB) is a psychoactive drug that produces a wide range of pharmacological effects. It is a sedative-hypnotic that produces dose and concentration dependent CNS effects in humans. The onset of effect is rapid, enhancing its desirability as a drug of abuse or misuse. The drug is purported to produce euphoria and libido increase in illicit use. The rapid onset of sedation, coupled with the amnestic features of sodium oxybate, particularly when combined with alcohol, has proven to be dangerous. The abuse of GHB is associated with drug dependence and tolerance. Physiological dependence has been reported after illicit use of GHB at frequent repeated doses (18 to 250 g/d), in excess of the therapeutic dose range for accepted medical use. In these cases, the signs and symptoms of abrupt discontinuation included a withdrawal syndrome consisting of insomnia, restlessness, anxiety, psychosis, lethargy, nausea, tremor, sweating, muscle cramps, and tachycardia. These symptoms generally abated in 3—14 days. The discontinuation effects of sodium oxybate have not been systematically evaluated in controlled clinical trials. Although the clinical trial experience with sodium oxybate in narcolepsy/cataplexy patients at therapeutic doses does not show clear evidence of a withdrawal syndrome, two patients reported anxiety and one reported insomnia following abrupt discontinuation; in the two patients with anxiety, the frequency of cataplexy had increased markedly at the same time.
 
Doses of GHB exceeding 50 mg/kg may produce symptoms of anesthesia and serious toxicity. Toxic effects of sodium oxybate become apparent within 15 minutes of oral ingestion due to the rapid absorption of the drug. Signs and symptoms of sodium oxybate intoxication include agitation, ataxia, sinus bradycardia, Cheyne-Stokes respiration, coma, confusion, delirium, dizziness, hallucinations, hypersalivation, hypertension, hypothermia, hypotonia, hypoxia, myoclonia, nausea/vomiting, nystagmus, respiratory depression, seizures, sinus tachycardia, and visual impairment. Death has occurred in association with sodium oxybate (GHB) toxicity. In clinical trials with sodium oxybate, two overdoses were reported. In the first case, an estimated dose of 150 grams, more than 15 times the maximum recommended dose, caused a patient to be unresponsive with brief periods of apnea and to be incontinent of urine and feces. This individual recovered without sequelae. In the second case, death was reported following a multiple drug overdose consisting of sodium oxybate and numerous other drugs.

Description
  Text
  Sodium oxybate is the sodium salt of gamma hydroxybutyric acid (GHB), a naturally occurring central nervous system transmitter with sedative and anesthetic properties. In Europe, sodium oxybate has been used for various medical purposes including narcolepsy, intravenous anesthesia, alcohol dependence, and opiate dependence. In the U.S., sodium oxybate is used for the treatment of cataplexy or excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) in patients with narcolepsy. Sodium oxybate differs from modafinil and other stimulant treatments for narcolepsy in that it significantly decreases cataplexy episodes in addition to EDS; the drug is frequently prescribed along with stimulant therapies for narcolepsy. A median decrease in the total number of weekly cataplexy episodes of nearly 70% has been reported in clinical trials in adults with narcolepsy receiving a dose of 9 grams/night. Historically, GHB was commonly used as a dietary supplement for enhancing athletic performance and as a sleep aid. As a result of serious adverse events associated with GHB use as a dietary supplement, the FDA prohibited the marketing of GHB in 1991. Commonly known by slang terms such as Liquid Ecstasy or Liquid E, GHB can produce euphoria, making it a popular but dangerous party drug of abuse. GHB has not been approved for any medical use and has gained notoriety as a date rape drug due to its sedative and amnestic properties.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Sodium oxybate is a CNS depressant. The mechanism of action of sodium oxybate in the treatment of narcolepsy is unknown. Sodium oxybate is the sodium salt of gamma-hydroxybutyrate (GHB), an endogenous compound and metabolite of the neurotransmitter GABA. It is hypothesized that the therapeutic effects of sodium oxybate on cataplexy and excessive daytime sleepiness are mediated through GABA-beta actions during sleep at noradrenergic and dopaminergic neurons, as well as at thalamocortical neurons. Sodium oxybate has been shown to effectively reduce stage 1 sleep, decrease wake-after-sleep onset, decrease the number of awakenings, and increase slow-wave sleep.

    Description
    Immediate-release solution (Xyrem): After administration of the immediate-release solution, pharmacokinetics are non-linear and increase more than dose proportionally, with blood concentrations increasing 3.7-fold as the dose is doubled from 4.5 to 9 g. Pharmacokinetics are similar with single or repeated dosing. The Cmax after administration of each of the two 2.25 gram doses given under fasting conditions 4 hours apart are similar. GHB is rapidly absorbed after oral administration, with an absolute bioavailability of 88%. The average Tmax ranges from 0.5 to 1.25 hours. Absorption is delayed and decreased (increase in average Tmax from 0.75 to 2 hours, 59% decrease in Cmax, 37% decrease in AUC) when the drug is administered with a high-fat meal. Sedation often occurs within 5 and usually within 15 minutes of dosing; rarely it may take up to 2 hours to fall asleep.
Extended-release suspension (Lumryz): After administration of the extended-release suspension, the plasma concentrations increase dose-proportionally for Cmax and more than dose-proportionally for AUC (respectively, 2-fold and 2.3-fold increases as total daily dose is doubled from 4.5 to 9 g). The Cmax after administration of a single 6 g dose of extended-release sodium oxybate solution was 66 mcg/mL and the Tmax was 1.5 hours. Administration immediately after a high-fat meal resulted in a mean reduction in Cmax and AUC of GHB by 33% and 14%, respectively; average Tmax increased from 0.5 hours to 1.5 hours. Sedation often occurs within 5 and usually within 15 minutes of dosing.

    The pharmacokinetics of sodium oxybate in 16 cirrhotic patients, half without ascites (Child-Pugh Class A) and half with ascites (Child-Pugh Class C) were compared to healthy adults after a single oral dose of 25 mg/kg.   Text
  Sodium oxybate is administered orally and is the sodium salt of gamma-hydroxybutyrate (GHB). GHB is a hydrophilic compound with an apparent volume of distribution averaging 190 to 384 mL/kg. At GHB concentrations ranging from 3 to 300 mcg/mL, less than 1% is bound to plasma proteins. Sodium oxybate readily crosses the blood-brain barrier and the placenta. Animal studies indicate that metabolism is the major elimination pathway for sodium oxybate, producing carbon dioxide and water via the Krebs cycle and secondarily via beta-oxidation. The primary pathway involves a cytosolic NADP+-linked enzyme, GHB dehydrogenase, which catalyzes the conversion of sodium oxybate to succinic semialdehyde, which is then transformed to succinic acid by succinic semialdehyde dehydrogenase. Succinic acid enters the Krebs cycle and is converted to carbon dioxide and water. A second mitochondrial oxidoreductase enzyme, a transhydrogenase, also contributes to the conversion to succinic semialdehyde in the presence of alpha-ketoglutarate. An alternate pathway of biotransformation involves beta-oxidation via 3,4-dihydroxybutyrate to carbon dioxide and water. Sodium oxybate is also metabolized in the central nervous system; however, the exact pathway has not been described. There are no active metabolites and the elimination of the drug appears to be capacity-limited. The clearance of GHB is almost entirely by biotransformation to carbon dioxide, which is then expired. On average, less than 5% of a dose is excreted renally as the parent compound within 6 to 8 hours of dosing. Fecal excretion is negligible. The drug is virtually undetectable in the urine after 12 hours. The elimination half-life of GHB is 0.5 to 1 hour. Although laboratory assays are not routinely available, GHB is detectable in plasma and urine using gas chromatographic-mass spectrometric (GC-MS).
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes and drug transporters: None
In vitro studies indicate that sodium oxybate does not significantly inhibit CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, CYP2E1, or CYP3A at a concentration that is significantly higher than plasma concentrations achieved with therapeutic doses.

Administration
  General Information
  For storage information, see the specific product information within the How Supplied section.
  Route Specific Administrations
    Name
    Oral Administration
    Description
    Oral solution (Xyrem)
Prepare all nightly doses prior to bedtime.
Dilute each dose with approximately 60 mL (one-fourth cup) of water in the empty child-resistant containers provided. Ensure the second dose is kept in a safe place until given, close to the individual's bed. Ensure the cap is applied and locked into the child-resistant position to prevent access to the medication by children.
First dose: Administer at bedtime or after an initial period of sleep in individuals that sleep more than 8 hours/night and at least 2 hours after eating.
Second dose: Administer the second dose 2.5 to 4 hours after the first dose. The individual and/or caregiver may need to set an alarm to awaken for the second dose.
Advise the individual to take the medication while in bed and to lie down immediately after dosing as they may fall asleep abruptly without first feeling drowsy. The individual must remain in bed after administration of each dose.
Missed dose: If the second dose is missed, skip the missed dose and do not administer again until the next night. Never administer both doses at once.
Storage: Discard any diluted solution that is not used within 24 hours. May discard down the sink drain.
Oral suspension (Lumryz)
Prepare the dose prior to bedtime.
Prior to ingestion, suspend the medication in approximately 80 mL (one-third cup) of water in the mixing cup provided. Do not use hot water.
Administer the medication within 30 minutes of mixing and at least 2 hours after eating.
Advise the individual to take the medication while in bed and to lie down immediately after dosing as they may fall asleep abruptly without first feeling drowsy. The individual must remain in bed after administration.
Storage: Discard any unused, diluted suspension. May discard down the sink drain.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Oral solution: Reduce the recommended initial dosage by 50%.
Oral extended-release (ER) suspension: Patients with hepatic impairment should not initiate treatment with the oral ER suspension due to a lack of appropriate dosage strengths; however, a patient maintained on the immediate-release oral solution may be switched to an equivalent ER suspension dosage if the appropriate dosage strength is available.
  Renal Impairment
  No dosage adjustments are needed.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
Due to the rapid metabolism of sodium oxybate, hemodialysis and other forms of extracorporeal drug removal are unlikely to impact drug clearance.
