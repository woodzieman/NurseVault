Drug Class: Sulfasalazine (salicylazosulfapyridine) is a prodrug that is metabolized by intestinal bacteria to sulfapyridine and 5-aminosalicylate (5-ASA) (mesalamine).

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Sulfasalazine is broken down to sulfapyridine (a sulfonamide) and 5-aminosalicylic acid (mesalamine). Therefore, sulfasalazine is contraindicated in patients with sulfasalazine hypersensitivity, salicylate hypersensitivity, sulfonamide hypersensitivity, and 5-aminosalicylates hypersensitivity. Due to structural similarity to sulfonamides, patients with furosemide hypersensitivity, thiazide diuretic hypersensitivity, sulfonylurea hypersensitivity, or carbonic anhydrase inhibitor hypersensitivity should use sulfasalazine with caution. Deaths related to hypersensitivity reactions and irreversible neuromuscular and central nervous system changes have been reported following sulfasalazine treatment.
 
Only after careful consideration should sulfasalazine be used in a patient with hematological disease since sulfonamides are associated with blood dyscrasias. Sulfasalazine should be used with caution in patients with pre-existing megaloblastic anemia due to folate deficiency since it decreases folate absorption. Deaths related to agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, and other blood dyscrasias have been reported following sulfasalazine treatment. Monitor for the onset of sore throat, fever, pallor, or purpura which may be signs of a serious blood disorder. Complete blood cell count (CBC) with differential should be done at baseline and then every other week during the first three months of therapy, monthly during the next 3 months, and thereafter, every 3 months or as clinically indicated. Discontinue treatment while awaiting the results of blood tests. In addition, patients with G6PD deficiency should be monitored carefully during sulfasalazine therapy for signs of hemolytic anemia.
 
Only after careful consideration should sulfasalazine be used in a patient with hepatic disease since sulfonamides are metabolized in the liver and are rarely associated with severe hepatotoxicity. Deaths related to liver damage have been reported following sulfasalazine treatment. Monitor for the onset of nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, loss of appetite, diarrhea, or jaundice which may be signs of a serious liver disorder. Liver function tests (LFTs) should be done at baseline and then every other week during the first three months of therapy, monthly during the next 3 months, and thereafter, every 3 months or as clinically indicated. Discontinue treatment while awaiting the results of blood tests.
 
In patients with porphyria, sulfasalazine is contraindicated because sulfonamides can precipitate an acute attack.
 
Only after careful consideration should sulfasalazine be used in a patient with renal impairment or renal failure because the drug is excreted in the urine, and accumulation is associated with an increased risk of toxicity. Deaths related to renal damage have been reported following sulfasalazine treatment. Sulfasalazine is contraindicated in patients with urinary tract obstruction. In patients with hypovolemia or dehydration, sulfasalazine may cause crystalluria and contribute to urinary obstruction. In adults, adequate fluid intake should be maintained to achieve urine output of at least 1200 ml/day during sulfasalazine treatment. Alkalinization of the urine may increase the solubility of sulfasalazine in patients with low urine output or if the urinary pH is low. Monitoring of renal function including urinalysis should be done periodically throughout sulfasalazine treatment.
 
Patients with severe allergic conditions or bronchial asthma are at risk of developing severe and potentially fatal exacerbations of asthma after taking sulfasalazine. Sulfasalazine should be avoided in asthmatics with a history of aspirin-induced bronchospasm. Deaths related to fibrosing alveolitis have been reported following sulfasalazine treatment.
 
Sulfasalazine is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category B. Sulfasalazine and sulfapyridine (an active moiety) crosses the placenta. Evidence suggests that sulfasalazine does not appear to be associated with a significant risk of teratogenicity. The benefits of sulfasalazine for the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease or rheumatoid arthritis in pregnancy may outweigh the potential risks. Sulfonamides, such as sulfapyridine, are generally considered category D when used near term due to the potential for jaundice, hemolytic anemia, and kernicterus in the newborn; therefore, caution is warranted for the use of sulfasalazine in pregnancy near term. However, the risk of kernicterus in the newborn if sulfasalazine is consumed in the last trimester appears low as sulfapyridine has poor bilirubin displacing capacity. Agranulocytosis has been reported in one infant whose mother received sulfasalazine as well as prednisone throughout pregnancy. Long-term effects of sulfasalazine on growth and maturation in the child is not known. Folate supplementation is especially important in pregnant women receiving sulfasalazine as it will impair folate absorption and metabolism. Because there are no controlled trials of sulfasalazine use in pregnant women, the drug should be used in pregnancy only if clearly needed.
 
Sulfasalazine should be used with caution in women who are breast-feeding. Unchanged sulfasalazine does not cross into breast milk in appreciable amounts; however, one active moiety (i.e., sulfapyridine, a sulfonamide) does enter the milk. Neonatal concentrations of sulfapyridine may be as high as 30—60% of maternal serum concentrations. In mothers who are breast-feeding infants and neonates less than 2 months of age, sulfonamides may increase the risk of kernicterus in the newborn by displacing bilirubin from plasma proteins. However, sulfapyridine has poor bilirubin displacing capacity and this may lessen the risk. The American Academy of Pediatrics reports that sulfapyridine is usually compatible with breast-feeding, but suggests to use with caution in stressed or ill infants, those with G-6PD deficiency, or premature infants. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
Oligospermia, infertility, abnormal sperm forms, and impaired sperm motility have occurred in men during sulfasalazine therapy but are reversible upon stopping sulfasalazine.
 
The safety and effectiveness of either the delayed-release or immediate-release sulfasalazine tablets in children or infants less than 2 years of age with ulcerative colitis have not been determined. Both formulations are only indicated for use in children in at least 6 years of age (see Dosage).
 
Sulfasalazine is contraindicated in patients with GI obstruction. Isolated cases have been reported where sulfasalazine enteric-coated tablets have passed undisintegrated. If this occurs, discontinue the administration of the enteric-coated tablets immediately.
 
Serum concentrations of sulfapyridine may be higher in patients who are slow acetylators, leading to an increased risk of adverse reactions. Serum concentrations of sulfapyridine > 50 mcg/ml are associated with more adverse reactions. Eskimo, Oriental, and American Indian populations have the lowest prevalence of slow acetylators, while Egyptian, Israeli, Scandinavian, other Caucasian, and black populations have the highest prevalence of slow acetylators.
 
Photosensitivity can occur with sulfa containing compounds, so patients should avoid or limit sunlight (UV) exposure, including sunlamps and tanning booths. Sunscreens should be employed, but may provide limited protection for this reaction. Discontinue drug use at the first sign of erythema.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Sulfasalazine is broken down to sulfapyridine (a sulfonamide) and 5-aminosalicylic acid (mesalamine). Therefore, sulfasalazine is contraindicated in people with salicylate hypersensitivity, sulfonamide hypersensitivity, and 5-aminosalicylates hypersensitivity. The drug should be avoided in people with asthma that have a history of aspirin-induced bronchospasm. Hypersensitivity reactions may present as serious skin reactions, internal organ involvement, including myocarditis, pericarditis, nephritis, hepatitis, alveolitis or pneumonitis, and hematologic abnormalities. Patients are at highest risk for serious cutaneous events early in therapy, with most events occurring within the first month of treatment. While desensitization has been attempted in some patients, desensitization should not be attempted in people who have a history of agranulocytosis, or who have experienced an anaphylactoid reaction while previously receiving sulfasalazine.

    Headline
    renal disease, urinary tract obstruction
    General Information
    Sulfasalazine is contraindicated in people with urinary tract obstruction. Only after careful consideration should sulfasalazine be used in an individual with known renal disease or damage. Sulfasalazine and its metabolites are excreted in the urine. Adequate fluid intake must be maintained in order to prevent crystalluria and stone formation. Deaths related to renal damage have been reported following sulfasalazine treatment. Monitor renal function, including urinalysis, periodically throughout sulfasalazine treatment. Discontinue sulfasalazine if renal function deteriorates while on therapy.

    Headline
    G6PD deficiency, hematological disease
    General Information
    Only after critical appraisal should sulfasalazine be given to people with pre-existing hematological disease or blood dyscrasias. Deaths related to agranulocytosis (severe neutropenia), aplastic anemia, and other blood dyscrasias have been reported following sulfasalazine treatment. People with glucose-6 phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency (G6PD deficiency) should be observed closely for signs of hemolytic anemia, as this reaction due to sulfasalazine is frequently dose-related. In all sulfasalazine recipients, a complete blood cell count (CBC) with differential should be monitored at baseline and then every other week during the first 3 months of therapy, monthly during the next 3 months, and then every 3 months or as clinically indicated after that. Discontinue treatment while awaiting the results of blood tests if serious hematologic events are suspected.
    Headline
    infection
    General Information
    Use caution in considering the use of sulfasalazine in people with a history of recurring or chronic infection or with underlying conditions or concomitant drugs that may predispose them to infection. Serious infections, including fatal sepsis and pneumonia, have been reported during use of sulfasalazine; some infection were associated with agranulocytosis, neutropenia, or bone marrow suppression. Closely monitor for the development of signs and symptoms of infection during and after treatment with sulfasalazine. For a patient who develops a new infection during treatment with sulfasalazine, perform a prompt and complete diagnostic workup for infection and myelosuppression. Discontinue sulfasalazine if a serious infection occurs.
    Headline
    hepatic disease, NAT2 slow acetylator
    General Information
    Only after careful consideration should sulfasalazine be used in a patient with hepatic disease or damage since sulfonamides are metabolized in the liver. Deaths related to drug-induced liver damage have been reported following sulfasalazine treatment. Monitor for the onset of symptoms which may be signs of a serious liver condition. In all sulfasalazine recipients, liver function tests (LFTs) should be monitored at baseline and then every other week during the first 3 months of therapy, monthly during the next 3 months, and every 3 months or as clinically indicated after that. Discontinue treatment while awaiting the results of LFTs if jaundice or other indicators of hepatotoxicity are present. Acetylation of sulfasalazine via N-acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2) plays an important role in drug metabolism, and research suggests people who are known to be a NAT2 slow acetylator of sulfapyridine, the metabolite of sulfasalazine, are at risk for serious adverse reactions.
    Headline
    skin photosensitivity disorder
    General Information
    Use sulfasalazine with caution in people with a previous history of a skin photosensitivity disorder. Photosensitivity has been reported in individuals receiving sulfa compounds and 5-ASA containing compounds. People with pre-existing skin conditions, such as atopic dermatitis and atopic eczema, have reported more severe photosensitivity reactions. Advise individuals to avoid sunlight (UV) exposure and tanning booths, and to wear sunscreen (and, if possible, protective clothing) on exposed skin during sulfasalazine treatment.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Limited human data, including observational and epidemiologic studies, have not identified an increased risk of congenital anomalies with sulfasalazine use during pregnancy. Sulfasalazine is considered compatible for use throughout pregnancy and may be continued when clinically indicated for maintenance of disease control in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or rheumatic disease. Sulfasalazine and its metabolite, sulfapyridine, cross the placenta. Case reports of neural tube defects (NTDs) have been described after in utero exposure; however, a causal role for sulfasalazine has not been established. Because sulfasalazine can inhibit folic acid absorption and metabolism, folate supplementation is especially important before conception and during pregnancy. Expert sources recommend folic acid 2 mg/day for patients receiving sulfasalazine.    Available pregnancy outcome data are generally reassuring. In a national survey of patients with IBD, fetal morbidity and mortality among 186 pregnancies exposed to sulfasalazine alone or with corticosteroids were comparable to outcomes in untreated IBD pregnancies and the general population. Additional data from 1,455 pregnancies exposed to sulfonamides, including sulfasalazine, did not suggest an association with fetal malformations, and a literature review of 1,155 pregnancies in patients with ulcerative colitis found outcomes similar to those expected in the general population. Although sulfapyridine has poor bilirubin-displacing capacity, kernicterus remains a theoretical neonatal concern. Agranulocytosis has also been reported in one infant exposed to sulfasalazine and prednisone during pregnancy.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with sulfasalazine. Because sulfasalazine can inhibit folic acid absorption and metabolism, folate supplementation is especially important before conception in those planning a pregnancy. Expert sources recommend folic acid 2 mg/day for patients receiving sulfasalazine.    Oligospermia and male infertility have been described in those treated with sulfasalazine. These effects appear to be reversible after drug discontinuation.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Monitor neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero for kernicterus. Although sulfapyridine has poor bilirubin-displacing capacity, kernicterus remains a theoretical neonatal concern.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use sulfasalazine with caution during breast-feeding. Sulfonamides, including sulfasalazine, are present in human milk. Sulfasalazine itself has been detected in negligible amounts, while its active metabolite, sulfapyridine, reaches milk concentrations about 30% to 60% of serum levels in the lactating patient. Sulfapyridine may theoretically cause hemolysis, particularly in newborns and infants with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. Bloody stools and diarrhea have been reported in breastfed infants exposed to sulfasalazine through human milk. When outcomes were reported, symptoms resolved after sulfasalazine was discontinued or breast-feeding was stopped. Because data are limited, a causal relationship cannot be confirmed or excluded. Monitor breastfed infants for diarrhea and/or bloody stools. Rheumatology and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) guidelines consider sulfasalazine compatible with breastfeeding; however, because sulfasalazine's sulfapyridine metabolite is excreted into breast milk, mesalamine is often considered a preferred alternative.

Desogestrel: Ethinyl Estradiol; Ferrous Bisglycinate: (Moderate) It would be prudent to recommend alternative or additional contraception when oral contraceptives (OCs) are used in conjunction with antibiotics. It was previously thought that antibiotics may decrease the effectiveness of OCs containing estrogens due to stimulation of metabolism or a reduction in enterohepatic circulation via changes in GI flora. One retrospective study reviewed the literature to determine the effects of oral antibiotics on the pharmacokinetics of contraceptive estrogens and progestins, and also examined clinical studies in which the incidence of pregnancy with OCs and antibiotics was reported. It was concluded that the antibiotics ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin, doxycycline, metronidazole, ofloxacin, roxithromycin, temafloxacin, and tetracycline did not alter plasma concentrations of OCs. Antituberculous drugs (e.g., rifampin) were the only agents associated with OC failure and pregnancy. Based on the study results, these authors recommended that back-up contraception may not be necessary if OCs are used reliably during oral antibiotic use. Another review concurred with these data, but noted that individual patients have been identified who experienced significant decreases in plasma concentrations of combined OC components and who appeared to ovulate; the agents most often associated with these changes were rifampin, tetracyclines, and penicillin derivatives. These authors concluded that because females most at risk for OC failure or noncompliance may not be easily identified and the true incidence of such events may be under-reported, and given the serious consequence of unwanted pregnancy, that recommending an additional method of contraception during short-term antibiotic use may be justified. During long-term antibiotic administration, the risk for drug interaction with OCs is less clear, but alternative or additional contraception may be advisable in selected circumstances. Data regarding progestin-only contraceptives or for newer combined contraceptive deliveries (e.g., patches, rings) are not available.

Estradiol; Levonorgestrel: (Moderate) It would be prudent to recommend alternative or additional contraception when oral contraceptives (OCs) are used in conjunction with antibiotics. It was previously thought that antibiotics may decrease the effectiveness of OCs containing estrogens due to stimulation of metabolism or a reduction in enterohepatic circulation via changes in GI flora. One retrospective study reviewed the literature to determine the effects of oral antibiotics on the pharmacokinetics of contraceptive estrogens and progestins, and also examined clinical studies in which the incidence of pregnancy with OCs and antibiotics was reported. It was concluded that the antibiotics ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin, doxycycline, metronidazole, ofloxacin, roxithromycin, temafloxacin, and tetracycline did not alter plasma concentrations of OCs. Antituberculous drugs (e.g., rifampin) were the only agents associated with OC failure and pregnancy. Based on the study results, these authors recommended that back-up contraception may not be necessary if OCs are used reliably during oral antibiotic use. Another review concurred with these data, but noted that individual patients have been identified who experienced significant decreases in plasma concentrations of combined OC components and who appeared to ovulate; the agents most often associated with these changes were rifampin, tetracyclines, and penicillin derivatives. These authors concluded that because females most at risk for OC failure or noncompliance may not be easily identified and the true incidence of such events may be under-reported, and given the serious consequence of unwanted pregnancy, that recommending an additional method of contraception during short-term antibiotic use may be justified. During long-term antibiotic administration, the risk for drug interaction with OCs is less clear, but alternative or additional contraception may be advisable in selected circumstances. Data regarding progestin-only contraceptives or for newer combined contraceptive deliveries (e.g., patches, rings) are not available.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The most common adverse reactions occurring during sulfasalazine therapy include anorexia, diarrhea, GI distress (e.g., abdominal pain and dyspepsia), headache, and nausea/vomiting. These reactions occur in up to 33% of patients. Total daily doses > 4 g/day or total serum sulfapyridine levels above 50 mcg/ml are associated with an increased incidence of adverse effects. GI distress occurring at the start of sulfasalazine therapy is probably due to direct irritation of GI mucosa and may be alleviated by dividing the total daily dose evenly throughout the day or by giving the enteric coated tablets. Symptoms occurring after the first few days are probably related to high serum concentrations of sulfapyridine. In this case, the total daily dose of sulfasalazine should be reduced by 50% and gradually increased over the next several days to the desired dose. If the symptoms persist, stop the drug for 5—7 days and restart at lower dosage and gradually increase the dose. Rarely, some patients may pass the enteric-coated tablets intact through the GI tract, possibly due to the lack of intestinal esterases capable of disintegrating the enteric coating. Do not administer additional enteric-coated tablets if this occurs. Exacerbation of ulcerative colitis occurs infrequently during sulfasalazine therapy and is manifested as bloody diarrhea, fever, and skin rash. Pancreatitis and neutropenic enterocolitis have been reported rarely (1 in 1000 patients) during sulfasalazine therapy. Stomatitis also occurs rarely with sulfonamide therapy.
 
Agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, cyanosis, hemolytic anemia, hypoprothrombinemia, leukopenia, megaloblastic anemia, methemoglobinemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, congenital neutropenia, purpura, thrombocytopenia, or other blood dyscrasias occur infrequently during sulfasalazine therapy and can be fatal. These blood dyscrasias, characterized by fever, pale skin, sore throat, fatigue, and unusual bleeding and bruising, require discontinuation of the drug. The risk of blood dyscrasias appears to be 10 times higher in patients with arthritic disorders treated with sulfasalazine (6.1/1,000 users) compared to patients being treated for inflammatory bowel disease (0.6/1,000 users). Desensitization to sulfasalazine should not be considered in patients who have experienced agranulocytosis or aplastic anemia.
 
Skin reactions such as rash (unspecified), urticaria, and pruritus are relatively frequent occurrences during sulfasalazine treatment, but the possibility of a more serious reaction should be considered. Skin rash appears to be more common in rheumatoid arthritis patients (13%) than in patients receiving sulfasalazine for ulcerative colitis (3%). Hypersensitivity reactions including allergic myocarditis, alopecia, anaphylactoid reactions, arthralgia, conjunctival and scleral injection, pruritus, urticaria, pericarditis with or without tamponade, periorbital edema, pleuritis, polyarteritis nodosa, pulmonary eosinophilia, rhabdomyolysis, serum sickness-like reactions, interstitial lung disease, or photosensitivity may also occur. Erythema multiforme, drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), exfoliative dermatitis, lupus-like symptoms, pneumonitis with or without eosinophilia, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis with corneal damage have been reported rarely in patients (1 in 1000 patients) treated with sulfasalazine. Desensitization to sulfasalazine should not be considered in patients who have experienced anaphylactic reactions, fibrosing alveolitis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, or toxic epidermal necrolysis. During hypersensitivity reactions to sulfasalazine, hepatitis, hepatic necrosis with or without immune complexes, and fulminant hepatitis sometimes leading to liver transplantation have been observed rarely (1 in 1000 patients). In post-marketing reports, related products that are metabolized to or contain mesalamine have been reported to cause rare cases of elevated hepatic enzymes, hyperbilirubinemia, jaundice, cholestasis with jaundice, cirrhosis, and possible hepatocellular damage including hepatic necrosis and hepatic failure. In some cases, the reactions were fatal. One case of Kawasaki-syndrome, which included hepatic changes, has also been reported. Sulfasalazine also frequently causes orange-yellow skin discoloration.
 
Sulfasalazine has also been associated with acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP). The non follicular, pustular, erythematous rash starts suddenly, is associated with fever above 38 degrees C, and is distinct from pustular psoriasis, although biopsy results in each reveal spongiform subcorneal pustules. Drugs are the main cause of AGEP. A period of 2—3 weeks after an inciting drug exposure appears necessary for a first episode of AGEP. Unintentional reexposure may cause a second episode within 2 days. Clinical presentation is diverse with cutaneous lesions beyond erythema and pustules present in half of the cases. For example, bullous lesions, edema, purpura, pruritus, and mucosal erosions are possible. The mean duration of the pustules is 9.7 days followed by an annular desquamation, as long as the causative drug or factor is discontinued. The physiopathological mechanisms of AGEP have not been determined but the pathological criteria of edema, leukocytoclastic vasculitis, eosinophil exocytosis, and keratinocyte focal necrosis are distinctive. Pustule confluence or very small pustules may lead a clinician to make an incorrect diagnosis of TEN, of drug-induced erythroderma, or of staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome.
 
Oligospermia, infertility, abnormal sperm forms, and impaired sperm motility have occurred in up to 33% of men receiving sulfasalazine therapy, most likely due to the sulfapyridine metabolite. The effects on male fertility appear to be reversible upon discontinuing sulfasalazine therapy.
 
Renal adverse reactions associated with sulfasalazine and other sulfonamides occur rarely. These reactions include crystalluria, hematuria, hemolytic-uremic syndrome, interstitial nephritis, nephrotic syndrome, proteinuria, toxic nephrosis with oliguria and anuria, and urinary tract infections. Sulfasalazine also frequently causes orange-yellow urine discoloration.
 
Sulfonamides have chemical similarities to some goitrogens, diuretics (acetazolamide and the thiazides), and oral hypoglycemic agents. It is possible that cross-sensitivity exists with these agents. Goiter production, diuresis, and hypoglycemia have occurred rarely in patients receiving sulfonamides.
 
Headache is the most common central nervous system adverse reaction reported with sulfasalazine. Other adverse CNS reactions occur rarely with sulfonamides and include ataxia, drowsiness, cauda equina syndrome, Guillain-Barre syndrome, hallucinations, hearing loss, insomnia, meningitis, mental depression, peripheral neuropathy, seizures, transient lesions of the posterior spinal column, tinnitus, transverse myelitis, and vertigo.

Hepatic failure, hepatic necrosis with or without immune complexes, and hepatitis necessitating liver transplant have been observed in patients who received sulfasalazine or other sulfonamides. Elevated hepatic enzymes occurred in 4% of patients during rheumatoid arthritis studies. Anaphylactoid reactions, arthralgia, conjunctival and scleral injection, pericarditis with or without tamponade, periorbital edema, pleuritis, polyarteritis nodosa, rhabdomyolysis, serum sickness-like reactions, interstitial lung disease, fibrosing alveolitis, alopecia, and photosensitivity were observed in patients treated with sulfasalazine or other sulfonamides. Severe dermatologic reactions including erythema multiforme; drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS); Stevens-Johnson syndrome; acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP); exfoliative dermatitis; lupus-like symptoms; and toxic epidermal necrolysis with corneal damage have been reported in patients treated with sulfasalazine or other sulfonamides. Early manifestations of DRESS, such as pyrexia or lymphadenopathy, may be present even though rash is not evident. If such signs or symptoms are present, evaluate the patient immediately. Discontinue sulfasalazine if an alternative etiology for the signs or symptoms cannot be established. Desensitization to sulfasalazine should not be considered in patients who have experienced severe reactions such as anaphylactic reactions, fibrosing alveolitis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, or toxic epidermal necrolysis. In patients with ulcerative colitis, rash, urticaria, pruritus, and fever occur in approximately 1 in every 30 patients or less with sulfasalazine treatment. Those who received the drug during rheumatoid arthritis studies experienced higher incidences of rash (13%), pruritis (4%), and fever (5%). In postmarketing reports, products that contain or are metabolized to mesalamine (e.g., sulfasalazine) have been reported to cause rare cases of angioedema, hyperbilirubinemia, jaundice, cholestasis with jaundice, cirrhosis, cholestatic hepatitis, cholestasis, and pallor. One case of Kawasaki-syndrome, which included hepatic changes, has also been reported. Sulfasalazine may cause orange-yellow skin discoloration. Patients are at highest risk for serious skin reactions early in therapy, with most events occurring within the first month of treatment. Discontinue treatment with sulfasalazine at the first appearance of skin rash, mucosal lesions, or any other sign of hypersensitivity and consider further evaluation.

Description
  Text
  Sulfasalazine (salicylazosulfapyridine) is a prodrug that is metabolized by intestinal bacteria to sulfapyridine and 5-aminosalicylate (5-ASA) (mesalamine). Sulfasalazine is used in the treatment of ulcerative colitis (UC), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA). Sulfasalazine is used as a conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) in selected adults with RA and pediatric individuals with JIA. In adults, sulfasalazine is considered an option for or DMARD-naive individuals with low RA disease activity or may be used as an add-on therapy; however, the side effects often limit its utility in treating RA long-term. The local actions of 5-ASA in the colonare believed to be responsible for the effectiveness of sulfasalazine in the treatment of ulcerative colitis (UC). The American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) guidelines recommend standard-dose mesalamine (2 to 3 g/day) or diazo-bonded 5-ASAs (balsalazide or olsalazine) over sulfasalazine for induction and maintenance of remission in adults with extensive mild-to-moderate UC. Sulfasalazine remains a reasonable option for individuals in remission or those with prominent arthritic symptoms, particularly when cost is a consideration. However, sulfasalazine is associated with a higher rate of intolerance than other oral 5-ASA formulations. Individuals with moderate disease activity may benefit from early combination oral and rectal 5-ASA therapy. In individuals with suboptimal response to standard-dose therapy, high-dose oral mesalamine (greater than 3 g/day) combined with rectal mesalamine may improve induction of remission. For adults with inadequate response to optimized 5-ASA therapy, oral prednisone or budesonide MMX 9 mg/day may be used to induce remission rather than switching to another 5-ASA formulation. In mildly active ulcerative proctitis, rectal mesalamine 1 g/day is preferred for maintenance of remission over oral 5-ASA therapy. Sulfasalazine is also used in pediatric individuals 2 years of age and older for induction and maintenance of remission of UC. While oral mesalamine is often better tolerated, the availability of a liquid dosage formulation makes sulfasalazine an alternative to mesalamine for younger pediatric patients. While sulfasalazine has been used off-label for adults with Crohn disease, its use is very limited; it is only used for the symptomatic treatment of mild, colonic CD. Sulfasalazine has not been shown effective for luminal healing in CD patients, is not effective for isolated small bowel disease, and it does not effectively help prevent CD relapse. Guidelines recommend against oral 5-ASA use for induction or maintenance treatment of CD. Sulfasalazine was FDA-approved in 1950.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Sulfasalazine is metabolized to its active components, sulfapyridine and mesalamine (5-ASA), by bacteria in the colon. When given as sulfasalazine, a larger quantity of sulfapyridine and mesalamine reach the colon than when these agents are administered as single agents. Once sulfapyridine and mesalamine reach the colon, the beneficial effects result primarily from the antiinflammatory properties of mesalamine. The antiinflammatory mechanism of mesalamine is believed to occur, at least in part, through the inhibition of arachidonic acid metabolism in the bowel mucosa by inhibition of cyclooxygenase. This effectively diminishes the production of prostaglandins, thereby reducing colonic inflammation. Production of arachidonic metabolites appears to be increased in patients with inflammatory bowel disease. Mesalamine also inhibits leukotriene synthesis, possibly through the inhibition of lipoxygenase. This action has been suggested as a major component of the drug's antiinflammatory effects. Inhibition of colonic mucosal sulfidopeptide leukotriene synthesis and chemotactic stimuli for polymorphonuclear leukocytes may also occur.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Approximately 20% of an oral dose is absorbed as unchanged sulfasalazine from the small intestine. A portion of the absorbed sulfasalazine is believed to be excreted into the intestine via the bile. The remainder of the oral dose passes to the colon where the azo-linkage is cleaved by intestinal bacteria, generating sulfapyridine and 5-aminosalicylic acid (mesalamine). The majority of sulfapyridine (60% to 80%) is then absorbed, while only about 25% of 5-aminosalicylic acid is absorbed systemically. Peak serum concentrations of sulfasalazine occur within 1.5 to 6 hours of oral administration of the uncoated-tablets and within 3 to 12 hours following administration of the enteric-coated tablets. Peak serum concentrations of sulfapyridine occur within 6 to 24 hours after oral administration of the uncoated-tablets and 12 to 24 hours after the enteric-coated tablets. Mean peak serum concentrations of sulfapyridine 12 hours after a single 2 g dose are 21 mcg/mL and 13 mcg/mL for uncoated and enteric-coated tablets, respectively. Serum concentrations of 5-aminosalicylic acid range from 0 to 4 mcg/mL in patients with ulcerative colitis.

    Name
    Other
    Description
    Slow Acetylators
In the intestine, sulfasalazine is metabolized by intestinal bacteria to sulfapyridine (SP) and 5-ASA. The primary route of metabolism of SP is via acetylation to form acetylsulfapyridine (AcSP). The rate of metabolism of SP to AcSP is dependent upon acetylator phenotype. In fast acetylators, the mean plasma half-life of SP is 10.4 hours while in slow acetylators, the mean half-life is prolonged to 14.8 hours. SP can also be metabolized to 5-hydroxy-sulfapyridine (SPOH) and N-acetyl-5-hydroxy-sulfapyridine. Approximately 60% of the White population can be classified as belonging to the slow acetylator phenotype. The clinical implication is unclear; however, in a small pharmacokinetic trial where acetylator status was determined, subjects who were slow acetylators of SP showed a higher incidence of adverse events.
  Text
  Sulfasalazine is administered orally. The absorbed sulfapyridine is acetylated and hydroxylated in the liver followed by glucuronide formation. Absorbed 5-aminosalicylic acid is acetylated in the intestinal mucosa or liver. The majority of a systemically-absorbed sulfasalazine is excreted in the urine, consisting of 15% unchanged drug, 60% sulfapyridine and its metabolites, and 20% to 33% 5-ASA and its metabolites. Approximately 67% of 5-ASA is excreted in the feces. The half-life of sulfasalazine is 5.7 hours following oral administration of a single dose and 7.6 hours after the administration of multiple doses. The half-life of sulfapyridine is 6 to 14 hours, depending upon the acetylator status. Slow acetylators have an increased incidence of adverse effects, perhaps due to increased sulfapyridine concentrations.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Sulfasalazine is partially metabolized in the liver by acetylation. Due to a risk for further hepatotoxicity, only after critical appraisal of risks and benefits should sulfasalazine be given to patients with hepatic damage. No specific dosage recommendations are available. In a pharmacokinetic study, patients who were slow acetylators were noted to have an increased incidence of adverse effects.
  Renal Impairment
  Sulfasalazine and its primary metabolite, sulfapyridine are excreted by the kidney. The drug may worsen renal function in those with pre-existing impairment; no specific dosage adjustments are available. Only after critical appraisal of risks and benefits should sulfasalazine be given to patients with renal damage. Discontinue therapy if renal function deteriorates while on therapy.
