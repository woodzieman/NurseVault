Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Do not initiate rilonacept in a patient with an active or chronic infection such as hepatitis. Serious, life-threatening infections may occur (see Adverse Reactions). If a patient develops a serious infection, discontinue rilonacept. Patients with a history of recurrent infections or with underlying conditions that may predispose them to infections (e.g., patients with advanced or uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, bone marrow suppression, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection), or those who have lived in tuberculosis or histoplasmosis endemic areas may not be appropriate candidates for rilonacept therapy. Theoretically, rilonacept may increase the risk of tuberculosis or other atypical or opportunistic infections. Follow current CDC guidelines to evaluate and to treat possible latent tuberculosis infections before rilonacept initiation.
 
Limited data on the use of rilonacept in children exist. Six children and adolescents 12 to 16 years of age were treated with rilonacept in a clinical trial; improvement from baseline in their symptom scores and in objective markers of inflammation were noted. The adverse event profile among children, adolescents, and adults was similar. Rilonacept is only indicated for use by children at least 12 years of age; safety and efficacy in children below the age of 12 years have not been established. Monitor children and adolescents who receive rilonacept for growth and development. Rilonacept administration to pregnant primates may have contributed to alterations in bone ossification in the fetus. Any alteration in bone development in humans by rilonacept is unknown.
 
Rilonacept is classified in FDA pregnancy risk category C. No adequate and well-controlled studies of rilonacept in pregnant women exist. Rilonacept may cause fetal harm, as animal toxicity has been noted. Multiple fusion and absence of the ribs and thoracic vertebral bodies and arches were noted in a monkey, and all doses of rilonacept reduced serum estradiol concentrations up to 64% as compared with controls and increased the incidence of lumbar ribs as compared with both control animals and historical control incidences. In mice, a 3-fold increase in the number of stillbirths was noted. Only administer rilonacept to a pregnant woman if the benefit to the mother justifies the potential risk to the fetus.
 
It is not known if rilonacept is secreted in human breast milk. Because many proteins are secreted in breast milk, caution should be exercised if rilonacept is given to a woman who is breast-feeding her infant.
 
Cautious use of rilonacept in patients with asthma is advised. Patients with asthma may have an increased risk of infection. Upper respiratory infection and cough were common adverse effects seen in the clinical trial (see Adverse Reactions).
 
Cautious use of rilonacept in the elderly may be warranted; limited data are available. In an open-label extension study of CAPS, a 71 year-old woman developed bacterial meningitis and died. Age did not appear to have a significant effect on rilonacept steady-state trough concentrations (see Pharmacokinetics).
 
Rilonacept is expressed in recombinant Chinese hamster ovary cells and may be inappropriate for use by patients with hamster protein hypersensitivity.
 
The only recommended route of administration is subcutaneous injection; rilonacept should not be given by intravenous administration or intramuscular administration.
 
No data are available on the effects of vaccination in patients receiving rilonacept; live virus vaccines should not be given to patients receiving rilonacept. As rilonacept may interfere with normal immune response mechanisms to new antigens, vaccination may not be effective (see Drug Interactions).
 
Cautious use of rilonacept is warranted in patients with hypercholesterolemia or hypertriglyceridemia. Rilonacept treatment may cause elevations in lipid concentrations (see Adverse Reactions).

    diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, infection
    General Information
    Interleukin-1 (IL-1) blockade may interfere with the immune response to infections. The impact of treatment with rilonacept on active and/or chronic infections, such as hepatitis, is not known. Rilonacept should not be initiated in individuals with an active or chronic infection. Individuals who receive rilonacept are at increased risk for developing a serious infection. If a patient develops a serious infection, discontinue rilonacept. Individuals with a history of recurrent infections or with underlying conditions that may predispose them to infections (e.g., patients with advanced or uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, bone marrow suppression, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection), or those who have lived in tuberculosis or histoplasmosis endemic areas may not be appropriate candidates for rilonacept therapy. Medications that block IL-1, like rilonacept, may increase the risk of tuberculosis or other atypical or opportunistic infections. Follow current practice guidelines to evaluate and to treat possible latent tuberculosis infections before rilonacept initiation. In clinical studies, the incidence of infections was greater with rilonacept compared to placebo. The risk of serious infections may be increased if rilonacept is coadministered with tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Limited data on the use of rilonacept during pregnancy are insufficient to evaluate for a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. In animal studies, subcutaneous rilonacept administered to pregnant monkeys during organogenesis was complicated by antibody formation, leading to reduced drug exposure over time. Despite this, doses up to 11 times the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) showed no effects on fetal survival or malformations. Some skeletal variations, such as an increased incidence of lumbar ribs, were observed at doses approximately 2 times the MRHD and higher, with incidences slightly above historical controls. Multiple fusion and absence of the ribs and thoracic vertebral bodies and arches were observed in one fetus of the only monkey with exposure to rilonacept during the later period of gestation at a dose approximately 6 times the MRHD. However, these findings were not seen in fetuses from monkeys with higher exposures during organogenesis at roughly 11 times the MRHD, making their significance unclear. Of note, there may be risks to the pregnant person and fetus associated with Cryopyrin Associated Periodic Syndromes (CAPS). Published evidence suggests that elevated maternal interleukin (IL)-1 beta levels, which induce inflammation in CAPS, may be associated with preterm birth.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Injection site reaction appears to be the most common adverse effect of rilonacept. Of 23 adults with CAPS who received a weekly SC dose of 160 mg, 11 had an injection site reaction. Erythema, swelling, pruritus, mass, ecchymosis, inflammation, pain, edema, dermatitis, discomfort, urticaria, vesicles, warmth, rash (unspecified), and bleeding may occur. Most reactions lasted for 1—2 days and were of mild to moderate severity.Interleukin-1 blockade by rilonacept may interfere with immune response to infection. Among 23 patients who got rilonacept in the clinical trial for CAPS, 6 had an upper respiratory tract infection, 2 had sinusitis, 2 had cough, and 1 had a urinary tract infection. Serious, life-threatening infections such as bronchitis and bacterial meningitis have occurred. In placebo-controlled studies across a variety of patient populations that included 360 patients treated with rilonacept and 179 treated with placebo, the incidence of infections was 34% (2.15 per patient-exposure year) for rilonacept recipients and 27% (1.81 per patient-exposure year) for placebo recipients. The risk of infection may be greater among patients taking immunosuppressants (see Drug Interactions). If a patient develops a serious infection, discontinue rilonacept. Do not initiate rilonacept in a patient with an active or chronic infection.Rilonacept may cause a hypersensitivity reaction. If a hypersensitivity reaction occurs, discontinue rilonacept administration.Patients with CAPS who are treated with rilonacept may have increased lipid concentrations (hypercholesterolemia or hypertriglyceridemia). Cholesterol and lipid concentrations may be reduced in patients with chronic inflammation. After 6 weeks of rilonacept treatment, the mean increase from baseline for total cholesterol was 19 mg/dl, for HDL cholesterol was 2 mg/dl, for LDL cholesterol was 10 mg/dl, and for triglycerides 57 mg/dl. Monitor lipid concentrations 2—3 months after rilonacept initiation. Lipid-lowering therapies may be needed.
  Adverse Reactions
    Headline
    ecchymosis, injection site reaction, pruritus, rash, urticaria
    General Information
    Injection site reaction is the most common adverse reaction to rilonacept. Of 23 adults with cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes (CAPS) who received a weekly subcutaneous dose of 160 mg, 11 (48%) had an injection site reaction. Most reactions lasted for 1 to 2 days and were of mild to moderate severity. In patients with deficiency of IL-receptor antagonist (DIRA), 5 of 6 pediatric patients receiving rilonacept reported rash. If a hypersensitivity reaction occurs, discontinue the drug and institute appropriate treatment.

    cough, infection, meningitis, pharyngitis, rhinorrhea, sinusitis
    General Information
    Rilonacept has been associated with an increased risk of infections during clinical trials. Interleukin-1 blockade by rilonacept may interfere with immune response to infection. During study 1 of consecutive clinical studies evaluating rilonacept in adult patients with cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes (CAPS), 48% of rilonacept patients compared to 17% of placebo patients developed an infection, with upper respiratory tract infection being the most frequently reported. In study 2 part A, in which all patients received rilonacept, 20% of patients developed an infection. In study 2 part B, 18% of rilonacept patients and 22% of placebo patients developed an infection. Weather may be a factor in the differences in infection incidences; study 1 was conducted in the winter months and study 2 part B in the summer months. Among 23 patients who got rilonacept in the clinical trial for CAPS, 6 had an upper respiratory tract infection (26%), 2 had sinusitis (9%), 2 had cough (9%), and 1 had a urinary tract infection (4%). Serious, life-threatening infections such as bronchitis, mycobacterium infection (M. intracellulare), and bacterial meningitis have occurred. In placebo-controlled studies across a variety of patient populations that included 360 patients treated with rilonacept and 179 treated with placebo, the incidence of infections was 34% (2.15 per patient-exposure year) for rilonacept recipients and 27% (1.81 per patient-exposure year) for placebo recipients. In an open-label extension study, a 71-year-old woman developed S. pneumoniae meningitis and died. The risk of infection may be greater among patients taking immunosuppressants, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors. In a study of pediatric patients (n = 6) with deficiency of IL-receptor antagonist (DIRA), the most common adverse reactions included upper respiratory tract infection (6 of 6), otitis media (3 of 6), pharyngitis (3 of 6), and rhinorrhea (3 of 6). If a patient develops a serious infection, discontinue rilonacept. Do not initiate rilonacept in a patient with an active or chronic infection.

Description
  Text
  Rilonacept is an interleukin-1 (IL-1) inhibitor approved for the treatment of cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes (CAPS) including familial cold autoinflammatory syndrome (FCAS) and Muckle-Wells syndrome (MWS) in adult and pediatric patients 12 years of age and older; deficiency of IL-1 receptor antagonist (DIRA) in adult and pediatric patients weighing at least 10 kg; and recurrent pericarditis in adult and pediatric patients 12 years of age and older. IL-1 is a key cytokine that mediates the pathophysiology of many inflammatory processes; therefore, rilonacept may help mitigate the role of IL-1 in these disease states. IL-1 blockade may interfere with the immune response to infections. The impact of treatment with rilonacept on active and/or chronic infections, such as hepatitis, is not known. Rilonacept should not be initiated in patients with an active or chronic infection. Avoid the use of live vaccines in patients on rilonacept. Injection site reaction is the most common adverse reaction associated with rilonacept therapy.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Rilonacept is an interluekin-1 (IL-1) alpha and IL-1 beta cytokine trap. It blocks IL-1 signaling by acting as a soluble decoy receptor that binds both IL-1 alpha and IL-1 beta and prevents its interaction with cell surface receptors. Rilonacept also binds IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra). Structurally, rilonacept is a dimeric fusion protein that consists of the ligand-binding domains of the extracellular portions of the human IL-1 receptor component and IL-1 receptor accessory protein linked in-line to the Fc portion of human IgG1. IL-1 is a key cytokine that mediates the pathophysiology of many inflammatory processes.
 
Binding of IL-1 beta by rilonacept and thus, interruption of IL-1 beta signaling, is beneficial in patients with cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes (CAPS). In excess, IL-1 has been shown to be a key driver of inflammation in CAPS, which is caused by a range of mutations in the gene CIAS1 that encodes the protein cryopyrin. Cryopyrin binds with an intrinsic inhibitor and controls the activation of caspase-1. Caspase-1 cleaves pro-IL-1 beta and IL-18 into the biologically active forms. Patients with CAPS have increased caspase activity and thus, increased biologically active IL-1 beta. Enhanced caspase-1 activity with subsequent enhanced IL-1 beta and IL-18 release has been demonstrated in a patient with chronic infantile neurologic, cutaneous, articular (CINCA) syndrome.
 
Deficiency of IL-1 receptor antagonist (DIRA) is due to loss of function mutations in the IL1RN gene, which encodes IL-1ra, resulting in unopposed signaling of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1 alpha and IL-1 beta through the IL-1 receptor. Rilonacept binds IL-1 alpha, IL-1 beta, and IL-1ra.
 
IL-1 has been implicated as a causative factor in pericarditis. IL-1 alpha and IL-1 beta bind to universally expressed cell surface receptor, IL-1 receptor type-1, triggering a cascade of inflammatory mediators. Pre-formed IL-1 alpha is released by damaged/inflamed pericardial cells and may contribute to the maintenance and amplification of inflammation via activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, which then augments the inflammatory response by production if IL-1 beta in a cascade amplification system.

  Rilonacept is administered subcutaneously. Rilonacept has an approximate bioavailability of 50% after subcutaneous administration. In adult patients with cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes (CAPS), the terminal half-life was 8.86 days. In patients with pericarditis, the circulation half-life was approximately 7 days. Limited data is available on whether rilonacept penetrates into the brain; however, it may be unlikely to cross the blood-brain barrier due to its size (251 kDa).
 
Rilonacept receipt led to sustained reductions from baseline in mean serum C-reactive protein (CRP) and serum amyloid A to normal concentrations during the clinical trial in patients with cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes (CAPS). In most patients, symptom reduction occurred within days of rilonacept initiation. Reduction of CRP was also observed in patients with deficiency of IL-1 receptor antagonist (DIRA) and pericarditis.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP450) isoenzymes and drug transporters: various CYP isoenzymes
The formation of CYP450 enzymes is suppressed by increased concentrations of cytokines (e.g., IL-1) during chronic inflammation. Thus, it is expected that for a molecule that binds to IL-1, such as rilonacept, the formation of CYP450 enzymes could be normalized. This is clinically relevant for CYP450 substrates with a narrow therapeutic index (NTI), where the dose is individually adjusted (e.g. warfarin). Upon initiation of rilonacept in patients being treated with drugs with an NTI, perform therapeutic monitoring of the effect or drug concentration and adjust the individual dose of the medicinal product as needed.

Reconstitution
Reconstitute each vial with 2.3 mL of the supplied preservative-free sterile water for injection. Shake the vial for approximately 1 minute and let it sit for 1 minute. If the powder has not completely dissolved, shake the vial quickly back and forth for an additional 30 seconds. Let the vial sit for about 1 minute. Repeat until the solution has completely dissolved.
The reconstituted drug solution is viscous, clear, colorless to pale yellow, and essentially free from particulates. Do not use if there is discoloration or particulate matter in the solution.
The reconstituted drug solution of 80 mg/mL is sufficient to allow a withdrawal volume of up to 2 mL. Use a new syringe and needle to withdraw the dose for subcutaneous administration. Discard unused portions; no preservatives are contained in the vial or diluent.
Storage: Reconstituted product may be kept at room temperature for up to 3 hours as long as it is protected from light.
 
Subcutaneous Injection
The maximum single injection volume is 2 mL (160 mg). If the initial dose is 320 mg, then 2 injections are needed. Give both injections on same day but at 2 different sites.
Proper injection sites are the front of the thighs and the abdomen. Avoid injecting the area that is 2 inches around the navel. If someone other than the patient is administering the injection, then the upper arm may also be used. Do not inject at sites that are bruised, red, tender, or hard.
Rotate sites with each injection.
Hold the syringe in 1 hand like you would hold a pencil.
Gently squeeze and hold firm the area of cleaned skin, and insert the needle using a quick dart like motion at a 90-degree angle. For children or patients with little subcutaneous fat, insert the needle at a 45-degree angle.
After the needle is completely in the skin, release the pinched skin. Ensure that rilonacept is not injected into a blood vessel. If no blood appears, slowly inject the solution at a steady rate, pushing the plunger all the way down; this may take up to 30 seconds.
Pull the needle out of the skin and do not replace the needle cover. Place a gauze pad over the injection site for several seconds.
Discard used vials, syringes, and needles in an FDA-cleared sharps disposal container.
