Drug Class: Valbenazine is an oral pre-synaptic human vesicular monoamine transporter type 2 (VMAT2) inhibitor indicated for the treatment of tardive dyskinesia (TD) and chorea associated with Huntington's disease.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 80 mg/day PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): akathisia, anxiety, dizziness, drowsiness, dyskinesia, fatigue, headache, hypersalivation, insomnia, pseudoparkinsonism, restlessness, tremor, blurred vision, cholestasis, urinary retention, weight gain, arthralgia, back pain, infection, angioedema, pruritus, rash, urticaria

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Use caution in treating people with Huntington's disease who have a pre-existing history of depression or prior suicide attempts or ideation.

  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  Contraindications And Precautions
    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    depression, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Use caution in treating people with Huntington's disease who have a pre-existing history of depression or prior suicide attempts or ideation. People with Huntington's disease are at increased risk for depression and suicidal ideation or behaviors. Vesicular monoamine transporter-2 (VMAT2) inhibitors, including valbenazine, can further increase the risk for depression as well as suicidal ideation and behaviors in people with Huntington's disease. Balance these risks with the clinical need for treatment of chorea. Closely monitor valbenazine recipients for the emergence or worsening of depression, suicidal ideation, or unusual changes in behavior. Patients, caregivers, and families should be informed of the risks and instructed to report any mood or behavior changes of concern promptly to the treating provider.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Valbenazine can cause somnolence and sedation. Patients should be advised to avoid driving or operating machinery and performing other activities requiring coordination and concentration until they know how valbenazine affects them. The use of alcohol or coadministration with other CNS depressants may further exacerbate drowsiness associated with the medication.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Valbenazine dosage reductions are recommended for people with moderate (Child-Pugh class B) to severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure. These individuals have an increased exposure to valbenazine and its active metabolite than people with normal hepatic function.
    Headline
    parkinsonism
    General Information
    Valbenazine should be used with caution in people with parkinsonism, including Parkinson disease. Valbenazine may cause parkinsonism due to dopamine-receptor blockade, which can cause worsening of movement disorders in affected individuals. Postmarketing reports have linked VMAT-2 inhibitors to Parkinson-like symptoms, some severe and requiring hospitalization. Most cases of severe parkinsonism occurred within 2 weeks of starting or dosage escalation, and symptoms typically resolved after discontinuing valbenazine. In people who develop clinically significant parkinsonism signs or symptoms, reduce the dose or discontinue valbenazine.
    Headline
    bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, CYP2D6 poor metabolizer, heart failure, myocardial infarction, QT prolongation
    General Information
    Valbenazine use should be avoided in people with congenital long QT syndrome or bradycardia associated with a prolonged QT interval. Valbenazine may prolong the QT interval in some people, although the degree of QT prolongation is not clinically significant at concentrations expected with recommended dosing. Caution is also advised in people with unstable heart disease and those who have heart failure or a recent myocardial infarction. People with CYP2D6 poor metabolizer status (CYP2D6 PMs) or receiving medications that reduce valbenazine metabolism may have higher concentrations of valbenazine and/or its active metabolite, which may produce clinically significant QT prolongation. For patients at increased risk of QT prolongation, assess the QT interval before increasing the dose.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There is insufficient data regarding valbenazine use during pregnancy to inform a drug-associated risk. In animal studies, no malformations were observed in rats and rabbits when given doses above the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) during organogenesis. However, administration of valbenazine to pregnant rats during the time of organogenesis through lactation produced an increase in the number of stillborn pups and postnatal pup mortalities at doses less than 1 times the MRHD. Advise pregnant individuals of the potential fetal risk.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking valbenazine and for 5 days after the last dose. There are no data on the presence of valbenazine in human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effects on milk production. Valbenazine and its metabolites are present in rat milk at concentrations higher than in plasma. Based on animal data suggesting increased perinatal mortality in exposed fetuses and pups, avoidance is recommended.

Interactions
Adagrasib: (Major) Limit the maximum daily dose of valbenazine to 40 mg if concomitant use with adagrasib is necessary. Concomitant use may increase valbenazine exposure and the risk for valbenazine-related adverse effects, such as QT prolongation. Valbenazine is a CYP3A substrate and adagrasib is a strong CYP3A inhibitor. Concomitant use with another strong CYP3A inhibitor increased exposure to both valbenazine and its active metabolite by approximately 2-fold.
Amobarbital: (Major) Co-administration of strong CYP3A4 inducers, such as barbiturates, and valbenazine, a CYP3A4 substrate, is not recommended. Strong CYP3A4 inducers can decrease systemic exposure of valbenazine and its active metabolite compared to the use of valbenazine alone. Reduced exposure of valbenazine and its active metabolite may reduce efficacy.
Amoxicillin; Clarithromycin; Omeprazole: (Major) The dose of valbenazine should be reduced to 40 mg once daily during co-administration with a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor, such as clarithromycin. QT prolongation is not clinically significant at valbenazine concentrations expected with recommended dosing; however, valbenazine concentrations may be higher in patients taking a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor and QT prolongation may become clinically significant.
Apalutamide: (Major) Avoid concomitant use of valbenazine and apalutamide. Concurrent use may decrease the exposure of valbenazine and its active metabolite which may reduce valbenazine efficacy. Valbenazine is a CYP3A substrate and apalutamide is a strong CYP3A inducer.     General Information
    In the postmarketing setting, neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) has been reported in patients taking vesicular monoamine transporter-2 (VMAT2) inhibitors, including valbenazine. Description
  Text
  Valbenazine is an oral pre-synaptic human vesicular monoamine transporter type 2 (VMAT2) inhibitor indicated for the treatment of tardive dyskinesia (TD) and chorea associated with Huntington's disease. The efficacy of valbenazine in treating TD was shown in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial (the KINECT 3 trial). After 6 weeks, adult subjects who received valbenazine had improvement in the severity of abnormal involuntary movements, as measured by the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS). The efficacy of valbenazine for the treatment of chorea associated with Huntington's disease was observed in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 KINECT-HD study, as well as an open-label extension trial. Following 12 weeks of treatment, subjects receiving valbenazine had a statistically significant reduction in chorea severity as indicated by changes in scores on the Unified Huntington's Disease Rating Scale Total Maximal Chorea (UHDRS TMC) assessments. Somnolence and sedation are common adverse effects of valbenazine for the treatment of TD; urticaria, rash, and insomnia were also noted to occur more frequently with valbenazine treatment than placebo in trials for treatment of chorea associated with Huntington's disease. QT prolongation may occur during coadministration with certain medications and in individuals who are poor metabolizers of CYP2D6. Valbenazine was FDA-approved in April 2017.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The mechanism of action of valbenazine in the treatment of tardive dyskinesia and chorea in patients with Huntington's disease is unknown, but is thought to be mediated through the reversible inhibition of vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2), a transporter that regulates monoamine uptake from the cytoplasm to the synaptic vesicle for storage and release. The active metabolite of valbenazine, [+]-alpha-dihydrotetrabenazine ([+]-alpha-HTBZ), also binds with relatively high affinity to human VMAT2. The drug and its active metabolite cause an inhibition of dopamine release via this mechanism. Valbenazine has no appreciable binding affinity for VMAT1. Valbenazine and [+]-alpha-HTBZ have no appreciable binding affinity for dopaminergic (including D2), serotonergic (including 5-HT2B), adrenergic, histaminergic, or muscarinic receptors.

Following oral administration, the Cmax is attained in 0.5 to 1 hour. Steady-state plasma concentrations are reached within 1 week. The absolute oral bioavailability of valbenazine is about 49%.   Text
  Valbenazine is administered orally. The mean steady state Vd is 92 L. The plasma protein binding of valbenazine and its active metabolite are greater than 99% and approximately 64%, respectively. Valbenazine is extensively metabolized by hydrolysis to form the active metabolite [+]-alpha-dihydrotetrabenazine ([+]-alpha-HTBZ) and by oxidative metabolism, primarily by CYP3A4/5, to form other minor metabolites. The active metabolite appears to be further metabolized in part by CYP2D6. Valbenazine and [+]-alpha-HTBZ have half-lives of 15 to 22 hours, respectively. Approximately 60% of a dose was recovered in urine and 30% of a dose was recovered in feces. Less than 2% is excreted as unchanged valbenazine or its active metabolite in either urine or feces.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4, CYP2D6, P-gp
Valbenazine is a substrate of CYP3A4 and CYP3A5. The active metabolite [+]-alpha-HTBZ appears to be further metabolized in part by CYP2D6. Valbenazine appears to inhibit intestinal P-gp, as evidenced by a drug interaction study with digoxin. In vitro data indicate that valbenazine and its active metabolite are unlikely to inhibit CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2E1 or CYP3A4/5, BCRP, OAT1, OAT3, OCT2, OATP1B1, or OATP1B3 or induce CYP1A2, CYP2B6 or CYP3A4/5 at clinically relevant concentrations.
