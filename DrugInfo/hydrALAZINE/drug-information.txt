Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Hydralazine may produce a clinical picture simulating systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) including glomerulonephritis. Complete blood counts and antinuclear antibody titer determinations are indicated before and periodically during prolonged therapy, even if the patient does not have any symptoms; these tests are also indicated if a patients develops arthralgia, fever, chest pain, continued malaise, or other unexplained symptoms. Hydralazine is metabolized by acetylation. Patients with higher plasma concentrations are more likely to develop a lupus-like syndrome than the general population. Examples of patients with high plasma concentrations include those who have 'slow acetylation', those with renal disease that causes decreased renal function, or those receiving high doses (i.e., >= 300 mg/day) (see Adverse Reactions). If high doses of hydralazine are required to achieve hypotensive goals, consider lowering the dose of hydralazine and combining with other antihypertensive agents. It is difficult to provide guidelines regarding administration of hydralazine to patients with preexisting systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Although hydralazine has been administered safely to these patients, with no exacerbation of underlying disease, and the mechanism of hydralazine-induced lupus syndrome appears to be distinct from idiopathic lupus, hydralazine should nevertheless be used cautiously in this population. If hydralazine-induced SLE occurs, the drug should be discontinued, unless the benefit of continued antihypertensive therapy with this drug outweighs the risk. Signs and symptoms of lupus usually regress when the drug is discontinued; however, residual signs and symptoms have been detected many years later. Long-term treatment with systemic steroids may be necessary.
 
The dosage of hydralazine should be modified in patients with renal failure or severe renal impairment with a CrCl < 10 ml/min (see dosage). Elderly patients are more sensitive to the orthostatic hypotensive effects of hydralazine, and are also more likely to have age-associated renal impairment. Initiation of a reduced dosage, or beginning at the lower end of the adult dosage range (see adult dosage) is prudent.
 
Hydralazine is contraindicated in patients with mitral valve rheumatic heart disease because it can increase pulmonary artery pressure. Additionally, it has been implicated in causing angina and myocardial infarction secondary to reflex sympathetic nervous system stimulation (i.e., reflex tachycardia); therefore, hydralazine is contraindicated in patients with coronary artery disease as reflex tachycardia increases myocardial oxygen demand and can aggravate angina and ischemia and precipitate acute myocardial infarction. Use hydralazine cautiously in patients with an aortic aneurysm. Because hydralazine can cause sodium and fluid retention, its use is generally not recommended in patients with congestive heart failure, although it has been used in patients with intractable left ventricular dysfunction. However, when used with isosorbide dinitrate, this 2-drug combination is considered to be an appropriate alternative to patients who cannot tolerate standard heart failure therapy, mainly angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors. Furthermore, combination therapy of isosorbide dinitrate and hydralazine, in conjunction with standard therapy, has been shown to improve mortality, rate of first hospitalizations, and quality of life in black patients; a fixed-dose combination of isosorbide dinitrate and hydralazine (BiDIl) is FDA-approved for the treatment of heart failure in black patients.
 
Use hydralazine cautiously in patients with acute stroke or cerebral vascular accident as hydralazine in this setting can further worsen brain function.
 
Hydralazine is classified as pregnancy risk category C. Although no adequate human studies have examined the effects of this drug on the fetus, animal reproduction studies have shown hydralazine to have adverse fetal effects. Therefore, in making the decision to administer this drug during pregnancy, the potential risks to the fetus must be weighed against the potential benefits to the mother. Historically, hydralazine has been regarded as the antihypertensive of choice during preeclampsia ; however, most recent evidence indicates that other drugs such as labetalol and/or calcium channel blockers are associated with improved maternal and fetal outcomes and should be considered before hydralazine. In a meta-analysis on the use of hydralazine for preeclampsia, hydralazine was associated with more maternal hypotension, placental abruption, cesarean section, maternal oliguria, adverse fetal heart rates, and lower Apgar scores at one minute when compared with labetalol or nifedipine. It should be noted that adverse effects associated with hydralazine, such as headache, nausea, and vomiting, can mimic deteriorating pre-eclampsia.
 
Hydralazine is distributed into human milk. However, the American Academy of Pediatrics considers hydralazine to be generally compatible with breast-feeding.
 
The safety and effectiveness of hydralazine in children and infants has not been established in controlled clinical trials, although there is clinical experience with the use of hydralazine in these populations. In recent recommendations and guidelines, hydralazine is listed as a possible drug for the treatment of hypertension or hypertensive emergencies in children.
 
Some preparations of hydralazine hydrochloride contain sulfites and should be used with caution in patients with a known sulfite hypersensitivity because they could precipitate severe allergic reactions including life-threatening asthmatic attacks or anaphylaxis.
 
Certain preparations of hydralazine, including Apresoline 100 mg tablets, contain tartrazine dye and can cause allergic responses in patients who are hypersensitive to this dye. Although the incidence of tartrazine dye hypersensitivity is low, it can be serious, so caution should be used when administering the drug to patients with tartrazine hypersensitivity or with aspirin sensitivity.
 
Hydralazine can induce significant drowsiness, impaired cognition or asthenia, particularly during the initiation of treatment. Patients should use caution when driving or operating machinery until they are aware of the effects of the medication.

    Headline
    coronary artery disease, heart failure, mitral valve disease, mitral valve rheumatic heart disease
    General Information
    Hydralazine is contraindicated in individuals with mitral valve rheumatic heart disease and should be used with caution in individuals with mitral valve disease because it can increase pulmonary artery pressure. Additionally, it has been implicated in causing angina and myocardial infarction secondary to reflex sympathetic nervous system stimulation (i.e., reflex tachycardia); therefore, hydralazine is contraindicated in individuals with coronary artery disease as reflex tachycardia increases myocardial oxygen demand, which may aggravate angina and ischemia and precipitate acute myocardial infarction. Although hydralazine has been used in individuals with intractable left ventricular dysfunction, it is generally not recommended in patients with heart failure because it can cause sodium and fluid retention. Guidelines recommend hydralazine in combination with isosorbide dinitrate in self-identified African American patients with NYHA class III or IV heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) receiving optimal medication therapy. Hydralazine in combination with isosorbide dinitrate may also be considered in individuals with HFrEF who are unable to receive an angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (ARNi), ACE inhibitor, or ARB due to intolerance or renal impairment. Use of hydralazine without a nitrate for the treatment of hypertension in patients with heart failure is not recommended. Due to hydralazine-related sodium and fluid retention and reflex tachycardia, guidelines recommend coadministration with a diuretic and a beta-blocker when used for the treatment of resistant hypertension.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    In individuals with hypertension with normal renal function who are treated with hydralazine, there is evidence of increased renal blood flow and a maintenance of glomerular filtration rate. In some instances where control values were below normal, improved renal function has been noted after administration of hydralazine; however, as with any antihypertensive agent, hydralazine should be used with caution in individuals with advanced renal impairment or failure. Some references state to consider extended dose intervals in individuals with renal impairment (CrCl 10 to 50 mL.minute) and renal failure (CrCl less than 10 mL/minute).
    Headline
    stroke
    General Information
    Use hydralazine with caution in individuals with acute stroke or cerebral vascular accident, as hydralazine can further worsen brain function in this setting.
    Headline
    females, HLA-DR4-positive, NAT2 slow acetylator, White patients
    General Information
    Hydralazine may produce a drug-induced lupus-like syndrome, including glomerulonephritis (i.e., drug-induced lupus nephritis), and systemic vasculitis. Females, White patients, older individuals, and people receiving high daily doses of hydralazine or therapy for more than 3 months are at higher risk of developing hydralazine-induced lupus. Individuals who are a NAT2 slow acetylator or are HLA-DR4-positive are also at an increased risk of hydralazine-induced lupus. If symptoms develop, discontinue hydralazine unless benefits of continued therapy outweigh the risks to the individual. Symptoms usually regress following hydralazine discontinuation; however, residual symptoms have been detected in some individuals many years later. Complete blood counts and antinuclear antibody titer determinations are indicated before and periodically during prolonged therapy, even if the individual does not have any symptoms; these tests are also indicated if an individual develops arthralgia, fever, chest pain, continued malaise, or other unexplained symptoms.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Guidelines recommend parenteral hydralazine for urgent control of acute severe hypertension during pregnancy. Clinical experience has not identified adverse fetal effects with use of oral or parenteral hydralazine, but there are no well-controlled studies evaluating its use during human pregnancy. Higher or more frequent doses of parenteral hydralazine are associated with hypotension and headaches in the pregnant person and abnormal fetal heart rate tracings; these adverse effects may be more common with hydralazine compared to other agents. In animal studies, hydralazine was teratogenic in mice at 20 to 30 times the maximum daily human dose (MDHD) of 200 to 300 mg and possibly in rabbits at 10 to 15 times the MDHD. Teratogenic effects included cleft palate and malformations of facial and cranial bones. Hydralazine was not teratogenic in rats.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Hydralazine is compatible with breast-feeding. Hydralazine is present in human milk in clinically insignificant amounts, with a relative infant dose of 1.2%. Due to limited exposure via human milk, no side effects are expected in the breast-fed child.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The most common adverse effects of hydralazine administration are headache, angina pectoris, flushing, palpitations, sinus tachycardia, anorexia, nausea/vomiting, and diarrhea. Initiation of therapy with low doses and a gradual increase until a satisfactory antihypertensive effect is obtained will help to minimize adverse reactions.
 
Hydralazine has been used in pregnancy for the treatment of hypertension associated with severe pre-eclampsia. It should be noted that adverse effects associated with hydralazine, such as headache, nausea, and vomiting, can mimic deteriorating pre-eclampsia.
 
Hydralazine is a potent vasodilator and can result in hypotension including orthostatic hypotension, dizziness, syncope, reflex sinus tachycardia, peripheral vasodilation, peripheral edema, and fluid retention. Due to the pharmacology of hydralazine, complications of excessive dosage (or overdosage) with hydralazine can include hypotension, sinus tachycardia, angina, myocardial ischemia and myocardial infarction.
 
Less frequently occurring gastrointestinal adverse effects with hydralazine are constipation and paralytic ileus.
 
Several drugs are implicated in producing lupus-like symptoms. Hydralazine is the most frequent offender in this category. Hydralazine is metabolized by acetylation. The patient's acetylation phenotype will affect the plasma concentration of hydralazine. Patients who are 'slow acetylators' as well as patients with decreased renal function are more likely than fast acetylators to develop lupus-like symptoms. Patients with decreased renal function, patients receiving more than 200 mg/day, and patients with a family history of auto-immune disease, are also more likely to develop this condition. Although some manifestations are similar, drug-induced lupus occurs via a different mechanism than does idiopathic disease. Lupus-like symptoms, manifested by fever, chills, sore throat, myalgia, arthralgia, pericarditis, pleuritis, cutaneous vasculitis (skin blisters), rash (unspecified), pruritus, urticaria, and erythema can occur with hydralazine. Patients should be instructed to report adverse symptoms of joint pain, muscle pain, angina, fever or continued weakness to their physician. If hydralazine-induced lupus syndrome is suspected, the drug should be discontinued.
 
Although uncommon, hydralazine can cause a glomerulonephritis that is probably immune-mediated. Symptoms associated with glomerulonephritis include azotemia, proteinuria, urinary casts, and hematuria.
 
Hydralazine-induced fluid retention can cause edema and weight gain. These effects usually can be alleviated by concomitant administration of a thiazide diuretic.
 
Hydralazine can cause peripheral neuropathy, evidenced by paresthesias (numbness, pain, and tingling in the hands and feet). Significant increases in urinary xanthurenic acid may indicate pyridoxine deficiency. An antipyridoxine effect has been suggested and if symptoms do develop, the addition of pyridoxine to the regimen is suggested.
 
Blood dyscrasias such as agranulocytosis, leukopenia, and purpura have been reported. Complete blood counts should be undertaken before therapy with hydralazine and periodically thereafter, especially during prolonged therapy. Hydralazine therapy should be discontinued if there is evidence of blood dyscrasias.
 
There have been occasional reports of dyspnea, lacrimation, muscle cramping, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and nasal congestion with hydralazine therapy.
 
Hydralazine can induce significant drowsiness, impaired cognition or asthenia, particularly during the initiation of treatment. Patients should use caution when driving or operating machinery until they are aware of the effects of the medication.

Description
  Text
  Hydralazine is an oral and parenteral antihypertensive agent. Because it is a peripheral vasodilator, hydralazine also has been used to treat congestive heart failure, although ACE inhibitors have largely replaced hydralazine for this use. In those patients that cannot tolerate ACE inhibitors or angiotensin II blockers, hydralazine in combination with isosorbide dinitrate is an acceptable alternative for the treatment of heart failure. Hydralazine was one of the earliest orally active antihypertensive agents and has been commonly used for hypertension during pregnancy. Hydralazine also has been used to treat pulmonary hypertension, but the actions are unpredictable, causing either improved hemodynamic status or symptomatic hypotension. More recently, hydralazine's use in the general population has declined due to adverse effects, tachyphylaxis, and the release of newer and better-tolerated agents. In addition, injectable hydralazine has historically been used as a first-line agent in the treatment of hypertensive emergencies and urgencies including preeclampsia; however, due to unpredictable falls in blood pressure, a prolonged duration of action (up to 12 hours or more), and adverse effects, its use as a first-line agent for these indications is being replaced by newer and more predictable injectable antihypertensives.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Hydralazine is a peripheral vasodilator; it causes relaxation of arteriolar smooth muscle via a direct effect. Although stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system is associated with hydralazine administration, this is a compensatory response and not a component of its mechanism. The molecular explanation of hydralazine's mechanism is not fully understood; however, similar to organic nitrates and nitroprusside, it is thought that hydralazine interferes with the calcium movements within vascular smooth muscle that are responsible for initiating and maintaining the contractile state. In contrast to organic nitrates and sodium nitroprusside, however, hydralazine is selective for arterioles. The peripheral vasodilating effects of hydralazine result in decreased arterial blood pressure (diastolic more than systolic) and peripheral vascular resistance. In addition, the hydralazine-induced reflex autonomic response increases heart rate, stroke volume, cardiac output, and left ventricular ejection fraction. The preferential dilation of arterioles, as compared to veins, minimizes postural hypotension and promotes the increase in cardiac output even though the hypotensive effects of hydralazine are diminished somewhat by this increase in cardiac output. There is also evidence suggesting hydralazine exerts a positive inotropic effect on the failing human ventricle.
 
Animal and limited human data indicate that nitric oxide (NO) may be generated from hydralazine that also has an antioxidant quality to enhance the effects of nitrates and to mitigate the tolerance associated with chronic nitrate therapy. The antioxidant effect of hydralazine can be attributed to its ability in inhibiting oxidase formation. The accumulation of oxidative free radicals creates an environment where chronic reductions in NO bioavailability contribute to a loss of skeletal muscle microvessels. This effect, in turn, leads to impaired muscle perfusion with elevated metabolic demand. Studies show that treatment with hydralazine markedly inhibits oxidase which plays a role in regulating the bioactivity of NO, produced either endogenously or when administered exogenously.
 
Cerebral, coronary, splanchnic, and renal blood flow usually increase following administration of hydralazine, while urinary parameters are generally unaffected. Hydralazine increases renin activity in plasma, presumably by the renal juxtaglomerular cells in response to sympathetic nervous system stimulation; the increase in renin activity leads to the production of angiotensin II, which stimulates aldosterone and thus, sodium reabsorption. Due to fluid retention, plasma volume increases. As a result, tolerance can develop, which may account for the absence of improvement in some patients receiving the drug for prolonged periods of time.
 
As an antihypertensive, hydralazine does not lead to improvements in LVH. Hydralazine may actually worsen LVH, potentially due to reflex tachycardia and sympathetic stimulation, which may counteract the benefits of afterload reduction.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Although hydralazine is approximately 90% absorbed following oral administration, systemic bioavailability is considerably lower after oral versus intramuscular or intravenous administration due to extensive first-pass metabolism. Oral bioavailability is also dependent on the acetylation phenotype of the patient, and it is approximately 50% in 'slow' acetylators and 30% in 'fast' acetylators. Although food in the gut enhances absorption of hydralazine, food-related reductions in hydralazine blood levels have been associated with reduced antihypertensive effects, possibly due to an increase in intravascular conversion of the drug to hydralazine pyruvic acid hydrazone. For this reason, it has been suggested that patients take this medication at a fixed time in relationship to meals. Peak hydralazine plasma concentration is reached in 1 to 2 hours. Hypotensive effects occur 20 to 30 minutes following an oral dose. The antihypertensive effects of an oral hydralazine dose last about 2 to 4 hours.
    Name
    Intravenous Route
    Description
    Hypotensive effects occur 5 to 30 minutes after an IV dose. The antihypertensive effects of an IV dose last 2 to 6 hours on average, although the effects of a parenteral dose can last up to 12 hours; the affinity of hydralazine for arterial walls may partially explain the prolonged effect.

    Intramuscular Route
    Description
    Hypotensive effects occur 10 to 30 minutes after an IM dose. The antihypertensive effects of an IM dose last 2 to 6 hours on average, although the effects of a parenteral dose can last up to 12 hours possibly due to the affinity of hydralazine for arterial walls.

  Text
  Hydralazine is administered orally and parenterally. Hydralazine distributes throughout the body tissues and has a particularly high affinity for arterial walls. The drug crosses the placenta and distributes in breast milk, but not to a clinically significant degree. Plasma protein binding is about 87%. Hydralazine is extensively hepatically metabolized, and plasma levels can vary due to polymorphic acetylation. Both the unchanged drug (25%) and its metabolites are excreted in the urine and feces.
