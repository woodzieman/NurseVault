Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Primaquine is contraindicated in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or rheumatoid arthritis because these conditions can lead to granulocytopenia. Prior to prescribing primaquine, physicians should completely familiarize themselves with the current prescribing information for primaquine.
 
Primaquine is associated with a number of adverse hematological effects, and patients with preexisting hematological disease can develop hemolytic anemia. Hemolysis appears to be dose-related and race-dependent when coupled with G6PD deficiency. Patients with a personal or familial history of favism or G6PD deficiency are more likely to develop hemolytic anemia and should not be treated with primaquine. Areas of high prevalence of G6PD deficiency are Africa, Southern Europe, Mediterranean region, Middle East, South-East Asia, and Oceania; therefore, people from these regions have a greater tendency to develop hemolytic anemia while receiving primaquine and related drugs. Test patients for G6PD deficiency; document G6PD level in the normal range before primaquine use. Any condition involving bone marrow suppression also should be treated with caution. Primaquine should be used with caution in patients with NADH methemoglobin reductase deficiency because methemoglobinemia can develop. Close monitoring of the patient should be undertaken, with particular attention to possible darkening of the urine, changes in leukocyte counts, or reduction in hemoglobin levels. Discontinuation of the drug is indicated immediately if adverse reactions occur. Prior to prescribing primaquine, physicians should completely familiarize themselves with the current prescribing information for primaquine.
 
Primaquine is contraindicated for use with quinacrine. Antimalarial compounds structurally related to primaquine appear to have their toxic effects potentiated by quinacrine. Primaquine should also not be given to patients who have received quinacrine recently since toxicity is increased.
 
Although no reports have linked primaquine with congenital defects, primaquine is not recommended for use during pregnancy (FDA pregnancy risk category C). Primaquine crosses the placenta and the fetus could develop hemolytic anemia if G6PD-deficient. It is not known if primaquine is excreted into breast milk, and safe use during breast-feeding has not been established. No problems have been documented in humans during nursing. Prior to prescribing primaquine, physicians should completely familiarize themselves with the current prescribing information for primaquine.
 
Primaquine should not be used in patients with a known 8-aminoquinolines hypersensitivity or primaquine hypersensitivity. For example, patients with iodoquinol hypersensitivity (an 8-aminoquinoline) may have cross sensitivity to primaquine. Other 8-aminoquinolines can include chloroxine, pamaquine, and pentaquine. Prior to prescribing primaquine, physicians should completely familiarize themselves with the current prescribing information for primaquine.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in individuals with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Primaquine is also contraindicated in individuals with known hypersensitivity to other 8-aminoquinolones.
    Headline
    G6PD deficiency, hemolytic anemia, leukopenia, methemoglobin reductase deficiency, methemoglobinemia, severe G6PD deficiency, unknown G6PD status
    General Information
    Primaquine is contraindicated in individuals with severe G6PD deficiency. However, guidelines suggest that primaquine may be considered at a reduced dose with close monitoring for hemolysis in G6PD deficient individuals for the treatment of P. vivax or P. ovale malaria for radical cure of liver-stage infections. Due to the risk of hemolytic anemia in individuals with G6PD deficiency, G6PD testing must be performed prior to use. In cases of mild to moderate G6PD deficiency, assess the risks and benefits of using primaquine. If primaquine use is to be considered, obtain baseline hematocrit and hemoglobin before treatment, and close hematological monitoring (e.g., at Days 3 and 8) is recommended. For individuals with intermediate G6PD deficiency and treatment of P. vivax or P. ovale malaria for radical cure of liver-stage infections, consider using primaquine at a reduced dose with close monitoring for hemolysis. A single low dose may also be considered without the need for extra monitoring as a gametocytocide in individuals with G6PD deficiency for treatment of P. falciparum malaria. In individuals with G6PD deficiency with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia (CNSHA), guidelines suggest avoiding primaquine. If unknown G6PD status and testing is not available, assess the risks and benefits of treatment and risk factors for G6PD deficiency. Ensure adequate medical support to manage hemolytic risk is available. Areas of high prevalence of G6PD deficiency are Africa, Southern Europe, Mediterranean region, Middle East, South-East Asia, and Oceania. Individuals from these regions have a greater tendency to develop hemolytic anemia (due to a congenital deficiency of erythrocytic G6PD) while receiving primaquine. Moderate to severe hemolytic reactions may also occur in individuals with a family or personal history of favism. Closely observe an individual who has shown a previous idiosyncratic reaction to primaquine as manifested by hemolytic anemia, methemoglobinemia, or leukopenia or a family or personal history of hemolytic anemia or nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) methemoglobin reductase deficiency if primaquine is to be used. In G6PD normal individuals, it is also advisable to perform routine blood examinations (particularly blood cell counts and hemoglobin determinations) during therapy. In all individuals, discontinue primaquine immediately if marked darkening of the urine or sudden decrease in hemoglobin concentration or leukocyte count occurs.
    Headline
    bradycardia, cardiac disease, cardiomyopathy, congenital long QT syndrome, coronary artery disease, females, geriatric, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, QT prolongation
    General Information
    Use primaquine with caution in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk. Additional risk factors may include cardiac disease or a history of ventricular arrhythmias. Monitor ECG if primaquine is used in these individuals.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Primaquine is contraindicated during pregnancy. Safe use of primaquine during pregnancy has not been established. Primaquine may cause hemolytic anemia in a glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)-deficient fetus even if the pregnant person has normal concentrations of G6PD. Animal data have demonstrated reproductive and embryofetal developmental toxicity. Nonclinical data from studies conducted in bacteria and animals treated with primaquine show evidence of gene mutations and chromosomal/DNA damage, teratogenicity, and injury to embryos and developing fetuses.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Primaquine is contraindicated during breast-feeding when the child is found to be glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)-deficient or if G6PD status is unknown. Determine the child's G6PD status before breast-feeding begins. A breast-fed child with G6PD deficiency is at risk for hemolytic anemia from exposure to primaquine. The presence of primaquine and its major metabolite in human milk and the plasma of children exposed via breast-milk was evaluated in 21 G6PD-normal lactating individuals and their G6PD-normal children aged 28 days or older. After maternal administration of 0.5 mg/kg/day primaquine base for 14 days, low concentrations of primaquine and carboxyprimaquine were present in milk and the child's plasma. The estimated ingested dose was less than 1% of a 0.5 mg/kg/day primaquine base dose determined from an observed milk to maternal plasma AUC ratio of 0.34 (range: 0.12 to 0.64) and assuming milk consumption of 150 mL/kg/day. Plasma primaquine concentrations were below measurement thresholds (2.28 nanograms/mL) in all but 1 capillary plasma sample (2.6 nanograms/mL); carboxyprimaquine plasma concentrations were also unmeasurable in the majority of samples. There are no data on the effects of primaquine on the breast-fed child or its effects on milk production. Because the quantity of antimalarial drugs present in human milk is insufficient to provide adequate protection against malaria, a child who requires chemoprophylaxis must receive the recommended dosages of antimalarial drugs.
    Headline
    CYP2D6 intermediate metabolizer, CYP2D6 poor metabolizer
    General Information
    Primaquine activity probably depends on the formation of CYP2D6 metabolite(s) based on published nonclinical data. Limited clinical data report higher primaquine treatment failure rates in a CYP2D6 poor metabolizer or CYP2D6 intermediate metabolizer than in individuals with normal/extensive metabolizer status. Therefore, CYP2D6 polymorphism or drugs that impact CYP2D6 activity may be associated with variability in clinical response to primaquine. Consider alternative treatment to primaquine for a CYP2D6 poor or intermediate metabolizer. In case of treatment failure, after confirming compliance with primaquine treatment, reassess the use of CYP2D6 inhibitors and assess the individual's CYP2D6 status, if feasible.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with primaquine. Primaquine may cause fetal harm. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception during treatment and for 6 months after stopping treatment. For those that can cause pregnancy in others, contraception should be used during treatment and for 3 months after the last dose.

Description
  Text
  Description: Primaquine is an oral antimalarial agent chemically related to chloroquine. Primaquine is used for the treatment of malaria caused by Plasmodium vivax, P. ovale, and P. falciparum. However, a blood schizonticidal agent, such as chloroquine, should be used in conjunction with primaquine for treatment of P. ovale and P. vivax malaria, since primaquine is not effective against asexual erythrocytic forms. Primaquine was approved by the FDA in 1951. More recently, primaquine has been identified as an alternative agent for the treatment of mild-to-moderate Pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP) when administered in combination with oral clindamycin.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: Primaquine's exact mechanism of action has not been fully elucidated. Unlike other antimalarials, primaquine is a tissue schizonticidal agent used in the treatment of malaria caused by P. vivax and P. ovale and is also effective against gametocytes of P. falciparum. Primaquine is effective against the preerythrocytic and exoerythrocytic forms of P. falciparum, P. malariae, P. oval, and P. vivax. It is believed that action stems from the drug's ability to interfere with the function of plasmodial DNA. Activity of primaquine against gametocytes of P. falciparum disrupts transmission of the disease.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    The oral bioavailability of primaquine is more than 70% in healthy subjects. After single oral dosing, the AUC and Cmax of primaquine increase approximately dose-proportionally over a primaquine base dose range of 15 to 45 mg. In subjects with P. vivax malaria receiving primaquine base 15 mg once daily, the primaquine AUC and Cmax were 0.48 mcg/mL x hour and 50.7 nanograms/mL on Day 1 and 0.49 mcg/mL x hour and 49.7 nanograms/mL on Day 14, respectively, and the carboxyprimaquine AUC0-24 and Cmax were 5.15 mcg/mL x hour and 291 nanograms/mL on Day 1 and 7.24 mcg/mL x hour and 432 nanograms/mL on Day 14, respectively. Peak plasma concentration is achieved in 2.3 hours (Tmax). Administration of primaquine with food increased the AUC by 14% and Cmax by 26%.

    Description
    CYP2D6 Intermediate and Poor Metabolizers
Published clinical reports indicate that primaquine is a substrate of CYP2D6, and nonclinical studies suggest that its antimalarial activity depends on the formation of CYP2D6-mediated metabolites. Genetic polymorphisms in CYP2D6 affect metabolic activity; individuals who are CYP2D6 poor metabolizers have little to no CYP2D6 function, while intermediate metabolizers have reduced activity. Compared with CYP2D6 normal metabolizers, individuals who are CYP2D6 intermediate or poor metabolizers exhibit a prolonged primaquine half-life and increased plasma concentrations, which may contribute to variability in clinical response to primaquine.
  Text
  Primaquine is administered orally. Primaquine is 74% bound to human plasma proteins, primarily alpha 1 acid glycoprotein, with a Vd of 243 L. Primaquine undergoes oxidative deamination by monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A), hydroxylation of the quinolone ring by CYP2D6, and direct conjugation. Nonclinical studies indicate that primaquine activity may be dependent on the formation of CYP2D6 metabolites. Primaquine is primarily eliminated through metabolic pathways; 64% of the dose is excreted in the urine, including 3.6% as primaquine with the remaining being metabolites other than carboxyprimaquine. Carboxyprimaquine, the main circulating metabolite, is subjected to further metabolism and not eliminated through the urine. The apparent clearance (CL/F) of primaquine is 37.6 L/hour. The mean terminal half-life of primaquine is 5.6 hours, and the mean terminal half-life of carboxyprimaquine is approximately 22 hours.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP1A2, CYP2D6, CYP3A, P-gp
Primaquine is a CYP2D6 substrate. In vitro data indicate that primaquine has the potential to inhibit CYP1A2 and the P-gp transporter. Published clinical reports also suggest that primaquine may inhibit CYP3A4, although the magnitude of this effect is unknown. Primaquine is not expected to meaningfully inhibit other CYP450 enzymes.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; however primaquine is extensively metabolized by the liver. Use with caution in patients with hepatic impairment.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available; it appears that dosage adjustments are not necessary.
