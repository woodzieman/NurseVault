Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Because patients may experience elevations in hepatic enzymes during entecavir treatment, the manufacturer recommends periodic monitoring of hepatic function during therapy. In addition, severe, acute exacerbations of hepatitis B (defined as ALT elevations > 10 times the upper limit of normal and > 2 times baseline) have been reported in patients who have discontinued anti-hepatitis B therapy, including entecavir. During phase 3 clinical trials, a subset of patients were allowed to discontinue treatment at 52 weeks if they achieved a protocol-defined response to therapy. Six percent of nucleoside naive patients (1% of HBeAg-positive and 8% of HBeAg-negative patients) experienced hepatitis B exacerbations; the median time to exacerbation was 23—24 weeks after therapy discontinuation. Hepatic function should be monitored closely for at least several months after patients discontinue anti-hepatitis B therapy. If appropriate, initiation of anti-hepatitis B therapy may be warranted.
 
Patients co-infected with hepatitis B virus (HBV) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection should not receive entecavir unless they are receiving concomitant treatment for HIV. Entecavir has not been evaluated in HIV/HBV co-infected patients who were not simultaneously receiving effective HIV treatment. Limited clinical data indicate that in patients co-infected with HIV and HBV who are not receiving adequate treatment for HIV, the use of entecavir to treat HBV may induce resistant HIV strains. A man co-infected with HIV and HBV who received entecavir had selection of an HIV variant containing the M184V resistance substitution. At the time of entecavir initiation for HBV, he was not receiving highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) for HIV, though he was clinically stable in regard to his HIV infection. Within 2 months of entecavir initiation, the HBV DNA decreased by approximately 5.5 log10 IU/ml, and the HIV RNA decreased to approximately 2,000 copies/ml which was below baseline concentrations. While no HIV resistance was detected before entecavir receipt, the M184V substitution was detected 6 months after the initiation of entecavir therapy. HIV resistance may develop in a co-infected patient who is not receiving HAART and is taking entecavir. In contrast, among 68 patients taking lamivudine-containing HAART for HIV with stable HIV viral loads and CD4 counts, no differences in the HIV RNA concentrations or CD4 counts were noted for patients who got either entecavir or placebo for 24 weeks for concurrent HBV infection. Before initiating entecavir in HIV/HBV co-infected patients who are currently receiving treatment for HIV, offer the patient HIV antibody testing in order to monitor for the development of treatment resistant-HIV strains. Entecavir has not been studied, and is not recommended, for the treatment of HIV infection.
 
The safety and effectiveness of entecavir in organ transplant recipients, including liver transplant recipients, is unknown. If entecavir treatment is determined to be necessary in a transplant recipient who is receiving an immunosuppressant that may affect renal function (i.e., cyclosporine, tacrolimus), renal function should be monitored carefully both before and during treatment with entecavir (see Drug Interactions).
 
Lactic acidosis and severe hepatomegaly with steatosis, including fatal cases, have been reported with the use of nucleoside analogs alone or in combination with antiretrovirals. A majority of these cases have been in females; obesity and prolonged nucleoside exposure may also be risk factors. Particular caution should be exercised when administering nucleoside analogs to any patient with known risk factors for hepatic disease; however, cases have also been reported in patients with no known risk factors. Treatment with entecavir should be suspended in any patient who develops clinical or laboratory findings suggestive of lactic acidosis or pronounced hepatotoxicity (which may include hepatomegaly and steatosis even in the absence of marked transaminase elevations).
 
Dosage adjustments are recommended in patients with renal impairment (CrCl < 50 ml/min) or renal failure, including patients on dialysis (see Dosage). Clinical studies of entecavir did not include sufficient numbers of subjects aged 65 years and over to determine whether they respond differently from younger subjects; however, entecavir is substantially excreted by the kidney and the risk of toxic reactions to this drug may be greater in patients with impaired renal function. Because elderly patients are more likely to have decreased renal function, the dose of entecavir may need to be modified based on renal function; in addition, it may be useful to monitor renal function during entecavir therapy.
 
Safety and efficacy of entecavir in adolescents < 16 years, children, infants, and neonates have not been established.
 
Entecavir is classified in FDA pregnancy category C. NRTIs are known to induce mitochondrial dysfunction. An association of mitochondrial dysfunction in infants and in-utero antiretroviral exposure has been suggested, but not established. While the development of severe or fatal mitochondrial disease in exposed infants appears to be extremely rare, more intensive monitoring of hematologic and electrolyte parameters during the first few weeks of life is advised. Nucleoside analogs have been associated with the development of lactic acidosis, especially during pregnancy. It is unclear if pregnancy augments the incidence of lactic acidosis/hepatic steatosis in patients receiving nucleoside analogs. However, because pregnancy itself can mimic some of the early symptoms of the lactic acid/hepatic steatosis syndrome or be associated with other significant disorders of liver metabolism, clinicians need to be alert for early diagnosis of this syndrome. Pregnant women receiving nucleoside analogs should have LFTs and serum electrolytes assessed more frequently during the last trimester of pregnancy and any new symptoms should be evaluated thoroughly. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of entecavir in pregnant women. Reproduction studies have been performed in rats and rabbits at orally administered doses of up to 200 mg (16 mg/kg/day) and showed no maternal or embryo toxicity; these dosages were equivalent to 28—212 times the systemic exposures that are achieved at a dosage of 1 mg/day in humans. At exposures of 3100 times those used in humans, maternal toxicity and embryo-fetal toxicities have been observed in rats. Likewise, embryo-fetal toxicities have been observed in rabbits at exposures of 883 times those seen in humans. Because animal reproduction studies are not always predictive of human response, entecavir should be used during pregnancy only if clearly needed and after careful consideration of the risks and benefits. It is strongly encouraged that health care providers who are treating pregnant women or their newborns report cases of prenatal exposure to the Antiretroviral Pregnancy Registry; telephone 800—258—4263; fax 800—800—1052; the Antiretroviral Pregnancy Registry is also accessible via the Internet. There are no data on the effect of entecavir on transmission of hepatitis B virus from mother to infant during labor and obstetric delivery; therefore, appropriate interventions should be used to prevent neonatal acquisition of HBV including immunizations in accordance with current guidelines.
 
It is not known whether or not entecavir is excreted in breast milk; breast-feeding should be avoided during the use of entecavir.

    Headline
    hepatitis B exacerbation
    General Information
    Individuals who discontinue use of entecavir may experience a severe acute hepatitis B exacerbation. Close monitoring, both clinical and laboratory, is recommended for several months after stopping treatment with entecavir. If appropriate, resumption of anti-hepatitis B treatment may be required.
    Headline
    decompensated hepatic cirrhosis, females, hepatotoxicity or lactic acidosis, obesity
    General Information
    Hepatotoxicity or lactic acidosis, including fatal cases, have been reported with the use of nucleoside analogs, including entecavir. A majority of these cases have occurred in females. Obesity and prolonged nucleoside exposure may be risk factors. Exercise particular caution when administering nucleoside analogs to any individual with known risk factors for hepatic disease. However, cases have also been reported in individuals with no known risk factors. Individuals with decompensated hepatic cirrhosis may be at higher risk of developing lactic acidosis. Suspend entecavir treatment in any individual who develops clinical or laboratory findings suggestive of lactic acidosis or pronounced hepatotoxicity (which may include hepatomegaly and steatosis even in the absence of marked transaminase elevations).
    Headline
    undiagnosed HIV-1 infection
    General Information
    The use of entecavir to treat hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection in individuals with untreated or undiagnosed HIV-1 infection may increase the risk of developing HIV resistance. Offer HIV antibody testing to all individuals prior to starting entecavir. Avoid the use of entecavir in individuals with HBV and HIV coinfection unless they are also receiving a fully suppressive antiretroviral (ARV) treatment regimen for HIV; entecavir should not be considered part of the ARV regimen. HIV guidelines recommend the use of entecavir in combination with a fully suppressive ARV regimen, as an alternative treatment option for individuals with coinfection who are unable to take tenofovir. Most individuals with coinfection should continue treatment indefinitely with the goal of maximal HIV suppression and prevention of HBV relapse. If treatment must be discontinued, monitor transaminase concentrations every 6 weeks for the first 3 months, and every 3 to 6 months thereafter. Instruct individuals to avoid consuming alcohol, and offer vaccinations against hepatitis A and hepatitis B as appropriate.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Entecavir dosage adjustments are recommended in persons with renal impairment (CrCl less 50 mL/minute) or renal failure, including those on hemodialysis or continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD).
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Prospective data on the use of entecavir during human pregnancy are insufficient to determine the risk of birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. In animal reproduction studies, no adverse developmental effects were observed with entecavir at clinically relevant exposures. In rats and rabbits, embryo-fetal toxicity occurred at systemic exposures about 3,100 times and 883 times, respectively, the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD). There are no data on the effect of entecavir on transmission of hepatitis B virus (HBV) from the pregnant person to the child during labor and delivery; therefore, appropriate interventions should be used to prevent neonatal acquisition of HBV including immunizations in accordance with current guidelines. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant individuals exposed to entecavir; information about the registry can be obtained at www.apregistry.com or by calling 1-800-258-4263.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use entecavir with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of entecavir in human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effects on milk production. Entecavir is present in the milk of lactating rats. Antiviral medications whose passage into human milk have been evaluated include tenofovir and lamivudine. A consensus among healthcare providers has not been established regarding the treatment of hepatitis B infection and breast-feeding. In a survey of 226 physicians, 30.5% would recommend breast-feeding for individuals with hepatitis B infection on antiviral therapy, 44% would not recommend breast-feeding during antiviral therapy, and 25.2% stated they were unsure. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breast-fed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Assessment of adverse events are based on clinical studies comparing entecavir (0.5 mg/day or 1 mg/day) and lamivudine (100 mg/day). The reported adverse events with entecavir were similar to adverse events with lamivudine with regards to type and frequency. One percent of entecavir-treated patients compared with 4% of lamivudine-treated patients withdrew from trials due to either adverse events or abnormal laboratory tests. The most commonly reported adverse events for entecavir were headache (2% with 0.5 mg dose, 4% with 1 mg dose), fatigue (1% with 0.5 mg dose, 3% with 1 mg dose), and dizziness ( 250 mg/dL) was noted in 2% of patients in clinical trials, while glycosuria occurred in 4% of patients.
 
Hematuria was observed in 9% of patients in clinical trials.
 
Alopecia and rash (unspecified) have been reported in post-marketing surveillance.
 
Lactic acidosis and severe hepatomegaly with steatosis, including fatal cases, have been reported with the use of nucleoside analogs alone or in combination with antiretroviral agents. A majority of these cases have been in females; obesity and prolonged nucleoside exposure may be other risk factors. Particular caution should be exercised when administering nucleoside or nucleotide analogs to any patient with known risk factors for hepatic disease; however, cases have also been reported in patients with no known risk factors. Elevated hepatic enzymes were reported during clinical trials of entecavir. Description
  Text
  Entecavir is a nucleoside analog indicated for the treatment of chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. The drug is approved for use in nucleoside inhibitor-treatment-naive patients, lamivudine-resistant patients with compensated liver disease and evidence of active viral replication, and patients with decompensated liver disease. Entecavir slows the progression of chronic HBV infection and improves liver inflammation and fibrosis caused by HBV. Entecavir also displays weak activity against human immunodeficiency virus (HIV); however, it must not be considered an active component of a highly active antiretroviral (HAART) regimen. Use of entecavir in HBV/HIV coinfected patients without the addition of a fully suppressive HIV treatment regimen may result in NRTI resistance.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Entecavir is a deoxyguanosine nucleoside analog with selective and potent activity against hepatitis B virus (HBV), including lamivudine-resistant HBV. Entecavir is phosphorylated to the active triphosphate form and competes with the natural substrate deoxyguanosine triphosphate thereby suppressing HBV replication; suppression of replication occurs at all 3 activities of HBV polymerase (reverse transcriptase): (1) base priming, (2) reverse transcription of the negative strand from the pregenomic messenger RNA, and (3) synthesis of the positive strand of HBV DNA. In addition, entecavir triphosphate is a weak inhibitor of cellular DNA polymerase alpha, beta, and delta and mitochondrial DNA polymerase-gamma.
 
Entecavir displays weak activity against human immunodeficiency virus (HIV); thus, this drug must not be considered an active component in a fully suppressive HIV treatment regimen. The EC50 value of entecavir against a variety of HIV type 1 laboratory strains ranged from 0.026 to more than 10 micromolar in cell culture assays. Three patients coinfected with HBV and HIV not taking highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) had a 1-log10 reduction in HIV RNA while taking entecavir; however, selection for an HIV-resistant strain may occur. In cell cultures, entecavir selected for an M184I substitution in HIV reverse transcriptase.
 
Data from cell-based assays show an 8- to 30-fold reduction in entecavir phenotypic susceptibility in strains conferring lamivudine-resistance. Lamivudine-resistance strains harboring amino acid substitutions rtL180M, rtM204V, and rtA181C confer a 16- to 122-fold reduction in entecavir phenotypic susceptibility. A postmarketing analysis of entecavir resistance data from 17 phase 2 and 3 clinical trials identified emergent entecavir resistance-associated substitution rtA181C in 5 out of 1,461 (0.3%) subjects. This substitution was detected only in the presence of lamivudine resistance-associated substitutions rtL180M plus rtM204V.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Entecavir oral solution is 100% bioavailable compared to the tablet; the dosage forms can be used interchangeably. Oral absorption of entecavir is affected by food; food delays absorption, decreases Cmax concentrations by 44—46%, and decreases AUC concentrations by 18—20%. Administer entecavir at least 2 hours before or after a meal. Peak plasma concentrations of entecavir occur 0.5—1 hour after oral administration and Cmax and AUC concentrations increase in proportion to the dose following multiple, daily dosing. Steady state concentrations are achieved after 6—10 days of once daily dosing. In adult patients, Cmax concentrations at steady state are 4.2 ng/mL for a 0.5 mg oral dose and 8.2 ng/mL for a 1 mg oral dose. Corresponding trough concentrations are 0.3 ng/mL and 0.5 ng/mL for the 0.5 mg and 1 mg doses, respectively. After oral administration, no oxidative or acetylated metabolites are observed.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    No substantial alterations in pharmacokinetics are seen in patients with moderate or severe hepatic impairment compared to non-impaired, healthy subjects; the safety and efficacy of entecavir in liver transplant recipients is unknown.
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    In patients with moderate or severe renal impairment (i.e., CrCl < 50 mL/min) or with end-stage renal disease requiring hemodialysis or CAPD, Cmax, AUC, and clearance are increased when compared to patients with normal renal function. In patients with CrCl < 50 mL/min, it is recommended that the dose of entecavir be modified. A 4-hour period of hemodialysis removes approximately 13% of the entecavir dose; CAPD removes approximately 0.3% of the dose over 7 days. Entecavir AUC is approximately 29% greater in elderly subjects compared to young subjects; the disparity in exposure is most likely attributable to differences in renal function. The manufacturer recommends adjusting the dose of entecavir based on renal function rather than age.
    Name
    Pediatrics
    Description
    In a pharmacokinetic study of entecavir in pediatric patients with compensated liver disease, entecavir exposure among nucleoside-inhibitor naive patients (n = 24) receiving once-daily doses of 0.015 mg/kg (Max: 0.5 mg/dose) and lamivudine-experienced patients (n = 19) receiving once-daily doses of 0.03 mg/kg/dose (Max: 1 mg/dose) was similar to that observed in adult patients receiving doses of 0.5 mg and 1 mg, respectively. Cmax concentrations were 6.31 ng/mL for the 0.015 mg/kg dose group and 14.48 ng/mL for the 0.03 mg/kg dose group. Corresponding trough concentrations were 0.28 ng/mL and 0.47 ng/mL for the 0.015 mg/kg and 0.03 mg/kg dose groups, respectively.
  Text
  Entecavir is administered orally as a tablet or oral solution. It is extensively distributed into tissues and is approximately 13% bound to serum proteins. Peak plasma concentrations decrease in a biexponential manner with a terminal half-life of 128—149 hours; an effective accumulation half-life of 24 hours is suggested as the accumulation index is approximately 2-fold. Entecavir is predominately eliminated by the kidney with 62—73% of the dose recovered as unchanged drug in the urine. Entecavir undergoes both glomerular filtration and net tubular secretion. Entecavir is not a substrate, inhibitor, or inducer of the CYP 450 enzyme system.

Pediatrics
Although data are lacking for dose recommendations in pediatric patients with renal impairment, the manufacturer recommends that dose adjustments similar to those recommended in adult patients should be considered.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
Nucleoside-treatment naive patients should receive 0.05 mg PO once daily or 0.5 mg PO every 7 days and lamivudine refractory-patients should receive 0.1 mg PO once daily or 1 mg PO every 7 days. On days of dialysis, the dose should be administered after dialysis.
 
Peritoneal dialysis
Nucleoside-treatment naive patients should receive 0.05 mg PO once daily or 0.5 mg PO every 7 days and lamivudine refractory-patients should receive 0.1 mg PO once daily or 1 mg PO every 7 days.
