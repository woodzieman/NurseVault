          Dosage Description
          0.1 to 0.2 mg/kg orally once daily (average of 4 to 10 mg/day) for 3 to 6 weeks is the usual initial dosage or the dosage for short courses of treatment. Most patients require 0.1 mg/kg daily. Adjust the chlorambucil dosage based on patient response; reduce the dosage when there is an abrupt fall in white blood cell count. The dosage should not exceed 0.1 mg/kg daily (average of about 6 mg/day) in patients with hypoplastic bone marrow or if lymphocytic infiltration of the bone marrow is present. Alternate chlorambucil dosage schedules include intermittent (starting at a single dose of 0.4 mg/kg), biweekly, or once monthly pulse dosing. Doses are usually increased by 0.1 mg/kg until lymphocytosis is controlled or toxicity is observed; subsequent doses are adjusted to result in mild hematologic toxicity. Biweekly or once monthly chlorambucil administration appears to be at least similar in efficacy but with less than or equal hematologic toxicity compared with daily chlorambucil. Maintenance therapy (if needed) should not exceed 0.1 mg/kg daily and may be as low as 0.03 mg/kg daily. Most patients require a dosage of 2 to 4 mg/day or less. Consider discontinuing therapy after maximal control has been achieved; intermittent therapy reinstituted at time of relapse may be as effective as continuous treatment.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Chlorambucil is carcinogen in humans; it is also probably mutagenic in humans. Due to its carcinogenic effects chlorambucil should not be administered to patients with a neoplastic disease other than chronic lymphocytic leukemia or lymphoma. Although the exact risk cannot be quantified, chlorambucil therapy has been associated with secondary leukemia. The risk of secondary malignancy due to alkylating agents such as chlorambucil is associated with the length of therapy (chronicity) and large cumulative doses. The potential benefits of chlorambucil therapy must be considered on an individual basis against the risk of secondary leukemia.A cross-sensitivity can exist between chlorambucil and other alkylating agents, resulting in a hypersensitivity reaction manifested as a skin rash. Patients who have demonstrated chlorambucil hypersensitivity should not be retreated. In addition, chlorambucil is contraindicated if resistance to prior treatments with this agent was observed.If thrombocytopenia or lymphopenia are depressed due to underlying disease, chlorambucil therapy should be instituted at a reduced dose.The hematological status (leukocyte and platelet counts) of a patient receiving chlorambucil should be monitored closely because chlorambucil can cause bone marrow suppression and irreversible bone marrow toxicity. Patients usually develop slowly progressing lymphopenia during chlorambucil therapy. Neutropenia can occur after the third week of chlorambucil therapy and this may continue for up to 10 days after the last dose. It is recommended that weekly blood counts be done to determine hemoglobin, white blood cell (WBC) counts with differential, and platelet counts during treatment. Also, during the first 3 to 6 weeks of therapy, it is recommended that WBC counts be checked every 3 or 4 days after each of the weekly complete blood counts. Patients should not go more than 2 weeks without hematological and clinical examination during chlorambucil treatment. Severe neutropenia is dose-related and usually only occurs in patients who have received >= 6.5 mg/kg during one course of therapy with continuous dosing. Irreversible bone marrow damage may occur with total chlorambucil doses of > 6.5 mg/kg. The dose should be decreased if lymphopenia or thrombocytopenia occurs (i.e., lymphocyte or platelet counts below normal limits); chlorambucil should be discontinued for severe bone marrow suppression. Presence of persistently low neutrophils or platelets or peripheral lymphocytosis suggest bone marrow infiltration. If bone marrow infiltration is confirmed, the daily dose of chlorambucil should not exceed 0.1 mg/kg/day. Following a full course of radiation therapy or chemotherapy, chlorambucil should be administered at a reduced dose for at least 4 weeks due to the increased risk of bone marrow damage. Patients with an active infection should be treated prior to receiving chlorambucil. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with chemotherapy.Myelosuppressive effects of chlorambucil can increase the risk of infection or bleeding; therefore, dental work should be delayed until blood counts have returned to normal. Patients, especially those with dental disease, should be instructed in proper oral hygiene, including caution in use of regular toothbrushes, dental floss, and toothpicks.Intramuscular injections should not be administered to patients with platelet counts = 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    Child-Pugh class A, Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use chlorambucil with caution in patients with hepatic impairment (e.g., Child-Pugh class A, Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C) including hepatic failure. Monitor patients with hepatic impairment for signs of chlorambucil toxicity; consider a dose reduction in these patients.
    Headline
    serious adverse effects
    General Information
    Chlorambucil may cause serious adverse effects including severe bone marrow suppression (e.g., lymphopenia, neutropenia). An interruption in therapy, dosage reduction, or discontinuation of therapy may be needed based on severity. Monitor a complete blood cell (CBC) panel prior to and then weekly during therapy and more frequently during the first 3 to 6 weeks (e.g., white blood cell count at 3 or 4 days after each weekly CBC panel). Hematological and clinical examinations should occur at least every 2 weeks.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Chlorambucil may cause fetal harm in administered during pregnancy. Females of reproductive potential should be advised to avoid becoming pregnant while receiving chlorambucil treatment. If this drug is used during pregnancy or if the patient becomes pregnant while receiving chlorambucil, the patient should be apprised of the potential risks to the fetus. Animal studies have demonstrated fetal abnormalities (i.e., urogenital malformations in rats); however, there have been no well-controlled trials in pregnant woman. Unilateral renal agenesis has been observed in 2 cases when the mother received chlorambucil during the first trimester.
    Headline
    infertility
    General Information
    Chlorambucil causes chromatid or chromosome damage in humans. Infertility has been reported in patients who received chlorambucil for malignant and nonmalignant diseases; permanent sterility has occurred in both males and females. Prepubertal and pubertal males have an increased risk of becoming sterile following chlorambucil therapy.
    Headline
    head trauma, seizure disorder
    General Information
    Use chlorambucil with caution in children with nephrotic syndrome (off-label use) and in patients receiving high-dose pulse therapy, receiving concomitant drugs that may also cause seizures, or who have a history of head trauma or seizure disorder. Rare, focal and/or generalized seizures have been reported with chlorambucil therapy. Seizures have occurred at therapeutic daily doses, with pulse-dosing regimens, and in acute overdose situations.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Women should avoid breast-feeding during chlorambucil therapy due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in breastfed infants. It is unknown whether chlorambucil is excreted into breast milk.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Bone marrow suppression is the major and dose-limiting adverse reaction to treatment with chlorambucil. Neutropenia usually is observed after the third week of treatment and can persist for up to 4 weeks following discontinuation of chlorambucil. Following a short course of therapy, thrombocytopenia has generally been reported to occur after 1—2 weeks, with a duration of up to 4 weeks. Single-dose administration generally results in leukopenia, neutropenia and thrombocytopenia after 1—2 weeks, with a return to baseline levels after 2—3 weeks. Lymphopenia can occur with long-term use. Prolonged therapy or excessive dosages can result in pancytopenia and irreversible bone marrow damage. The manufacturer states that these sequelae occur when the total chlorambucil dosage approaches 6.5 mg/kg during one course of therapy, but these reactions can occur at lower dosages. Although irreversible bone marrow damage can occur, pretreatment bone marrow function is usually restored within weeks following discontinuation of the drug.Nausea and vomiting appear to occur most frequently with administration of doses over 20 mg. At typical low doses, nausea/vomiting usually are not a clinical problem.Seizures have been observed in children with nephrotic syndrome receiving chlorambucil. Seizure activity generally occurred 6—90 days after initiation of treatment in dosages of 0.2—0.7 mg/kg daily and resolved spontaneously without causing permanent neurologic damage. Generalized or myoclonic seizures can occur in high-dose therapy or accidental overdoses. In rare instances, myoclonia and peripheral neuropathy have been reported.Bronchopulmonary dysplasia, interstitial pneumonitis, or pulmonary fibrosis have occurred with chlorambucil, but the incidence is rare. The syndrome of bronchopulmonary dysplasia is manifested as persistent nonproductive cough, fever, rales, dyspnea, respiratory distress, and/or hypoxia and has been observed following intermittent or prolonged continuous dosing.Chlorambucil-induced cellular destruction can result in tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) which includes hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, and hypocalcemia. Uric acid nephropathy, acute renal failure due renal tubular obstruction, metabolic acidosis, and nephrolithiasis can also occur. These problems are more severe when a highly chemosensitive disease is treated (i.e., leukemia or lymphoma). Aggressive alkalinization of the urine and use of allopurinol can prevent urate nephropathy; close monitoring of serum electrolytes and renal function is recommended.Secondary malignancy (acute myelogenous leukemia) can occur with continuous chlorambucil therapy lasting 4 years or longer. In patients with ovarian cancer taking chlorambucil for more than 10 years, the incidence was as high as 5—10%. Teratogenesis also can occur in women who take chlorambucil during the first trimester of pregnancy.Gonadal suppression in males and females is not universal, but patients should be aware of the possibility. Atrophy of the testis or ovary has been reported. Amenorrhea also is seen in women. Fertility and normal menses may return but also could be lost with chlorambucil therapy.Other reported adverse reactions with chlorambucil include drug fever and hepatoxicity with jaundice.Maculopapular rash, edema, and urticaria have been noted occasionally. Toxic epidermal necrolysis also has been seen.

Description
  Text
  Chlorambucil is an oral alkylating agent. Chlorambucil is approved for palliative treatment of chronic lymphocytic leukemia, Hodgkin lymphoma, and certain non-Hodgkin's lymphomas including lymphosarcoma and giant follicular lymphoma. Product labels have boxed warnings about the potential for severe myelosuppression, carcinogenicity, teratogenicity, and infertility. Because of its carcinogenic properties and potential for serious toxicities, chlorambucil should not be given to people for the treatment of non-malignant diseases. Chlorambucil was approved by the FDA in 1957.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Chlorambucil is a bifunctional alkylating agent and exerts its chemotherapeutic effects by substituting alkyl groups for hydrogen ions in a number of organic compounds. DNA-DNA interstrand crosslinking and DNA-protein crosslinking occur, leading to interference in DNA replication, transcription of RNA, and nucleic acid function. Chlorambucil's onset of activity is extremely slow, which probably explains the drug's lack of acute toxicity. The predominant metabolite of chlorambucil, phenylacetic acid mustard, also has antineoplastic properties and may contribute to the cytotoxicity of the drug.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Chlorambucil is well absorbed after oral administration. The oral bioavailability of chlorambucil ranges from 56—105%. When taken on an empty stomach, bioavailability can be as low as 50%, but when taken with food the bioavailability can be 75%. Peak plasma concentrations are achieved within 1 hour after administration. The active metabolite, phenylacetic acid mustard, achieves a peak concentration in 2—4 hours.

  Chlorambucil is administered orally. Chlorambucil binds extensively to plasma proteins (99%). Some distribution to adipose tissue does occur, which leads to a depot of drug that slowly leaks out over time and prolongs chlorambucil's myelosuppressive effects. Chlorambucil crosses the placenta and distributes into ascitic fluid. It is unknown if this drug crosses the blood-brain barrier or if it enters breast milk. Metabolism to the active metabolite, phenylacetic acid mustard, occurs in the liver. Chlorambucil and phenylacetic acid undergo hydrolysis to inactive compounds that are extensively eliminated in the urine. Only 1% of a dose is eliminated as active drug. The half-life of chlorambucil is approximately 92 minutes, and the half-life of phenylacetic acid is 145 minutes. The clinical importance of a longer exposure to the active metabolite has not been extensively explored.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Chlorambucil is primarily metabolized in the liver. Consider an initial dose reduction in these patients.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
