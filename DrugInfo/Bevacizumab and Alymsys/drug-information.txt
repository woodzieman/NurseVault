Drug Class: Bevacizumab is a recombinant humanized monoclonal IgG1 antibody that binds to vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF); it is an angiogenesis inhibitor.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC), intraocular; Common adult doses: Oncology: 15 mg/kg IV every 3 weeks or 10 mg/kg IV every 2 weeks.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): nephrotic syndrome, proteinuria, thrombotic microangiopathy, bleeding, epistaxis, gi bleeding, hematemesis, hematoma, hemoptysis, hemorrhoids, intracranial bleeding, vaginal bleeding, biliary fistula, bladder fistula, bronchopleural fistula, gastrointestinal fistula, tracheoesophageal fistula, vaginal fistula, pulmonary embolism, thromboembolism, thrombosis, angina, chest pain (unspecified), myocardial infarction, stroke, ileus, metallic taste, weight loss, arthralgia, back pain

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Bevacizumab should be used with caution in patients with known murine protein hypersensitivity, hamster protein hypersensitivity, or hypersensitivity to any component of the product. Infusion reactions during administration of the first dose of bevacizumab are uncommon (see Adverse Reactions). Emetic risk: Minimal

          15 mg/kg IV over 90 minutes plus atezolizumab (1,200 mg IV over 60 minutes), repeated every 3 weeks until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. The primary endpoint of the single-agent bevacizumab trial, 6-month PFS, was 29% with a 6-month OS of 57%. Additionally, treatment with single-agent bevacizumab led to an estimated 6-month PFS rate of 42.6%, a median PFS time of 4.2 months, and a median OS time of 9.2 months in patients with recurrent glioblastoma (n = 85) in a randomized, noncomparative study.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Bevacizumab should be used with caution in patients with known murine protein hypersensitivity, hamster protein hypersensitivity, or hypersensitivity to any component of the product. Infusion reactions during administration of the first dose of bevacizumab are uncommon (see Adverse Reactions). Bevacizumab infusion should be interrupted in all patients with a severe infusion reaction and appropriate medical treatment administered.
 
Bevacizumab can result in the development of wound dehiscence, which may lead to death. Although the appropriate interval between surgery and initiation of bevacizumab therapy required to avoid risks of impaired wound healing and/or wound dehiscence has not been determined, it is recommended that bevacizumab therapy should not be initiated for at least 28 days following major surgery. There have been reports of anastomotic dehiscence when bevacizumab was initiated at least 28 days following surgery (see Adverse Reactions). The surgical incision should be fully healed prior to beginning bevacizumab. Because of the potential for impaired wound healing, bevacizumab therapy should be suspended at least several weeks prior to elective surgery and not resumed until the surgical incision is fully healed. When attempting to determine the interval between termination of bevacizumab therapy and subsequent elective surgery or extensive dental work, the calculated half-life of bevacizumab (approximately 20 days) should be considered. Bevacizumab therapy should be permanently discontinued in patients with wound dehiscence requiring medical intervention.
 
Bleeding is a reported complication of bevacizumab therapy (see Adverse Reactions). Patients with serious bleeding (i.e., bleeding requiring medical intervention such as GI bleeding or intracranial bleeding) should discontinue bevacizumab therapy; patients with a recent history of hemoptysis of >= 1/2 teaspoon red blood should not receive bevacizumab. Serious bleeding events have occurred primarily in patients with non-small cell lung cancer. Of the patients experiencing events of life-threatening pulmonary hemorrhage, many had cavitation and/or necrosis of the tumor, either pre-existing or developing during bevacizumab therapy. These serious hemorrhagic events occurred suddenly without an antecedent history of minor hemoptysis and presented as major or massive hemoptysis. The risk of CNS bleeding in patients with CNS metastasis was not evaluated in early trials since these patients were excluded from clinical trials following the development of CNS bleeding in a patient with CNS metastasis in a phase 1 trial. A retrospective exploratory analysis was conducted across 13 randomized controlled phase II and III trials of bevacizumab, involving 8443 patients with non-small cell lung, pancreatic, renal, breast, and colon cancers. CNS metastases were identified in 187 of these patients. Grade 4 cerebral hemorrhage occurred in 3/91 patients receiving bevacizumab; 1/96 patients in the control arm developed a grade 5 cerebral hemorrhage. In addition, an interim safety analysis of 4 open-label trials report 1 episode of cerebral hemorrhage (grade 2) out of 151 patients with CNS metastasis treated with bevacizumab.
 
Bevacizumab therapy should be permanently discontinued in patients with GI perforation requiring medical intervention. Cases of gastrointestinal perforation complicated by intra-abdominal abscesses or fistula formation, with fatal outcome in some cases, have been reported during treatment with bevacizumab (see Adverse Reactions). The incidence of gastrointestinal perforation, fistula formation, and/or intra-abdominal abscess was 2.4% in patients with colorectal cancer and 0.9% in patients with non-small cell lung cancer. Gastrointestinal perforation was not correlated to duration of bevacizumab exposure. The typical presentation was reported as abdominal pain associated with symptoms such as constipation, fever, nausea, and vomiting. GI perforation should be included in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with abdominal pain and receiving bevacizumab. A meta-analysis of 12,294 patients in 17 randomized trials was conducted to determine the risk of GI perforation with bevacizumab treatment. Among 6,490 patients receiving bevacizumab, the incidence of GI perforation was 0.9%. The relative risk of developing a GI perforation with bevacizumab compared to controls was 2.14 (p = 0.011). Patients with colorectal cancer and patients who received a dose equivalent to 5 mg/kg/week had a significantly increased risk of developing GI perforation. Consider the patient's history of ulcers or diverticulitis, radiation exposure, recent sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy, resection of the primary tumor, GI obstruction, and current tumor presentation and placement. In the meta-analysis, most GI perforations occurred within the first 6 months of treatment.
 
Use caution when treating patients with preexisting hypertension with bevacizumab; the drug may increase blood pressure in both hypertensive and non-hypertensive patients. Blood pressure monitoring should be conducted every 2—3 weeks during treatment with bevacizumab. Patients who develop hypertension may require blood pressure monitoring at more frequent intervals and medical management of hypertension. Bevacizumab therapy should be permanently discontinued in patients with hypertensive crisis; temporary suspension of therapy is recommended in patients with severe hypertension not controlled with medical management. Patients who discontinue therapy due to bevacizumab-induced or exacerbated hypertension should continue to have their blood pressure monitored at regular intervals.
 
The safety and efficacy of bevacizumab in patients with cardiac disease, including coronary artery disease, have not been evaluated; patients were excluded from clinical trials if during the previous year they had experienced clinically significant cardiovascular disease. Patients with previous bevacizumab exposure, of advanced age (elderly >= 65 years of age), thromboembolic disease, cerebrovascular disease, a history of arterial thromboembolism before bevacizumab therapy appear to be at higher risk for the development of a fatal or serious arterial thrombotic events (e.g., stroke, myocardial infarction, coronary thrombosis, transient ischemic attack, unstable angina; see Adverse Reactions). In an exploratory analysis pooling the data from five randomized, placebo controlled, clinical trials conducted in patients without a recent history of clinically significant cardiovascular disease, the overall incidence of arterial thromboembolic events, the incidence of fatal arterial thromboembolic events, and the incidence of cardiovascular thromboembolic events were increased in patients receiving bevacizumab plus chemotherapy as compared to chemotherapy alone. Bevacizumab should be permanently discontinued in a patient who experiences a severe arterial thromboembolic event; the safety of resuming bevacizumab therapy after such an event has not been studied. Congestive heart failure (CHF) and left ventricular dysfunction have been reported during clinical trials for metastatic colorectal cancer (see Adverse Reactions). Prior exposure to anthracycline chemotherapy or chest wall radiation therapy may be associated with an increased incidence of CHF (see Adverse Reactions). The safety of continuation or resumption of bevacizumab therapy in patients with cardiac dysfunction has not been studied. Elderly patients receiving bevacizumab in clinical trials experienced a higher incidence of severe adverse reactions as compared to younger adult patients.
 
Use bevacizumab with caution in patients with renal disease. Patients receiving bevacizumab should be monitored for the development or worsening of proteinuria with serial urinalysis. Patients with a 2+ protein or greater urine dipstick reading should undergo further assessment (e.g., a 24-hour urine collection). Bevacizumab therapy should be discontinued in patients with nephrotic syndrome; the safety of continued bevacizumab therapy in patients with moderate to severe proteinuria has not been evaluated. Bevacizumab therapy should be suspended in patients with >= 2 grams of proteinuria/24 hours and resumed when < 2 grams of proteinuria/24 hours.
 
An increased incidence of severe neutropenia, febrile neutropenia, and infection with severe neutropenia has been reported in patients receiving bevacizumab in combination with myelosuppressive chemotherapy (see Adverse Reactions). Patients with an active infection should be treated prior to receiving bevacizumab plus chemotherapy. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with myelosuppressive chemotherapy.
 
Bevacizumab therapy should be discontinued in patients who develop reversible posterior leuko-encephalopathy (RPLS), and treatment of hypertension, if present should be started. The safety of reinitiating bevacizumab therapy in these patients is not known.
 
If bevacizumab, a compound similar to ranibizumab, is used for age-related macular degeneration as an intravitreal injection, an off-label use, it should be avoided in patients with ocular infection. Intravitreal injections have been associated with endophthalmitis and retinal detachments, which may lead to blindness. The risk for serious complications from single intraocular injections is low, but the cumulative risk exposure can be greater. A broad-spectrum ocular microbicide should be given prior to the injection. Use controlled aseptic conditions, which includes the use of sterile gloves, a sterile drape, and a sterile eyelid speculum (or equivalent). In addition, patients should be monitored during the week following the injection to permit early treatment should an infection occur.
 
Intravitreous injections have been associated with endophthalmitis. Any ocular surgery that disrupts the integrity of the globe can lead to exogenous endophthalmitis (e.g., cataract, retinal surgeries, radial keratotomy). Patients who have received or will receive bevacizumab as an intravitreal injection and have recently undergone or will undergo an ocular surgical procedure should be monitored for signs and symptoms of endophthalmitis (pain, redness, lid swelling, decreased visual acuity) and be counseled on when to seek medical attention. Monitor patients during the week following injection to permit early treatment should an infection occur.
 
Increased intraocular pressure (IOP) has been seen within 60 minutes of ranibizumab injection, a compound similar to bevacizumab. Therefore, if bevacizumab is used for age-related macular degeneration as an intravitreal injection (an off-label use), monitor for IOP and the perfusion of the optic nerve head. Instruct patients to seek immediate care with their ophthalmologist if the treated eye(s) becomes red, sensitive to light, painful, or develops a change in vision. Use with caution in patients with a history of glaucoma.
 
Bevacizumab is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C, reflecting the lack of human pregnancy data. However, angiogenesis is critical to fetal development and the inhibition of angiogenesis following administration of bevacizumab is likely to have adverse effects on the pregnancy. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is required for angiogenesis during the development of the corpus luteum. Chronic anti-VEGF effects during embryonic development may also lead to neurologic and lung impairment. In animal studies, adverse fetal outcome was noted at all doses tested, and teratogenic effects were noted at doses twice the recommended human dose on a mg/kg basis. Observed effects included decreases in maternal and fetal body weights, an increased number of fetal resorptions, and increased incidence of specific gross and skeletal fetal alterations. There are no adequate studies in pregnant women. Bevacizumab should not be used during pregnancy or in females of childbearing potential not using adequate contraception unless the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus. All patients should be counseled regarding the potential risks of bevacizumab therapy to the developing fetus prior to beginning therapy. If a patient becomes pregnant while receiving bevacizumab, she should be told of the potential hazard to the fetus and/or the potential risk of loss of pregnancy. Patients who discontinue bevacizumab therapy should also be counseled concerning the prolonged exposure to bevacizumab following discontinuation of therapy (half-life approximately 20 days) and the possible effects on fetal development. Bevacizumab therapy may contribute to female infertility. Dose-related decreases in ovarian and uterine weights, endometrial proliferation, number of menstrual cycles, and arrested follicular development or absent corpora lutea were observed in female primates treated with 10 or 50 mg/kg bevacizumab for 13 or 26 weeks.
 
According to the manufacturer, bevacizumab or breast-feeding should be discontinued because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in nursing infants from bevacizumab. Further, breast-feeding avoidance for a prolonged period after bevacizumab use is advised; the half-life of bevacizumab is approximately 20 days (range, 11—50 days). Bevacizumab excretion into human milk is unknown, but human IgG is secreted into human milk. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
The safety and effectiveness of bevacizumab in children and infants have not been studied. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is required for normal vascular development in neonates during the first weeks post-natally; inactivation of VEGF at this time may result in regression of the vasculature, renal failure, and death. Physeal dysplasia has been observed in juvenile cynomolgus monkeys with open growth plates treated for 4 weeks with doses that were less than the recommended human dose based on mg/kg and exposure. The incidence and severity of physeal dysplasia were dose-related and were at least partially reversible upon cessation of treatment.

    hypertension
    General Information

    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Bevacizumab may cause fetal harm if given during pregnancy based on animal studies and its mechanism of action. Angiogenesis is critical to fetal development, and the inhibition of angiogenesis following administration of bevacizumab is likely to have adverse effects on the pregnancy. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is required for angiogenesis during the development of the corpus luteum. Individuals of reproductive potential should avoid pregnancy during bevacizumab therapy; these patients should be advised of the potential risk to a fetus if pregnancy occurs. Fetal malformations have been reported in post-marketing surveillance of bevacizumab; however, these reports are insufficient to determine drug-associated risks. In animal studies, bevacizumab doses of approximately 1 to 10 times the clinical dose administered during organogenesis in rabbits resulted in fetal resorptions, decreased maternal and fetal weight gain, and multiple congenital malformations, including corneal opacities and abnormal ossification of the skull and skeleton, including limb and phalangeal defects. Skeletal deformities were observed at all dose levels, with meningocele observed only at 10-times the clinical dose; the number of litters with malformed fetuses increased with increasing dose.

    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information

Discuss contraception requirements; advise individuals of reproductive potential to use effective contraception during and for at least 6 months after treatment with intravenous bevacizumab. People who may become pregnant who are treated with intravitreal bevacizumab should use effective contraception during treatment with intravitreal bevacizumab and for at least 3 months after the last dose.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Avoid the use of systemic bevacizumab during breast-feeding. It is not known if bevacizumab is secreted in human milk or if it has effects on the breastfed child or on milk production. Although human IgG is present in human milk, published data suggest that breast milk antibodies do not enter the neonatal and infant circulation in significant amounts. Due to the risk of serious adverse reactions in the breastfed child, individuals should discontinue breast-feeding during systemic bevacizumab therapy and for 6 months after the last dose. INTRAVITREAL BEVACIZUMAB: Intravitreal bevacizumab should be used with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data available on the presence of bevacizumab in human milk or the effects on the breastfed infant or on milk production following intravitreal administration. The developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding should be considered along with the clinical need for intravitreal bevacizumab use and any potential adverse effects from bevacizumab on the breastfed child. One infant was breastfed, apparently without harm, after maternal intravitreal bevacizumab injections in low doses. The mother was treated with 14 intravitreal bevacizumab injections over a 20-month period and 5 of the injections were given while she was breast-feeding (age of infant not stated). No mention was made of infant adverse effects, but the patient became pregnant again, received 3 additional injections, and delivered an infant who developed normally at 12 months of age.

Skeletal deformities were observed at all dose levels, with meningocele observed only at 10-times the clinical dose; the number of litters with malformed fetuses increased with increasing dose.
  Breastfeeding
    General Information
    Avoid the use of systemic bevacizumab during breast-feeding. It is not known if bevacizumab is secreted in human milk or if it has effects on the breastfed child or on milk production. Although human IgG is present in human milk, published data suggest that breast milk antibodies do not enter the neonatal and infant circulation in significant amounts. Due to the risk of serious adverse reactions in the breastfed child, individuals should discontinue breast-feeding during systemic bevacizumab therapy and for 6 months after the last dose. INTRAVITREAL BEVACIZUMAB: Intravitreal bevacizumab should be used with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data available on the presence of bevacizumab in human milk or the effects on the breastfed infant or on milk production following intravitreal administration. The developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding should be considered along with the clinical need for intravitreal bevacizumab use and any potential adverse effects from bevacizumab on the breastfed child. One infant was breastfed, apparently without harm, after maternal intravitreal bevacizumab injections in low doses. The mother was treated with 14 intravitreal bevacizumab injections over a 20-month period and 5 of the injections were given while she was breast-feeding (age of infant not stated). No mention was made of infant adverse effects, but the patient became pregnant again, received 3 additional injections, and delivered an infant who developed normally at 12 months of age.

Interactions
Cholera Vaccine: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the live cholera vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to cholera bacteria after receiving the vaccine.
Dengue Tetravalent Vaccine, Live: (Contraindicated) Avoid administration of the live dengue virus vaccine with immunosuppressive drug therapy and prior to immune recovery following treatment with immunosuppressive drug therapy. When feasible, administer indicated live virus vaccines at least 4 weeks before planned immunosuppression or wait until at least 3 months after discontinuation. The time to restoration of immune competence may be longer in some patients. Patients with altered immunocompetence may be at increased risk for severe adverse reactions due to uninhibited growth of the attenuated live virus. Additionally, vaccines may be less effective if administered during a period of altered immunocompetence.
Panitumumab: (Major) Do not use panitumumab with bevacizumab in combination with chemotherapy as increased mortality and toxicity may occur. In an interim analysis of an open-label, multicenter, randomized clinical trial of patient with previously untreated metastatic colorectal cancer, the combination of panitumumab, bevacizumab, and chemotherapy resulted in decreased overall survival and an increased incidence of grade 3 to 5 adverse reactions compared with bevacizumab plus chemotherapy without panitumumab, including fatalities. Patients randomized to the panitumumab arm also received a lower mean relative dose intensity of each chemotherapeutic agent (oxaliplatin, irinotecan, and 5-fluorouracil) over the first 24 weeks on study compared with those randomized to bevacizumab and chemotherapy.

Description
  Text
  Bevacizumab is a recombinant humanized monoclonal IgG1 antibody that binds to vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF); it is an angiogenesis inhibitor. It is indicated for the treatment of colorectal cancer; non-small cell lung cancer; glioblastoma; hepatocellular cancer; renal cell carcinoma; cervical cancer; and epithelial ovarian, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal cancer. It is not indicated for adjuvant treatment of colon cancer. Bevacizumab is indicated for the treatment of neovascular (wet) age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). It has also been studied as an intravitreal injection for diabetic retinopathy as a cost-effective alternative for individuals not responsive to other therapies. Gastrointestinal perforations and fistula, wound healing complications, hemorrhage, arterial and venous thromboembolic events, hypertension, renal injury and proteinuria, and congestive heart failure have been reported with systemic bevacizumab use. Withhold intravenous bevacizumab for at least 28 days before and after elective surgery and until adequate wound healing due to wound healing complications associated with treatment. Bevacizumab was initially approved by the FDA in February 2004.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Bevacizumab binds to vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and prevents the binding of VEGF with its receptors, VEGFR-1 (Flt-1) and VEGFR-2 (Flk-1/KDR), found on the surface of endothelial cells thereby inhibiting the activation of VEGF signaling pathways that promote neovascularization. The interaction of VEGF with its receptors leads to endothelial cell proliferation and new blood vessel formation in in vitro models of angiogenesis. VEGF-A also increases vascular permeability resulting in increased intra-tumoral interstitial fluid pressure, which reduces the delivery of antineoplastic therapies. In vivo studies have shown that bevacizumab inhibits vessel growth, induces regression of newly formed vessels, and normalizes the vasculature to facilitate the delivery of cytotoxic chemotherapy. In addition, administration of bevacizumab to xenotransplant models of colon cancer in nude (athymic) mice caused a reduction of microvascular growth and inhibition of metastatic disease progression. VEGF signaling has additional angiogenesis-independent effects which contribute to tumor progression including promotion of cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasiveness through activation of VEGFR-1 signaling, promoting stemness through the activation of VEGF-A/neropilin-1 pathway and self-renewal through VEGFR-2/STAT3 signaling, and promoting immune suppression in the tumor microenvironment. VEGF signaling supports immune suppression through a wide range of mechanisms, including impaired maturation and function of dendritic cells and T cells, aberrant hematopoiesis, and promoting the activity of immunosuppressive cells such as T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells. Considering the role of VEGF/VEGFR in promoting cancer immune tolerance, bevacizumab also enhances antitumor immunity through VEGF signaling inhibition.

Dilution
Withdraw the appropriate amount of bevacizumab and dilute in a total volume of 100 mL of 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection; do not administer or mix with Dextrose Injection.
No incompatibilities have been found between bevacizumab (Avastin, Alymsys, Avzivi, Jobevne, Mvasi, Zirabev) and polyvinylchloride or polyolefin bags.
No incompatibilities have been found between bevacizumab (Vegzelma) and polyolefin (polypropylene and polyethylene) bags.
Discard any unused portion left in the preservative-free vial.
Storage after dilution (Alymsys): Store diluted bevacizumab solution under refrigeration at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) for up to 4 hours.
Storage after dilution (Avastin, Avzivi, Jobevne, Mvasi): Store diluted bevacizumab under refrigeration at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) for up to 8 hours.
Storage after dilution (Vegzelma): Store diluted bevacizumab solution under refrigeration at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) for up to 24 hours or at room temperature up to 30 degrees C (86 degrees F) for up to 4 hours.
Storage after dilution (Zirabev): Store diluted bevacizumab under refrigeration at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) for up to 16 days.

Product Preparation
Prepare for intravitreal injection with the following recommended devices for one-time use (not provided):
5-micron sterile filter needle (18-gauge x 1.5-inch)
1 mL sterile syringe (with marking to measure 0.05 mL)
sterile injection needle (30-gauge x 0.5-inch)
alcohol swab
Remove the protective plastic cap from the vial. Clean the top of the vial with an alcohol swab.
Attach the 5-micron filter needle (18-gauge x 1.5-inch) to the syringe by twisting it onto the syringe tip.
Push the filter needle into the center of the vial stopper and ensure the tip of the needle remains within the solution to minimize the potential for air bubbles.
Withdraw the total volume of bevacizumab, keeping the vial in an upright position, slightly inclined to ease sufficient withdrawal.
Ensure that the plunger rod is drawn back sufficiently when drawing up bevacizumab in order to completely empty the filter needle.
The filter needle should be discarded after withdrawal of the vial contents and must not be used for the intravitreal injection.
Attach the 30-gauge x 0.5-inch sterile injection needle to the syringe by firmly screwing it onto the syringe hub. Carefully remove the needle cap by pulling it straight off. Do not wipe the needle at any time.
Hold the syringe with the needle pointing up. If there are any air bubbles, gently tap the syringe with your finger until the bubbles rise to the top.
Hold the syringe at eye level and slowly push the plunger rod until the plunger tip is aligned with the line that marks 0.05 mL on the syringe.
 
Intravitreal Administration
The intravitreal injection procedure must be carried out under aseptic conditions, which includes use of surgical hand disinfection, sterile gloves, a sterile drape, and sterile eyelid speculum (or equivalent). Also, ensure the availability of sterile paracentesis equipment (if required).
Adequate anesthesia and a broad-spectrum, topical microbicide to disinfect the periocular skin, eyelid, and ocular surface should be administered prior to the injection.
Prior to and 30 minutes following the intravitreal injection, monitor for elevation of intraocular pressure. Appropriate monitoring may consist of a check for perfusion of the optic nerve head or tonometry.
Instruct patients to urgently report any symptoms suggestive of endophthalmitis (e.g., ocular pain, redness of the eye, photophobia, blurred vision).
Each vial should only be used for treatment of a single eye. If the contralateral eye requires treatment, a new vial should be used, and the sterile field, gloves, drapes, eyelid speculum, filter needle, and injection needles should be changed.
