Drug Class: Ifosfamide is an intravenous alkylating agent related to the nitrogen mustards; it is a prodrug requiring activation by CYP3A4 for its cytotoxic activity.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: FDA approved: 1,200 mg/m2 IV on days 1 through 5, every 21 days.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Ifosfamide has a low therapeutic index, and therapeutic doses are likely to cause toxicity. Ifosfamide should be used cautiously in patients who have had previous myelosuppressive therapy such as chemotherapy or radiation therapy. Severe bone marrow suppression, including neutropenia and/or thrombocytopenia, is a contraindication to ifosfamide use. Unless clinically indicated, ifosfamide should not be given to patients with a WBC count 10 RBCs per high-power field) is present, then subsequent administration should be held until hematuria has resolved.
 
Ifosfamide is associated with nephrotoxicity (see Adverse Reactions). Patients with renal impairment or renal failure may experience worsening of renal function during or following treatment with ifosfamide. Some experts recommend dosage adjustments of ifosfamide in patients with a creatinine clearance 60 g/m2, prior or concurrent cisplatin or carboplatin treatment, pre-existing renal impairment, nephrectomy, or renal infiltration of tumor, and interpatient variability in metabolism.
 
Ifosfamide should not be used during pregnancy and is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category D. Females of childbearing age should avoid becoming pregnant while receiving ifosfamide. Although human studies are not available, animal studies indicate ifosfamide is capable of causing gene mutations and chromosomal damage in vivo. Embryotoxic and teratogenic effects have been observed in animal models at doses of 0.05—0.075 times the human dose. Ifosfamide can cause fetal damage when administered to a pregnant woman. If ifosfamide is used during pregnancy, or if the patient becomes pregnant while taking this drug, the patient should be apprised of the potential hazard to the fetus.
 
Ifosfamide should be avoided while breast-feeding. Since ifosfamide passes into breast milk a decision should be made whether to discontinue breast-feeding or to discontinue ifosfamide, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother.
 
Ifosfamide should be administered under the supervision of a qualified physician experienced in the use of cancer chemotherapeutic agents. Neurologic manifestations consisting of somnolence, confusion, hallucinations, and in some instances, coma have been reported following ifosfamide therapy (see Adverse Reactions). The occurrence of these symptoms requires discontinuation of ifosfamide therapy. The symptoms have usually been reversible and supportive therapy should be maintained until their complete resolution.
 
Use care to avoid accidental exposure to ifosfamide during preparation, handling, and administration. The use of protective gowns, gloves and goggles is recommended. Following skin or ocular exposure, skin and eyes should be thoroughly rinsed.
 
Vaccination during chemotherapy or radiation therapy should be avoided because the antibody response is suboptimal. When chemotherapy is being planned, vaccination should precede the initiation of chemotherapy by >= 2 weeks. The administration of most live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    Headline
    bone marrow suppression, infection
    General Information
    Avoid ifosfamide administration in patients with an active infection or in the presence of severe immunosuppression. Ifosfamide can cause bone marrow suppression that results in severe or fatal infections including sepsis or septic shock. The risk is dose-dependent and increased in patients with reduced renal function, bone marrow metastases, prior radiation therapy and concomitant or prior therapy with other cytotoxic agents. Closely monitor complete blood counts prior to each dose of ifosfamide, at appropriate intervals during treatment, and as clinically indicated.
    Headline
    urotoxicity
    General Information
    Avoid ifosfamide use in patients with an active urinary tract infection. Ifosfamide can cause severe urotoxicity including hemorrhagic cystitis. Hemorrhagic cystitis is dose-dependent and the risk is increased with past or concomitant bladder radiation or busulfan treatment. Administer ifosfamide with extensive daily hydration to force diuresis and reduce the risk of bladder toxicity; administer mesna to reduce the incidence and severity of urotoxicity. Obtain a urinalysis prior to each dose and monitor for hematuria; an interruption in therapy may be necessary for microscopic hematuria.
    Headline
    nephrotoxicity, renal failure, renal impairment, urinary tract obstruction
    General Information
    Ifosfamide is contraindicated in individuals with urinary tract obstruction. Ifosfamide can cause severe and fatal nephrotoxicity that can result in renal failure; the risk is increased in patients with baseline renal impairment or reduced nephron reserve. Ifosfamide may accumulate in individuals with decreased renal function which increases the risk of myelosuppression, central nervous system (CNS) toxicity, and cardiac toxicity. Closely monitor these individuals as they may also experience worsening of renal function during or after ifosfamide treatment; tubular damage may occur up to years after treatment. Administer adequate amounts of fluid during or immediately after administration of ifosfamide to force diuresis and reduce the risk of nephrotoxicity. Evaluate glomerular and tubular kidney function before starting treatment, during treatment, and as clinically indicated. Regularly monitor urinary sediment for the presence of erythrocytes and other signs of nephrotoxicity. Monitor serum and urine chemistries regularly, including phosphorus and potassium.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery, encephalopathy, ethanol ingestion, hypoalbuminemia
    General Information
    Ifosfamide can cause severe and fatal encephalopathy. Risk factors for the development of central nervous system (CNS) toxicity include high ifosfamide dosage, hypoalbuminemia, impaired renal function, poor performance status, bulky abdominal-pelvic disease, nephrotoxic treatments, CNS active drugs, or ethanol ingestion. Due to the risk of neurotoxic effects, advise patients about the risks of driving or operating machinery or performing activities requiring coordination and concentration during therapy. Monitor for signs and symptoms of encephalopathy during and after treatment; symptoms may occur within hours to days after administration. An interruption of therapy or permanent discontinuation may be necessary based on individual safety and tolerability.
    Headline
    cardiac disease
    General Information
    Use ifosfamide with caution in patients with pre-existing cardiac disease. Ifosfamide can cause severe and fatal cardiotoxicity. Cardiotoxic effects are dose-dependent and the risk is increased in patients with cardiac disease, prior or concomitant treatment with other cardiotoxic agents, radiation to the cardiac region, and renal impairment.
    Headline
    radiation therapy
    General Information
    Use ifosfamide with caution in patients who have received radiation therapy; these patients are at an increased risk of myelosuppression. The risk of cardiac toxicity is increased in patients who have received radiation to the cardiac region and past or concomitant radiation of the bladder may increase the risk of hemorrhagic cystitis.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Ifosfamide may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy, based on human and animal data and its mechanism of action. Advise pregnant people of the potential fetal risk. Fetal growth delay and neonatal anemia have been reported after in utero exposure to ifosfamide-containing treatment regimens during pregnancy. Animal data indicated ifosfamide is capable of causing gene mutations and chromosomal damage. In mice, increased resorptions and anomalies were reported when ifosfamide was given at a dose of 30 mg/m2 on day 11 of gestation. Embryo-lethal effects were observed in rats receiving ifosfamide doses of 54 mg/m2 from gestation day 6 through 15, and embryotoxic effects were apparent in dams receiving 18 mg/m2 over the same dosing period. Ifosfamide was also embryotoxic in rabbits receiving 88 mg/m2/day, with a significantly increased number of anomalies compared to the control group. The American Society of Clinical Oncology provides additional information on the use of certain medications for the management of cancer during pregnancy.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with ifosfamide. Ifosfamide can cause fetal harm. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking ifosfamide and for 12 months after the last dose. For those that can cause pregnancy in others, contraception is recommended during ifosfamide treatment and for 6 months after the last dose. Based on animal studies, ifosfamide may cause male and female infertility; the reversibility of these effects is unknown.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking ifosfamide and for 1 week after the last dose, due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in the breastfed child. Ifosfamide is excreted in breast milk.

Interactions
Adagrasib: (Moderate) Monitor for a decrease in the efficacy of ifosfamide if coadministration with adagrasib is necessary. Ifosfamide is metabolized by CYP3A to its active alkylating metabolites. Adagrasib is a strong CYP3A inhibitor. Coadministration may decrease plasma concentrations of these active metabolites, decreasing the effectiveness of ifosfamide treatment.
Amobarbital: (Moderate) Closely monitor for increased ifosfamide-related toxicities (e.g., neurotoxicity, nephrotoxicity) if coadministration with amobarbital is necessary; consider adjusting the dose of ifosfamide as clinically appropriate. Ifosfamide is metabolized to its active alkylating metabolites by CYP3A4; amobarbital is a CYP3A4 inducer. Concomitant use may increase the formation of the neurotoxic/nephrotoxic ifosfamide metabolite, chloroacetaldehyde.

Aprepitant, Fosaprepitant: (Moderate) Monitor for a decrease in the efficacy of ifosfamide if coadministration with aprepitant, fosaprepitant is necessary. Depending on the severity of symptoms, patients may respond to supportive care; more severe symptoms may require treatment with methylene blue, exchange transfusion, or hyperbaric oxygen.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The incidence of alopecia can be as high as 100% if ifosfamide is used in combination with other antineoplastic agents. When ifosfamide is used as a single agent, the incidence is about 83%. Alopecia is reversible upon discontinuation of treatment. It appears, on average, near day 19 after administration. It is more common with bolus doses than with continuous infusions.The incidence of nausea/vomiting is high during treatment with ifosfamide, occurring in about 58% of patients treated. The use of a 5HT3 antagonist with or without dexamethasone is recommended to control these reactions. Nausea and vomiting are more common with bolus administration. Nausea/vomiting can last for 3 days on average, and longer periods of delayed emesis have been observed. Elevated hepatic enzymes and/or hyperbilirubinemia were noted in 3% of patients. Other gastrointestinal side effects (< 1%) include anorexia, diarrhea, and in some cases, constipation.Bone marrow depression is dose-related and is the dose-limiting toxicity in standard regimens. Approximately 50% of patients present with a WBC of < 3000/µL after 5 days of treatment with ifosfamide. Thrombocytopenia occurs in about 20% of patients at this level. At higher dosage levels, the incidence of leukopenia and neutropenia is almost 100%. After a 3—4 week interval, the WBC count generally recovers, and treatment can be repeated. Severe bone marrow depression (pancytopenia) greatly increases the risk of infection and the patient's ability to fight the infection; infection was reported in 8% of all patients in published clinical trials. Particular caution should be exercised in isolating patients from contacts with virus infection. Anemia can be manifested as a slow and persistent fall in hemoglobin values. When ifosfamide is used in combination with other myelosuppressive agents, adjustments in dosing may be necessary. Ifosfamide can be associated with impaired wound healing.Ifosfamide is urotoxic, and between 6—92% of patients experience hematuria, dysuria, and increased urinary frequency. The urotoxicity is dose-related. Ifosfamide is metabolized to acrolein, which seeks binding sites on the bladder mucosa, damages the membranes, and leads to bleeding. The symptoms begin 1—2 days after administration and, on average, last for 9 days. The incidence and severity of the response can be reduced by concomitant administration of a detoxifying agent such as mesna, a fractionated dose schedule, or vigorous hydration. Without use of a urinary protectant such as mesna, microscopic hematuria is expected in about 50% of patients and gross hematuria/hemorrhagic cystitis in about 8% of patients. Treatment of hemorrhagic cystitis requires evacuation of clots and continuous bladder irrigation. Instillation of 1% alum, prostaglandins E2 and F2, high-dose carboprost tranexamic acid, or formalin have been utilized to varying degrees of efficacy.Renal insufficiency occurs in about 6% of patients. Patients can develop acute renal tubular necrosis consisting of a proximal tubular effect and Fanconi syndrome. There may be a rise in the blood urea nitrogen (e.g., azotemia) and serum creatinine. Additional clinical signs include polyuria, metabolic acidosis, and renal phosphate wasting, leading to hypophosphatemia-induced rickets. This effect is usually reversible, but there have been reports of renal failure (unspecified) requiring dialysis. Metabolic acidosis was reported in 31% of patients receiving ifosfamide 2—2.5 g/m2/day for 4 days. An additional 15% of patients may develop subclinical renal tubular dysfunction. It has been suggested that following β2-microglobulin or urinary retinol binding protein levels may be helpful in determining ifosfamide-induced nephrotoxicity. Following high-dose ifosfamide regimens, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatemia, and reduced serum bicarbonate levels may occur. Acute renal failure may be dose-limiting when high-dose ifosfamide is used as part of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Risk factors which predispose patients to developing nephrotoxicity include concomitant administration of nephrotoxic drugs, especially cisplatin or carboplatin, young age, and higher cumulative dose of ifosfamide. Mesna does not protect against the nephrotoxicity of ifosfamide. Direct comparisons of continuous infusion or fractionated dosing of ifosfamide reveal an increased incidence of urologic toxicity associated with infusional ifosfamide (14.1% versus 2.7%, respectively) while an increase in neurologic toxicity is noted following prolonged continuous infusions (5.5% versus 1%, respectively).The most common CNS adverse reactions to ifosfamide therapy are confusion, drowsiness/somnolence, hallucinations, and depressive psychosis, which occur in about 12% of all patients treated with ifosfamide. Other less common effects include ataxia, dizziness, disorientation, extrapyramidal effects, and cranial nerve dysfunction, with occasional seizures and coma reported. Encephalopathy becomes one of the dose-limiting toxicities of high-dose ifosfamide. The chloroacetaldehyde metabolite of ifosfamide is most likely responsible for these reactions. The onset of CNS toxicity may occur during ifosfamide infusion or after completion. In general, symptoms spontaneously reverse within 48—72 hours of ifosfamide discontinuation. Risk factors for the development of CNS toxicity include the following: oral route of administration utilized, high ifosfamide dosing regimen, short IV infusions, poor performance status prior to treatment, decreased albumin levels, renal dysfunction, and female gender. The use of methylene blue may effectively reduce the risk of ifosfamide-induced encephalopathy to almost 0% (see Methylene Blue monograph). Methylene blue has also been used as an effective treatment for ifosfamide-induced CNS toxicity.     General Information
    Ifosfamide interferes with oogenesis and spermatogenesis, which may result in both male and female infertility. Development of sterility appears to depend on the dose of ifosfamide, duration of therapy, and state of gonadal function at the time of treatment; sterility may be irreversible in some patients. Amenorrhea has been reported in postmarketing experience with ifosfamide; the risk of permanent chemotherapy-induced amenorrhea increases with age. Pediatric patients treated with ifosfamide during prepubescence may not subsequently conceive, and those who retain ovarian function after completing treatment are at increased risk of developing premature menopause. Pediatric patients treated with ifosfamide during prepubescence might not develop secondary sexual characteristics normally, but may have oligospermia or azoospermia. Sexual function and libido are generally unimpaired in these patients. Some degree of testicular atrophy may occur. Additionally, ovarian failure, premature menopause, ovarian disorder, ovulation disorder (anovulation), spermatogenesis inhibition, decreased blood estrogen, and increased blood gonadotropin were reported in postmarketing experience with humans treated with ifosfamide.

Description
  Text
  Ifosfamide is an intravenous alkylating agent related to the nitrogen mustards; it is a prodrug requiring activation by CYP3A4 for its cytotoxic activity. It is a synthetic analog of cyclophosphamide, although it has a different spectrum of activity. Ifosfamide carries a black box warning for myelosuppression, neurotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and hemorrhagic cystitis; it should be administered with mesna and high volume IV fluids. Ifosfamide is FDA approved for the treatment of advanced testicular cancer; however, it also has several off-label uses including sarcoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and other cancers.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Ifosfamide is an oxazaphosphorine alkylating agent related to the nitrogen mustards. It is a prodrug requiring activation by cytochrome P450 3A4 for its cytotoxic activity; activation occurs by hydroxylation at the ring carbon atom forming the unstable intermediate 4-hydroxyifosfamide and its ring-opened aldo tautomer, which decomposes to yield the cytotoxic and urotoxic compound acrolein, an alkylating isophosphoramide mustard, and multiple nontoxic products. The exact mechanism of action has not been determined, but its cytotoxic action is primarily through DNA crosslinks caused by alkylation of the isophosphoramide mustard at guanine N-7 positions. The formation of inter- and intrastrand crosslinks in the DNA results in cell death.

  Text
  Ifosfamide is administered intravenously. Ifosfamide shows little protein binding, but it and its active metabolites are extensively bound by red blood cells. It exhibits both dose-dependent and time-dependent pharmacokinetics. At single doses ranging from 3.8 to 5 g/m2, plasma concentrations decay biphasically and the mean terminal half-life is about 15 hours; however at lower doses (1.6 to 2.4 g/m2 per day), plasma decay is monoexponential and the terminal elimination half-life is about 7 hours. When administered intravenously over 30 minutes once daily for 5 days, the median elimination half-life decreased from 7.2 hours on day 1 to 4.6 hours on day 5; median clearance increased from 66 mL/minute on day 1 to 115 mL/minute on day 5. There was no significant change in volume of distribution on day 5 (0.72 L/kg) compared with day 1 (0.64 L/kg). The volume of distribution (Vd) of ifosfamide is approximates the total body water volume, suggesting minimal tissue binding.
 
Ifosfamide is extensively metabolized through 2 metabolic pathways: ring oxidation to form the active metabolite (4-hydroxy-ifosfamide), and side-chain oxidation to form the inactive metabolites (3-dechloro-ethylifosfamide or 2-dechloroethylifosfamide with liberation of the toxic metabolite, chloroacetaldehyde). Small quantities (nmol/mL) of ifosfamide mustard and 4-hydroxyifosfamide are detectable in human plasma. Metabolism is required for generation of biologically active species and while metabolism is extensive, it is also variable. After administration of radiolabeled ifosfamide at a dose of 5 g/m2, 70% to 86% of the dosed radioactivity was recovered in urine as metabolites, with about 61% of the dose excreted as parent compound. At lower doses (1.6 to 2.4 g/m2), only 12% to 18% of the dose was excreted as unchanged drug within 72 hours. Two different dechloroethylated derivatives of ifosfamide, 4-carboxyifosfamide, thiodiacetic acid and cysteine conjugates of chloroacetic acid have been identified as the major urinary metabolites of ifosfamide in humans and only small amounts of 4-hydroxyifosfamide and acrolein are present.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes: CYP3A4, CYP2B6
Ifosfamide is metabolized primarily in the liver by the hepatic cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A4 and 2B6 isoenzymes. It appears to undergo 2-component Michaelis-Menton model pharmacokinetics including both low Km and high Km CYP 4-hydroxylases. Hepatic metabolism is extensive but variable among patients, producing eight possible metabolites. There are two dechloroethylated derivatives that are identified as the inactive major urinary metabolites. A third metabolite is chloroacetaldehyde, which is chemically related to chloral hydrate and explains some adverse effects of ifosfamide. It appears these N-dechloroethylated metabolites are formed via CYP2B6 to a significant degree. Ketone and thiol groups at the 4 position form two more inactive metabolites.The active component is formed via CYP3A4 which transforms ifosfamide to 4-hydroxyifosfamide, an intermediate derivative that appears in equilibrium with aldoifosfamide. Aldoifosfamide breaks down spontaneously to active ifosforamide mustard with the liberation of acrolein. With ifosfamide, only 50% of a dose is metabolized compared with 90% for cyclophosphamide. Autoinduction of ifosfamide metabolism has been observed, but the mechanism is unknown.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Use ifosfamide with caution in patients with impaired hepatic function. No formal studies have been conducted in these patients, and ifosfamide is extensively metabolized in the liver to both efficacious and toxic metabolites.
  Renal Impairment
  Closely monitor patients with renal impairment for toxicity; consider a dose reduction if appropriate. Formal studies have not been conducted in patients with renal impairment; however, ifosfamide and its metabolites are excreted by the kidneys and may accumulate in patients with decreased renal function. Ifosfamide and its metabolites are dialyzable.
