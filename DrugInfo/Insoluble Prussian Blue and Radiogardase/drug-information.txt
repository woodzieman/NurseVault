Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Insoluble Prussian Blue is used to decrease radiation exposure and does not treat the complications due to radiation exposure. Patients contaminated with high doses of radioactive cesium may develop radiation toxicity including bone marrow suppression with severe neutropenia and thrombocytopenia. Supportive treatment for radiation toxicity symptoms should be given concomitantly with Insoluble Prussian Blue treatment.
 
In radiological emergencies, the type of elemental exposure may not be known. Insoluble Prussian Blue may not bind to all radioactive elements and some radioactive elements may not undergo enterohepatic circulation which is needed for Insoluble Prussian Blue binding and elimination. Patients contaminated with unknown or multiple radioactive elements may require treatment with other agents, in addition to Insoluble Prussian Blue. Before treatment, establish if the patient suffers from a single or combined injury (e.g., radiation, burns, trauma, chemical, biological, etc.) and whether the contaminant may be internalized. The route of entry of the radiation contaminant needs to be identified and recorded. The route of entry will determine other treatment methods needed (e.g., wound debridement or stomach lavage if ingested). Patients need to be triaged based on their injuries and the level and type of contamination.
 
Decreased gastrointestinal motility will slow the transit time of radioactive cesium bound to Insoluble Prussian Blue in the GI tract and may increase the amount of radiation absorbed. Insoluble Prussian Blue can cause constipation that may be treated with a fiber based laxative and/or a high fiber diet. Insoluble Prussian Blue should be used with caution in patients with GI obstruction, ileus, preexisting constipation, or disorders associated with decreased gastrointestinal motility.
 
Insoluble Prussian Blue may be less effective in patients with hepatic disease due to decreased excretion of cesium and thallium in the bile; however, no dosage adjustments are necessary.
 
Insoluble Prussian Blue is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. Since the drug is not absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, effects on the fetus are not expected. Radioactive cesium and thallium are known to cross the human placenta. Reported fetal effects of thallium include fetal death, failure to thrive, alopecia, or in some instances outwardly normal development. The risk of toxicity from untreated radioactive cesium or thallium exposure is expected to be greater than any potential toxicity risk from Insoluble Prussian Blue .
 
Studies to determine if Insoluble Prussian Blue is excreted in human milk have not been conducted. Since the drug is not absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, its excretion in breast milk is highly unlikely. However, cesium and thallium will be transmitted from mother to infant in breast milk. Breast-feeding is contraindicated in women internally contaminated with cesium or thallium, regardless of Insoluble Prussian Blue administration.
 
The safety and efficacy of Insoluble Prussian Blue and its dosing for children was extrapolated from adult data and supported by pediatric patients who were internally contaminated with radioactive cesium and treated with Insoluble Prussian Blue. In patients for whom the rate of radiation elimination data are available, the rate was similar to adults at the recommended dosages. Pediatric patients aged 2 to 4 years are expected to have biliary and gastrointestinal function that is comparable to a 4-year old. There are variations in the developmental maturity of the biliary system and gastrointestinal tract of neonates and infants (0—2 years) and the dose-related adverse effects of Insoluble Prussian Blue on an immature gastrointestinal tract are not known. Dosing in infants and neonates has not been established.
 
Insoluble Prussian Blue may bind electrolytes in the gastrointestinal tract causing asymptomatic hypokalemia. Serum electrolytes should be closely monitored during Insoluble Prussian Blue treatment and caution should be exercised when treating patients with pre-existing cardiac arrhythmias or an electrolyte imbalance.

Description
  Text
  Insoluble Prussian blue is an orally administered decorporation agent indicated for the treatment of known or suspected internal contamination with radioactive cesium and radioactive or non-radioactive thallium. The drug contains ferric hexacyanoferrate, a cubic lattice with iron atoms on the corners and cyanide groups on the sides. This molecule has a high affinity for cesium and thallium, trapping these metals within the crystal structure. Insoluble Prussian blue is not absorbed through the gastrointestinal wall; thus, binding occurs in the GI tract and requires the cesium and thallium elements to undergo enterohepatic circulation in order to remove them from systemic circulation. Once bound, the drug increases the rate of cesium and thallium elimination from the body. Begin treatment as soon as possible. If treatment cannot be started right away, patients should be given insoluble Prussian blue as soon as it becomes available, as treatment is still effective even after time has elapsed since exposure. Treatment has been associated with electrolyte abnormalities (hypokalemia), constipation, and reduced GI motility. Additionally, advise patients that their stools may turn blue and, if the capsules are opened and ingested with food, may color their oral mucosa and teeth blue.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Insoluble Prussian blue is an orally administered decorporation agent that contains ferric hexacyanoferrate, a cubic lattice with iron atoms on the corners and cyanide groups on the sides. This molecule acts by ion-exchange, adsorption, and mechanical trapping within the crystal structure, and has a high affinity for radioactive and non-radioactive cesium and thallium. Insoluble Prussian blue binds to cesium and thallium in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract after they are ingested or excreted in the bile via enterohepatic circulation; thereby increasing their rates of elimination. In studies of animals internally contaminated with cesium and thallium, binding with Prussian blue formed insoluble complexes in the gastrointestinal lumen, changed the primary elimination route from the kidney to the feces, and increased the rate of elimination.
 
Based on animal data, concurrent administration with other decorporation agents does not affect the efficacy of insoluble Prussian blue.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name

    Description
    Insoluble Prussian blue is not absorbed through the intact gastrointestinal (GI) wall. The drug forms insoluble complexes with cesium and thallium in the GI tract. Food may increase the effectiveness of the drug by stimulating bile secretion of cesium and thallium into the GI lumen, where they are available for binding with insoluble Prussian blue and ultimate elimination via the feces. Clearance of these complexes from the body depends on the GI tract transit time; the rate of cesium and thallium elimination is proportional to the duration of administration and the dose of insoluble Prussian blue.
Cesium-137 (137Cs) Contamination: In literature reports, 72 people have received insoluble Prussian blue after exposure to 137Cs. In adults, the untreated whole body effective half-life of 137Cs was 80 +/- 15 days. With administration of insoluble Prussian blue, the mean whole body effective half-life of 137Cs was reduced by 69%.
Thallium Contamination: Thirty-four patients treated with insoluble Prussian blue for non-radioactive thallium poisoning are reported in the literature. Use of the drug reduced the mean serum biologic half-life of thallium from 8 days to 3 days.

NOTE: Prior to starting treatment, follow radioactive decontamination safety procedures including:
Use of appropriate radiation protective attire and close monitoring of personnel and treatment area for radiation levels using either radiation detection, indication, and computation devices (RADIAC) or thermal luminescent devices (TLD).
Controlling the spread of radiation contamination by establishing a patient decontamination area and a contaminated material disposal site (proper labeling, handling, disposal of contaminated material).
Inform individuals of safety measures to be taken to minimize radiation exposure to others or reexposure to self, including appropriate use of the toilet, handwashing, and handling of items that might get contaminated with body fluids.
