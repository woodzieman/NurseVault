Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Golimumab is only administered subcutaneously. Do not give by intravenous administration.
 
Do not start golimumab in a patient who has an active infection including chronic, localized, or systemic infections such as sepsis or influenza. Also, discontinue golimumab if a serious infection develops. Because golimumab neutralizes the biological activity of TNF-alpha, there is a possibility that golimumab will affect host defenses against infection. Infliximab, etanercept, adalimumab, certolizumab, and golimumab labels have a black box warning regarding the risk of tuberculosis and other opportunistic infections. Serious infections including disseminated or extrapulmonary tuberculosis, fungal infection (e.g., histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, pneumocystosis), and infections due to bacterial, viral, and opportunistic pathogens have occurred in patients who received golimumab. Patients with invasive fungal infections may present with disseminated disease, and antigen and antibody testing for histoplasmosis may be negative in some patients with an active infection. Consider empiric antifungal therapy in patients at risk for invasive fungal infections who develop severe systemic illness. Patients who have surgery while taking golimumab may be at greater risk for postoperative infections. Patients need to be evaluated for tuberculosis risk factors and for latent or active tuberculosis infection with a tuberculin skin test before and during golimumab receipt. The possibility of anergy needs to be considered when interpreting the test result. If tuberculin skin testing is performed for latent tuberculosis infection, an induration size of 5 mm or greater should be considered positive even if the patient was vaccinated previously with Bacille Calmette-Guerin. As cases of tuberculosis have occurred in patients who received golimumab, treatment of latent infection should be started before golimumab initiation. Treatment of latent tuberculosis infection before therapy with TNF-blockers has been shown to reduce the risk of tuberculosis reactivation during therapy. Consider antituberculosis therapy before golimumab initiation in patients with a history of latent or active tuberculosis in whom an adequate course of treatment cannot be confirmed and in patients who have several or highly significant risk factors for tuberculosis infection and have a negative test for latent tuberculosis. Monitor all patients, even those with an initial negative latent tuberculosis test, for active tuberculosis and other infections during golimumab receipt. Patients with a history of recurrent infections or with underlying conditions that may predispose them to infections (e.g., patients with advanced or uncontrolled diabetes mellitus or immunosuppression), or those who have lived in tuberculosis or histoplasmosis endemic areas may not be appropriate candidates for golimumab therapy. Many of the serious infections occurred in patients on immunosuppressive therapy who received golimumab. Carefully consider the benefits and risks of golimumab therapy before drug initiation, especially in patients with chronic or recurrent infection.
 
Because golimumab neutralizes the biological activity of TNF-alpha, golimumab may affect host defenses against neoplastic disease. An increased risk of neoplastic disease is associated with the use of TNF blockers such as golimumab in patients less than 18 years of age. Consider the risks and benefits of golimumab before treatment initiation in patients with a known malignancy other than a successfully treated non-melanoma skin cancer. Also, consider the risks and benefits of golimumab continuation in patients who develop a malignancy. Use of golimumab by patients with a history of malignancy or current malignancy may be unadvisable (see Adverse Effects).
 
Hepatitis B virus (HBV) carriers may not be appropriate candidates for golimumab receipt, as golimumab may cause HBV reactivation in patients who are HBV carriers. Evaluate patients at risk for HBV infection for prior evidence of infection before golimumab initiation. Adequate data are not available on the safety or efficacy of golimumab receipt in HBV carriers who are on antiviral therapy to prevent HBV reactivation. If golimumab is initiated in an HBV carrier, closely monitor the patient for clinical and laboratory signs of active HBV infection during golimumab receipt and for several months after drug discontinuation. Golimumab discontinuation is advised if HBV reactivation occurs (see Adverse Reactions). The safety of resuming TNF-blockers after HBV reactivation has been controlled is not known.
 
Patients with heart failure may not be appropriate candidates for golimumab receipt. Cases of worsening congestive heart failure (CHF) and new onset CHF have been reported with other TNF-blockers. Infliximab, a similar therapeutic agent to golimumab, is contraindicated in patients with moderate to severe heart failure (NYHA Class III/IV). Increased mortality and worsening heart failure were noted in a study of infliximab-treated patients. Although golimumab has not been studied in patients with a history of CHF, worsening heart failure may develop. Cautious use of golimumab is advised for patients with CHF; if golimumab is initiated, closely monitor patients. Golimumab cessation is recommended if new or worsening heart failure symptoms develop.
 
Exercise caution when considering the use of golimumab in patients with preexisting or recent-onset neurological disease. Patients with CNS demyelinating disorders such as multiple sclerosis may not be appropriate candidates for golimumab receipt. Use of other TNF-blockers has been associated with cases of new onset or exacerbation of central nervous system demyelinating disorders. For example, use of another TNF blocker was associated with increased disease activity in patients with multiple sclerosis (see Adverse Reactions).
 
Patients with significant cytopenias may not be appropriate candidates for golimumab receipt. Pancytopenia, aplastic anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and neutropenia have been noted with other TNF blockers. Cautious use of golimumab is advised for patients with significant cytopenias.
 
The needle cover on the prefilled syringe and the prefilled syringe in the autoinjector contain dry, natural rubber and may be inappropriate for patients with latex hypersensitivity.
 
Limited data that examine the responses to vaccination in patients receiving golimumab exist (see Interactions). Live virus vaccines should not be given concurrently with golimumab.
 
The safety and efficacy of golimumab in children have not been established. An increased risk of lymphoma and other cancers is associated with the use of TNF blockers in children and adolescents (see Adverse Reactions).
 
In clinical trials, no overall difference was noted in the safety profile of golimumab between and elderly and younger patients. Because there is a higher incidence of infections in the elderly in general, cautious golimumab use is advised.
 
Golimumab is a FDA pregnancy category B drug. The ability of golimumab to cause fetal harm or to affect reproduction capacity is unknown. As there are no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women, the use of golimumab is only recommended if clearly needed.
 
According to the manufacturer, golimumab or breast-feeding should be discontinued because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in nursing infants. Golimumab excretion into human milk or systemic absorption after ingestion is unknown. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.

    Headline
    infection
    General Information
    Treatment with golimumab increases the risk of serious infections that may lead to hospitalization or death. Do not initiate golimumab in people with an active infection, including clinically important localized infections. Use golimumab with caution in people at increased risk for serious infections, including those with chronic or recurrent infections, a history of opportunistic infections, or underlying conditions that may predispose to infection. Patients with co-morbid conditions and those receiving concomitant immunosuppressants (e.g., corticosteroids, methotrexate) are at greater risk. Patients residing in or with travel history to regions where mycoses are endemic (e.g., Ohio and Mississippi River valleys, Southwest) are at increased risk for invasive fungal infections. For patients who develop a serious systemic illness and reside or have traveled to such regions, consider empiric antifungal therapy while a diagnostic workup is being performed. Health care providers are advised that antigen and antibody testing for histoplasmosis may be negative in some patients with active infection. Consider consultation with a physician experienced in fungal infections. Test all potential drug recipients for hepatitis B infection prior to initiating treatment with golimumab. For patients who test positive for hepatitis B surface antigen, consult a physician with expertise in the treatment of hepatitis B infection before starting golimumab. The use of TNF blockers, including golimumab, in patients who are chronic hepatitis B carriers (i.e., surface antigen positive) has been associated with reactivation of hepatitis B virus, with some cases resulting in death. Patients who are carriers of hepatitis B virus and require treatment with golimumab should be closely monitored for clinical and laboratory signs of active hepatitis B infection throughout therapy and for several months after treatment discontinuation. Discontinue golimumab and initiate antiviral therapy if hepatitis B reactivation occurs. Golimumab also increases the risk of reactivation of latent tuberculosis or new tuberculosis infection. Screen all patients for latent TB infection prior to initiating therapy and periodically during treatment. The possibility of anergy needs to be considered when interpreting the test result. Consider an induration size of 5 mm or greater as a positive tuberculin skin test, even if the patient was vaccinated previously with Bacille Calmette-Guerin. Initiate treatment for latent TB prior to starting golimumab. Consider anti-tuberculosis therapy in patients with a history of latent or active TB in whom an adequate course of treatment cannot be confirmed, and in those with risk factors for TB infection. Monitor for signs and symptoms of TB and other infections during and after therapy, even in patients who tested negative for latent TB prior to initiation. For patients who develop an infection during treatment with golimumab, perform a prompt and complete diagnostic workup appropriate for immunocompromised patients and initiate appropriate antimicrobial therapy. Discontinue golimumab if a patient develops a serious infection, sepsis, or opportunistic infection. In addition, care should be taken when switching from one biologic product to another as overlapping biological activity may further increase the risk of infection. Health care providers are encouraged to report adverse events to the MedWatch Safety Information and Adverse Event Reporting Program by telephoning 1-800-332-1088 or by completing the form online.
    Headline
    malignancy, primary sclerosing cholangitis
    General Information
    Consider the risks and benefits of golimumab before treatment initiation in people with a known malignancy other than a successfully treated non-melanoma skin cancer. Incidences of new malignancy, some fatal, have been reported among children, adolescents, and young adults receiving treatment with TNF blockers (therapy initiation prior to 18 years of age). Approximately half of the cases were lymphomas, including both Hodgkin's and non-Hodgkin's lymphoma. Hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma, often fatal, has also occurred, especially in adolescent and young adult males with Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis receiving concomitant azathioprine or 6-mercaptopurine. The other cases represented a variety of malignancies, including rare malignancies that are usually associated with immunosuppression, and malignancies that are not usually observed in pediatric patients. Leukemia, melanoma, and Merkel cell carcinoma have been reported in patients treated with TNF-blocking agents, including golimumab. Most malignancies occurred after a median of 30 months (range 1 to 84 months) after the first dose of TNF-blocker therapy. Most of the patients were receiving concomitant immunosuppressants. Screen all patients with ulcerative colitis who are at increased risk for dysplasia or colon carcinoma (such as those with long-standing ulcerative colitis or primary sclerosing cholangitis) and those with a history of dysplasia or colon carcinoma for dysplasia at regular intervals before and during golimumab receipt. Periodic skin examinations are recommended for all drug recipients, particularly those with risk factors for skin cancer. Consider also the risks and benefits of golimumab continuation in patients who develop a malignancy including newly diagnosed dysplasia.

Closely monitor patients with CHF during golimumab therapy. Discontinue golimumab if new or worsening CHF symptoms occur.

    demyelinating disorder
    General Information
    Use golimumab with caution in patients with a history of a demyelinating disorder, including multiple sclerosis and acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy or Guillain-Barre syndrome. Rare cases of new-onset or exacerbation of central and peripheral demyelinating disorders have been reported with the use of TNF blockers. Consider discontinuation of golimumab if a demyelinating disease develops.
    Headline
    planned procedure
    General Information
    Patients who undergo surgery while taking biologic therapy, such as golimumab, may be at greater risk for postoperative infections. For a planned procedure, balance the risk of postoperative infection against the risk of developing a severe or unstable disease by stopping the biologic therapy. Recommendations for the use of immunomodulatory agents in the perioperative setting vary. The American Academy of Dermatology and National Psoriasis Foundation recommend patients with psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis continue to receive TNF-blockers through low-risk surgeries or procedures, but hold these medications for one full dosing interval before undergoing total knee or total hip arthroplasties. The British Association of Dermatologists advise stopping biologic therapy 3- to 5-times the half-life or the length of the treatment cycle (whichever is longer) between the last dose and a planned surgery. Restart the biologic therapy postoperatively if there is no evidence of infection and wound healing is satisfactory.

    geriatric
    General Information
    Use golimumab with caution in geriatric adults (age 65 years and older), as there is a higher incidence of infections with TNF blocker therapy in this population. In clinical trials, no overall differences in adverse reactions were observed between older and younger adults; however, increased monitoring for infection is recommended.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data from case reports and observational studies of golimumab use during pregnancy have not identified a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. In general, TNF inhibitors are considered to have low to moderate risk when used during pregnancy. Monoclonal antibodies, such as golimumab, are transported across the placenta during the third trimester of pregnancy and may affect immune response after birth in the exposed neonate and infant. Expert consensus recommends continuing maintenance anti-TNF therapy throughout pregnancy for patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). However, for IBD and other indications, it has also been suggested to pause golimumab treatment in the third trimester (several half-lives prior to delivery) if the patient's disease is under good control, to minimize drug transfer to the fetus. Data indicate increased disease activity is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes in people with rheumatoid arthritis or inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), such as preterm delivery, low birth weight, and small for gestational age infants. Individualize treatment to optimize maternal and pregnancy outcomes.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Due to its inhibition of TNF-alpha, golimumab may affect the immune response of neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero. Neonates and infants born to individuals treated with TNF-blockers during their pregnancy may be at increased risk of infection for up to 6 months. Per FDA-approved labeling, administration of live vaccines to infants exposed to golimumab in utero is not recommended for 6 months after the last golimumab injection during pregnancy. Limited data and expert opinion suggest inactive vaccines may be administered without adjustment; biologic exposure in utero does not appear to affect the normal immune response to inactive vaccines in exposed infants. However, the safety of administering live or live-attenuated vaccines in exposed infants is unknown. Consider the benefits and risks of administering live and live-attenuated vaccines in this population. Limited data and expert opinion suggest the live rotavirus vaccine may be provided to children with in utero exposure to biologics. However, it is recommended to avoid administration of the live Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) vaccine in the first 6 months of life in those exposed to anti-TNF therapy due to the risk of disseminated infection and associated mortality.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Experts and guidelines consider golimumab compatible with breast-feeding. There is no information on the presence of golimumab in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Maternal IgG is present in human milk. In cynomolgus monkeys, golimumab concentrations in milk were approximately 400 times lower than the maternal serum concentrations. If golimumab is transferred to human milk, the effects of local exposure to the gastrointestinal tract and potential limited systemic exposure to the child are unknown. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition. Adalimumab and infliximab are potential alternatives to consider during breast-feeding. However, indication and patient-specific factors should be assessed before choosing an alternative agent. In a study involving infants breastfed by patients with inflammatory bowel disease who were receiving biologic therapies, low concentrations of adalimumab and infliximab were detected in human milk samples. Golimumab was not detected in the milk of the 1 patient enrolled in the study who was receiving golimumab. Infection risk and developmental milestone achievement were similar between breastfed infants of patients on biologics, immunomodulators, or combination therapies compared to infants not exposed to these medications or non-breastfed infants.

Interactions
Abatacept: (Major) Concomitant use of abatacept with other biologic agents, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, is not recommended because of the possibility of additive immunosuppression and increased risk of infection. In controlled clinical trials, patients receiving concomitant abatacept and TNF inhibitor therapy experienced more infections (63%) and serious infections (4.4%) as compared with patients treated with only TNF inhibitors (43% and 0.8%, respectively). These trials failed to demonstrate an important enhancement of efficacy with concomitant administration of abatacept with a TNF inhibitor. Monitor patients for signs of infection during the transition from TNF antagonist therapy to abatacept therapy.
Adalimumab: (Contraindicated) Do not use golimumab in combination with other tumor necrosis factor (TNF) modifier therapy, including adalimumab, due to the potential for additive effects and drug toxicity. It is unknown if any adverse effects would occur if golimumab is used concomitantly with adalimumab. A potential exists for increased risk of infection or an impact on the development of malignancies from increased activity toward TNF.
Anakinra: (Major) Avoid the concomitant use of anakinra with biological DMARDs, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) modifiers; coadministration has not been studied and may result in additive immunosuppression and increased risk of infection with no additional clinical benefit. Data suggest a higher rate of serious infections when anakinra and a TNF inhibitor is used in combination with anakinra compared with either drug given alone. Neutropenia (1,000/mcL or less) was observed in 2% of patients receiving the combination. The use of anakinra with a TNF inhibitor in combination did not yield any additional clinical benefit as compared to the use of the TNF inhibitor alone.
Anifrolumab: (Major) Coadministration is not recommended. Anifrolumab has not been studied in combination with other biologic therapies including tumor necrosis factor modifiers (TNF blockers). Potential concerns with use of these drugs together include an increased susceptibility to immunosuppression and serious infections, some of which might be fatal.
Antithymocyte Globulin: (Moderate) The safety and efficacy of golimumab in patients with immunosuppression have not been evaluated. Patients receiving immunosuppressives along with golimumab may be at a greater risk of developing an infection.
azaTHIOprine: (Moderate) The safety and efficacy of golimumab in patients with immunosuppression have not been evaluated. Patients receiving immunosuppressives along with golimumab may be at a greater risk of developing an infection.
Bacillus Calmette-Guerin Vaccine, BCG: (Contraindicated) Do not administer live vaccines to golimumab recipients. Limited data are available on the response to live vaccination or on the risk of infection or infection transmission after the administration. Live virus vaccines should generally not be administered to an immunosuppressed patient. Live virus vaccines may induce the illness they are intended to prevent and are generally contraindicated for use during immunosuppressive treatment. The immune response of the immunocompromised patient to vaccines may be decreased, even despite alternate vaccination schedules or more frequent booster doses. If immunization is necessary, choose an alternative to live vaccination, or, consider a delay or change in the immunization schedule. Practitioners should refer to the most recent CDC guidelines regarding vaccination of patients who are receiving drugs that adversely affect the immune system.
Baricitinib: (Major) Concomitant use of baricitinib with biologic DMARDs, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) modifiers, is not recommended because of the possibility of additive immunosuppression and increased infection risk. Baricitinib may be used as monotherapy or concomitantly with methotrexate or other nonbiologic DMARDs.
Basiliximab: (Moderate) The safety and efficacy of golimumab in patients with immunosuppression have not been evaluated. Patients receiving immunosuppressives along with golimumab may be at a greater risk of developing an infection.
Belimumab: (Major) Avoid use together. Belimumab has not been studied in combination with other biologic therapies including tumor necrosis factor modifiers (TNF blockers). Therefore, belimumab use is not recommended in combination with TNF blockers. Potential concerns with use of these drugs together include an increased susceptibility to immunosuppression and serious infections, some of which might be fatal.
Canakinumab: (Major) Avoid concomitant administration of canakinumab with tumor necrosis factor (TNF) modifiers; coadministration has not been studied and may result in additive immunosuppression and increased risk of infection. An increased incidence of serious infections and an increased risk of neutropenia have been associated with administration of another IL-1 blocker in combination with TNF inhibitors. Based upon the potential for similar interactions, concomitant administration of canakinumab and TNF inhibitors is not recommended.
Certolizumab pegol: (Contraindicated) Do not use certolizumab in combination with other tumor necrosis factor (TNF) modifier therapy, including golimumab, due to the potential for additive effects and drug toxicity. It is unknown if any adverse effects would occur if certolizumab is used concomitantly with golimumab. A potential exists for increased risk of infection or an impact on the development of malignancies from increased activity toward TNF.
Chikungunya Vaccine, Live: (Contraindicated) Do not administer live vaccines to golimumab recipients. Limited data are available on the response to live vaccination or on the risk of infection or infection transmission after the administration. Live virus vaccines should generally not be administered to an immunosuppressed patient. Live virus vaccines may induce the illness they are intended to prevent and are generally contraindicated for use during immunosuppressive treatment. The immune response of the immunocompromised patient to vaccines may be decreased, even despite alternate vaccination schedules or more frequent booster doses. If immunization is necessary, choose an alternative to live vaccination, or, consider a delay or change in the immunization schedule. Practitioners should refer to the most recent CDC guidelines regarding vaccination of patients who are receiving drugs that adversely affect the immune system.
Cholera Vaccine: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the live cholera vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications.   General Information
  Golimumab may cause elevated hepatic enzymes. In trials up to 16 weeks in duration, increased alanine aminotransferase (ALT) concentrations occurred in 4% of 1659 golimumab recipients and in 3% of 639 placebo recipients. Elevations of ALT of at least 5 times the upper limit of normal were noted in 0.7% of golimumab recipients as compared with 0.2% of control recipients. Elevations of ALT of at least 3 times the upper limit of normal were noted in 2% of patients in both groups. The relationship between golimumab receipt and ALT elevation is unclear, as many patients were taking medications such as methotrexate and NSAIDs that cause liver enzyme elevations. Of importance, reports exist of severe hepatic reactions such as acute liver failure in TNF-blocker recipients. Reactivation of hepatitis B virus (HBV) may occur in patients who are chronic HBV carriers and who take golimumab. Viral reactivation has been fatal in some patients who took TNF blocker therapy. Most reports of HBV reactivation have been in patients who were taking other medicines that suppress the immune system; immunosuppressant drugs may also contribute to HBV reactivation. Stop golimumab and start effective antiviral therapy in patients who develop HBV reactivation. The safety of resuming golimumab after control of HBV reactivation is achieved is unknown. If golimumab is restarted, carefully monitor patients.
 
The possibility exists for golimumab to affect host defenses against infection, as TNF mediates inflammation and modulates cellular immune responses. In clinical trials, infections were noted in 28% of golimumab recipients and in 25% of control-treated recipients. Upper respiratory tract infections and nasopharyngitis were the most commonly reported infections. Serious infections such as tuberculosis, sepsis, and opportunistic infections may occur. In clinical trials, sepsis, influenza, pneumonia, cellulitis, abscess, pulmonary and extra-pulmonary tuberculosis, invasive fungal infections, and hepatitis B infection were noted. Serious infections occurred in 1.4% of golimumab recipients and in 1.3% of control-treated patients in phase 3, 16-week trials. The incidence of active tuberculosis was 0.23 per 100 patient-years for golimumab recipients as compared with 0 per 100 patient-years for placebo recipients. Testing for latent tuberculosis is needed before and during golimumab initiation. Start treatment for tuberculosis for a positive test result for latent tuberculosis (see Contraindications). An induration of at least 5 mm with tuberculin skin testing is considered a positive result even for patients previously vaccinated with BCG. Monitor all patients regardless of latent TB test result for active TB. Patients taking TNF blockers are at increased risk of serious infections including invasive fungal infections such as histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, blastomycosis, aspergillosis, candidiasis, cryptococcosis, as well as other opportunistic infections. Histoplasmosis and other invasive fungal infections are not consistently recognized in patients taking TNF blockers, which has resulted in delayed appropriate treatment that sometimes led to death. Consider empiric antifungal treatment for patients who develop a systemic illness and reside or travel to regions where mycoses are endemic. A complete diagnostic workup that may include fungal cultures, histopathological or cytological evaluations, antigen detection, and serum antibody titers is advised. Signs and symptoms of possible systemic fungal infection include fever, malaise, weight loss, sweats, cough, dyspnea, pulmonary infiltrates, or other serious systemic illness with or without concomitant shock. If possible, consultation with an infectious diseases specialist should be sought. Closely follow patients who develop an infection while taking golimumab; drug discontinuation is recommended in patients who develop a serious infection, as fatalities have resulted in some cases. The decision to restart the TNF blocker should include a reevaluation of the benefits and risks of TNF blockers, especially in patients who live in regions of endemic mycoses.
 
The FDA has concluded that there is an increased risk of lymphoma and other cancers (secondary malignancy) associated with the use of TNF blockers in children and adolescents. However, due to the relatively rare occurrence of these cancers, the limited number of pediatric patients treated with TNF blockers, and the possible role of other immunosuppressive therapies used concomitantly with TNF blockers, the FDA is unable to fully characterize the strength of the association. Further, the observed reporting rates offer very limited inference into potential differences in malignancy risk among the TNF blockers because of actual patient exposure uncertainty and possible malignancy case underreporting. The FDA investigated 48 cases of cancer in children and adolescents who took a TNF blocker; most (88%) also took other immunosuppressive medications such as azathioprine and methotrexate. Approximately half of the cancers were lymphomas and included both Hodgkin's and non-Hodgkin's lymphoma. Other cancers reported included leukemia, melanoma, and solid organ cancers; rare cancers in children such as renal cell carcinoma, leiomyosarcoma, and hepatic malignancies were also reported. Golimumab is not indicated for use in children and was not included in the FDA's review. A possible association exists between TNF blocker receipt and leukemia development. The FDA reviewed 147 postmarketing reports of leukemia including acute myeloid leukemia (44 cases), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (31 cases), and chronic myeloid leukemia (23 cases). Most cases (n = 143) were in adults, and 61% of the 147 patients were also receiving other immunosuppressive therapies. The average time to leukemia onset was within the first 1 to 2 years of therapy. In clinical trials, the incidence of malignancies other than lymphoma in golimumab-treated patients was similar to that expected in the general US population, but a higher incidence of lymphoma was observed among golimumab recipients as compared with the general US population. The incidence of lymphoma among golimumab recipients was 3.8-fold higher than expected in the general US population. Of note, patients with chronic inflammatory diseases, particularly patients with highly active disease and/or chronic exposure to immunosuppressant therapies, may be at several fold higher risk for lymphoma development than the general population even in the absence of TNF-blocking therapy. Monitor patients for the emergence of cancer during and after golimumab receipt. Healthcare professionals are encouraged to report side effects to the FDA by calling 800—332—1088.
 
An injection site reaction such as erythema, itching, or burning may occur. In clinical trials, 6% of 1659 golimumab recipients had an injection site reaction as compared with 2% of 639 placebo recipients. Most reactions were mild and consisted of injection site erythema.
 
A possible association exists between the development of psoriasis and the use of a TNF blocker such as golimumab. The FDA reviewed 69 cases of new onset psoriasis including 17 cases of pustular psoriasis and 15 cases of palmoplantar psoriasis in patients using TNF blockers for the treatment of autoimmune and rheumatic conditions other than psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis. The development of psoriasis during TNF blocker receipt occurred from weeks to years after drug initiation. Most patients had psoriasis improvement after TNF blocker discontinuation. None of the patients reported pre-existing psoriasis before TNF blocker initiation. Also, exacerbation of existing psoriasis has been noted with other TNF blockers; TNF-blocker discontinuation led to psoriasis improvement in most patients. Psoriasis recurrence upon rechallenge with a different TNF-blocker has been noted among some patients. Monitor patients for the emergence of or worsening of psoriasis during TNF blocker receipt. Consider golimumab discontinuation for severe cases and for cases that do not improve or that worsen despite topical treatments.
 
In clinical trials, hypertension occurred in 3% of 1659 patients who received golimumab and in 1% of 639 patients who received placebo. Dizziness was noted in 2% of golimumab recipients and in 1% of placebo recipients.
 
Cases of worsening congestive heart failure (CHF) and new onset CHF have been reported with TNF antagonists other than golimumab. Golimumab has not been formally studied in patients with heart failure (see Contraindications). Golimumab cessation is recommended if new or worsening heart failure symptoms develop.
 
Antibody formation against golimumab during 6 months of drug receipt occurred in 57 of 1659 patients. Patients who received golimumab with methotrexate had a lower rate of antibody development (2%) than patients who did not receive methotrexate (7%). Most patients with a positive antibody response to golimumab were determined to have neutralizing antibodies to golimumab, but the ability to draw definitive conclusions about the relationship between antibodies to golimumab and clinical efficacy or safety is limited by the small numbers of patients. In addition to antibody formation against golimumab, use of TNF-blockers has been associated with the formation of autoantibodies and with the development of a lupus-like syndrome, albeit rarely. In clinical trials through week 14, no association was found between golimumab treatment and newly positive anti-dsDNA antibodies.
 
Rare cases of exacerbation of clinical symptoms or radiographic evidence of demyelinating disease have been associated with another TNF blocker. In clinical trial populations, paresthesias were noted in 1% of 1659 golimumab recipients. Caution is recommended for golimumab use for patients with preexisting or recent-onset central nervous system demyelinating disorders (see Contraindications). Explanation for the discordance of the effects of TNF-alpha antagonism for different autoimmune diseases is unclear. One proposed possibility is an imbalance between TNF-alpha antagonism and autoimmune exacerbation. It is possible that TNF blockers cannot penetrate the blood brain barrier to antagonize TNF-alpha. However, alteration of antigen-presenting cell function, increased T cell receptor signaling, and decreased apoptosis of potentially autoreactive T cells still occurs. Thus, disease worsening may occur with multiple sclerosis, but disease attenuation may occur with rheumatoid arthritis due to prevention of local TNF-alpha-mediated tissue injury.
 
Rare reports of pancytopenia including aplastic anemia have occurred with TNF-alpha therapy. Reports of medically significant thrombocytopenia and leukopenia have also been received. During placebo-controlled trials, none of the patients who received golimumab had agranulocytosis, granulocytopenia, leukopenia, pancytopenia, or polycythemia. Tell patients who develop possible signs and symptoms of blood dyscrasias to seek immediate medical attention. Consider golimumab discontinuation in patients with confirmed significant hematologic abnormalities.

Post-marketing cases of hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma (HSTCL), a cancer with a very aggressive disease course that is usually fatal, have been reported in patients treated with TNF blockers. In a trial of 1-year duration, no cases of lymphoma were noted among golimumab recipients for ulcerative colitis. The incidence of malignancies other than lymphoma in golimumab-treated patients was similar to that expected in the general US population. Description
  Text
  Golimumab is a TNF-alpha blocker (TNF blocker) indicated for the treatment of moderately to severely active rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), ankylosing spondylitis (AS), polyarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis (pJIA), and moderate to severe ulcerative colitis (UC). In moderately to severely active RA or PsA, TNF blockers improve clinical signs and symptoms, inhibit radiographic progression of joint damage, and improve physical function. In AS, TNF blockers improve signs and symptoms of active disease. For RA, PsA, or AS, golimumab and other TNF-blockers may be used as monotherapy or may be used with conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs). The ideal combination of therapy for individuals with inflammatory arthritis conditions is determined by treat-to-target strategies, as well as factors such as severity of disease and comorbid conditions. Golimumab is a guideline-recommended TNF antagonist for induction and maintenance of remission in adults with moderately to severely active UC; however, the AGA categorizes it as an intermediate-efficacy option in advanced-therapy-naive patients and the ACG favors infliximab for these patients. As with other TNF blockers, golimumab carries a boxed warning for the risk of serious infections and potential malignancy. Golimumab was initially FDA-approved in 2009.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Golimumab neutralizes the biological activity of tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-alpha by binding to it and blocking its interaction with cell surface TNF receptors. Golimumab binds to both the soluble and the transmembrane bioactive forms of human TNF-alpha. Golimumab does not bind to or inactivate lymphotoxin (TNF-beta), which is a related cytokine that utilizes the same receptors as TNF-alpha. Elevated TNF-alpha concentrations in the blood, synovium, and joints have been implicated in the pathophysiology of rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and ankylosing spondylitis. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha is a naturally occurring cytokine that is involved in normal inflammatory and immune responses. Biological activities attributed to TNF-alpha include induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, G-CSF, and GM-CSF; enhancement of leukocyte migration by increasing endothelial layer permeability; expression of adhesion molecules responsible for leukocyte infiltration such as E-selectin, intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) by endothelial cells and leukocytes; activation of neutrophil and eosinophil functional activity; proliferation of fibroblasts; inhibition of osteoblast differentiation; upregulation of Fas-mediated apoptosis of osteoblasts; synthesis of prostaglandins; indirect induction of osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption; and induction of acute phase and other liver proteins. Activated macrophages release TNF-alpha, which acts on chondrocytes, fibroblasts, and osteoclasts to release metalloproteinases (MMP) and other effector molecules that induce the migration of polymorphonuclear cells.
 
Golimumab receipt to adults with rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, or psoriatic arthritis was associated with reductions in C-reactive protein, IL-6, matrix MMP-3, ICAM-1, and vascular endothelial growth factor concentrations.

Immediately before use, remove the needle cover by pulling it straight off; do not twist off or recap. Inject golimumab within 5 minutes of needle cover removal.
Hold the syringe in one hand like a pencil and use the other hand to gently pinch a fold of skin at the cleaned injection site. Do not touch the plunger or the area above finger flange prior to administration as this may cause the needle safety device to activate.
Insert the needle at a 45-degree angle to the skin. Inject golimumab by pushing the plunger all the way down until it stops. Release pressure from the plunger. The safety guard will cover the needle and lock into place, removing the needle from the skin.
Do not rub the injection site.
Properly dispose of the used prefilled syringe.
Storage: Store in a refrigerator between 2 and 8 degrees C (36 and 46 degrees F) in the original carton to protect from light.
Simponi prefilled syringe: If needed, Simponi prefilled syringe may be stored at room temperature, up to 25 degrees C (77 degrees F) for up to 30 days. Do not return to the refrigerator once stored at room temperature.
Immgolis prefilled syringe: If needed, Immgolis prefilled syringe may be stored at room temperature, up to 25 degrees C (77 degrees F) for up to 15 days. Do not return to the refrigerator once stored at room temperature.
