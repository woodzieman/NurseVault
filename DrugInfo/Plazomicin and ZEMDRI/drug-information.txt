Drug Class: Plazomicin is a parenteral aminoglycoside antibiotic indicated for complicated urinary tract infections (cUTI), including pyelonephritis, caused by certain Enterobacteriaceae in adults.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 15 mg/kg/day IV.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): nephrotoxicity, renal failure (unspecified), renal tubular acidosis (rta), renal tubular necrosis, hearing loss, ototoxicity, tinnitus, vertigo, c. difficile-associated diarrhea, pseudomembranous colitis, superinfection

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Plazomicin is also contraindicated in individuals with history of hypersensitivity to other aminoglycosides, as cross-sensitivity among aminoglycosides has been demonstrated.

  Contraindications And Precautions
    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Plazomicin is also contraindicated in individuals with history of hypersensitivity to other aminoglycosides, as cross-sensitivity among aminoglycosides has been demonstrated.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, hypovolemia, nephrotoxicity, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Systemic exposure to aminoglycosides, including plazomicin, can cause nephrotoxicity, most often presenting as acute tubular necrosis. The risk of nephrotoxicity increases with renal impairment, elevated serum concentrations, high doses or prolonged therapy, advanced age, hypovolemia, diabetes mellitus, and concomitant use of other nephrotoxic agents. Dosage adjustment is required in individuals with renal impairment or renal failure. Monitor serum plazomicin trough concentrations, renal function, serum electrolytes, urine output, and urinalysis in all individuals. Avoid concurrent or sequential administration with other nephrotoxic agents. Reduce the dose or discontinue therapy if renal impairment occurs. Aminoglycoside-induced nephrotoxicity is usually reversible but may not become clinically apparent until after therapy is completed.
    Headline
    family history of hearing loss, hearing impairment, increased risk for inherited aminoglycoside-induced hearing loss, ototoxicity, vestibular disorder
    General Information
    Ototoxicity, manifested as hearing loss, tinnitus, and/or vertigo, has been reported with aminoglycosides. Symptoms may be irreversible and may not become evident until after therapy is completed. The risk is increased in individuals with underlying hearing impairment or a family history of hearing loss (excluding age-related loss), elevated serum concentrations, high doses or prolonged/prior therapy, renal impairment, extremes of age, or concurrent use of other nephrotoxic or ototoxic agents. Cases have also been observed in individuals with variants in the mitochondrially encoded 12S rRNA gene (MT-RNR1), particularly m.1555A>G, who may develop ototoxicity even at recommended serum concentrations. These variants occur in less than 1% of the U.S. population, and the proportion of carriers who develop ototoxicity, including severe or irreversible cases, is unknown. In individuals at increased risk for inherited aminoglycoside-induced hearing loss (e.g., maternal history of aminoglycoside-related ototoxicity or a documented mitochondrial DNA variant), consider alternative treatments unless the severity of infection and lack of alternatives outweigh the risk. To minimize the risk of ototoxicity, use the shortest appropriate duration of therapy, ensure adequate hydration, avoid other ototoxic drugs, and closely monitor plazomicin concentrations. Monitor individuals with known or suspected auditory dysfunction or a vestibular disorder for signs of auditory or vestibular toxicity and consider serial audiograms in high-risk individuals. Discontinue plazomicin if ototoxicity occurs.
    Headline
    neuromuscular blockade, neuromuscular disease
    General Information
    Systemic aminoglycosides, including plazomicin, may cause neuromuscular blockade with respiratory paralysis and respiratory failure. These reactions may be more common and more severe in individuals with neuromuscular disease, including myasthenia gravis or Parkinson disease, and in those receiving concomitant neuromuscular blocking agents such as succinylcholine. If neuromuscular blockade occurs, it may be reversed with calcium salts, although mechanical respiratory support may be required.
    Headline
    obesity
    General Information
    Use adjusted body weight (ideal body weight plus 40% of excess weight) for aminoglycoside dosing in individuals with marked obesity, as distribution into adipose tissue is limited. Dosing based on actual body weight may result in supratherapeutic concentrations.
    Headline
    embryo-fetal toxicity, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk and embryo-fetal toxicity associated with aminoglycosides. There have been reports of total irreversible bilateral congenital deafness in children who were exposed to a related aminoglycoside, streptomycin, in utero. Fetal harm was not observed after administration of plazomicin during animal reproductive studies.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Use cautious dose selection for plazomicin in geriatric adults due to a higher likelihood of decreased renal function and increased risk of adverse reactions. Older adults may be particularly susceptible to aminoglycoside-associated ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use plazomicin with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of plazomicin in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Aminoglycosides are generally excreted into human milk in low concentrations. Plazomicin is present in rat milk.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Systemic aminoglycosides may cause fetal harm if administered during pregnancy. Aminoglycosides cross the placenta. There have been reports of total irreversible bilateral congenital deafness in children who were exposed to a related aminoglycoside, streptomycin, in utero. There are no available data on the use of plazomicin during human pregnancy to advise a drug-associated risk of adverse development outcomes. Fetal harm was not observed after administration of plazomicin during animal reproductive studies. Inform pregnant individuals of the potential fetal risk. Guidelines recommend plazomicin for the treatment of plague in pregnant individuals if first-line agents are not available. Physiologic changes during pregnancy, such as increased plasma volume and increased glomerular filtration rate, may decrease aminoglycoside exposure; check drug concentrations as appropriate.

Monitor serum creatinine and/or creatinine clearance in patients who are receiving deferasirox and aminoglycosides concomitantly.
Desogestrel: Ethinyl Estradiol; Ferrous Bisglycinate: (Moderate) It would be prudent to recommend alternative or additional contraception when oral contraceptives (OCs) are used in conjunction with antibiotics. It was previously thought that antibiotics may decrease the effectiveness of OCs containing estrogens due to stimulation of metabolism or a reduction in enterohepatic circulation via changes in GI flora. One retrospective study reviewed the literature to determine the effects of oral antibiotics on the pharmacokinetics of contraceptive estrogens and progestins, and also examined clinical studies in which the incidence of pregnancy with OCs and antibiotics was reported. It was concluded that the antibiotics ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin, doxycycline, metronidazole, ofloxacin, roxithromycin, temafloxacin, and tetracycline did not alter plasma concentrations of OCs. Antituberculous drugs (e.g., rifampin) were the only agents associated with OC failure and pregnancy. Based on the study results, these authors recommended that back-up contraception may not be necessary if OCs are used reliably during oral antibiotic use. Another review concurred with these data, but noted that individual patients have been identified who experienced significant decreases in plasma concentrations of combined OC components and who appeared to ovulate; the agents most often associated with these changes were rifampin, tetracyclines, and penicillin derivatives. These authors concluded that because females most at risk for OC failure or noncompliance may not be easily identified and the true incidence of such events may be under-reported, and given the serious consequence of unwanted pregnancy, that recommending an additional method of contraception during short-term antibiotic use may be justified. During long-term antibiotic administration, the risk for drug interaction with OCs is less clear, but alternative or additional contraception may be advisable in selected circumstances. Data regarding progestin-only contraceptives or for newer combined contraceptive deliveries (e.g., patches, rings) are not available.

    nephrotoxicity, renal failure (unspecified), renal tubular acidosis (RTA), renal tubular necrosis
    General Information
    Nephrotoxicity is a well-known adverse reaction to systemic aminoglycoside antibiotics and has been reported with the use of plazomicin. Decreased renal function occurred in 11% of plazomicin-treated patients vs. 4% of meropenem-treated patients in clinical trials. Of these, the incidence during IV therapy was 3.7% vs. 3% for plazomicin-treated and meropenem-treated patients, respectively. Serum creatinine increases were also observed after completion of IV therapy. The majority of plazomicin-treated patients with serum creatinine increases had fully recovered renal function by the last follow-up visit during clinical trials. Aminoglycoside antibiotics are taken up by lysosomes in cells lining the proximal tubule, which, in turn, leads to necrosis and/or fibrosis. With continued exposure, interstitial fibrosis, renal tubular necrosis, and renal tubular acidosis (RTA) occur. If aminoglycoside therapy is discontinued prior to this, renal dysfunction can be reversible. Various studies have identified risk factors for developing nephrotoxicity from aminoglycosides: excessive trough serum concentrations, other nephrotoxic agents, total dose or treatment duration, and preexisting renal disease.

Description
  Text
  Plazomicin is a parenteral aminoglycoside antibiotic indicated for complicated urinary tract infections (cUTI), including pyelonephritis, caused by certain Enterobacteriaceae in adults. Due to limited safety and efficacy data, reserve plazomicin use for patients who have limited or no alternative treatment options. Plazomicin is not inhibited by most aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes known to affect gentamicin, amikacin, and tobramycin; therefore, plazomicin may be effective for certain gram-negative infections that are resistant to other aminoglycosides. Therapeutic drug monitoring with plazomicin trough concentrations is recommended for patients with diminished renal function. The major adverse reactions associated with plazomicin are due to drug-related nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, and neuromuscular blockade.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Plazomicin is bactericidal in action. Similar to other aminoglycosides, it works by inhibiting bacterial protein synthesis through irreversible binding to the 30 S ribosomal subunit of susceptible bacteria. Aminoglycosides are actively transported into the bacterial cell where they bind to receptors present on the 30 S ribosomal subunit. This binding interferes with messenger RNA (mRNA). As a result, abnormal, nonfunctional proteins are formed due to misreading of the bacterial DNA. Eventually, susceptible bacteria die because of the lack of functional proteins. One aspect essential to aminoglycoside lethality is the need to achieve intracellular concentrations in excess of extracellular. Anaerobic bacteria are not susceptible to aminoglycosides due, at least in part, to a lack of an active transport mechanism for aminoglycoside uptake. The uptake of aminoglycosides may be facilitated by the presence of inhibitors of the bacterial cell wall (i.e. beta-lactams, vancomycin).
 
Plazomicin exhibits 'concentration-dependent killing' and a 'post-antibiotic effect' (PAE). 'Concentration-dependent killing' describes the principle that bactericidal effects increase as the concentration increases. The major pharmacodynamic parameter that determines efficacy of plazomicin is the ratio of area under the plasma concentration-time curve to the minimum inhibitory concentration (AUC:MIC) in animal and in vitro models. PAE is where suppression of bacterial growth continues after the antibiotic concentration falls below the bacterial MIC. In vitro studies demonstrated a plazomicin PAE ranging from 0.2 to 2.6 hours at 2 times the MIC against Enterobacteriaceae.
 
The mechanism of renal toxicity with aminoglycosides is associated with accumulation in the renal tubule, which is a saturable process. Elevated serum trough concentrations are associated with an increased risk of toxicity.
 
The mechanism of ototoxicity relates to the aminoglycoside-induced destruction of sensory hair cells of the inner ear. The cochlear sensory cells that are most vulnerable are in the basal end, thereby leading to high-frequency hearing loss first. As ototoxicity ascends toward the apex of the cochlea, the lower frequencies are affected. Sensory cells that deal with vestibular function may also be affected. Aminoglycosides may cause free-radical damage to sensory cells and neurons. Biochemically, aminoglycosides may bind to polyphosphoinositides, which are part of the transmembrane signaling system mediating physiological effects of hormones, neurotransmitters, and neuromodulators which may interfere with essential mechanisms of cell physiology. Neural destruction without any cochlear hair cell damage has also been described. There may also be a genetic mitochondrial RNA mutation that may predispose some patients to aminoglycoside ototoxicity. Aminoglycosides enter the inner ear rapidly, but it is suggested that concentrations do not correlate with the development of ototoxicity. Likely, aminoglycoside concentrations in the inner ear dissipate slowly, which is consistent with the possibility of developing ototoxicity days to weeks after drug discontinuation.
 
The interpretive breakpoint criteria define MICs of 2 mcg/mL or less as susceptible, 4 mcg/mL as intermediate, and 8 mcg/mL or more as resistant for plazomicin against Enterobacterales based on a dosage of 15 mg/kg every 24 hours. Breakpoints do not apply to the family Morganellaceae, which includes Morganella, Proteus, and Providencia. Plazomicin has no in vitro activity against streptococci, enterococci, anaerobes, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, and Acinetobacter sp. It has variable activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
 
Aminoglycoside resistance is well documented. There are a variety of resistance mechanisms employed by different pathogens. Enzymatic inhibition via aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (AMEs) is achieved by modification of the aminoglycoside as it is transported across the cytoplasmic membrane. Plazomicin is not inhibited by most AMEs known to affect gentamicin, amikacin, and tobramycin, including acetyltransferases (AACs), phosphotransferases (APHs), and nucleotidyltransferases (ANTs). Other mechanisms include alteration of the ribosomal target sites through production of 16S rRNA methyltransferases, up-regulation of efflux pumps, and reduced permeability into the bacterial cell due to loss of outer membrane porins. Plazomicin, like other aminoglycosides, is inactive against bacterial isolates that produce 16S rRNA methyltransferases and may have reduced activity against Enterobacteriaceae that overexpress certain efflux pumps (e.g., acrAB-tolC) or lower expression of porins (e.g., ompF or ompK36). Plazomicin demonstrates in vitro activity in the presence of certain beta-lactamases, including extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (TEM, SHV, CTX-M, AmpC), serine carbapenemases (KPC-2, KPC-3), and oxacillinase (OXA-48). Bacteria producing metallo-beta-lactamases often co-express 16S rRNA methyltransferase, conferring resistance to plazomicin.
 
No antagonism was observed during in vitro studies of plazomicin in combination with clindamycin, colistin, daptomycin, fosfomycin, levofloxacin, linezolid, rifampin, tigecycline, and vancomycin. Few isolates showed synergy with ceftazidime, meropenem, and piperacillin; tazobactam, but the clinical significance of these findings is unknown.

  Plazomicin is administered intravenously. The average binding of plazomicin to human plasma proteins is approximately 20% and is concentration-independent. The mean volume of distribution is 17.9 L (+/- 4.8 L) in healthy adults and 30.8 L (+/- 12.1 L) in patients with complicated urinary tract infections.
 
Plazomicin does not appear to be metabolized to any appreciable extent and is primarily eliminated by the kidneys. After a single radiolabeled plazomicin 15 mg/kg IV dose, 97.5% of the dose was recovered in the urine as unchanged plazomicin; 56% of the total administered radioactivity was recovered in urine within 4 hours with less than 0.2% in feces. The mean renal clearance of plazomicin is similar to total body clearance.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: MATE1, MATE2-K
Plazomicin selectively inhibits the MATE1 and MATE2-K renal transporters in vitro. However, a clinical drug-drug interaction study that evaluated the effect of a single plazomicin 15 mg/kg dose on the single-dose pharmacokinetics of a MATE substrate did not demonstrate any affect of plazomicin on the pharmacokinetics of the MATE substrate.
