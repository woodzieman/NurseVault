    Headline
    undiagnosed HIV-1 infection
    General Information
    Due to the potential for developing drug resistance, the use of lenacapavir for pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is contraindicated in individuals with an undiagnosed HIV-1 infection. Drug-resistant HIV-1 variants have been identified with the use of lenacapavir by individuals with undiagnosed HIV-1 infection. Individuals must be tested for HIV-1 infection prior to initiating lenacapavir, with each subsequent injection, and as clinically appropriate (e.g., upon diagnosis of other sexually transmitted disease or clinical symptoms of acute HIV-1 infection), using a test approved or cleared by the FDA for the diagnosis of acute or primary HIV-1 infection. When screening for HIV-1 infection before starting lenacapavir, if an antigen/antibody-specific test is used and provides negative results, then such negative results should be confirmed using an RNA-specific assay, even if the results of the RNA-assay are available after administration of lenacapavir. When screening for HIV-1 infection prior to continuing lenacapavir, negative results from a rapid, point-of-care antigen/antibody test should be confirmed using a more sensitive assay. Do not initiate lenacapavir for HIV-1 PrEP unless negative infection status is confirmed. Individuals who acquired HIV-1 while receiving lenacapavir for PrEP must transition to a complete HIV-1 treatment regimen. Lenacapavir is not always effective in preventing HIV-1 acquisition. The time from initiation of lenacapavir for HIV-1 PrEP to maximal protection against HIV-1 infection is unknown. Counsel individuals on the use of other prevention measures (e.g., consistent and correct condom use; knowledge of partner(s)' HIV-1 status, including viral suppression status; regular testing for STIs that can facilitate HIV-1 transmission). Inform individuals about and support their efforts in reducing sexual risk behavior. Counsel HIV-1 uninfected individuals to strictly adhere to the recommended dosing schedule, on the use of other measures to reduce risk of STIs, and on the importance of routine testing. Some individuals, such as adolescents, may benefit from frequent visits and counseling to support adherence to the dosing and testing schedule. In addition to HIV screening, all recipients of HIV PrEP should undergo testing for a STI before initiating therapy and approximately every 4 to 12 months thereafter, depending on specific population and/or STI.

    Headline
    General Information
    General Information
    During baseline evaluation of people with HIV, discuss risk reduction measures and the need for status disclosure to sexual or needle-sharing partners, especially with untreated patients who are still at high risk of HIV transmission. Include the importance of adherence to therapy to achieve and maintain a plasma HIV RNA less than 200 copies/mL. Maintaining a plasma HIV RNA less than 200 copies/mL, including any measurable value below this threshold, with antiretroviral therapy prevents sexual transmission of HIV to their partners. Patients may recognize this concept as Undetectable = Untransmittable or U=U. Instruct patients to achieve sustained viral suppression (i.e., 2 recorded measurements of plasma viral loads that are below the limits of detection and taken at least 3 months apart) before attempting to conceive a child in order to maximize their health, prevent HIV sexual transmission, and minimize the risk of HIV transmission to the infant once conception occurs. For partners with different HIV status when the person with HIV is on antiretroviral therapy and has achieved sustained viral suppression, sexual intercourse without a condom allows conception without sexual HIV transmission to the person without HIV; expert consultation is recommended. If the partner with HIV has not been able to achieve viral suppression, has an unknown viral suppression status, or has the potential for inconsistent adherence during the periconception period, the use of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) by the partner without HIV is an option to reduce the risk of sexual HIV acquisition.
 
Testing for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection resistance is recommended in all antiretroviral treatment-naive patients at the time of HIV diagnosis, regardless of whether treatment will be initiated. Additionally, perform resistance testing prior to initiating or changing any HIV treatment regimen. Transmission of drug-resistant HIV strains has been both well documented and associated with suboptimal virologic response to initial antiretroviral therapy. The prevalence of transmitted drug resistance (TDR) in high-income countries ranges from 9% to 14% and varies by country. In most TDR surveys, non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI) resistance and nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) resistance are the most common mutation class types detected, followed by protease inhibitor (PI) and integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI) resistance mutations, respectively. Resistance testing at baseline can help optimize treatment and, thus, virologic response. In the absence of therapy, resistant viruses may decline over time to less than the detection limit of standard resistance tests, but may still increase the risk of treatment failure when therapy is eventually initiated. Thus, if therapy is deferred, resistance testing should still be performed during acute HIV infection with the genotypic resistance test result kept in the patient's medical record until it becomes clinically useful. Additionally, because of the possibility of acquisition of another drug-resistant virus before treatment initiation, repeat resistance testing at the time therapy is initiated would be prudent.
 
Perform hepatitis A virus (HAV) and hepatitis B virus (HBV) screening in any patient who presents with HIV infection to assure appropriate treatment. If the HBV screening is positive, additional testing for hepatitis D virus (HDV) is recommended. Patients with HIV and HBV/HDV coinfection should be referred to an expert in the management of viral hepatitis. Patients with hepatitis B and HIV coinfection should be started on a fully suppressive antiretroviral (ARV) regimen with activity against both viruses (regardless of CD4 counts and HBV DNA concentrations). HIV treatment guidelines recommend these patients receive an ARV regimen that contains a dual NRTI backbone of tenofovir alafenamide or tenofovir disoproxil fumarate with either emtricitabine or lamivudine. Both tenofovir products are active against HBV and can be used as HBV monotherapy. Emtricitabine and lamivudine are also active against HBV; however, they are not recommended for use as monotherapy due to the potential for HBV resistance. Therefore, if tenofovir cannot be used, entecavir should be given in combination with a fully suppressive ARV regimen (NOTE: entecavir should not be considered part of the ARV regimen). Avoid HBV treatment regimens that include adefovir or peginterferon alfa, as these regimens are associated with a higher incidence of toxicities and increased rates of HBV treatment failure. In rare cases, peginterferon alfa can remain a treatment option after consultation with an expert. Most patients with coinfection should continue treatment indefinitely with the goal of maximal HIV suppression and prevention of HBV relapse. If treatment must be discontinued, monitor transaminase concentrations every 6 weeks for the first 3 months, and every 3 to 6 months thereafter. Instruct patients with hepatitis and HIV coinfection to avoid consuming alcohol, and offer vaccinations against hepatitis A and hepatitis B as appropriate.
 
HIV treatment guidelines recommend all patients presenting with HIV infection undergo routine screening for hepatitis C virus (HCV). For HCV seronegative individuals who are at continued high risk of acquiring hepatitis C, specifically men who have sex with men (MSM) or persons who inject drugs, additional HCV screening is recommended annually or as indicated by clinical presentation (e.g., unexplained ALT elevation), risk activities, or exposure. Similarly, the AASLD/IDSA HCV guidelines and the CDC preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP) guidelines recommend HCV serologic testing at baseline and every 12 months for MSM, transgender women, and persons who inject drugs. Use an FDA-approved immunoassay licensed for detection of HCV antibodies (anti-HCV); in settings where acute HCV infection is suspected or in persons with known prior infection that cleared spontaneously or after treatment, use of nucleic acid testing for HCV RNA is recommended. If hepatitis C and HIV coinfection is identified, consider treating both viral infections concurrently. It is recommended to use a fully suppressive antiretroviral therapy and an HCV regimen in all patients with coinfection regardless of CD4 count, as lower CD4 counts do not appear to compromise the efficacy of HCV treatment. In most patients, a simplified pangenotypic HCV regimen (i.e., glecaprevir; pibrentasvir or sofosbuvir; velpatasvir) may be an appropriate choice; however, these regimens are NOT recommended for use in persons with HCV and HIV coinfection who: are treatment-experienced with HCV relapse (reinfection after successful therapy is not an exclusion); have decompensated cirrhosis; on a tenofovir disoproxil fumarate containing regimen with eGFR less than 60 mL/minute; on efavirenz, etravirine, nevirapine, or boosted protease inhibitor; have untreated chronic hepatitis B; are pregnant. Patients with HCV and HIV coinfection who meet these exclusion criteria should be treated for HCV following standard approaches as described in the AASLD/IDSA HCV guidelines. Treatment of HCV infection in children younger than 3 years is not usually recommended; however, treatment should be considered for all children 3 years and older with HCV and HIV coinfection who have no contraindications to treatment. Instruct patients with coinfection to avoid consuming alcohol, limit ingestion of potentially hepatotoxic medications, avoid iron supplementation in the absence of documented iron deficiency, and receive vaccinations against hepatitis A and hepatitis B as appropriate.
 
Patients with HIV who must undergo a solid organ transplant or allogeneic stem cell transplant should be managed by a multidisciplinary team before, during, and after the transplant to ensure comprehensive care. Prior to the transplant, ensure viral suppression is obtained, and the patient is up-to-date on their vaccinations. Review the patient's antiretroviral history, prior drug resistance results, adherence history, and the potential for drug interactions. Bictegravir- or dolutegravir-based regimens are preferred in most patients needing a transplant. Consider using tenofovir alafenamide over tenofovir disoproxil fumarate due to the lower risk of affecting renal function and bone mineral density. In general, avoid use of potent CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., ritonavir, cobicistat, protease inhibitors) or CYP3A4 inducing non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (efavirenz, nevirapine, etravirine). If a change in the antiretroviral regimen is necessary, the switch should be completed several weeks before the transplant whenever possible. Renal and hepatic function may fluctuate immediately post-transplant; thus, close monitoring is recommended to ensure appropriate dosing and to avoid potential drug toxicities. Additionally, therapeutic drug monitoring of the immunosuppressive medications should be performed to guide dose adjustments. Make every effort to avoid post-transplant interruption of antiretroviral therapy. If an interruption is necessary, stop all components of the regimen simultaneously and restart treatment as soon as possible.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Lenacapavir is recommended as a long-acting pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) option during pregnancy for individuals at risk of HIV acquisition. For HIV treatment during pregnancy, lenacapavir is not recommended except in special circumstances involving substantial prior ART experience when needed to achieve or maintain viral suppression. Data on lenacapavir use for HIV treatment during pregnancy, including pharmacokinetics, safety, and efficacy, remain limited. Available data from a randomized, controlled trial in individuals without HIV-1 have not identified a drug-associated risk of miscarriage or adverse pregnancy outcomes compared with an active control. Two major birth defects, both ventricular septal defects, occurred among 208 lenacapavir-exposed pregnancies; the observed rate did not exceed the background prevalence. However, risk estimates remain imprecise because of the limited number of exposed pregnancies. No adverse developmental effects were observed in animal studies at lenacapavir exposures up to 16 times the human exposure at the recommended human dose. In humans, no clinically relevant changes in lenacapavir exposure occurred during pregnancy or postpartum. A pregnancy exposure registry monitors outcomes in individuals exposed to lenacapavir during pregnancy; health care providers are encouraged to register individuals with the Antiretroviral Pregnancy Registry at 1-800-258-4263.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use lenacapavir with caution during breast-feeding. Lenacapavir is present in human milk; however, infant exposure appears to be low, and no adverse effects have been observed. In a small study of 8 lactating individuals receiving injectable lenacapavir, the median milk-to-plasma ratio was 0.63 (range, 0.29 to 1.9). Among 10 breastfed infants exposed to lenacapavir from birth to less than 13 weeks postpartum, the median infant-to-lactating person plasma ratio was 0.06 (range, 0.01 to 0.2). It is not known whether lenacapavir affects milk production. For lactating individuals without HIV who are at risk of HIV acquisition, guidelines recommend injectable lenacapavir for pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). Counseling should address the benefits of PrEP in reducing HIV acquisition and perinatal or postnatal HIV transmission, as well as the potential risks of therapy.

Interactions
Abacavir; Dolutegravir; lamiVUDine: (Moderate) Monitor for increased toxicity of dolutegravir if coadministered with lenacapavir. Concurrent use may increase the plasma concentrations of dolutegravir. Dolutegravir is a CYP3A, P-gp, and BCRP substrate and lenacapavir is a moderate CYP3A, P-gp, and BCRP inhibitor.
Abemaciclib: (Moderate) Monitor for an increase in abemaciclib-related adverse reactions if coadministration with lenacapavir is necessary; consider reducing the dose of abemaciclib in 50-mg decrements if toxicities occur. Discontinue abemaciclib for patients unable to tolerate 50 mg twice daily. Abemaciclib is a CYP3A substrate and lenacapavir is a moderate CYP3A inhibitor. Coadministration with other moderate CYP3A inhibitors is predicted to increase the relative potency adjusted unbound AUC of abemaciclib plus its active metabolites (M2, M18, and M20) by approximately 1.6- to 2.4-fold.
Acalabrutinib: (Major) Decrease the acalabrutinib dose to 100 mg PO once daily if coadministered with lenacapavir. Coadministration may result in increased acalabrutinib exposure and toxicity (e.g., infection, bleeding, and atrial arrhythmias). Acalabrutinib is a CYP3A substrate; lenacapavir is a moderate CYP3A inhibitor. In physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) simulations, the AUC of acalabrutinib was increased by approximately 2- to 3-fold when acalabrutinib was coadministered with moderate CYP3A inhibitors.
Acetaminophen; Caffeine; Dihydrocodeine: (Moderate) Consider a reduced dose of dihydrocodeine with frequent monitoring for respiratory depression and sedation if concurrent use of lenacapavir is necessary. If lenacapavir is discontinued, consider increasing the dihydrocodeine dose until stable drug effects are achieved and monitor for evidence of opioid withdrawal. Concomitant use of dihydrocodeine with lenacapavir may increase dihydrocodeine plasma concentrations, resulting in greater metabolism by CYP2D6, increased dihydromorphine concentrations, and prolonged opioid adverse reactions, including hypotension, respiratory depression, profound sedation, coma, and death. If lenacapavir is discontinued, monitor the patient carefully and consider increasing the opioid dosage if appropriate. Lenacapavir is a moderate inhibitor of CYP3A, an isoenzyme partially responsible for the metabolism of dihydrocodeine.
Acetaminophen; Codeine: (Moderate) Concomitant use of codeine with lenacapavir may increase codeine plasma concentrations, resulting in greater metabolism by CYP2D6, increased morphine concentrations, and prolonged opioid adverse reactions, including hypotension, respiratory depression, profound sedation, coma, and death. It is recommended to avoid this combination when codeine is being used for cough. If coadministration is necessary, monitor patients closely at frequent intervals and consider a dosage reduction of codeine until stable drug effects are achieved. Discontinuation of lenacapavir could decrease codeine plasma concentrations, decrease opioid efficacy, and potentially lead to a withdrawal syndrome in those with physical dependence to codeine. If lenacapavir is discontinued, monitor the patient carefully and consider increasing the opioid dosage if appropriate. Codeine is primarily metabolized by CYP2D6 to morphine, and by CYP3A to norcodeine; norcodeine does not have analgesic properties. Lenacapavir is a moderate inhibitor of CYP3A.
Acetaminophen; HYDROcodone: (Moderate) Consider a reduced dose of hydrocodone with frequent monitoring for respiratory depression and sedation if concurrent use of lenacapavir is necessary. It is recommended to avoid this combination when hydrocodone is being used for cough. Hydrocodone is a CYP3A substrate, and coadministration with CYP3A inhibitors like lenacapavir can increase hydrocodone exposure resulting in increased or prolonged opioid effects including fatal respiratory depression, particularly when an inhibitor is added to a stable dose of hydrocodone. These effects could be more pronounced in patients also receiving a CYP2D6 inhibitor. If lenacapavir is discontinued, hydrocodone plasma concentrations will decrease resulting in reduced efficacy of the opioid and potential withdrawal syndrome in a patient who has developed physical dependence to hydrocodone.
Acetaminophen; oxyCODONE: (Moderate) Consider a reduced dose of oxycodone with frequent monitoring for respiratory depression and sedation if concurrent use of lenacapavir is necessary. If lenacapavir is discontinued, consider increasing the oxycodone dose until stable drug effects are achieved and monitor for evidence of opioid withdrawal. Oxycodone is a CYP3A substrate, and coadministration with moderate CYP3A inhibitors like lenacapavir can increase oxycodone exposure resulting in increased or prolonged opioid effects including fatal respiratory depression, particularly when an inhibitor is added to a stable dose of oxycodone. If lenacapavir is discontinued, oxycodone plasma concentrations will decrease resulting in reduced efficacy of the opioid and potential withdrawal syndrome in a patient who has developed physical dependence to oxycodone.
Afatinib: (Moderate) If the concomitant use of lenacapavir and afatinib is necessary, monitor for afatinib-related adverse reactions. If the original dose of afatinib is not tolerated, consider reducing the daily dose of afatinib by 10 mg; resume the previous dose of afatinib as tolerated after discontinuation of lenacapavir. The manufacturer of afatinib recommends permanent discontinuation of therapy for severe or intolerant adverse drug reactions at a dose of 20 mg per day, but does not address a minimum dose otherwise. Afatinib is a P-gp substrate and lenacapavir is a P-gp inhibitor. Administration with another P-gp inhibitor, given 1 hour before a single dose of afatinib, increased afatinib exposure by 48%; there was no change in afatinib exposure when the P-gp inhibitor was administered at the same time as afatinib or 6 hours later. In healthy subjects, the relative bioavailability for AUC of afatinib was 119% when coadministered with the same P-gp inhibitor, and 111% when the inhibitor was administered 6 hours after afatinib.
Albuterol; Budesonide: (Moderate) Avoid coadministration of oral budesonide with lenacapavir due to increased budesonide exposure; use caution with inhaled budesonide, as systemic exposure may increase. Budesonide is a CYP3A substrate and lenacapavir is a moderate CYP3A inhibitor.
ALFentanil: (Moderate) Consider a reduced dose of alfentanil with frequent monitoring for respiratory depression and sedation if concurrent use of lenacapavir is necessary. If lenacapavir is discontinued, consider increasing the alfentanil dose until stable drug effects are achieved and monitor for evidence of opioid withdrawal. Alfentanil is a sensitive CYP3A substrate, and coadministration with CYP3A inhibitors like lenacapavir can increase alfentanil exposure resulting in increased or prolonged opioid effects including fatal respiratory depression, particularly when an inhibitor is added to a stable dose of alfentanil. If lenacapavir is discontinued, alfentanil plasma concentrations will decrease resulting in reduced efficacy of the opioid and potential withdrawal syndrome in a patient who has developed physical dependence to alfentanil.
Alfuzosin: (Moderate) Monitor for evidence of alfuzosin-related adverse effects including hypotension and QT prolongation if coadministered with lenacapavir. Increased alfuzosin exposure may occur. Alfuzosin is a CYP3A substrate; lenacapavir is a moderate CYP3A inhibitor. Coadministration of another moderate CYP3A inhibitor increased the alfuzosin AUC by 1.3-fold.
Alpelisib: (Major) Avoid coadministration of alpelisib with lenacapavir due to increased exposure to alpelisib and the risk of alpelisib-related toxicity. If concomitant use is unavoidable, closely monitor for alpelisib-related adverse reactions. Alpelisib is a BCRP substrate and lenacapavir is a BCRP inhibitor.
ALPRAZolam: (Major) Avoid coadministration of alprazolam and lenacapavir due to the potential for elevated alprazolam concentrations, which may cause prolonged sedation and respiratory depression. If coadministration is necessary, consider reducing the dose of alprazolam as clinically appropriate and monitor for an increase in alprazolam-related adverse reactions. Lorazepam, oxazepam, or temazepam may be safer alternatives if a benzodiazepine must be administered in combination with lenacapavir, as these benzodiazepines are not oxidatively metabolized. Alprazolam is a CYP3A substrate and lenacapavir is a moderate CYP3A inhibitor. Coadministration with other moderate CYP3A inhibitors increased alprazolam exposure by 1.6- to 1.98-fold.
Amiodarone: (Moderate) Monitor for an increase in amiodarone-related adverse effects if concomitant use with lenacapavir is necessary. Concomitant use may increase amiodarone exposure. Amiodarone is a CYP3A substrate and lenacapavir is a moderate CYP3A inhibitor.
amLODIPine: (Moderate) Monitor for symptoms of hypotension and edema if coadministration of amlodipine with lenacapavir is necessary; adjust the dose of amlodipine as clinically appropriate. Amlodipine is a CYP3A substrate; lenacapavir is a moderate CYP3A inhibitor. Coadministration with a moderate CYP3A inhibitor in elderly hypertensive patients increased systemic exposure to amlodipine by 60%. However, coadministration with another moderate CYP3A inhibitor in healthy volunteers did not significantly change amlodipine exposure.
amLODIPine; Atorvastatin: (Moderate) Monitor for an increase in atorvastatin-related adverse reactions, including myopathy and rhabdomyolysis, if coadministration with lenacapavir is necessary. Concomitant use may increase atorvastatin exposure. Atorvastatin is a P-gp and BCRP substrate; lenacapavir is a P-gp and BCRP inhibitor. Amobarbital: (Major) Concurrent use of lenacapavir for HIV treatment and barbiturates is contraindicated due to the risk for reduced lenacapavir exposure, which may lead to loss of therapeutic efficacy and development of resistance. If lenacapavir is used for HIV PrEP and use with barbiturates is necessary, supplemental lenacapavir doses are required to maintain effective drug concentrations. On the day barbiturates are initiated, which should be at least 2 days after lenacapavir initiation, a supplemental dose of 927 mg subcutaneously and 600 mg orally should be given (Step 1). The following day, an additional oral dose of 600 mg should be administered (Step 2). If the barbiturate is continued for longer than 6 months, this 2-step supplemental dosing regimen must be repeated every 6 months. After discontinuation of barbiturates, the standard 6-month injection schedule for lenacapavir should be resumed. Recommendations are not available for those already on barbiturates at the time of lenacapavir initiation or those receiving weekly oral lenacapavir. Lenacapavir is a CYP3A substrate and barbiturates are strong CYP3A inducers. Concomitant use with another strong CYP3A inducer reduced lenacapavir overall exposure by 84%.

When initiating cariprazine in adults receiving lenacapavir, reduce the cariprazine starting dose to 0.75 mg PO once daily; in adults with schizophrenia or bipolar mania, the cariprazine dose may be increased to 1.5 mg PO once daily, if needed, based on clinical response. Chlorpheniramine; Codeine: (Moderate) Concomitant use of codeine with lenacapavir may increase codeine plasma concentrations, resulting in greater metabolism by CYP2D6, increased morphine concentrations, and prolonged opioid adverse reactions, including hypotension, respiratory depression, profound sedation, coma, and death. It is recommended to avoid this combination when codeine is being used for cough. If coadministration is necessary, monitor patients closely at frequent intervals and consider a dosage reduction of codeine until stable drug effects are achieved. Discontinuation of lenacapavir could decrease codeine plasma concentrations, decrease opioid efficacy, and potentially lead to a withdrawal syndrome in those with physical dependence to codeine. If lenacapavir is discontinued, monitor the patient carefully and consider increasing the opioid dosage if appropriate. Codeine is primarily metabolized by CYP2D6 to morphine, and by CYP3A to norcodeine; norcodeine does not have analgesic properties. Lenacapavir is a moderate inhibitor of CYP3A.

    General Information
    An injection site reaction (ISR) was the most frequently reported adverse event with subcutaneous lenacapavir, occurring in 65% to 83% of participants in clinical trials. Nodules and indurations had longer resolution times than other ISRs; for ISRs excluding these, the median duration was 4 to 9 days (range: 2 to 30 days). In HIV treatment trials, nodules resolved in a median of 148 days (range: 41 to 727 days) and indurations in 70 days (range: 3 to 252 days); in HIV PrEP trials, median resolution times were 297 to 350 days (range: 176 to 470 days) and 151 to 173 days (range: 15 to 267 days), respectively. After 553 days of follow-up in HIV treatment trials, 30% of nodules and 13% of indurations (in 10% and 1% of participants, respectively) remained unresolved. Postmarketing reports have linked improper intradermal injections to serious ISRs, including skin necrosis and ulcers.

Description
  Text
  Lenacapavir is a first-in-class, long-acting HIV-1 capsid inhibitor. The brand name Sunlenca is indicated in combination with other antiretroviral agents for the treatment of HIV-1 infection in heavily treatment-experienced individuals with multidrug-resistant HIV-1 whose current antiretroviral regimen is failing because of resistance, intolerance, or safety considerations. Sunlenca tablets are also indicated for oral loading as part of the Bixlenvo (bictegravir; lenacapavir) initiation regimen in individuals with HIV-1 infection who are virologically suppressed on a stable antiretroviral regimen and have no known or suspected resistance to bictegravir or lenacapavir. The brand name Yeztugo is indicated for HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) to reduce the risk of sexually acquired HIV-1 in individuals who are at risk of HIV acquisition. Before and during use of Yeztugo for PrEP, all potential drug recipients must have a negative HIV result using a test approved or cleared by the FDA for diagnosis of acute or primary HIV-1 infection. Once therapy initiation is completed, lenacapavir is administered subcutaneously once every 6 months in regimens using subcutaneous maintenance dosing, providing convenient twice-yearly dosing for individuals. Lenacapavir was FDA-approved in 2022.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Lenacapavir is a multistage, selective inhibitor of HIV-1 capsid function that directly binds to the interface between capsid protein (p24) subunits in hexamers. Surface plasmon resonance sensorgrams showed dose-dependent and saturable binding of lenacapavir to cross-linked wild-type capsid hexamer with an equilibrium binding constant (KD) of 1.4 nM. Lenacapavir inhibits HIV-1 replication by interfering with multiple essential steps of the viral lifecycle, including capsid-mediated nuclear uptake of HIV-1 proviral DNA (by blocking nuclear import proteins binding to capsid), virus assembly and release (by interfering with Gag/Gag-Pol functioning, reducing production of capsid protein subunits), and capsid core formation (by disrupting the rate of capsid subunit association, leading to malformed capsids). Lenacapavir has antiviral activity against HIV-1 and HIV-2; however, the drug is 15- to 25-fold less active against HIV-2 isolates relative to HIV-1.
 
Resistance (Cell Culture)
In cell culture studies, HIV-1 variants with reduced susceptibility to lenacapavir have been selected. Resistance selections with lenacapavir identified 7 substitutions in capsid that conferred reduced phenotypic susceptibility relative to wild-type virus: L56I, M66I, Q67H, K70N, N74D/S, and T107N. The M66I substitution alone or in combination conferred more than 3,226-fold decreased susceptibility to lenacapavir in a single-cycle infectivity assay; substitutions Q67H and T107N conferred 4- to 6.3-fold decreased susceptibility; K70N, N74D, and Q67H/N74S conferred 22- to 32-fold decreased susceptibility; and L56I conferred 239-fold decreased susceptibility.
 
Resistance (HIV Treatment Clinical Trials)
In a clinical trial of heavily treatment-experienced individuals, lenacapavir resistance-associated capsid substitutions were identified in 57% (16 of 28) of those with confirmed virologic failure who had post-baseline genotypic resistance data. Emergent substitutions included M66I in combination with other substitutions (Q67H/K/N, K70N/R/S, N74D, A105T, T107A/C/S), Q67H+K70R with or without A105T and/or T107N/S, T107A or T107S alone, Q67K+K70H, K70N+N74K+T107T/N, Q67H alone, and N74D alone. Phenotypic analyses demonstrated approximately 4.5-fold to greater than 1,428-fold decreases in lenacapavir susceptibility compared with wild-type virus. Among the 16 individuals with virologic failure who developed capsid resistance-associated substitutions, 6 received lenacapavir in combination with a background regimen containing no fully active antiretrovirals, consistent with functional monotherapy; therefore, careful consideration should be given to including fully active agents in the treatment regimen. Additionally, 9 individuals with virologic failure had emergent resistance substitutions to components of the optimized background regimen, including non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI) substitutions (K103N/S, V106M), nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) substitutions (L74I, M184I/V, K65K/R, and K65R and S68N from mixtures at baseline), and protease inhibitor (PI) substitutions (M46I, I47I/V, I54I/L, V82V/A, I84I/V, L90L/M). Lenacapavir resistance has also been observed in the setting of suboptimal adherence to concomitant antiretroviral therapy; in some individuals, viral resuppression (HIV-1 RNA less than 50 copies/mL) occurred despite the presence of resistance-associated substitutions, although the contribution of lenacapavir to resuppression remains unclear.
 
Resistance (HIV PrEP Clinical Trials)
In the lenacapavir arms of the PURPOSE 1 and 2 trials, there were a total of 5 incident and 8 prevalent HIV-1 infections. In PURPOSE 1, 2 incident infections occurred after the primary analysis, 1 after lenacapavir discontinuation with no resistance-associated mutations detected, and the other with viral loads too low for genotyping. Among the 4 prevalent infections, 3 had resistance-associated capsid substitutions (2 with N74D and 1 with T107A). In PURPOSE 2, 3 incident infections were observed, all with detectable resistance mutations (2 with N74D and 1 with Q67H/K70R), and among 4 prevalent infections, 2 of 3 genotyped cases had N74D substitutions.
 
Cross-resistance
Data from cell culture studies have not identified any overlapping resistance between lenacapavir and other antiretroviral agents, including PI, NNRTI, NRTI, integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTI), fostemsavir, ibalizumab, maraviroc, or enfuvirtide.

  Text
  Lenacapavir is administered orally and subcutaneously. The steady state Vd of lenacapavir is 1,657 L. It is more than 98.5% bound to human plasma proteins and has a blood-to-plasma ratio of 0.5 to 0.7. The drug is primarily metabolized by CYP3A and uridine diphosphate (UDP)-glucuronosyl transferase (UGT1A1). Approximately 69% of the dose is eliminated as unchanged drug in plasma. Excretion occurs primarily through the feces (76% of the dose); less than 1% of the dose is excreted in the urine. The clearance of lenacapavir is 3.4 L/hour.
HIV Treatment
Lenacapavir AUC at the end of month 6 after the recommended dosing regimen option 1 (oral load + subcutaneous injection on day 1 and oral load on day 2) is predicted to be similar to that reported after the recommended dosing regimen option 2 (oral load on days 1, 2, and 8 and subcutaneous injection on day 15) from the CAPELLA study (234,294 hour x ng/mL vs. 251,907 hour x ng/mL).
HIV PrEP
After oral and subcutaneous administration of lenacapavir in adults, pharmacokinetic assessments showed that from Day 1 to Month 6, the mean Cmax was 73.8 ng/mL (CV 48.6%), AUC was 188,108 hour x ng/mL (CV 41.0%), and Ctrough was 27 ng/mL (CV 51.1%). At steady state, these values increased to a mean Cmax of 82.4 ng/mL (CV 40.4%), AUC of 257,334 hour x ng/mL (CV 38.7%), and Ctrough of 36.9 ng/mL (CV 53.5%).
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4, P-gp, UGT1A1
Lenacapavir is a substrate of CYP3A, P-gp, and UGT1A1. Drugs that are strong or moderate inducers of CYP3A may significantly decrease plasma concentrations of lenacapavir. Combined P-gp, UGT1A1, and strong CYP3A inhibitors may significantly increase plasma concentrations of lenacapavir. Lenacapavir is also a moderate CYP3A inhibitor. Due to the long half-life of lenacapavir after subcutaneous administration, lenacapavir may increase the exposure of drugs primarily metabolized by CYP3A initiated within 9 months after the last subcutaneous dose of lenacapavir.

HIV treatment
NOTE: Counsel individuals about the importance of medication adherence and adhering to the scheduled dosing visits. Missing doses or non-adherence to injections could result in loss of virologic response and development of resistance.
Individuals must adhere to the required 6 month dosing schedule. Instruct individuals to immediately contact their health care provider if they stop taking lenacapavir or any other medication in their antiviral regimen.
Missed injections:
Planned missed injections: If a scheduled injection is expected to be delayed by more than 2 weeks, oral lenacapavir may be taken on an interim basis for up to 6 months until injections resume. Administer the maintenance injection within 7 days after the last oral dose.
Unplanned missed injections: Clinically reassess individuals who miss a scheduled injection, including consideration of lenacapavir resistance testing, to ensure resumption of therapy remains appropriate. If more than 28 weeks have elapsed since the last injection and oral lenacapavir was not taken, restart the initiation regimen from Day 1 using 1 of the recommended options.
If lenacapavir is stopped and will not be restarted, it is essential to initiate an alternative, fully suppressive antiretroviral regimen where possible no later than 28 weeks after the final lenacapavir injection.
Planned long-term treatment interruptions are not recommended due to the potential for HIV disease progression (i.e., declining CD4 counts, viral rebound, acute viral syndrome), development of minor HIV-associated manifestations or serious non-AIDS complications, development of drug resistance, increased risk of HIV transmission, and increased risk for opportunistic infections.
If therapy must be discontinued, counsel individuals on the potential risks and closely monitor for any clinical or laboratory abnormalities.
 
HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)
NOTE: Counsel individuals about the importance of adhering to the scheduled dosing visits.
Adherence to the every-6-month injection dosing schedule is strongly recommended. Use of the drug is recommended only for those individuals who agree to the required injection dosing schedule.
Missed injections:
Planned missed injections: If a scheduled injection is expected to be delayed by more than 2 weeks, oral lenacapavir may be taken on an interim basis for up to 6 months until injections resume. Administer the maintenance injection within 7 days after the last oral dose.
Unplanned missed injections: Individuals who miss a scheduled injection visit should be clinically reassessed to determine whether resuming therapy remains appropriate and whether they remain HIV-1 negative. If more than 28 weeks have elapsed since the last injection and oral lenacapavir was not taken, restart the initiation regimen from Day 1.
Residual concentrations of lenacapavir may remain in the systemic circulation of individuals for prolonged periods (up to 12 months or longer). Health care providers should take the prolonged-release characteristics of lenacapavir into consideration when the drug is prescribed.
After discontinuation of lenacapavir for those individuals with HIV-1 negative status who are at continued risk of HIV-1 acquisition, consider alternate forms of PrEP initiated within 28 weeks of the last lenacapavir injection.

      Preparation
The injection kit components are for single use only. Two vials (two 1.5 mL injections) are required for a complete dose.
Vial access device injection kit
Remove the vial cap, clean the vial stopper with an alcohol wipe, and attach the vial access device by pushing down on the vial. Twist off the top of the vial access device.
Attach a syringe and inject 1.5 mL of air into the vial and then withdraw all contents (1.5 mL).

Withdrawal needle injection kit
Remove the vial cap, clean the vial stopper with an alcohol wipe, and attach the included 18-gauge withdrawal needle to the syringe.
Inject 1.5 mL of air into the vial and withdraw all contents.
Remove the 18-gauge withdrawal needle from the syringe.
Once the solution is withdrawn from the vials, the subcutaneous injections should be administered as soon as possible.
 
Subcutaneous injection
Attach a 22-gauge injection needle, expel air bubbles, and prime to 1.5 mL.
Administer subcutaneously in the abdomen at least 2 inches from the navel (the thigh can be used as an alternative injection site if preferred). A 90-degree injection angle is preferred, but between 45 degrees and 90 degrees is acceptable.
Ensure the injection is only administered subcutaneously. Do not administer via intradermal, intramuscular, or intravenous injection. Improper intradermal administration has been associated with serious injection site reactions, including skin necrosis and ulceration.
Two 1.5 mL injections are required for a complete dose. Administer the second injection at least 4 inches from the first injection site.
