Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Patients with significant GI disease, such as acute peptic ulcer disease, ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease, or GI obstruction were not included in clinical trials of lanthanum carbonate. Caution is recommended when lanthanum carbonate is used in patients with these conditions.Abdominal x-rays of patients taking lanthanum carbonate may have a radiopaque appearance typical of an imaging agent. Patients should notify their physicians that they are taking lanthanum carbonate prior to any diagnostic procedures.No overall differences in safety or effectiveness of lanthanum carbonate have been observed between the elderly and younger patients.Lanthanum carbonate is categorized FDA pregnancy risk category C. Adequate and well-controlled studies have not been conducted in pregnant women. The effects lanthanum carbonate on vitamin and nutrient absorption has not been studied. The use of lanthanum carbonate is not recommended for use during pregnancy.It is not known if lanthanum carbonate is excreted in human breast milk. Although lanthanum carbonate is not systemically absorbed, and excretion in human breast milk is unlikely, administration to mothers who are breast-feeding should be done cautiously.Long-term animal studies have shown lanthanum carbonate to be deposited into developing bone; including growth plate. Since the consequences of this deposition in developing bone in pediatric patients is not known, the use of lanthanum carbonate in children and adolescents is not recommended.

    Headline
    constipation, diverticulosis, gastrointestinal cancer, gastrointestinal surgery, gastroparesis, intestinal obstruction, peptic ulcer disease, peritonitis
    General Information
    Lanthanum carbonate is contraindicated in individuals with intestinal obstruction, including ileus and fecal impaction. Serious cases of GI obstruction, ileus, subileus, GI perforation, and fecal impaction, some necessitating surgery or hospitalization, have been reported in individuals receiving lanthanum carbonate. Risk factors for GI obstruction and perforation identified in postmarketing reports include abnormal GI anatomy (e.g., diverticulosis, peritonitis, history of gastrointestinal surgery, gastrointestinal cancer, gastrointestinal ulceration or peptic ulcer disease), hypomotility disorders (e.g., constipation, diabetic gastroparesis, ileus, subileus), and concomitant medications known to potentiate these effects. Some cases of GI obstruction were reported in individuals with no history of GI disease. Subjects with acute peptic ulcer, ulcerative colitis, Crohn disease, or bowel obstruction, were not included in lanthanum carbonate clinical trials.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, infants, neonates
    General Information
    Lanthanum carbonate is not recommended in neonates, infants, children, and adolescents. Although long-term animal studies did not identify growth abnormalities, lanthanum was deposited in developing bone, including the growth plate. The consequences of such deposition in developing bone in pediatric populations are unknown.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on the use of lanthanum carbonate during pregnancy are insufficient to evaluate a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. Use a non-lanthanum containing phosphate binder in pregnant individuals. In animal reproduction studies, oral administration of lanthanum carbonate to rats and rabbits during organogenesis at doses 3 and 2.5 times, respectively, the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD), resulted in no adverse developmental effects. In rabbits, lanthanum carbonate at doses 5 times the MRHD was associated with maternal toxicity and resulted in increased post-implantation loss, reduced fetal weights, and delayed fetal ossification. In long-term studies, deposition of lanthanum into developing bone, including growth plate, was observed in juvenile animals.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking lanthanum carbonate. Use alternative phosphate-binding medications in lactating patients. There are no data on the presence of lanthanum carbonate in human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effect on milk production. Deposition of lanthanum into developing bone, including growth plate, was observed in juvenile animals in long-term animal studies.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  In two double-blind, placebo-controlled trials, a total of 180 and 95 end stage renal disease (ESRD) patients were randomized to receive lanthanum carbonate and placebo, respectively, for 4 to 6 weeks. Adverse events that were more common in the lanthanum carbonate group with a >= 5% difference in frequency when compared to placebo included nausea/vomiting (11%/9% vs. 5%/4%), dialysis graft occlusion (8% vs. 1%), and abdominal pain (5% vs. 0%). Lanthanum carbonate was compared to alternative therapy in a long-term (2 year), open-label, active controlled study. In this study 682 patients received lanthanum carbonate and 676 received alternative therapy. Adverse event rates in the study were adjusted for mean exposure differences between treatment groups (mean exposure of 0.9 years for lanthanum carbonate and 1.3 years for alternative therapy). The adjustment for mean exposure was achieved by multiplying the observed adverse event rates of the alternative therapy group by 0.71. The reported rates of adverse events in the lanthanum carbonate vs. alternative therapy trial were reported as nausea (36% vs. 28%), vomiting (26% vs. 21%), dialysis graft complication (26% vs. 25%), diarrhea (23% vs. 22%), headache (21% vs. 20%), dialysis graft occlusion (21% vs. 20%), abdominal pain (17% vs. 17%), hypotension (16% vs. 17%), constipation (14% vs. 13%), bronchitis (5% vs. 6%), rhinitis (5% vs. 7%), and hypercalcemia (4% vs. 8%). Another long-term study (6 months duration) compared lanthanum carbonate treated patients (N=533) to those receiving calcium carbonate (n=267). The adverse reactions reported during this study were similar to those reported in the 2 year open-label, active controlled study but results were not adjusted for mean exposure. The frequency of events in the lanthanum carbonate group compared to those of the calcium carbonate group were nausea (16% vs. 13%), vomiting (18% vs. 11%), dialysis graft complication (3% vs. 5%), diarrhea (13% vs. 10%), headache (5% vs. 6%), dialysis graft occlusion (4% vs. 6%), abdominal pain (5% vs. 3%), hypotension (8% vs. 9%), constipation (6% vs. 7%), bronchitis (5% vs. 6%), rhinitis (7% vs. 6%), and hypercalcemia (0% vs. 20%).

Description
  Text
  Lanthanum carbonate is an oral, non-aluminum, non-calcium phosphate binding agent indicated for the management of hyperphosphatemia in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD). In 2 large, randomized clinical trials conducted in patients with ESRD undergoing dialysis, lanthanum carbonate was shown to be as effective as calcium carbonate and other conventional phosphate binders in reducing and maintaining serum phosphorus concentrations to 5.6 mg/dL or less and 5.9 mg/dL or less over 6 months and 2 years, respectively. Thus, lanthanum carbonate is an effective alternative to these therapies in ESRD patients with hyperphosphatemia. Since it does not contain aluminum, lanthanum carbonate does not cause aluminum intoxication. Lanthanum carbonate does not contain calcium and should not directly cause hypercalcemia. Conversely, in clinical trials of 1 year and 6 months duration comparing calcium carbonate to lanthanum carbonate, a small incidence of hypercalcemia was reported in patients receiving lanthanum carbonate. Serious cases of GI obstruction, perforation, and ileus have been reported, particularly in association with unchewed or incompletely chewed lanthanum tablets.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  After oral administration, lanthanum carbonate dissociates to lanthanum ions in the acidic environment of the upper GI tract. The lanthanum ions bind dietary phosphate released from food during digestion. Once lanthanum binds phosphate, the water-insoluble lanthanum phosphate complex formed is poorly absorbed across the gut wall and is eventually excreted in the feces. Ultimately, with repeated dosing, serum phosphate concentrations are decreased.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Systemic absorption following oral administration is very low, with a bioavailability of < 0.002% following single or multiple oral doses to healthy subjects. Furthermore, no accumulation in plasma is apparent following administration of standard therapeutic doses of lanthanum carbonate for over 2 years. The effect of food on the bioavailability of lanthanum carbonate has not been evaluated but the timing of a dose relative to food intake (with and 30 minutes after food) has a negligible effect on lanthanum plasma concentrations. In order to bind phosphate efficiently, and since tolerability is poor when given without food, lanthanum carbonate should be administered with or immediately after a meal.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Pediatrics
    Description
    The use of lanthanum carbonate is not recommended in pediatric patients, since long-term animal studies have shown lanthanum carbonate to be deposited into developing bone; including the growth plate.
  Text
  Lanthanum is administered orally. In vitro, lanthanum is > 99% bound to plasma proteins. Animal studies have revealed lanthanum concentrations in many tissues, particularly GI tract, bone, and liver, were several times greater than plasma concentrations and remained in these tissues for longer than 6 months after cessation of dosing. Bone biopsies from subjects treated with lanthanum carbonate for up to 4.5 years revealed rising levels of lanthanum over time, the effect of which is unknown. The half-life of lanthanum is approximately 53 hours. Nearly all (94—99%) of an oral dose is excreted in the feces. Biliary excretion is the predominant route of elimination for circulating lanthanum.
