    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. In individuals with a history of allergy or sensitivity to serum of equine (horse) origin, administer diphtheria equine antitoxin, especially via the intravenous route, under close medical supervision. Obtain a thorough history to identify risk factors and perform sensitivity testing before administration. Consider desensitization in individuals with positive sensitivity testing or a history suggesting increased risk from diphtheria antitoxin administration. Consider premedication with antihistamines and corticosteroids if there is a history of previous use of heterologous serum (anti-tetanus, anti-rabies, anti-venom) and allergic events.

    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no known extra risks from diphtheria antitoxin for a pregnant woman or her unborn baby and no contraindication to diphtheria antitoxin use during pregnancy.  Because about one-third of all patients who have diphtheria do not survive without diphtheria antitoxin, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recognizes treating diphtheria during pregnancy to be of much greater benefit to the mother or the fetus than the possible risk of treatment. However, other guidelines recommend against use of diphtheria antitoxin during pregnancy.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  Adverse Reactions
    Headline
    anaphylactic shock, anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema, cough, cyanosis, dyspnea, flushing, hypotension, nausea, pruritus, urticaria, vomiting, wheezing
    General Information
    Although onset and severity are highly variable, anaphylaxis (less than 0.6%) or anaphylactoid reactions usually begin within minutes after exposure to diphtheria antitoxin. In general, the more rapid the onset, the more severe the reaction. Major manifestations include cutaneous, respiratory, and cardiovascular reactions. Fatal anaphylactic shock was reported in 1:50,000 patients using the horse serum. Cutaneous reactions include pruritus, flushing, urticaria, and angioedema. Respiratory reactions include cough, hoarse voice and stridor, wheezing, dyspnea, and cyanosis. Cardiovascular reactions include a rapid and weak pulse, hypotension, and arrhythmias. Nausea and vomiting may also occur. In the event of an anaphylactic reaction, treatment will depend on the nature and severity of the reaction. Parenterally-administered epinephrine is the primary drug for all types of reactions. Antihistamines should also be given. Depending on the severity of the reaction, additional medications may include corticosteroids, alpha-adrenergic blocking agents, bronchodilators, aminophylline, and beta-2 agonists. Allergic reactions to horse serum varying from anaphylaxis to rash can be expected in 5% to 20% of patients.

    General Information
    Febrile reactions usually develop within 20 to 60 minutes after exposure to diphtheria antitoxin. Fever (4%) is characterized by chills, slight dyspnea, and a rapid rise in temperature. Most febrile reactions are mild and can be treated with antipyretics alone. Severe reactions may require other measures (tepid water baths, etc.) to reduce the temperature. Febrile reactions are not IgE-mediated and are not predicted by skin testing.

    Headline
    arthralgia, glomerulonephritis, Guillain-Barre syndrome, lymphadenopathy, myocarditis, neuritis, serum sickness
    General Information
    Serum sickness (8.8%) usually occurs 7 to 10 days (range 5 to 25 days) after initial exposure to diphtheria antitoxin. Onset can be as short as several hours to 3 days after administration of diphtheria antitoxin in patients who have received a dose of animal serum in the past; these individuals are also more likely to develop serum sickness. Symptoms include fever, maculopapular rash, or urticaria in milder forms (90% of instances). Arthritis, arthralgia, and lymphadenopathy are also possible in more severe forms. Angioedema, glomerulonephritis, Guillain-Barre syndrome, peripheral neuritis, or myocarditis may rarely occur. Mild cases frequently resolve spontaneously over a few days to 2 weeks. Antihistamines, NSAIDs, and corticosteroids may be helpful. Serum sickness is not IgE-mediated and is not predicted by skin testing.

Description
  Text
  Diphtheria antitoxin (DAT) is a parenteral polyclonal IgG antibody indicated for use in combination with antibiotic therapy to treat diphtheria. It is generally not indicated for diphtheria prophylaxis. DAT is produced using serum from horses that are hyperimmunized with diphtheria toxoid. Patients with a history of asthma, allergic rhinitis, urticaria, or equine protein hypersensitivity may be at increased risk of developing serious anaphylactic reactions upon receipt of DAT. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends all patients have an appropriate history taken for factors suggesting increased risk and undergo sensitivity testing to DAT; perform desensitization in patients with positive or equivocal sensitivity testing. Conversely, routine sensitivity testing is not recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO), as routine testing may decrease accessibility and timeliness to receiving DAT. Drug recipients must be closely monitored by a sufficiently trained staff with access to emergency medications and equipment. DAT is only available through the CDC. Healthcare providers can obtain DAT by contacting the CDC's Emergency Operations Center after consulting with their state health department. DAT is available from CDC under an IND protocol. A CDC diphtheria duty officer will discuss the protocol for DAT release with the treating clinician. If the clinician decides DAT is indicated, it will be dispatched from one of the U.S. Quarantine Stations. The clinician makes the final decision on its use, may revise the diagnosis, and decide against administering DAT after it is released.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Diphtheria antitoxin is a polyclonal IgG antibody that binds to circulating diphtheria toxin in the serum produced by toxigenic strains of C. diphtheriae. Diphtheria antitoxin will not neutralize toxin that has already entered cells and fixed to tissues, but it will neutralize circulating (unbound) toxin and prevent disease progression. The antitoxin is most effective when administered as soon as possible after the presumptive diagnosis is made.

NOTE: Sensitivity testing to diphtheria antitoxin:
The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends all patients undergo testing for sensitivity to diphtheria antitoxin before initiation of full-dose therapy. Perform desensitization in patients with positive sensitivity testing or a history suggesting increased risk from diphtheria antitoxin administration.
The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends not preforming routine sensitivity testing prior to administering diphtheria antitoxin, as not routinely testing all patients may increase accessibility and timeliness to receiving diphtheria antitoxin. However, due to the risk of allergic reactions, patients must be monitored closely by a sufficiently trained staff with access to the following:
Monitoring equipment (pulse oximeter, blood pressure monitoring, thermometer)
Emergency equipment (age appropriate equipment for airway management and suction, oxygenation, cardiovascular support)
Emergency medications (adrenaline, intravenous antihistamine, corticosteroid, intravenous fluid, oxygen supply and delivery devices)
NOTE: Consider administration of antihistamines (H1 and H2 antagonists) and corticosteroids 15 minutes prior to the dose if there is a history of previous use of heterologous serum (anti-tetanus, anti-rabies, anti-venom) and allergic events. Premedications should not delay administration of diphtheria antitoxin.
