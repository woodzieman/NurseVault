  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV) is not recommended for routine immunization in the US and is no longer distributed in the country.
Interruption of the recommended schedule with a delay between doses should not interfere with the final immunity achieved with poliovirus vaccine, inactivated, IPV. There is no need to restart the primary series, regardless of the time elapsed between doses.
If an individual previously experienced clinical poliomyelitis, vaccination with IPV is still indicated because disease is usually caused by infection with a single poliovirus type.
Clinically stable premature infants or low-birth-weight infants should generally receive routine immunizations at the same chronological age as term and normal-birth weight infants.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Prior to administration, health care personnel should inform the patient, parent, guardian, or responsible adult of the vaccine's benefits and risks. This should include the provision of the vaccine information statement from the manufacturer. The responsible adult should report any adverse reaction following vaccine administration to the health care provider. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services has established a Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) to accept all reports of suspected adverse events after the administration of any vaccine. This includes, but is not limited to, the reporting of events required by the National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act of 1986. The toll-free number for VAERS is (800) 822—7967. All adverse events experienced by a vaccine recipient need to be reported by the health care professional. The vaccine manufacturer also requests that any adverse events be reported to them by calling (800) 822—2463.
 
The inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPV) is contraindicated in patients who have anaphylaxis or anaphylactic shock within 24 hours of a previous dose of the vaccine or any of its components. Patients with streptomycin hypersensitivity, neomycin hypersensitivity, or polymyxin hypersensitivity should not receive the IPV. Streptomycin, neomycin, and polymyxin B are used in the manufacturing process. Trace amounts of these antibiotics may be present in the vaccine. Although the vial stopper and the syringe plunger are not made of any kind of rubber, the needle cover on the syringes of the inactivated poliovirus vaccine contains dry natural latex rubber. Health care professionals should administer the vaccine with caution to patients with a history of latex hypersensitivity, or a condition with the potential for latex hypersensitivity (e.g., spina bifida). Extreme caution is needed if the allergic reaction to latex is severe (i.e., anaphylactic or anaphylactoid). With any biologic product, the prescriber or health care professional should take precautions to prevent allergic reactions. The health care professional should have immediate availability of epinephrine (1:1000) injection and other agents used in the treatment of severe anaphylaxis in the event of a serious allergic reaction.
 
Do not give inactivated poliovirus vaccine, IPV via intravenous administration or intraarterial administration. IPV should be administered intramuscularly or subcutaneously only. Patients with thrombocytopenia, vitamin K deficiency, a coagulopathy (e.g., hemophilia), or those receiving anticoagulant therapy should be monitored closely when given IPV because bleeding can occur at the intramuscular or subcutaneous injection site.
 
Defer use of the inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPV) in patients with any acute, febrile illness. Administration of the vaccine to patients with minor illness, such as diarrhea or mild upper respiratory infection with or without fever is acceptable. Try not to miss available vaccination opportunities, especially among possible noncompliant patients or caregivers.
 
Safety and efficacy of the inactivated poliovirus vaccine in neonates or infants less than 6 weeks of age have not been established. The immune system is not fully developed in newborn babies. Immunogenicity to the vaccine would likely not occur if the vaccine was administered to neonates or infants less than 6 weeks of age. Anti-idiotypic antibodies to poliovirus present in the mother's serum may cross the placenta and actively induce an immune response by the fetus. Anti-idiotypic antibodies are antibodies that recognize idiotypic determinants on antibodies. Thus, babies born to mothers that have been vaccinated for poliovirus may have anti-poliovirus antibody production without direct antigen (poliovirus) exposure. The secretory antibodies, such as IgG and IgM against poliovirus found in newborns are not transported from the mother to the fetus. Vaccination of all infants is still imperative, as the efficacy and duration of any maternally-induced immunity are unknown. For premature infants, the vaccine series should begin at the child's chronological age (2 months after birth). Some data suggest a reduced immunological response to the IPV, especially in children less than 32 weeks gestational age and less than 1750 grams at birth. More data are needed. The usual vaccine series given at the recommended chronological age should be followed.
 
According to the Centers for Disease Control, patients with immunosuppression should receive the inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPV) instead of the live oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Patients with impaired immunity could develop polio if they receive the OPV. Immunosuppression can also cause a decreased response to vaccination. Patients on immunosuppressive therapy including chemotherapy, radiation therapy, corticosteroid therapy, or who have other immunodeficiency may need to delay therapy with IPV because of decreased response. Preferably, immunization should be witheld until 3 months after resolution of immunosuppressive conditions or completion of immunosuppressive treatments. Patients with HIV infection, renal failure, diabetes mellitus, alcoholic cirrhosis, or asplenia should receive immunization with IPV.
 
Inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPV) is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. Administration of IPV during pregnancy is not recommended unless there is an eminent risk of poliovirus exposure. Animal reproduction studies have not been conducted with IPOL®; the risk of vaccination to a human fetus or to a woman's reproductive capacity is unknown.
 
There is no information on the excretion of the IPV in breast milk during breast-feeding. According to the ACIP guidelines, breast-feeding does not adversely affect immunization of the vaccine recipient. Breast feeding actively stimulates the infant's immune response. Human milk of women vaccinated for poliovirus contains anti-idiotypic antibodies to poliovirus. Anti-idiotypic antibodies are antibodies that recognize idiotypic determinants on antibodies to poliovirus. Thus, breast-fed babies of mothers that have been vaccinated for poliovirus may have anti-poliovirus antibody production without direct antigen (poliovirus) exposure. Vaccination of these infants is still imperative, as the efficacy and duration of any maternally-induced immunity are unknown.
 
Vaccination of children with a seizure history is not recommended by the American College of Immunization Practices (ACIP) until the seizure etiology is determined. A family history of seizures is not a contraindication for vaccination with the inactivated poliovirus vaccine. For children at higher risk of seizures than the general population, the ACIP recommends that acetaminophen or ibuprofen be administered at the time of vaccination and for 24 hours after to reduce the possibility of postvaccination fever.
 
A causal relationship between IPOL® inactivated polio vaccine and Guillain-Barre syndrome has not been established. However, a temporal relationship between Guillain-Barre syndrome and another inactivated poiliovirus vaccine has been observed.

    Headline
    immunosuppression
    General Information
    Patients with significant immunosuppression may not have an adequate antibody response to poliovirus vaccine, inactivated, IPV. Immunosuppressed persons may include patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection; severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID); hypogammaglobulinemia; agammaglobulinemia; altered immune states due to generalized neoplastic disease; or an immune system compromised by radiation therapy or drug therapy (e.g., chemotherapy or corticosteroid therapy with greater than physiologic doses). Short-term (less than 2 weeks) corticosteroid therapy or intra-articular, bursal, or tendon injections with corticosteroids should not be immunosuppressive. Patients vaccinated with IPV within 2 weeks before starting immunosuppressive therapy or while receiving immunosuppressive therapy should be considered unvaccinated and should be revaccinated at least 3 months after therapy is discontinued if immune competence has been restored. Hematopoietic stem cell tranplant recipients should be revaccinated routinely 6 months after the transplant, regardless of the source of the transplanted stem cells.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The most common adverse reaction to the inactivated poliovirus vaccine is an injection site reaction (usually erythema, swelling and tenderness). Of patients that received an earlier formulation of the vaccine, 3.2% had erythema, 1% had induration, and 13% had pain within 48 hours of vaccine receipt. Among children 2—18 months of age that received inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPOL®) intramuscularly, 0—1.4% had erythema within 48 hours after the injection. More children had swelling (0—11.4%) or tenderness (0—29.4%).Systemic adverse events, such as fever, restlessness, drowsiness, anorexia, nausea/vomiting, and inconsolable crying observed in children that received the inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPOL®) are difficult to ascertain because the vaccine was given concomitantly with the DTP (acellular pertussis) vaccine or with Tripedia®. The percentages of patients that had each adverse event are comparable in frequency and severity to the numbers reported for the whole-cell DTP given alone. Temperatures of 39 degrees C (102 degrees F) or greater within 48 hours of vaccine receipt occurred in 0—4.2% of vaccine recipients. The percentages of patients that had irritability (restlessness) were 6.7—64.5%; inconsolable crying (0—1.4%); anorexia (1.3—16.6%); nausea/vomiting (0—2.8%); and sleepiness (described as lethargy, malaise, or drowsiness, 4—60.7%).Allergic reactions and anaphylactoid reactions, such as anaphylactic shock and angioedema are acknowledged possible adverse effects associated with the inactivated poliovirus vaccine. Serious allergic reactions usually occur within a few hours after vaccine receipt. The vaccine recipient may have difficulty breathing, wheezing, tachycardia, urticaria, dizziness, or swelling of the face and throat areas. Development of a severe allergic reaction precludes subsequent IPV administration.A causal relationship has not been established between the inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPOL®) and either Guillain-Barre syndrome or death. However, temporal relationships between deaths of infants that received the inactivated poliovirus vaccine and Guillain-Barre syndrome and another inactivated poliovirus vaccine have been observed. Currently, IPOL® is the only inactivated poliovirus vaccine available in the United States.

Description
  Text
  Poliovirus vaccine, inactivated, IPV (IPOL) is a non-live vaccine with enhanced potency consisting of inactivated poliovirus type 1 (Mahoney), type 2 (MEF-1), and type 3 (Saukett). The vaccine induces active immunity against poliovirus types 1, 2, and 3. Since 2000, IPV has been the only polio vaccine used in the United States; oral polio vaccine (OPV) is no longer licensed or available in the U.S. and is not recommended for routine immunization. An all-IPV schedule is recommended for routine childhood vaccination, and adults who are known or suspected to be unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated against polio should complete a primary IPV series. Fully vaccinated adults at increased risk for poliovirus exposure may receive a single lifetime IPV booster dose. Vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis (VAPP) has not been reported in association with IPOL administration. The first IPV formulation, the Salk inactivated polio vaccine, was licensed in the U.S. in 1955.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Poliovirus vaccine, inactivated, IPV provides long-term protection against paralytic poliomyelitis primarily through humoral immunity by stimulating production of serum neutralizing antibodies against poliovirus types 1, 2, and 3. Protection after a complete IPV series is long-lasting; the exact duration is not known, although protection is likely to persist for many years and may be lifelong. IPV can also induce secretory antibody (IgA) in the pharynx and gut. Since poliovirus enters through the mouth and replicates in the oropharynx and gastrointestinal tract, mucosal antibodies may help reduce viral replication and shedding at these sites. IPV has been shown to reduce pharyngeal excretion of Poliovirus type 1, which may reduce oral/oropharyngeal transmission; pharyngeal excretion was reduced from 75% in children with neutralizing antibody concentrations less than 1:8 to 25% in children with concentrations greater than 1:64. However, IPV produces less intestinal immunity than OPV and may not substantially reduce fecal shedding after exposure. Evidence supports herd immunity with IPV, including in populations vaccinated only with IPV, particularly when vaccine coverage is high.

Pharmacokinetics
  Special Populations
    Name
    Pediatrics
    Description
    Infants and Children 2 to 12 months
In a study of infants vaccinated with IPV at 2, 4, and 12 months of age, serum neutralizing, nasopharyngeal neutralizing, and nasopharyngeal IgA antibody responses were evaluated. Serum and nasopharyngeal secretions were collected 1 month after vaccination. Among evaluable infants after 2 doses, 96.4% had detectable serum neutralizing antibodies to poliovirus types 1 and 3, and 100% had detectable antibodies to type 2. After 3 doses, 100% had detectable serum neutralizing antibodies to poliovirus types 2 and 3, and 96.2% had detectable antibodies to type 1; final geometric mean titers were 1,954.28, 5,835.37, and 5,187.4 for types 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Detectable nasopharyngeal neutralizing antibodies to poliovirus types 1, 2, and 3 were present in 43.4%, 60.4%, and 66% of evaluable infants, respectively, after the third dose. Nasopharyngeal IgA antibodies to poliovirus were detected in 88.7%, 90.6%, and 88.7% of evaluable infants for types 1, 2, and 3, respectively, after the third dose.
  Text
  Poliovirus vaccine, inactivated, IPV is administered intramuscularly or subcutaneously. Conventional pharmacokinetic parameters are not well defined for the vaccine; clinical activity is assessed primarily by immunogenicity. IPV induces serum neutralizing antibodies against each of the three poliovirus types, which are associated with protective efficacy. IPV may also induce secretory antibody, IgA, produced in the pharynx and gut. A direct relationship exists between the antigenic content of IPV, the frequency of seroconversion, and resulting antibody titers. Detectable serum neutralizing antibodies to all three types of poliovirus have been reported for at least 10 years in children and young adults who received an IPV-only immunization schedule.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific dosage adjustment guidelines for IPV in renal impairment are not available; no IPV dosage adjustments appear necessary. Administer IPV according to the recommended age and risk-based immunization schedule. For pediatric patients with chronic kidney disease, including those receiving dialysis, consult current immunization schedules to ensure all indicated and risk-based vaccines are administered. ACIP states that all routine vaccines are likely effective in individuals with chronic renal disease, although vaccine response may be attenuated in some individuals with altered immunocompetence.
