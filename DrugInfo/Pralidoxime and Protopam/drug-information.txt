Drug Class: Pralidoxime, also known as 2-PAM, is an antidote approved to treat poisoning from organophosphate pesticides and chemicals (nerve agents) that have anticholinesterase activity.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 2,000 mg per dose IV; 1,800 mg per dose IM or subcutaneously.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): blurred vision, diplopia, dizziness, drowsiness, headache, hypertension, hyperventilation, myasthenia, sinus tachycardia, weakness, cardiac arrest, hypertonia, injection site reaction, laryngospasm, muscle paralysis

In an Overdose: Pralidoxime, also known as 2-PAM, is an antidote approved to treat poisoning from organophosphate pesticides and chemicals (nerve agents) that have anticholinesterase activity. The drug is also approved to control overdosages of anticholinesterase medications used in the treatment of myasthenia gravis.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  The possibility of precipitating a myasthenic crisis should be considered if pralidoxime is administered to patients receiving anticholinesterase agents for the treatment of myasthenia gravis.Excretion of pralidoxime is mainly renal. Renal dysfunction can increase blood concentrations of pralidoxime through decreased excretion. Patients with renal disease should be treated with caution, and dosage should be reduced in patients with renal impairment.Pralidoxime is classified as pregnancy category C. Because pralidoxime is used only in emergency situations, no adequate human studies have examined the effects of this drug on the fetus. Therefore, in making the decision to administer this drug during pregnancy, the potential risks to the fetus must be weighed against the potential benefits to the mother. In addition, it is unknown if pralidoxime is excreted in breast milk, therefore, administer pralidoxime with caution to breast-feeding mothers.Clinical studies of pralidoxime did not include sufficient numbers of elderly patients (>= 65 years of age) to determine whether they respond differently from younger patients. Other reported clinical experience has not identified differences in responses between the elderly and younger adults. In general, dose selection for an elderly patient should be cautious, usually starting at the low end of the dosing range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function, and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy.

    General Information

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    The drug is not effective for poisoning due to phosphorus, inorganic phosphates, or organophosphates not having anticholinesterase activity. Additionally, it is not indicated as an antidote for intoxications by pesticides of the carbamate class, since pralidoxime may increase carbaryl toxicity.
    Headline
    myasthenia gravis
    General Information
    Use pralidoxime with caution in patients with myasthenia gravis, as it may precipitate a myasthenic crisis. Carefully weigh the risks and benefits of treatment in this population.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    A pralidoxime dose reduction may be needed for patients with renal impairment, including those with renal failure. Decreased renal function can result in increased blood concentrations of the drug, potentially increasing the risk of adverse reactions. Monitor patients with impaired renal function closely for signs of toxicity.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of pralidoxime use during pregnancy. Animal studies have not been conducted. It is unknown whether pralidoxime can cause fetal harm when used during pregnancy or affect reproductive capacity. Use pralidoxime during pregnancy only when clearly needed.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use pralidoxime with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of pralidoxime in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. The molecular weight suggests that pralidoxime may be excreted into human milk; however, its rapid elimination should limit its transfer into the milk. Due to the emergency nature for the use of pralidoxime, breast-feeding is not likely to occur when pralidoxime is used. Advise patients to hold breast-feeding for at least 6 to 7 hours (about 5 half-lives) after the dose is administered.

Amitriptyline: (Moderate) Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) may antagonize some of the effects of parasympathomimetics (e.g., cholinesterase inhibitors) due to their anticholinergic activity. However, parasympathomimetics like bethanechol have occasionally been used historically to offset some of the adverse peripheral antimuscarinic (anticholinergic) effects of TCAs, such as dry mouth, constipation, or urinary retention. For years, physostigmine was used as an adjunct to the treatment of TCA overdose; however, its efficacy was limited to addressing anticholinergic effects. Additionally, case reports suggest that harmful effects such as seizures and bradyarrhythmias progressing to asystole, especially in patients with cardiac conduction abnormalities at baseline, are possible. For these reasons, physostigmine is no longer considered a standard of care in the treatment of TCA overdose.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information

Description
  Text
  Pralidoxime, also known as 2-PAM, is an antidote approved to treat poisoning from organophosphate pesticides and chemicals (nerve agents) that have anticholinesterase activity. The drug is also approved to control overdosages of anticholinesterase medications used in the treatment of myasthenia gravis. Pralidoxime reactivates cholinesterase (primarily outside the cental nervous system), allowing for the breakdown of accumulated acetylcholine and thereby reversing muscle paralysis, the most critical of which being those muscles involved in respiration. However, because pralidoxime is less effective in relieving depression of the respiratory center, it must always be administered concurrently with atropine (an antimuscarinic agent with activity against acetylcholine at this site). In addition to pralidoxime and atropine, treatment of organophosphate poisoning should also include decontamination (removal of exposed clothing, washing hair and skin with sodium bicarbonate or alcohol) and general supportive care (airway management, respiratory and cardiovascular support, correction of metabolic abnormalities, seizure control). Pralidoxime is preferentially administered via intravenous infusion; however, it may be given via intramuscular or subcutaneous injection if intravenous administration is not feasible. Pralidoxime was FDA-approved in 1964.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Pralidoxime is a cholinesterase reactivator that reverses muscle paralysis after poisoning by organophosphate pesticides and nerve agents. Although it acts primarily at the skeletal neuromuscular junction, pralidoxime also reactivates cholinesterase at autonomic receptors, thereby relieving muscarinic signs and symptoms, salivation, and bronchospasm; however, the clinical importance of pralidoxime-induced relief of these effects is questionable because the concomitant administration of atropine will also alleviate these symptoms. Pralidoxime is most effective when given as soon as possible after organophosphate exposure, and as a general rule, little clinical effect is accomplished if the drug is administered more than 36 hours after exposure. Additionally, it should only be used as an antidote against organophosphates with anticholinesterase activity. Pralidoxime is not effective in treating poisoning by phosphorus, inorganic phosphates, or organophosphates not having anticholinesterase activity.
 
Organophosphate compounds inhibit cholinesterase (the enzyme that regulates acetylcholine activity) by phosphorylation of the active site of the enzyme. The inhibited cholinesterase is unable to metabolize acetylcholine, resulting in an accumulation of the acetylcholine within the synaptic cleft. Acetylcholine is present in the central nervious system, parts of the autonomic nervous system, and at the skeletal muscle end plates; therefore, accumulation of this neurotransmitter after organophosphate poisoning adversely affects each of these systems. In the somatic nervous system, acetylcholine accumulation leads to paralysis. The clinical effects of pralidoxime are most evident at skeletal neuromuscular junctions. Pralidoxime reverses the paralysis by removing the phosphoryl group from the inhibited cholinesterase molecule, reactivating the enzyme and restoring the body's ability to metabolize acetylcholine. In addition, the drug may have a direct protective effect on cholinesterase and may combine with certain organophosphates, detoxifying them before they can inhibit cholinesterase.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Intravenous Route
    Description
    Following a single 600 mg pralidoxime injection, the minimum therapeutic drug concentration of 4 mcg/mL is achieved in about 16 minutes. However, simulations (using a single 1,000 mg IV dose) suggest that drug concentrations will fall below 4 mcg/mL in about 1.5 hours. To maintain therapeutic drug concentrations, consider administering a loading dose followed by continuous infusion. In a cross-over study involving 7 healthy adults (ages 18 to 50 years), a short IV infusion of 16 mg/kg over 30 minutes was compared to an IV loading dose of 4 mg/kg over 15 minutes, followed by 3.2 mg/kg/hour for 3.75 hours (total dose 16 mg/kg). The mean time in which plasma concentrations remained greater than the minimum therapeutic concentration of 4 mcg/mL was 118 +/- 52.1 minutes for the short IV infusion versus 257.5 +/- 50.5 minutes for the loading dose plus continuous infusion. In case reports, the use of continuous infusions (with and without loading doses and at infusion rates of 400 to 600 mg/hour) has been described in adults with organophosphate poisoning. In one case, a dose of 400 mg/hour over 5 days resulted in plasma drug concentrations (measured at 5, 10, and 18 hours) of 11.6 to 13.7 mcg/mL. In another report, plasma drug concentrations were 11.79 mcg/mL and 17.26 mcg/mL when an initial loading dose of 1,000 mg was followed by infusions of 500 mg/hour and 600 mg/hour, respectively. In the latter case, the elimination half-life was 4 hours.
    Name
    Intramuscular Route
    Description
    In a study involving healthy adult volunteers and patients with organophosphate self-poisoning, a single 1,000 mg IM injection of pralidoxime resulted in mean Cmax of 7.5 +/- 1.7 mcg/mL and 9.9 +/- 2.4 mcg/mL, respectively. The time to reach mean Cmax was approximately 33 minutes and the mean half-life was about 3 hours in both groups. The renal clearance was 7.2 +/- 2.9 mL/minute/kg in the healthy volunteers and 3.6 +/- 1.5 mL/minute/kg in the organophosphate-poisoned patients.

    Description
    Pralidoxime IV loading dose of 15 to 50 mg/kg (mean 29 mg/kg) followed by a continuous infusion of 10 to 16 mg/kg/hour (mean 14 mg/kg/hour) over 12 to 43 hours (mean 27 +/- 8 hours) was evaluated in 11 organophosphate-poisoned pediatric patients (ages 0.8 to 18 years).   Text
  Pralidoxime is primarily administered intravenously as an injection or continuous infusion; however, intramuscular or subcutaneous injection may also be administered when necessary. Once in systemic circulation, the drug distributes throughout the extracellular water with an apparent steady-state volume of distribution of 0.6 to 2.7 L/kg; it does not bind to plasma proteins. Following partial metabolism by the liver, both the unchanged drug and the metabolite are rapidly excreted in the urine by renal tubular secretion. The apparent half-life is 74 to 77 minutes.
 
Pharmacodynamics:
Pralidoxime is relatively short-acting; therefore, repeated dosing may be needed to maintain drug concentrations above the minimum therapeutic plasma concentration target of 4 mcg/mL, unless administered via continuous infusion.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: none
