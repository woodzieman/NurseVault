    General Information

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Dextrose solutions may also be contraindicated in individuals with known corn hypersensitivity, as dextrose used in these products is typically derived from corn.
    Headline
    alcohol withdrawal delirium, dehydration, intracranial bleeding, intraspinal bleeding, thiamine deficiency
    General Information
    Concentrated intravenous dextrose solutions are contraindicated in individuals with intracranial bleeding or intraspinal bleeding, severe dehydration, or alcohol withdrawal delirium. Administration of these solutions may worsen cerebral edema by promoting fluid shifts across the blood-brain barrier, exacerbate hyperosmolar states, and precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy or cardiomyopathy in individuals with thiamine deficiency.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, hyperglycemia, stroke
    General Information
    Avoid dextrose injection in individuals with clinically significant hyperglycemia. Use of dextrose infusions in individuals with diabetes mellitus or impaired glucose tolerance may further increase blood glucose concentrations. Administering dextrose at a rate that exceeds the individual's metabolic capacity may result in the development of hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS). Individuals with underlying central nervous system (CNS) disorders (e.g., history of stroke), renal impairment, or known carbohydrate intolerance may be at increased risk for HHS during dextrose infusion therapy. Monitor blood glucose concentrations closely during treatment and manage hyperglycemia promptly, including insulin administration when necessary.
    Headline
    critical illness, electrolyte imbalance, malnutrition
    General Information
    Dextrose-containing solutions administered as part of parenteral nutrition or during nutritional repletion may precipitate refeeding syndrome in high-risk individuals. To minimize risk, initiate parenteral nutrition gradually in individuals with severe malnutrition, critical illness, or electrolyte imbalance, particularly hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, or hypomagnesemia. Correct any electrolyte abnormalities prior to starting nutrition support and monitor serum electrolytes closely during therapy. Refeeding syndrome is a potentially fatal complication characterized by metabolic and pathophysiologic derangements that may result in cardiac, respiratory, hepatic, and neuromuscular dysfunction.
    Headline
    neonates, premature neonates, renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Concentrated intravenous dextrose solutions contain aluminum, and aluminum toxicity may occur with prolonged administration in high-risk individuals, including those with renal impairment or renal failure and premature neonates. Premature neonates are at particular risk for aluminum toxicity because of immature renal function, and they require large amounts of calcium and phosphate solutions, which contain aluminum. Research indicates that individuals with renal impairment, who receive parenteral aluminum at more than 4 to 5 mcg/kg/day, may develop aluminum-related CNS and bone toxicities. Tissue loading may occur at lower administration rates. In very-low-birth-weight infants, excessive or rapid administration of dextrose injection may increase serum osmolality and the risk of intracerebral hemorrhage. In addition, neonates, especially low-birth-weight premature neonates, are at increased risk of developing hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia during treatment with intravenous glucose infusions. Monitor serum glucose and plasma electrolyte concentrations closely.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, females, geriatric, hypoxemia, infants
    General Information
    Depending on the tonicity of the solution, the volume and rate of infusion, and an individual's underlying clinical condition and ability to metabolize glucose, intravenous administration of dextrose solutions may cause fluid and solute overload. This may result in dilution of serum electrolytes including the development of hypoosmotic hyponatremia, overhydration, congestive states such as congestive heart failure, or pulmonary edema. Individuals at increased risk of developing hyponatremic encephalopathy include geriatric adults, pediatric patients (infants, children, and adolescents), females (particularly premenopausal females), individuals with hypoxemia, and individuals with underlying CNS disease.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Appropriate administration of dextrose injection during pregnancy is not expected to cause major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. However, maternal hyperglycemia secondary to the infusion of glucose-containing products at the time of delivery has been associated with adverse neonatal outcomes such as hypoglycemia. Animal reproduction studies have not been conducted with injectable dextrose solutions. Oral glucose supplement products are not harmful when used as directed in the recommended doses to treat episodes of hypoglycemia in the conscious pregnant individual as per guideline recommendations.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Simple sugars, such as glucose and dextrose, are compatible with breast-feeding. Exogenous administration is not expected to cause harm to a breastfed child.

Description
  Text
  Glucose (also known as dextrose) is used orally and parenterally. Parenteral dextrose is added to many large volume intravenous solutions, including parenteral nutrition (PN), for the purpose of supplying carbohydrate and fluid replacement, and to treat hypoglycemia. Intravenous glucose is available as dextrose solution for injection and is available in a variety of percentage strengths. Oral glucose products are available as gels, tablets, and oral solutions and may come in different flavors and are primarily used to raise blood glucose in patients who are hypoglycemic (e.g., diabetic patients). Oral glucose (dextrose) solutions are also marketed as products for oral glucose tolerance tests for diagnostic purposes.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Glucose is also known as dextrose. Glucose is a monosaccharide, also known as a simple sugar. As a carbohydrate, glucose supplies energy to cells, organs, and tissues. Some tissues can also use fat or protein as an energy source but others, such as the brain and red blood cells, only use glucose. Dextrose in intravenous fluids undergoes oxidation to carbon dioxide and water, and quickly provides fluid and calories. Oral glucose also works quickly (usually within 15 minutes) by raising blood glucose concentrations to alleviate symptoms of hypoglycemia.

  Text
  Dextrose (glucose) is administered orally and intravenously. As a carbohydrate, glucose supplies energy to cells, organs, and tissues. Some tissues can also use fat or protein as an energy source but others, such as the brain and red blood cells, only use glucose. Glucose can be stored in the body as glycogen and the liver is the primary site of glycogen storage. Glycogen is mobilized from the liver and converted to glucose by gluconeogenesis when the blood glucose concentration is low. Gluconeogenesis is stimulated in response to the release of glucagon by the alpha cells of the pancreas when low blood glucose concentrations occur. Glucose may also be produced from non-carbohydrate precursors, such as pyruvate, amino acids and glycerol, by gluconeogenesis. It is gluconeogenesis that maintains blood glucose concentrations, for example during starvation or intense exercise. Cellular uptake of glucose occurs in response to insulin, and glucose is subsequently broken down through glycolysis, lowering blood sugar levels. Glycolysis is the metabolic pathway that converts glucose into pyruvate. The free energy released in this process is used to form the molecules ATP and NADH which are needed for many cellular functions/reactions.

Glucose (dextrose) oral solutions for diagnostic use (glucose tolerance test, GTT)
The oral GTT test should not be administered if 1) the patient is ill; 2) the patient has fasted less than 10 hours or more than 16 hours, or 3) if dietary carbohydrate intake has been restricted to less than 150 g/day for 3 days prior to testing.
The oral GTT is to be administered in the morning. The subject is to fast overnight, usually for 8 to 14 hours. No medications, caffeine, or tobacco are to be taken until the completion of the test. Water is permitted.
The patient should remain seated throughout the test.
The patient's medications should be screened for drugs that may cause glucose elevations.
The maximum glucose concentration in the beverage is 25 g/100 mL.
The beverage is consumed in 5 minutes or less.
For the diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus, diagnostic criteria following the oral GTT are as follows:
Prediabetes (impaired glucose tolerance): 2-hour plasma glucose 140 to 199 mg/dL
Diabetes: 2-hour plasma glucose 200 mg/dL or more
Per the ADA, a fasting blood glucose (FPG) is the recommended screening test for most individuals because it is faster and easier to perform, more convenient, acceptable to patients, and less expensive. The oral GTT may be necessary for the diagnosis of diabetes when the FPG is normal.
For the diagnosis of gestational diabetes using the 1-step approach, a fasting sample is obtained, followed by 3 blood samples in the 3 hours after glucose ingestion. In the absence of criteria that meet the traditional diagnosis of diabetes, gestational diabetes is diagnosed when 2 or more values meet or exceed the following:
Fasting: 95 mg/dL
1 hour: 180 mg/dL
2 hours: 155 mg/dL
3 hours: 140 mg/dL.
For the diagnosis of gestational diabetes using the 2-step approach, a sample is taken 1 hour after the 50-g glucose challenge; the normal response is plasma glucose less than 140 mg/dL. Perform the usual gestational 100 g oral GTT on those patients with a plasma glucose level that exceeds this threshold value.

Intravenous Infusion
Dextrose solutions should be administered by slow intravenous infusion. Excessive or rapid administration may increase the risk of intracerebral hemorrhage, particularly in very-low-birth-weight infants.
Solutions containing more than 5% dextrose or parenteral nutrition solutions with an osmolarity of 900 mOsm/L or more should be infused via a central vein. Infusion into a peripheral vein may result in vein irritation, vein damage, thrombosis, and venous thrombophlebitis.
Do not administer dextrose injection without electrolytes simultaneously with blood through the same infusion set because of the possibility that pseudo-agglutination of red cells may occur.
When a concentrated dextrose infusion is abruptly withdrawn, it is advisable to follow with the administration of 5% Dextrose Injection or 10% Dextrose Injection to avoid rebound hypoglycemia.
The maximum rate of infusion to avoid glycosuria is 0.5 g/kg/hour. Approximately 95% of the dextrose is retained when infused at a rate of 0.8 g/kg/hour.
 
Parenteral Nutrition (PN) Preparation
Slight opacity of the plastic due to moisture absorption during the sterilization process may be observed in the 2,000 mL flexible pharmacy bulk packages of 50% Dextrose Injection and 70% Dextrose Injection. The opacity will gradually diminish.
During use, container must be stored, and all manipulations performed, an appropriate laminar flow hood.
When ready for compounding, insert piercing pin of transfer set into outlet port at bottom of container and suspend unit in laminar flow hood. Insertion of pin should only be performed once. Once the outlet has been entered, withdraw contents promptly and in 1 continuous operation. If this is not possible, a maximum time of 4 hours from pin placement is permitted to complete fluid transfer. Discard container no later than 4 hours after pin placement.
Compounded admixtures utilizing 2,000 mL flexible pharmacy bulk packages of 50% Dextrose Injection and 70% Dextrose Injection may be stored under refrigeration for up to 24 hours. Complete administration of admixtures within 24 hours after removal from refrigeration.

Due to a critical shortage of large volume parenteral fluid solutions in the United States market, the FDA is allowing Baxter to temporarily import 50% Glucose Injection (3,000 mL) and 70% Glucose Injection (500 mL) from their facility in the United Kingdom as of November 2024. There are important differences between these imported Glucose Injection products and the FDA-approved Dextrose Injection products; these products are not equivalent in caloric content, osmolarity, and specific gravity. Read and become familiar with the Dear Healthcare Professional letter, including the labeling and product comparison, which is attached to the letter. Protocols, order entry, and compounding systems will need to be adjusted. The American Society of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition has published a resource to assist clinicians in the conversion of Dextrose Injection to Glucose Injection.
The imported products do not contain barcodes on the unit label. Manually input the imported product information, including the NDC number, into the system and confirm that the linear barcode, if scanned, provides correct information. Follow alternative procedures to ensure that the correct drug product and concentration are being used and administered to individuals. The imported products are not intended for direct patient administration. When compounding with the imported products, check for compatibility of all additives and stability of the resulting preparation.
Do not give parenteral dextrose subcutaneously or intramuscularly. Administer dextrose intravenously, taking care to insure that the needle (or catheter) is well within the lumen of the vein and that extravasation does not occur.
