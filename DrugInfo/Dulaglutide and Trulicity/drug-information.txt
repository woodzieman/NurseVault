Drug Class: Dulaglutide is a synthetic glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) and belongs to a class of antidiabetic agents called incretin mimetics.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 4.5 mg/week subcutaneously.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): dysgeusia, eructation, gastroesophageal reflux, gi obstruction, hyperamylasemia, ileus, acute kidney injury, dehydration, renal failure, av block, pr prolongation, sinus tachycardia, anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema, edema, erythema, injection site reaction, rash, urticaria, cholecystitis, cholelithiasis, cholestasis, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatitis

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Anaphylaxis and angioedema have been reported with dulaglutide and other GLP-1 receptor agonists.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  Contraindications And Precautions
    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Anaphylaxis and angioedema have been reported with dulaglutide and other GLP-1 receptor agonists. Use dulaglutide with caution in people with a history of angioedema or anaphylaxis with a GLP-1 receptor agonist because it is unknown whether such patients will be predisposed to these reactions with dulaglutide.
    Headline
    medullary thyroid carcinoma, multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2, thyroid C-cell tumors
    General Information
    Dulaglutide is contraindicated in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or in people with multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2). Dulaglutide has been shown to cause dose-dependent and treatment duration-dependent increase in the incidence of thyroid C-cell tumors (adenomas and carcinomas) in animal studies. It is unknown whether dulaglutide causes thyroid C-cell tumors, including MTC, in humans. It is not known whether monitoring serum calcitonin or performing thyroid ultrasounds will diminish human risk of thyroid C-cell tumors. Dulaglutide recipients should be counseled on the risk and symptoms of thyroid tumors (e.g. a mass in the neck, dysphagia, dyspnea or persistent hoarseness). Although routine monitoring of serum calcitonin is of uncertain value during dulaglutide treatment, if serum calcitonin is measured and found to be elevated, the individual should be referred to an endocrinologist for further evaluation.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Use caution when initiating or increasing doses of dulaglutide in people with renal impairment or in dulaglutide recipients reporting severe gastrointestinal adverse reactions; monitor renal function. There is limited information available on the use of dulaglutide in people with severe renal impairment, renal failure, or end stage renal disease (ESRD). Monitor renal function in people reporting adverse reactions to dulaglutide that could lead to volume depletion. There have been postmarketing reports of acute kidney injury, acute renal failure and worsening of chronic renal failure with dulaglutide, which may sometimes require hemodialysis. The majority of the reported events occurred in people who experienced gastrointestinal adverse reactions leading to dehydration such as nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea.
    Headline
    gastroparesis
    General Information
    Dulaglutide is not recommended for people with severe gastroparesis. Dulaglutide may slow gastric emptying, consistent with the actions of other GLP-1 receptor agonists, and has been associated with gastrointestinal adverse reactions that are sometimes severe.
    Headline
    diabetic retinopathy
    General Information
    Monitor for visual changes in people with a history of diabetic retinopathy. Inform patients to contact their care team if changes in vision are experienced during treatment. Rapid improvement in glucose control has been associated with a temporary worsening of diabetic retinopathy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use dulaglutide with caution during breast-feeding. It is not known if dulaglutide is excreted into human milk. Dulaglutide is a large peptide, so it is unlikely it would transfer into the breast milk after the first week following birth. However, decreased body weight in offspring was observed in rats treated with dulaglutide during gestation and lactation. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure including the potential for tumorigenicity, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated maternal condition. If dulaglutide is discontinued and blood glucose is not controlled on diet and exercise alone, insulin therapy may be considered. Oral hypoglycemics may also be considered. Metformin monotherapy may be appropriate for some patients as available studies indicate low excretion in milk and that maternal use during breast-feeding is not expected to result in side effects to a healthy nursing infant. Some experts recommend using metformin with caution if the patient is breastfeeding a newborn or a premature neonate with reduced renal function. Because acarbose has limited systemic absorption, which results in minimal maternal plasma concentrations, clinically significant exposure via breast milk is not expected; therefore, this agent may be an alternative if postprandial glucose control is needed. Glyburide may be a suitable alternative since it was not detected in the breast milk of lactating women who received single and multiple doses of glyburide. If any oral hypoglycemics are used during breast-feeding, the breastfed infant should be monitored for signs of hypoglycemia, such as increased fussiness or somnolence.
    Headline
    pancreatitis
    General Information
    Use dulaglutide with caution in people with a history of pancreatitis. After initiation of dulaglutide, observe individuals carefully for signs and symptoms of pancreatitis (which may include persistent or severe abdominal pain (sometimes radiating to the back) and which may or may not be accompanied by nausea or vomiting). Acute pancreatitis, including fatal and non-fatal hemorrhagic or necrotizing pancreatitis, has been observed in individuals treated with GLP-1 receptor agonists. If pancreatitis is suspected, discontinue dulaglutide and initiate appropriate management.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Avoid the use of dulaglutide during pregnancy. Counsel individuals on the potential risk to the fetus. Limited data on the use of dulaglutide during human pregnancy are insufficient to determine a drug-associated risk for major birth defects and miscarriage. However, animal studies suggest fetal harm. In pregnant rats and rabbits, dulaglutide given during organogenesis was associated with fetal growth reduction, malformations, and irregular skeletal ossification. These effects occurred with reduced maternal weight and food consumption, which was attributed to the dulaglutide's pharmacologic effects. Increased post-implantation loss was also seen in pregnant rats. Memory-related findings were reported in female offspring of treated rats, but the relevance of these findings to humans is unknown. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommend human insulin as the standard of care for pregnant individuals with diabetes or gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) requiring medical therapy; insulin does not cross the placenta.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with dulaglutide. Based on animal reproduction studies, dulaglutide may cause fetal harm. For those who may become pregnant, contraception is recommended during treatment with dulaglutide and other glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA). Discontinue GLP-1 RA therapy before a planned pregnancy. Advise individuals that several months may be needed before conception to allow for drug elimination and transition to a therapy appropriate for use during pregnancy.

Acebutolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Description
  Text
  Dulaglutide is a synthetic glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) and belongs to a class of antidiabetic agents called incretin mimetics. Incretins are endogenous compounds, including glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), that improve glycemic control once released into the circulation via the gut. Dulaglutide subcutaneous injection is given just once weekly and is used as an adjunct to diet and exercise to improve glycemic control in adults and pediatric individuals 10 years and older with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Dulaglutide is also used for the reduction of cardiovascular (CV) mortality due to major cardiovascular events (MACE) in T2DM adult individuals with established CV disease or multiple risk factors for CV disease. In a CV outcomes trial (median follow-up of 5.4 years), the primary composite outcome of non-fatal myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke, or death from CV causes (including unknown causes) occurred in 594 (12%) participants (incidence rate of 2.4 per 100 person-years) in the dulaglutide group and in 663 (13.4%) participants (an incidence rate of 2.7 per 100 person-years) in the placebo group (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.79 to 0.99; p = 0.026). When assessed within subgroups, the HR of the intervention on the primary outcome was similar in participants with and without previous CV disease. As with other agents in this class, dulaglutide has a boxed warning regarding rodent C-cell tumor findings and the uncertain relevance to humans. In pediatric individuals 10 to 17 years with T2DM, treatment with dulaglutide resulted in a significant reduction in A1C and fasting blood glucose, with no between-group differences in BMI changes and the safety profile was consistent with that reported in adults. First-line T2DM therapy depends on comorbidities, individual-centered treatment factors, and management needs. In adults with T2DM and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or indicators of high ASCVD risk, sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2 inhibitor) and/or GLP-1 RA with proven CV benefit (e.g. dulaglutide, liraglutide, and semaglutide), should be initiated as a first-line therapy independent of A1C goal or other antihyperglycemic treatments, including metformin. In adults with T2DM and heart failure (HF), an SGLT2 inhibitor with proven benefit should be initiated as first-line therapy to reduce hospitalizations for HF. Certain GLP-1-based therapy (tirzepatide and semaglutide) have proven benefits in individuals with HF and obesity making them alternative treatment options in individuals with T2DM with HF who cannot tolerate an SGLT2 inhibitor. In adults with T2DM and chronic kidney disease (CKD), an SGLT2 inhibitor with primary evidence of reducing CKD progression should be initiated as first-line therapy; however, the glycemic benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors are reduced at eGFR less than 45 mL/minute per 1.73 m2. Evidence from cardiovascular outcome trials indicate that dulaglutide, semaglutide, and liraglutide provide renal benefits, mainly through reductions in albuminuria. Notably, semaglutide has also been shown to slow CKD progression, positioning these agents as alternative options for individuals with T2DM and established CKD who are unable to use SGLT2 inhibitors. In individuals with T2DM who do not have ASCVD/indicators of high-risk, HF, or CKD and who need to minimize hypoglycemia and/or promote weight loss, GLP-1-based therapy is generally recommended as a second-line option as add-on to metformin therapy. Dulaglutide has a very high glucose-lowering efficacy and high efficacy for weight loss. For people requiring injectable medication, GLP-1-based therapy is preferred to insulin due to similar or even better efficacy in A1C reduction, lower risk of hypoglycemia, and reductions in body weight. Dulaglutide was initially FDA approved in 2014.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Dulaglutide is an incretin mimetic; specifically, dulaglutide is a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist. The GLP-1 analog portion of dulaglutide is 90% homologous to native human GLP-1 (7-37). GLP-1 (7-37) represents less than 20% of total circulating endogenous GLP-1. Dulaglutide binds and activates the GLP-1 receptor. GLP-1 is an important, gut-derived, glucose homeostasis regulator that is released after the oral ingestion of carbohydrates or fats. In patients with type 2 diabetes, GLP-1 concentrations are decreased in response to an oral glucose load. GLP-1 enhances insulin secretion; it increases glucose-dependent insulin synthesis and in vivo secretion of insulin from pancreatic beta cells in the presence of elevated glucose. In addition to increases in insulin secretion and synthesis, GLP-1 suppresses glucagon secretion, slows gastric emptying, reduces food intake, and promotes beta-cell proliferation.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Subcutaneous Route
    Description
    The mean absolute bioavailability of dulaglutide following subcutaneous administration of a single 0.75 mg or 1.5 mg dose was 65% and 47%, respectively. Absolute subcutaneous bioavailability for 3 mg and 4.5 mg doses were estimated to be similar to 1.5 mg although this has not been specifically studied. Dulaglutide concentrations increased approximately proportional to dose from 0.75 mg to 4.5 mg. Following subcutaneous administration, the time to maximum plasma concentration (Tmax) of dulaglutide at steady-state ranges from 24 to 72 hours, with a median of 48 hours. After multiple-dose administration of 1.5 mg to steady-state, the mean peak plasma concentration (Cmax) was 114 ng/mL (range 56 to 231 ng/mL) and the total systemic exposure (AUC) of dulaglutide was 14,000 ng x hour/mL (range 6,940 to 26,000 ng x hour/mL); accumulation ratio was approximately 1.56. Steady-state plasma dulaglutide concentrations were achieved between 2 and 4 weeks following once-weekly administration. The site of subcutaneous administration (i.e., abdomen, upper arm, and thigh) had no statistically significant effect on the exposure to dulaglutide.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    In clinical studies, systemic exposure (AUC) of dulaglutide decreased by 23%, 33%, and 21% in patients with mild, moderate, and severe hepatic impairment, respectively, compared to subjects with normal hepatic function. The maximum concentration (Cmax) was decreased by a similar magnitude. These changes are not clinically relevant; however, caution should be exercised in patients with hepatic impairment.
    Name

    Description
    In clinical studies, systemic exposure (AUC) of dulaglutide was increased by 20%, 28%, 14%, and 12% in patients with mild, moderate, severe, and ESRD renal impairment, respectively, compared to subjects with normal renal function. The corresponding values for increase in Cmax were 13, 23, 20 and 11%, respectively. These changes are not clinically relevant; however, caution should be exercised when initiating or increasing doses of dulaglutide in patients with renal impairment.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  No dosage adjustments are needed; however, there is limited clinical experience in patients with mild, moderate, or severe hepatic impairment. Use with caution.
  Renal Impairment
  No dosage adjustment is needed in patients with renal impairment, including patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Monitor renal function in patients with renal impairment who report severe adverse gastrointestinal reactions. There is limited clinical experience with dulaglutide in patients with severe renal impairment or ESRD.
