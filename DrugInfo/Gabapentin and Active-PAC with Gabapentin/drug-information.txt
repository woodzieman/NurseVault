Drug Class: Gabapentin is an analog of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) that is approved for use as an adjunct therapy in the treatment of partial seizures with or without secondary generalized tonic-clonic seizures, postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), and moderate to severe restless legs syndrome (RLS).

Routes: oral, topical; Common adult doses: 3,600 mg/day PO immediate-release; 1,800 mg/day PO modified-release; 1,200 mg/day PO extended-release.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Gabapentin is contraindicated in patients who have demonstrated hypersensitivity to the drug or its ingredients.
 
In January 2008, the FDA alerted healthcare professionals of an increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior in patients receiving anticonvulsants to treat epilepsy, psychiatric disorders, or other conditions (e.g., migraine, neuropathic pain). This alert followed an initial request by the FDA in March 2005 for manufacturers of marketed anticonvulsants to provide data from existing controlled clinical trials for analysis. Prior to this request, preliminary evidence had suggested a possible link between anticonvulsant use and suicidality. The primary analysis consisted of 199 placebo-controlled clinical studies with a total of 27,863 patients in drug treatment groups and 16,029 patients in placebo groups (>= 5 years of age). There were 4 completed suicides among patients in drug treatment groups versus none in the placebo groups. Patients receiving anticonvulsants had approximately twice the risk of suicidal behavior or ideation (0.43%) as patients receiving placebo (0.24%), corresponding to an estimated 2.1 per 1000 (95% CI: 0.7—4.2) more patients in the drug treatment groups who experienced suicidal behavior or ideation. The relative risk for suicidality was higher in patients with epilepsy compared to those with other conditions; however, the absolute risk differences were similar in trials for epilepsy and psychiatric indications. Age was not a determining factor. The increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior was observed between 1 and 24 weeks after therapy initiation. However, a longer duration of therapy should not preclude the possibility of an association to the drug since most studies included in the analysis did not continue beyond 24 weeks. Data were analyzed from drugs with adequately designed clinical trials including carbamazepine, felbamate, gabapentin, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine, pregabalin, tiagabine, topiramate, valproate, and zonisamide. However, this is considered to be a class effect. All patients beginning treatment with anticonvulsants or currently receiving such treatment should be closely monitored for emerging or worsening depression or suicidal thoughts/behavior. Patients and caregivers should be informed of the increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors and should be advised to immediately report the emergence or worsening of depression, the emergence of suicidal thoughts or behavior, thoughts of self-harm, or other unusual changes in mood or behavior. Anticonvulsants should be prescribed in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management in order to reduce the risk of overdose.
 
Gabapentin should be used with caution in patients with renal impairment. Gabapentin is excreted unchanged in the urine and can accumulate with decreased renal function. Dosage adjustments are recommended (see Dosage). Elderly patients are more likely to have reduced renal function and should be treated with caution. There are no specific recommendations for dosage reduction in the elderly.
 
Gabapentin has not been evaluated for use during pregnancy in humans. Gabapentin is classified as FDA pregnancy category C. In animals, gabapentin has been fetotoxic during organogenesis at doses of 1-4 times the maximum recommended human dose on a mg/m2 basis. Delayed ossification of bones in the skull, limbs, and vertebrae were the predominant effects. An increased incidence of fetal loss was also noted. Based on the lack of protein binding and low molecular weight of the medication, placental transfer to the human fetus should be expected. A few cases of birth defects co-existing with gabapentin use in human pregnancy have been reported. In these cases, the women were also concomitantly taking other antiepileptic agents. An association of these birth defects with gabapentin is not conclusive. Gabapentin should be administered in pregnancy only if the benefit justifies the potential risks to the fetus. Physicians are advised to recommend that pregnant patients receiving gabapentin enroll in the North American Antiepileptic Drug (NAAED) Pregnancy Registry to provide information about the effects of in utero exposure to the drug. Patients must call 1-888-233-2334 to enroll in the registry.
 
Gabapentin commonly causes blurred vision, dizziness, drowsiness, and fatigue. Patients should be advised to use caution when driving or operating machinery, or performing other tasks that require mental alertness until they are aware of whether gabapentin adversely affects their mental and/or motor performance.
 
No studies or reports exist regarding the use of gabapentin during lactation. It is unknown if gabapentin is excreted in breast milk. Based on the lack of protein binding and low molecular weight of the medication, transfer of gabapentin to infants while breast-feeding should be expected. Gabapentin should only be used in women who are breast-feeding if the benefits clearly outweigh the potential risks to the infant.
 
The effectiveness of gabapentin in infants and children < 3 years of age has not been established. Gabapentin is approved for use in children aged 3—12 years; however; it has been associated with neuropsychiatric adverse events in this patient population. Pediatric patients should be monitored closely for CNS side effects (see Adverse Reactions).

    Headline
    respiratory insufficiency
    General Information
    Monitor for symptoms of respiratory depression and sedation, and consider initiating gabapentin at a low dose if using gabapentin concomitantly with another central nervous system (CNS) depressant, particularly an opioid, or in individuals with underlying respiratory insufficiency. Gabapentin is associated with serious, life-threatening, or fatal respiratory depression when coadministered with CNS depressants or in the setting of underlying respiratory impairment.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Gabapentin dosage adjustment is necessary in individuals with renal impairment or renal failure. Do not use modified-release gabapentin in individuals with CrCl less than 30 mL/minute or in individuals undergoing hemodialysis. Extended-release gabapentin is not recommended for use in individuals with restless leg syndrome on hemodialysis. Gabapentin has not been studied in pediatric populations with renal insufficiency. Gabapentin is known to be substantially excreted by the kidney.
    Headline
    substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    Carefully evaluate individuals receiving gabapentin for a history of substance abuse disorder and observe for signs of gabapentin misuse or abuse (e.g., development of tolerance, dose escalation, drug-seeking behavior). Gabapentin may be associated with risks of abuse and dependence.
    Headline
    mood disorder, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Monitor for worsening of depression, suicidal thoughts or behavior, or any unusual changes in behavior in individuals with mood disorder or suicidal ideation; antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), including gabapentin, increase the risk of suicidal thoughts or behavior in individuals taking these medications for any indication. Suicidal behavior and ideation may also occur after gabapentin discontinuation. When considering AED use, balance the risk of suicidal thoughts or behaviors with the risk of untreated illness. The relative risk for suicidal thoughts or behavior was higher in clinical trials in subjects with epilepsy than in clinical trials in subjects with psychiatric or other conditions, but the absolute risk differences were similar for the epilepsy and psychiatric indications. Epilepsy and many other illnesses for which AEDs are prescribed are themselves associated with morbidity and mortality and an increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behavior. If suicidal thoughts and behavior emerge during treatment, consider whether the emergence of these symptoms may be related to the illness being treated.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Advise individuals to avoid driving or operating machinery or participating in activities requiring coordination and concentration until they have gained sufficient experience with gabapentin to gauge whether it affects their mental, visual, and/or motor performance adversely. Gabapentin may cause dizziness, somnolence, and other symptoms and signs of central nervous system (CNS) depression.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    According to the Beers Criteria, anticonvulsants such as gabapentin are considered potentially inappropriate medications in geriatric adults with a history of falls or fractures and should be avoided in this population, except for treating seizure and mood disorders, since anticonvulsants can produce ataxia, impaired psychomotor function, syncope, and additional falls. If gabapentin must be used, consider reducing the use of other CNS-active medications that increase the risk of falls and fractures and implement strategies to reduce fall risk.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on the use of gabapentin during pregnancy do not indicate an increased risk of major birth defects or miscarriage. A large pooled Nordic analysis of approximately 1,500 pregnancies exposed to gabapentin in the first trimester found no increased prevalence of major congenital malformations compared with pregnancies unexposed to antiepileptic drugs (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR] 1; 95% CI, 0.8 to 1.24). When compared with lamotrigine monotherapy, gabapentin exposure showed a modestly higher prevalence of major congenital malformations (aPR 1.29; 95% CI, 1 to 1.67). In another observational study of US Medicaid data, no significant increase in the risk of major congenital malformations was seen with first trimester exposure to gabapentin compared to those with unexposed pregnancies (adjusted relative risk 1.07; 95% CI, 0.94 to 1.21). In a prospective cohort study, rates of major malformations among neonates were similar between 223 pregnancies with gabapentin exposure and 223 unexposed pregnancies (4.1% exposed vs. 2.5% unexposed, p = 0.555). Major malformations included 2 ventricular septal defects, anencephaly, macrocephaly, micro-retrognathism, cutis marmorata, pyloric stenosis, bilateral varus clubfoot, and cryptorchidism. In all cases of major malformations, pregnant individuals received concomitant treatment with other medications during pregnancy; therefore, a causal relationship to gabapentin cannot be established. No major malformations occurred in neonates born to pregnant individuals exposed to gabapentin monotherapy during pregnancy (n = 36). There were higher rates of preterm births (10.5% vs. 3.9%, p = 0.019), low birth weight (less than 2,500 g) (10.5% vs. 4.4%, p = 0.033), and admission to neonatal intensive care or special care nursery (38% vs. 2.9%, p less than 0.001) among neonates with gabapentin exposure compared to unexposed neonates. Cases of possible poor neonatal adaptation syndrome occurred in 2 neonates with gabapentin exposure late in pregnancy, compared to no cases among unexposed infants; these 2 neonates were also exposed to other psychotropic medications. In a cohort of 39 pregnant individuals who were exposed to gabapentin during their first trimester (97%) and throughout gestation (81.8%), malformations occurred in 3 of 44 live births. Hypospadia was reported in a neonate exposed to gabapentin and valproate; a missing kidney occurred in a neonate exposed to gabapentin and phenobarbital, and a minor malformation of the left external ear canal and 2 small skin tags in the jaw occurred in a neonate exposed to gabapentin and lamotrigine. Since exposure to multiple antiepileptic drugs occurred during these pregnancies, a causal relationship to gabapentin cannot be established. No malformations occurred in 11 neonates exposed to gabapentin monotherapy during pregnancy. Gabapentin actively crosses the placenta. Among 6 neonates born to pregnant individuals who were taking gabapentin (doses ranging from 900 to 3,200 mg/day), umbilical-to-maternal gabapentin plasma concentration ratios ranged from 1.3 to 2.11 (mean, 1.74) at delivery. Gabapentin concentrations in the neonates declined to an average of 27% (range, 12% to 36%) of cord plasma concentrations at 24 hours postpartum. A single infant was born premature at 33 weeks; however, all deliveries were uneventful, and all neonates were born in healthy condition. In nonclinical studies in mice, rats, and rabbits, gabapentin exposure resulted in developmental toxicity (increased fetal skeletal and visceral abnormalities, increased embryo-fetal mortality) when administered to pregnant animals at doses as low as or similar to doses used clinically. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant patients exposed to gabapentin; information about the registry can be obtained at www.aedpregnancyregistry.org or by calling 1-888-233-2334.

Pregnancy And Lactation
  Pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on the use of gabapentin during pregnancy do not indicate an increased risk of major birth defects or miscarriage. A large pooled Nordic analysis of approximately 1,500 pregnancies exposed to gabapentin in the first trimester found no increased prevalence of major congenital malformations compared with pregnancies unexposed to antiepileptic drugs (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR] 1; 95% CI, 0.8 to 1.24). When compared with lamotrigine monotherapy, gabapentin exposure showed a modestly higher prevalence of major congenital malformations (aPR 1.29; 95% CI, 1 to 1.67). In another observational study of US Medicaid data, no significant increase in the risk of major congenital malformations was seen with first trimester exposure to gabapentin compared to those with unexposed pregnancies (adjusted relative risk 1.07; 95% CI, 0.94 to 1.21). In a prospective cohort study, rates of major malformations among neonates were similar between 223 pregnancies with gabapentin exposure and 223 unexposed pregnancies (4.1% exposed vs. 2.5% unexposed, p = 0.555). Major malformations included 2 ventricular septal defects, anencephaly, macrocephaly, micro-retrognathism, cutis marmorata, pyloric stenosis, bilateral varus clubfoot, and cryptorchidism. In all cases of major malformations, pregnant individuals received concomitant treatment with other medications during pregnancy; therefore, a causal relationship to gabapentin cannot be established. No major malformations occurred in neonates born to pregnant individuals exposed to gabapentin monotherapy during pregnancy (n = 36). There were higher rates of preterm births (10.5% vs. 3.9%, p = 0.019), low birth weight (less than 2,500 g) (10.5% vs. 4.4%, p = 0.033), and admission to neonatal intensive care or special care nursery (38% vs. 2.9%, p less than 0.001) among neonates with gabapentin exposure compared to unexposed neonates. Cases of possible poor neonatal adaptation syndrome occurred in 2 neonates with gabapentin exposure late in pregnancy, compared to no cases among unexposed infants; these 2 neonates were also exposed to other psychotropic medications. In a cohort of 39 pregnant individuals who were exposed to gabapentin during their first trimester (97%) and throughout gestation (81.8%), malformations occurred in 3 of 44 live births. Hypospadia was reported in a neonate exposed to gabapentin and valproate; a missing kidney occurred in a neonate exposed to gabapentin and phenobarbital, and a minor malformation of the left external ear canal and 2 small skin tags in the jaw occurred in a neonate exposed to gabapentin and lamotrigine. Since exposure to multiple antiepileptic drugs occurred during these pregnancies, a causal relationship to gabapentin cannot be established. No malformations occurred in 11 neonates exposed to gabapentin monotherapy during pregnancy. Gabapentin actively crosses the placenta. Among 6 neonates born to pregnant individuals who were taking gabapentin (doses ranging from 900 to 3,200 mg/day), umbilical-to-maternal gabapentin plasma concentration ratios ranged from 1.3 to 2.11 (mean, 1.74) at delivery. Gabapentin concentrations in the neonates declined to an average of 27% (range, 12% to 36%) of cord plasma concentrations at 24 hours postpartum. A single infant was born premature at 33 weeks; however, all deliveries were uneventful, and all neonates were born in healthy condition. In nonclinical studies in mice, rats, and rabbits, gabapentin exposure resulted in developmental toxicity (increased fetal skeletal and visceral abnormalities, increased embryo-fetal mortality) when administered to pregnant animals at doses as low as or similar to doses used clinically. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant patients exposed to gabapentin; information about the registry can be obtained at www.aedpregnancyregistry.org or by calling 1-888-233-2334.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Interpretation of adverse events during gabapentin therapy requires consideration of several factors. Some adverse reactions occurred during trials in which gabapentin or placebo was added to existing anticonvulsant therapy. In other trials no placebo comparison was made. No apparent gender difference was observed in the incidence of adverse effects, but there was a slight age-related increase in frequency. Reporting terminology also varied. Adverse events described below are those most frequently reported.
 
For the body as a whole, the most frequently reported adverse events following gabapentin therapy were: fatigue (11%), weight gain (2.9%), back pain (1.8%), and peripheral edema (1.7%). Weight gain has been reported to be an average of 16 pounds. In non-placebo controlled trials asthenia, malaise and facial edema were most frequently reported.
 
Anticonvulsants are thought to carry an increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior. An analysis by the FDA of previously gathered drug data showed that patients receiving anticonvulsants had approximately twice the risk of suicidal behavior or ideation (0.43%) as patients receiving placebo (0.24%). The relative risk for suicidality was higher in patients with epilepsy compared to those with other conditions. Age was not a determining factor. The increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior occurred between 1 and 24 weeks after therapy initiation. However, a longer duration of therapy should not preclude the possibility of an association to the drug since most studies included in the analysis did not continue beyond 24 weeks. Suicide has occurred rarely ( 2%) adverse events reported in children 3—12 years of age and occurring at a greater frequency than placebo were: viral infection, fever, nausea/vomiting, somnolence (drowsiness), hostility, weight gain, fatigue, emotional lability, dizziness, hyperkinesia, and upper respiratory infection. Hepatitis has been reported rarely in pediatric clinical trials.
 
Adverse events affecting the digestive system during gabapentin therapy and occurring at a higher rate than with placebo were: dyspepsia (2.2%), xerostomia (1.7%), constipation (1.5%), dental abnormalities (1.5%), and appetite stimulation (1.1%). Dental abnormalities included glossitis, gingivitis, gingival hemorrhage, and tooth discoloration. There was no difference in the reporting of nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea in patients treated with either gabapentin or placebo. Other gastrointestinal effects reported frequently in non-placebo controlled trials were anorexia, and flatulence.
 
In add-on trials gabapentin produced a greater reduction in WBC than did placebo. However, the reported incidence of leukopenia was about 1.1%. The hematological effect most often reported in non-placebo controlled trials was purpura (bruising from physical trauma). Less frequently thrombocytopenia, anemia and lymphadenopathy have been reported.
 
In add-on trials patients taking gabapentin had a higher incidence of respiratory effects than those taking placebo, although these may not be statistically significant. Comparing gabapentin to placebo, the incidence of respiratory effects was as follows: rhinitis 4.1% vs 3.7%, pharyngitis 2.8% vs 1.6%, and cough 1.8% vs 1.4%. In the non-placebo controlled trials pneumonia was the most frequently reported respiratory effect.
 
Although gabapentin was reported to produce myalgia, the incidence was very little different from that reported for placebo. In non-placebo controlled trials, arthralgia was the most frequently reported adverse event.
 
In add-on placebo-controlled trials gabapentin affected vision in a number of patients. The most frequent effects were diplopia (5.9%), and amblyopia (4.2%). The latter was often described as blurred vision. Various other optical effects such as conjunctivitis, xerophthalmia, cataract, ocular pain and photophobia, were less frequently (< 1%) reported in non-placebo controlled trials.
 
Other adverse events reported with gabapentin therapy occurring at an incidence greater than 1% include, hypertension, skin abrasion, pruritus, and impotence (erectile dysfunction).
 
Gabapentin may potentially be associated with teratogenesis, but controlled human data are lacking. Gabapentin is classified as FDA pregnancy category C. In animals, gabapentin has been fetotoxic during organogenesis at doses of 1—4 times the maximum recommended human dose on a mg/m2 basis. Delayed ossification of bones in the skull, limbs, and vertebrae were the predominant effects. An increased incidence of fetal loss was also noted. Based on the lack of protein binding and low molecular weight of the medication, placental transfer to the human fetus should be expected. A few cases of birth defects co-existing with gabapentin use in human pregnancy have been reported. In these cases, the women were also concomitantly taking other antiepileptic agents. An association of these birth defects with gabapentin is not conclusive.
 
It is unknown whether gabapentin causes tumors in humans. A statistically significant increase in the incidence of pancreatic acinar cell adenomas and carcinomas was found in male rats, but not female rats, who received gabapentin at 10 times the maximal human dose equivalent over the course of 2 years. The significance of these carcinogenic findings is unknown, and is under further investigation per the manufacturer. In vitro testing has shown not mutagenic or clastogenic changes.

Description
  Text
  Gabapentin is an analog of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) that is approved for use as an adjunct therapy in the treatment of partial seizures with or without secondary generalized tonic-clonic seizures, postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), and moderate to severe restless legs syndrome (RLS). The American Association of Sleep Medicine strongly recommends the use of either gabapentin or gabapentin enacarbil for treatment of restless legs syndrome in adults due to clinical data showing significant improvements in disease severity and sleep quality. Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) such as gabapentin increase the risk of suicidal ideation and behavior; monitor all individuals receiving gabapentin for emerging or worsening suicidal thoughts/behavior or depression. Gabapentin is associated with respiratory depression and other serious reactions, including Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS). Gabapentin was initially approved by the FDA in 1993.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The exact mechanism by which gabapentin exerts its antiepileptic and analgesic effects is not known. Pharmacological effects may be mediated by the alpha-2-delta proteins at neuronal synapses, as gabapentin binds with high affinity to the alpha-2-delta subunit of voltage-activated calcium channels. Gabapentin may antagonize thrombospondin binding to the alpha-2-delta-1 receptor and block new excitatory synapse formation.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Gabapentin is rapidly absorbed within the proximal small bowel, in part by the saturable L-amino acid transport system. Bioavailability declines with higher doses. The absolute bioavailability of immediate-release gabapentin is 60% for a 300 mg dose and 35% for a 1,600 mg dose; the bioavailability values of the modified-release tablet formulation (Gralise) are not stated. One pharmacokinetic study assessed the bioavailability of immediate-release gabapentin following administration of the contents of opened capsules mixed with water, fruit juice, applesauce, or pudding. Food does not affect the absorption of immediate-release gabapentin, even when the capsules are opened for ease of administration. The mean bioavailability of gabapentin enacarbil (Horizant) extended-release tablets in the fed state is about 75%. Steady-state of Horizant extended-release tablets is reached in 2 days with daily administration. Modified-release and extended-release gabapentin should be taken with food; the rate and extent absorption are increased when administered with a fatty meal. Unlike immediate-release gabapentin, the Gralise modified-release tablets swell in gastric fluid, gradually releasing the medication and thus slowing drug absorption. The use of 1,800 mg of modified-release gabapentin (Gralise), administered once daily, as compared to 1,800 mg of immediate-release gabapentin, administered as 600 mg 3 times daily, resulted in higher Cmax (9.585 +/- 2.326 ng/mL vs 8.536 +/- 1.715 ng/mL) and longer median time to achieve maximal plasma concentrations (8 [range: 3 to 12] hours vs. 2 [range: 1 to 5] hours).
 
NOTE: Neither Gralise modified-release tablets or Horizant extended-release tablets are interchangeable with other gabapentin formulations or each other because of differing pharmacokinetic profiles.

  Text
  Gabapentin is administered orally. Plasma concentrations greater than 2 mcg/mL are considered therapeutic; however, it is not necessary to monitor gabapentin plasma concentrations to optimize therapy. Protein binding of gabapentin is less than 3%. Because of its high degree of lipid solubility, gabapentin readily distributes into the CNS. Gabapentin is not metabolized and is excreted intact in the urine. Gabapentin renal excretion is believed to involve a component of active secretion via an organic cation transporter (OCT2) present in the kidney. The elimination half-life in subjects with normal renal function administered 1,200 to 3,000 mg/day of immediate-release gabapentin ranges from 5 to 7 hours.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: None
Gabapentin is not a substrate, inhibitor, or inducer of the major CYP450 enzymes. Gabapentin is neither a substrate nor an inhibitor of P-glycoprotein in vitro. No clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interactions are expected between gabapentin and other substrates of organic cation transporter type 2 (OCT2) and/or monocarboxylate transporter type 1 (MCT-1).

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  No dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  Immediate-release formulations (adults, children and adolescents 12 to 17 years):
NOTE: The use of immediate-release gabapentin in children younger than 12 years of age with compromised renal function has not been studied.
CrCl 60 mL/minute or more: No dose adjustment needed.
CrCl 30 to 59 mL/minute: 400 to 1,400 mg/day PO daily in 2 evenly divided doses.
CrCl 15 to 29 mL/minute: 200 to 700 mg/day PO once daily.
CrCl = 15 mL/minute: 100 to 300 mg/day PO once daily given as 100, 125, 150, 200, or 300 mg.
CrCl less than 15 mL/minute: Reduce daily dose in proportion to CrCl (e.g., an individual with CrCl = 7.5 mL/minute should receive one-half the dose that an individual with CrCl of 15 mL/minute receives).
 
Modified-release tablets (Gralise, adults):
CrCl 60 mL/minute or more: No dose adjustment needed.
CrCl 30 to 59 mL/minute: 600 to 1,800 mg/day PO daily based on tolerability and desired clinical benefit.
CrCl less than 30 mL/minute: Use not recommended.
 
Extended-release tablets (Horizant, adults):
CrCl 60 mL/minute or more: No dose adjustment needed. Reduce the dose to 600 mg PO once daily for 1 week before discontinuation.
CrCl 30 to 59 mL/minute: For RLS, start at 300 mg/day PO and increase to 600 mg/day PO as needed. For PHN, start at 300 mg PO in the morning for 3 days, then increase to 300 mg PO twice daily. Increase to 600 mg PO twice daily as needed. Reduce the maintenance dose to once daily in the morning for 1 week before discontinuation.
CrCl 15 to 29 mL/minute: For RLS, 300 mg/day PO. For PHN, 300 mg PO on day 1 and day 3, followed by a maintenance dose of 300 mg PO once daily in the morning. Increase dose to 300 mg PO twice daily as needed. Reduce the dose to 300 mg PO once daily in the morning for 1 week before discontinuation.
CrCl less than 15 mL/minute: For RLS and PHN, 300 mg PO every other day. For PHN, the dose can be increased to 300 mg PO once daily in the morning if needed. No dose taper is required prior to discontinuation.
