Drug Class: Sapropterin is indicated in conjunction with a phenylalanine-restricted diet to reduce blood phenylalanine concentrations in patients with hyperphenylalaninemia due to tetrahydrobiopterin- (BH4-) responsive phenylketonuria (PKU).

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 20 mg/kg/day.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): cough, headache, nasal congestion, pharyngitis, rhinitis, rhinorrhea, bleeding, agitation, dizziness, hyperactivity, irritability, seizures, arthralgia, gi bleeding, myocardial infarction, peripheral edema, polyuria

In an Overdose: One case occurred in a patient who received an accidental overdose of the drug.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: In a 10-year post-approval safety surveillance program in Japan of a different formulation of sapropterin, 3 of 30 non-PKU patients (27 with neurological disease) developed seizures or an exacerbation of seizures. Fever and infection may cause blood phenylalanine (Phe) concentrations to increase.

Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General dosing information:
Not all patients respond to treatment with sapropterin. In 2 clinical trials, 56—75% of pediatric PKU patients responded to treatment with 20 mg/kg/day. In 1 clinical trial, 20% of adult and pediatric PKU patients responded to treatment with 10 mg/kg/day. Response can only be determined by a therapeutic trial of sapropterin.
All patients treated with sapropterin should continue to follow a Phe-restricted diet. In order to make an accurate assessment of responsiveness, patients should not modify their existing dietary Phe intake during the sapropterin evaluation period. If the patient is a responder and the dose of sapropterin has been established, continue active management of dietary Phe intake using medical foods and natural sources of proteins to ensure blood Phe control and adequate nutritional balance.

        Heading
        Adults, Adolescents, and Children 7 years and older
        Dosage Description
        Initially, 10 to 20 mg/kg (actual body weight) PO once daily. Measure blood phenylalanine (Phe) concentrations at baseline, after 1 week of treatment, and periodically for 1 month. If an initial dose of 10 mg/kg is used and blood Phe concentrations do not decrease from baseline after 1 month of therapy, the dose may be increased to 20 mg/kg PO once daily. Not all patients will respond to therapy; discontinue sapropterin if blood Phe concentrations do not decrease after 1 month of treatment at 20 mg/kg/day. Once responsiveness is established, adjust the dose within the range of 5 to 20 mg/kg PO once daily to maintain target blood Phe concentrations.

        Dosage Description
        Initially, 10 mg/kg (actual body weight) PO once daily. Check blood phenylalanine (Phe) concentration at baseline, after 1 week, and periodically for 1 month. Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  In a 10-year post-approval safety surveillance program in Japan of a different formulation of sapropterin, 3 of 30 non-PKU patients (27 with neurological disease) developed seizures or an exacerbation of seizures.
 
Fever and infection may cause blood phenylalanine (Phe) concentrations to increase. Patients with a fever or an infection may need an adjustment in their sapropterin dose or dietary management to keep blood Phe concentrations within the desired range.
 
Sapropterin tablets contain mannitol. Patients with a mannitol hypersensitivity should not take sapropterin.
 
Blood phenylalanine (Phe) concentrations should be closely monitored in patients with anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa. Adequate dietary intake of Phe is required to prevent blood Phe concentrations from being too low. Prolonged blood Phe concentrations that are too low may result in catabolism and protein breakdown. Sapropterin should be used with caution in these patients.
 
The safety and efficacy of sapropterin have not been established in children below the age of 4. Single-doses of endogenous tetrahydrobiopterin have been administered to neonates as young as 1 day old.
 
Clinical studies of sapropterin did not include patients over the age of 65. It is not known whether elderly patients respond differently than younger patients to sapropterin.
 
Patients with renal impairment have not been evaluated in clinical trials. Renally impaired patients should be closely monitored when receiving sapropterin.
 
Sapropterin has not been evaluated in patients with hepatic disease. In clinical trials, patients with elevations in alanine transaminase > 5 times the upper-limit of normal were excluded. Patients with hepatic disease should be closely monitored when receiving sapropterin, as hepatic damage has been associated with impaired phenylalanine (Phe) metabolism.
 
It is not known if sapropterin is excreted into breast milk. The manufacturer states that a decision should be made whether to discontinue breast-feeding or to discontinue the drug, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother. However, the benefits to the infant of continued breast-feeding should also be considered, along with consideration that blood phenylalanine concentrations can be controlled by non-drug methods (i.e., dietary restrictions).
 
The effects of sapropterin on labor and delivery in pregnant women are not known.
 
Sapropterin is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C and should be used during pregnancy only when the benefits of treatment outweigh the risks. No adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women exist. The possibility of pregnancy and contraceptive considerations should be discussed with the patient. Women who are exposed to sapropterin during pregnancy are encouraged to enroll in the Kuvan patient registry (see Kuvan web site for more information). In order to prevent embryofetopathy in children of mothers with hyperphenylalaninemia (HPA), blood phenylalanine (Phe) concentrations should be maintained between 60—250 micromol/L. In a study of 468 pregnancies and 331 live-births in PKU-affected women, uncontrolled Phe concentrations above 600 micromol/L were associated with very high rates of neurological, cardiac, facial dysmorphism, and growth anomalies. Good dietary control of Phe concentrations during pregnancy is essential to reducing the incidence of Phe-induced teratogenic effects. Sapropterin may help improve pregnancy outcomes among women with PKU. A woman with the PAH mutation of R408W/F39L and good response to tetrahydrobiopterin (it was not clarified if endogenous tetrahydrobiopterin or sapropterin was used) delivered a healthy, term baby; she ingested tetrahydrobiopterin and a Phe-restricted product throughout her pregnancy. Her tetrahydrobiopterin dose was 20 mg PO twice daily during the first trimester, 40 mg twice daily during the second trimester, and 50 mg twice daily during the third trimester. Her blood Phe concentration was 796 micromol/L at conception and was not higher than 456 micromol/L throughout gestation; the concentration was measured every 1—4 weeks.

    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Human data from pregnancy safety studies, pharmacovigilance, and published case reports do not indicate an increased risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse pregnancy outcomes with sapropterin use during pregnancy. Data from pregnancy sub-registries in the Phenylketonuria Developmental Outcomes and Safety (PKUDOS) Registry and the Kuvan Adult Maternal Pediatric European Registry (KAMPER) include 72 live births from 79 pregnancies in individuals with phenylketonuria (PKU) exposed to sapropterin. Three birth defects were reported (microcephaly, cleft palate, and tongue tie). The 2 major birth defects (microcephaly and cleft palate) occurred in pregnancies with phenylalanine (Phe) levels greater than 360 micromol/L. Animal data support these findings. In rats, oral sapropterin dihydrochloride doses up to 3 times the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) administered during organogenesis produced no embryo-fetal effects. In rabbits, oral doses up to 10 times the MRHD during organogenesis were associated with a non-statistically significant increase in holoprosencephaly. Uncontrolled maternal phenylalanine concentrations before and during pregnancy increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes and fetal abnormalities. To reduce the risk of hyperphenylalaninemia-related fetal effects, blood phenylalanine concentrations should be maintained between 120 and 360 micromol/L during pregnancy and for 3 months prior to conception. Data from the Maternal Phenylketonuria Collaborative Study (468 pregnancies, 331 live births) show that Phe levels above 600 micromol/L are associated with a high incidence of neurological, cardiac, facial, and growth abnormalities. Maintaining blood phenylalanine control during pregnancy is essential to reduce Phe-induced teratogenic effects. A pregnancy exposure registry monitors outcomes in individuals exposed to sapropterin during pregnancy. For more information, call 1-800-983-4587.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use sapropterin with caution during breast-feeding. There are insufficient data to assess the presence of sapropterin in human milk and no data on the effects on milk production. In postmarketing pregnancy registries, 13 infants were exposed to sapropterin through breast-feeding; no lactation-related safety concerns were reported in these infants. Sapropterin is present in the milk of lactating rats after intravenous administration, but not after oral administration. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition.

Interactions
Avanafil: (Moderate) Sapropterin acts as a cofactor in the synthesis of nitric oxide and may cause vasorelaxation. Caution should be exercised when administering sapropterin in combination with drugs that affect nitric oxide-mediated vasorelaxation such as phosphodiesterase inhibitors. When given together these agents may produce an additive reduction in blood pressure. The combination of sapropterin and a phosphodiesterase (PDE5) inhibitor did not significantly reduce blood pressure when administered concomitantly in animal studies. The additive effect of these agents has not been studied in humans.
Carbidopa; Levodopa: (Moderate) Monitor patients for neurologic adverse effects, such as seizures, over-stimulation, and irritability, during concomitant use of sapropterin and levodopa. Folate analogs: (Moderate) Significant increases in serum phenylalanine concentrations have been noted after methotrexate infusions of 58 g/m2 to 46 patients with an unknown PKU status. Increased concentrations occurred at the end of the infusion in 95% of methotrexate cycles, but large inter-individual variations in the concentrations existed. Drugs that inhibit folate metabolism should be used with caution in patients taking sapropterin.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  NOTE: Limited placebo-controlled data are available to assess potential adverse reactions of sapropterin. Phase III data reflect the assessment of 41 patients who received sapropterin for up to 6 weeks. Adverse reactions possibly related to sapropterin may be reported to the manufacturer by calling 1—866—906—6100.In a 10-year post-approval safety surveillance program in Japan of a different formulation of sapropterin, seizures or exacerbations of seizures occurred in 3 of 30 non-PKU patients. Of these 30 patients, 27 had underlying neurologic disorders. In the same surveillance program, an increase in gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) was observed in 2 of 30 non-PKU patients.In clinical trials, 24 of 579 (4%) patients receiving sapropterin developed mild to moderate neutropenia. It is unclear to what extent this effect is due to sapropterin.Respiratory adverse effects possibly related to sapropterin treatment include rhinorrhea (11%), pharyngitis (10%), and nasal congestion (4%). These events occurred more frequently than placebo across 133 patients in placebo-controlled studies.Tetrahydrobiopterin is a cofactor in the conversion of tyrosine to L-DOPA and in the conversion of tryptophan to serotonin; a similar reaction could be expected in patients receiving sapropterin. Adverse reactions are possible as a result of potential increases in these neurotransmitters caused by an increased availability of BH4. Examples of these adverse effects, which occurred commonly (>= 4%) in clinical trials of 579 patients are headache, dizziness, diarrhea, abdominal pain and nausea/vomiting.
  Adverse Reactions
    Headline
    General Information
    General Information
    A thorough QTc study was performed in 56 healthy adults. In a randomized, placebo and active controlled crossover study of a single supra-therapeutic (100 mg/kg) dose of sapropterin or a single therapeutic dose (20 mg/kg) of sapropterin, a dose-dependent shortening of the QT interval was demonstrated. The maximum placebo-subtracted mean change from baseline of the QTc interval was -3.69 and -8.32 ms (lower bound of 90% CI: -5.3 and -10.6 mg) at 20 and 100 mg/kg, respectively.
    Headline
    cough, headache, nasal congestion, pharyngitis, rhinitis, rhinorrhea
    General Information
    In clinical studies, sapropterin was administered to 579 patients with phenylketonuria in doses ranging from 5 to 20 mg/kg/day for lengths of treatment ranging from 1 to 164 weeks in patients ranging from 4 to 50 years of age. Among the most common adverse events reported in this trial and similar placebo-controlled trials were headache (15% vs. 14%), rhinorrhea (11% vs. 0%), pharyngolaryngeal pain (10% vs. 2%), cough (7% vs. 5%), and nasal congestion (4% vs. 0%). Rhinitis was infrequently reported, with 2 patients (7.4%) reporting this side effect in a study of 27 pediatric patients 0 to 4 years (n = 27). Post-marketing, oropharyngeal pain and pharyngitis were also reported.

    Headline
    anaphylactoid reactions, rash (unspecified)
    General Information
    Severe hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylactoid reactions and rash (unspecified) have occurred during sapropterin administration. Signs of anaphylaxis include wheezing, dyspnea, coughing, hypotension, flushing, nausea, and rash. Discontinue sapropterin immediately and initiate appropriate medical treatment if anaphylaxis is suspected. Continue dietary Phe restrictions in patients who experience anaphylaxis. Post-marketing, most reported hypersensitivity reactions occurred within several days of initiating treatment.

Description
  Text
  Sapropterin is indicated in conjunction with a phenylalanine-restricted diet to reduce blood phenylalanine concentrations in patients with hyperphenylalaninemia due to tetrahydrobiopterin- (BH4-) responsive phenylketonuria (PKU). Sapropterin is a synthetic form, 6R-BH4, of tetrahydrobiopterin and was the first FDA-approved drug to treat PKU. Phenylketonuria is a disease characterized by an increase in blood phenylalanine (Phe) concentrations. Increased blood Phe concentrations are caused by a genetic defect in the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH). PAH converts phenylalanine to tyrosine in the liver. Tetrahydrobiopterin acts as a cofactor to PAH in that reaction. It has been noted that PAH remains active in some patients with PKU. A trial of sapropterin is necessary to indicate patients that will respond to treatment. Even in patients who respond, the use of sapropterin does not eliminate the need for ongoing dietary management or for blood Phe concentration monitoring. Elevated blood Phe concentrations can cause severe neurological damage. Conversely, prolonged blood Phe concentrations that are too low may lead to catabolism and protein breakdown. A patient and physician support program is available; more information about this program is available at 1-877-695-8826.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: Sapropterin is a biologically active synthetic form of the naturally occurring enzyme cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4). Endogenous BH4 is a cofactor in multiple reactions including serving as a catalyst to phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) in the hydroxylation of phenylalanine (Phe) to tyrosine. Patients with phenylketonuria (PKU) have genetic mutations that result in deficient production or activity of PAH, which causes an increase in blood Phe concentrations. Over 500 mutations have been identified with considerable variability in the amount of residual PAH activity. Mutation severity is graded on the degree of PAH impairment. Severity of mutation does not accurately predict for responsiveness to BH4, only a trial of BH4 or sapropterin can determine responsiveness. Patients with PKU do not have an inherent deficiency in BH4 production or regeneration; however, increasing available BH4 has been shown to decrease blood Phe concentrations. The precise mechanism for this is unknown. One possible mechanism is that mutant PAH may require additional BH4 cofactor. Increasing available BH4 allows additional binding to PAH. Another possible mechanism is that BH4 exhibits a protective effect on mutant PAH. BH4 may increase the half-life of mutant PAH by preventing misfolding, which is a common PAH mutation.BH4 is also a cofactor in the hydroxylation of tyrosine to L-DOPA and of tryptophan to serotonin. Adverse reactions from BH4 supplementation may be a result of these pathways (see Adverse Reactions). Increased BH4 concentrations may lead to an increase in L-DOPA and serotonin.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    To increase absorption, sapropterin should be administered with food. Administration after a high-fat/high-calorie meal caused mean increases of 84% in Cmax and 87% in AUC; however, extensive variability was noted across administration modes and meal conditions.
 
In a study of 88 patients with PKU not on phenylalanine (Phe)-restricted diets previously determined to have tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4)-responsive disease, a mean decrease in blood Phe concentration from baseline of 236 micromol/L was seen at 6 weeks with sapropterin 10 mg/kg/day compared to a 3 micromol/L increase over the same time period with placebo. Responders in this trial showed significantly decreased blood Phe concentrations at the initial assessment after 1 week, and this benefit persisted throughout the 6 week trial. In patients who respond to sapropterin, blood Phe concentrations generally decrease within 24 hours of a single dose of sapropterin. Maximum effect may take up to 4 weeks. A trial of 80 patients with a known response to sapropterin was conducted in which patients were titrated in two-week intervals through three separate dose levels. Blood Phe concentrations measured after each two-week course reflected a more pronounced response with increased dosage. Decreases in blood Phe concentrations from baseline at 5, 10, and 20 mg/kg/day were 100, 204, and 263 micromol/L, respectively.

Affected Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) isoenzymes and drug transporters: BCRP
In healthy subjects, administration of a single dose of sapropterin 20 mg/kg had no effect on the pharmacokinetics of a single dose of digoxin (P-gp substrate) administered concomitantly. Based on in vitro studies, sapropterin does not inhibit CYP 1A2, 2B6, 2C8, 2C9, 2C19, 2D6, or 3A4/5, nor induce CYP 1A2, 2B6, or 3A4/5. In vitro sapropterin did not inhibit OAT1, OAT3, OCT2, MATE1, and MATE2-K transporters. The potential to inhibit OATP1B1 and OATP1B3 has not been adequately studied. In vitro, sapropterin inhibits breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), but the potential for a clinically significant increase in systemic exposure of BCRP substrates by sapropterin appears to be low.
