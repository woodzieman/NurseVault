    General Information
    No adequate and well-controlled studies have been conducted to evaluate use of botulism equine heptavalent antitoxin during pregnancy, and it is unknown if the antitoxin causes fetal harm or affects the reproductive system. Seventeen cases of pregnant women being treated with the antitoxin have been reported; no antitoxin-related complications were noted in the mothers or their fetuses.     fever, headache, nausea, pruritus, urticaria
    General Information
    During 2 safety studies conducted in healthy subjects (n = 66), no serious adverse reactions were reported following intravenous administration of the 7 antitoxins contained in botulism equine heptavalent antitoxin. The most frequently reported adverse events were headache (9%), pruritus (5%), nausea (5%), urticaria (5%), fever (less than 4%), and throat discomfort (less than 4%). Due to acute allergic reactions, 2 subjects prematurely discontinued the infusion and treatment. In addition to these studies, the CDC has actively collected safety data from physicians who have prescribed botulism equine heptavalent antitoxin. Of the 228 antitoxin recipients for whom there are data, fever was reported by 3.9% and nausea by 0.9%.

    General Information
    Botulism equine heptavalent antitoxin is a derivative of equine plasma. As with other products derived from or purified with blood components, the remote possibility of transmitting disease such as viral infection exists. To reduce this risk, the manufacturing process includes steps to inactivate and remove viruses, a solvent/detergent treatment and virus filtration; however, infectious agents may still be present in this product. All infections thought by a physician to have been possibly transmitted by the heptavalent antitoxin should be reported to the manufacturer. Health care providers are advised to discuss the risks and benefits of this product with the patient or legal guardian.

Description
  Text
  Botulism equine heptavalent antitoxin is a mixture of antibody fragments derived from horse plasma, which is used to provide passive immunity following documented or suspected exposure to botulinum neurotoxin serotypes A, B, C, D, E, F, or G. The antitoxin is the first product indicated for neutralization of all 7 known botulinum neurotoxins. Botulism equine heptavalent antitoxin is available in 20 mL and 50 mL vials; however regardless of the volume, each vial contains a minimum antitoxin potency of more than 4,500 units serotype A, more than 3,300 units serotype B, more than 3,000 units serotype C, more than 600 units serotype D, more than 5,100 units serotype E, more than 3,000 units serotype F, and more than 600 units serotype G. The amount of antitoxin required to counteract botulinum neurotoxin intoxication has not been established. Health care providers are advised to immediately contact their state public health department for consultation on any suspected non-infant botulism case. If there is no answer, contact the Center for Diseases Control and Prevention (CDC) at 770-488-7100. For infant botulism, the Infant Botulism Treatment and Prevention Program at the California Department of Public Health provides consultation and can be reached at 510-231-7600. If clinical consultation with state public health department and CDC supports botulism, request immediate delivery of the antitoxin and begin treatment as soon as it is available; DO NOT wait for laboratory confirmation.
 
Botulism is an acute neurological condition associated with symmetrical descending weakness that results from exposure to neurotoxins produced by Clostridium botulinum and, rarely, C. baratii or C. butyricum; these organisms are anaerobic, spore-forming, gram-positive bacilli. There are 7 known botulinum neurotoxins (serotypes A-G), with serotypes A, B, E, and F producing disease in humans. Botulinum neurotoxin exposure occurs in 1 of 5 ways: foodborne; wound; inhalational; infant botulism; and adult botulism of unknown etiology. During efficacy studies, guinea pigs and Rhesus macaques were exposed to botulinum neurotoxins via the intramuscular and intravenous routes, respectively. The animals were then administered either a placebo control or the heptavalent antitoxin; animals that received the antitoxin experienced improved survival rates (p < 0.0001). Although shown to be effective, treatment outcomes depend largely on the time from symptom onset to antitoxin administration.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Botulism equine heptavalent antitoxin provides passive immunity against botulinum neurotoxin serotypes A, B, C, D, E, F, G by blocking their activity at the peripheral neuromuscular junction and cholinergic autonomic sites. Antibody fragments contained within the antitoxin bind to circulating free botulinum neurotoxins, thereby preventing their neuronal uptake and resulting neurotoxicity. If antibodies against the neurotoxins are not available, the neurotoxins bind to presynaptic receptors and enter the neuron by receptor-mediated endocytosis. While inside the cell, the neurotoxins cleave peptide bonds of proteins that mediate the release of acetylcholine (ACh) from the synaptic vesicle or the presynaptic membrane. Preventing the release of ACh produces motor system paralysis and autonomic dysfunction. Early suspicion of botulism and timely administration of the heptavalent antitoxin will minimize nerve damage and severity of disease, but will not reverse existing paralysis. Depending on the time between exposure and administration, heptavalent antitoxin can reduce the time course and severity of symptoms or prevent disease manifestation.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Intravenous Route
    Description
    Pharmacokinetic parameters of botulism equine heptavalent antitoxin were evaluated during a study conducted in healthy humans. In this study, peak serum concentrations (Cmax) and systemic exposure (AUC) for all 7 antitoxin serotypes (A-G) were found to increase in a dose proportional manner as the dose increased from 1 to 2 vials. However, increasing the dose did not alter the mean clearance (mL/hr), thereby suggesting dose linearity.
  Text
  Botulism equine heptavalent antitoxin is administered via intravenous infusion. An evaluation of the antitoxin pharmacokinetic parameters in healthy humans found the mean volume of distribution varied among the 7 serotypes (A-G), with serotypes B and E being the greatest (9,607 to 14,865 mL and 11,596 to 14,172 mL, respectively) and serotype D being the lowest (1,465 to 1,653 mL). Mean half-lives also varied with the longest being serotype B and C (34.2 to 57.1 hours and 29.6 to 45.6 hours, respectively) and the shortest being serotype D and E (7.5 to 7.7 hours and 7.3 to 7.7 hours, respectively).
