Contraindications And Precaution
hereditary fructose intolerance


Anidulafungin is contraindicated for use in individuals with known or suspected hereditary fructose intolerance (HFI). Anidulafungin contains fructose, an inactive ingredient, and may precipitate a metabolic crisis that may include, but is not limited to, life-threatening hypoglycemia, hypophosphatemia, lactic acidosis, and hepatic failure in individuals with HFI. Obtain a careful history of HFI symptoms (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain) with fructose or sucrose exposure prior to administering anidulafungin because a diagnosis of HFI may not yet be established in pediatric individuals.


Drug Interactions

The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.

Hypersensitivity

This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.

pregnancy


There are no available human data on the use of anidulafungin during pregnancy to inform a drug-associated risk of adverse developmental outcomes. Animal studies show that anidulafungin may cause fetal harm. In rabbits, maternal exposure to drug concentrations 4 times the recommended human maintenance dose resulted in reduced fetal weights and incomplete ossification. Additionally, anidulafungin was detected in fetal plasma, indicating it crossed the placental barrier. Inform pregnant patients about the potential risk to the fetus.


breast-feeding


Use anidulafungin with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of anidulafungin in human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effects on milk production. Anidulafungin is excreted in animal milk. Consider the developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding along with the lactating person's clinical need for anidulafungin and any potential adverse effects on the breast-fed child from anidulafungin or the underlying maternal condition.

Interactions

Dichlorphenamide: (Moderate) Use dichlorphenamide and antifungals together with caution. Dichlorphenamide increases potassium excretion and can cause hypokalemia and should be used cautiously with other drugs that may cause hypokalemia including antifungals. Anidulafungin-associated nausea (7% to 24%), vomiting (7% to 18%), and diarrhea (9% to 18%) were among the most frequently reported adverse events during adult clinical trials. Other gastrointestinal adverse reactions reported by recipients of anidulafungin included dyspepsia (7%), abdominal pain (6%), and constipation (8%). cholestasis, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatic failure, hepatic necrosis, hepatitis, hyperbilirubinemia


During adult clinical trials, hepatobiliary adverse events reported in anidulafungin-treated subjects included cholestasis (less than 2%), hepatic necrosis (less than 2%), elevated bilirubin concentrations or hyperbilirubinemia (less than 2%), elevated gamma-glutamyl transferase (less than 2%), and elevated hepatic enzymes (5%). Description

Anidulafungin is an intravenous echinocandin antifungal approved for the treatment of candidemia and intra-abdominal abscesses and peritonitis caused by Candida in adult and pediatric patients. It is also approved to treat esophageal candidiasis in adults. Like other echinocandins, anidulafungin has fungicidal activity against Candida species, and is active against many Candida isolates that are resistant to other antifungal agents such as fluconazole and amphotericin B. Echinocandins also exert fungistatic effects against Aspergillus species; however, they are not effective for infections caused by Mucorales, Cryptococcus, Fusarium, Rhizopus, or Trichosporon. Additionally, activity against Histoplasma, Blastocystis, and Coccidioides is limited. Anidulafungin has unique pharmacokinetic properties among the echinocandins. It undergoes slow chemical degradation in the plasma and is subsequently eliminated in the feces. Since hepatic metabolism does not occur, and it is not a substrate, inhibitor, or inducer of cytochrome P450 isoenzymes, no clinically relevant drug interactions are expected. Anidulafungin was FDA-approved in 2006.

Mechanism Of Action

Anidulafungin is an echinocandin antifungal that inhibits the activity of 1,3-beta-D-glucan synthase by non-competitively binding to the Fks1p subunit of the enzyme. Inhibition of 1,3-beta-D-glucan synthase prevents the biosynthesis of 1,3-beta-D-glucan, a major fungal cell wall component. The lack of 1,3-beta-D-glucan in the cell wall causes either inhibition of fungal cell growth or fungal cell death via osmotic instability. While 1,3-beta-D-glucan is essential for viability of certain fungi, it is not present in mammalian cells.
 
Echinocandins exhibit fungicidal activity against Candida species through significant cell enlargement and distortion. Against Aspergillus species, echinocandins exert fungistatic activity by causing irregular growth of the hyphae with multiple branched tips and distended cells. Anidulafungin has been shown to be active against most isolates of the following microorganisms both in vitro and in clinical infections: Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, Candida parapsilosis, and Candida tropicalis. In addition, at least 90% of Candida guilliermondii and Candida krusei isolates have minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) less than or equal to the susceptible breakpoint for anidulafungin in vitro; however, the effectiveness of anidulafungin in treating clinical infections due to C. guilliermondii or C. krusei has not been established. The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) defines MICs for C. albicans, C. krusei, and C. tropicalis as susceptible at 0.25 mcg/mL or less, intermediate at 0.5 mcg/mL, and resistant at 1 mcg/mL or more. For C. glabrata, the MICs for susceptible, intermediate, and resistant are 0.12 mcg/mL or less, 0.25 mcg/mL, and 0.5 mcg/mL or more, respectively. C. parapsilosis and C. guilliermondii have the highest MIC break-points, susceptible at 2 mcg/mL or less, intermediate at 4 mcg/mL, and resistant at 8 mcg/mL or more. For Aspergillus sp., a minimum effective concentration (MEC) is used to determine susceptibility to echinocandins. The MEC is defined as the lowest concentration that results in morphologic changes and is used in place of MIC values because treatment with echinocandins does not completely inhibit growth in Aspergillus sp., making accurate MIC breakpoints difficult to determine. As with Candida sp., different species of Aspergillus have varying susceptibilities to echinocandins. Of the echinocandins, anidulafungin has the greatest activity against Aspergillus species; however, A. fumigatus, A. terreus, and A. flavus have comparable susceptibilities to anidulafungin, caspofungin, and micafungin. Echinocandins, without concurrent use of other antifungal agents, are not effective against infections caused by Mucorales, Cryptococcus, Fusarium, Rhizopus, or Trichosporon as the cell walls of these fungi contain mainly 1,6-beta-D-glucan, which limits their susceptibilities. Additionally, activity against Histoplasma, Blastocystis, and Coccidioides is limited.
 
Decreased susceptibility has been reported among Candida isolates, suggesting the potential for resistance. Echinocandin resistance is primarily caused by point mutations within the genes (FKS1 and FKS2) that encode the Fks1p subunit in the glucan synthase enzyme complex. Most Candida species acquire resistance to echinocandins through mutations in the FKS1 gene, but C. glabrata resistance can also occur through mutations in the FKS2 gene. Because of its unique mechanism of action, cross-resistance with amphotericin B and the azoles is not expected.

Pharmacokinetics

Anidulafungin is administered by intravenous infusion. After administration, anidulafungin has a short distribution half-life of 0.5 to 1 hour and a volume of distribution similar to total body fluid volume (approximately 30 to 50 L in adults). Anidulafungin is greater than 99% bound to human plasma proteins. It has excellent tissue penetration into tissues such as lung, liver, kidney, and spleen. Systemic exposure is dose-proportional with low intersubject variability (coefficient of variation less than 25%). Anidulafungin is not hepatically metabolized. Metabolism occurs through slow chemical degradation to a ring-opened peptide that does not have antifungal activity; this degradation occurs at physiologic temperature and pH with a half-life of about 24 hours. The resultant degradation product is further converted to peptide degradants and eliminated. Elimination data for anidulafungin are based on radiolabeled single-dose studies in healthy subjects. Approximately 30% of an administered dose is eliminated in the feces over 9 days; less than 10% is intact drug. Less than 1% of an administered dose is excreted in the urine. Anidulafungin concentrations fell below detectable levels 6 days post-dose; negligible amounts of drug-derived radioactivity were recovered in blood, urine, and feces 8 weeks post-dose. In adults, the clearance of anidulafungin is approximately 1 L/hour and the terminal elimination half-life is about 40 to 50 hours. Anidulafungin has the longest elimination half-life of all echinocandins.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes: none
Anidulafungin is not a clinically relevant substrate, inducer, or inhibitor of CYP450 isoenzymes, and it is unlikely to have a clinically relevant effect on drugs metabolized by CYP450 isoenzymes. Drug interaction studies report that no dosage adjustments of either drug are necessary when administered with cyclosporine, tacrolimus, or voriconazole and that no dosage adjustments of anidulafungin are required when administered with rifampin or liposomal amphotericin B.

Route-Specific Pharmacokinetics
Intravenous Route

Peak concentrations are achieved immediately after IV administration. Anidulafungin steady-state plasma concentrations are reached on the first day after an intravenous loading dose and the estimated plasma accumulation factor at steady state is approximately 2.

Hepatic Impairment

The pharmacokinetics of anidulafungin are not altered by hepatic insufficiency. Anidulafungin is not hepatically metabolized. Anidulafungin pharmacokinetics were examined in subjects with Child-Pugh class A, B, or C hepatic insufficiency. Anidulafungin concentrations were not increased in subjects with any degree of hepatic insufficiency. Though a slight decrease in AUC was observed in patients with Child-Pugh C hepatic insufficiency, it was within the range of population estimates noted for healthy subjects.

Renal Impairment

The pharmacokinetics of anidulafungin are not altered by renal insufficiency. Anidulafungin is not removed by hemodialysis.

Pediatrics

Children and Adolescents
The pharmacokinetics of anidulafungin in children and adolescents are similar to those observed in adults. In a pharmacokinetic study in 25 children (2 to 17 years), mean steady-state values for clearance, volume of distribution, and elimination half-life were 0.018 L/hour/kg, 0.54 L/kg, and 22.9 hours, respectively, for patients receiving doses of 0.75 mg/kg/day. Values were similar for patients receiving 1.5 mg/kg/day (0.016 L/hour/kg, 0.43 L/kg, and 19.9 hours, respectively).
 
Neonates and Infants
In a pharmacokinetic study of 8 neonates (6 born prematurely; 26 to 39 weeks gestational age) and 7 infants (50 to 451 days), median values for clearance and volume of distribution were 0.02 L/kg/hour and 1.7 L/kg, respectively, in neonates, and 0.017 L/kg/hour and 1.1 L/kg, respectively, in infants. The median elimination half-life of anidulafungin was 78 hours (range 40 to 219) in neonates, compared with 33 hours (range 30 to 173 hours) in infants; the difference was not statistically significant. The half-life reported in neonates and infants is longer than that reported in children (approximately 20 to 24 hours). This study suggested that a longer dosing interval (24 hours or greater) may be sufficient in neonates and infants; however, sampling times were variable among subjects, and data are limited to validate this conclusion.
 
Based on pharmacokinetic data, the following dosage equivalencies have been reported to provide similar exposure in pediatric and adult patients:
1.5 mg/kg IV load then 0.75 mg/kg/day in neonatal and pediatric patients provides similar exposure as a 100 mg IV load then 50 mg/day in adults.
3 mg/kg IV load then 1.5 mg/kg/day in neonatal and pediatric patients provides similar exposure as a 200 mg IV load then 100 mg/day in adults.


Geriatric

No specific anidulafungin dosage alterations are required based on age.

Gender Differences

No specific anidulafungin dosage alterations are required based on gender; plasma concentrations of anidulafungin in healthy men and women were similar. In multiple-dose studies, drug clearance was slightly faster (approximately 22%) in men.

Ethnic Differences

No specific anidulafungin dosage alterations are required based on race.

Other

The pharmacokinetics of anidulafungin are similar in healthy patients and those with fungal infections and there are no specific dosage alterations required based on HIV status, irrespective of concomitant anti-retroviral therapy.

Administration

For storage information, see the specific product information in the How Supplied section.

Injectable Administration


Visually inspect prepared infusions for particulate matter and discoloration prior to administration when solution and container permit. Discard solution if particulate matter or discoloration is identified.
Administer by intravenous infusion only, after appropriate dilution for IV infusion.


Intravenous Administration

Reconstitution
Reconstitute each 50 mg or 100 mg vial with 15 mL or 30 mL of Sterile Water for Injection, respectively, for a resultant concentration of 3.33 mg/mL.
Storage: Reconstituted solutions are stable for up to 24 hours at temperatures up to 25 degrees C (77 degrees F).
 
Dilution
Do not use any diluent other than 5% Dextrose Injection or 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection.
For preparation of 50 mg, 100 mg, or 200 mg doses:
50 mg dose: Withdraw the contents of one 50 mg reconstituted vial and add to an IV infusion bag or bottle containing 50 mL of 5% Dextrose Injection or 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection to give a total infusion volume of 65 mL.
100 mg dose: Withdraw the contents of one 100 mg reconstituted vial OR two 50 mg reconstituted vials and add to an IV infusion bag or bottle containing 100 mL of 5% Dextrose Injection or 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection to give a total infusion volume of 130 mL.
200 mg dose: Withdraw the contents of either four 50 mg reconstituted vials OR two 100 mg reconstituted vials and add to an IV infusion bag or bottle containing 200 mL of 5% Dextrose Injection or 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection to give a total infusion volume of 260 mL.
For preparation of pediatric doses below 50 mg:
Withdraw appropriate dose from the reconstituted vial (3.33 mg/mL) and dilute to a final concentration of 0.77 mg/mL.
Storage: Diluted solutions are stable for up to 48 hours at temperatures up to 25 degrees C (77 degrees F); do NOT freeze diluted solution.
 
Intermittent IV Infusion
Do not mix or infuse with other medications.
Administer as a slow infusion at a rate no greater than 1.1 mg/minute (equivalent to 1.4 mL/minute or 84 mL/hour when reconstituted and diluted per instructions). The minimum duration of infusion is 45 minutes for a 50 mg dose, 90 minutes for a 100 mg dose, and 180 minutes for a 200 mg dose.


Maximum Dosage Limits
Adults

200 mg/day IV for loading dose; 100 mg/day IV for maintenance.

Geriatric

200 mg/day IV for loading dose; 100 mg/day IV for maintenance.

Adolescents

3 mg/kg IV (Max: 200 mg/dose) for loading dose; 1.5 mg/kg/day IV (Max: 100 mg/day) for maintenance dose.

Children

3 mg/kg IV (Max: 200 mg/dose) for loading dose; 1.5 mg/kg/day IV (Max: 100 mg/day) for maintenance dose.

Infants

3 mg/kg IV for loading dose; 1.5 mg/kg/day IV for maintenance dose.

Neonates

Safety and efficacy have not been established; however, 3 mg/kg IV as a loading dose and 1.5 mg/kg/day IV as a maintenance dose have been used off-label.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
Hepatic Impairment

No dosage adjustment is needed. However, monitor patients for worsening hepatic function; consider risks and benefits of continuing treatment if further worsening of hepatic function occurs during anidulafungin treatment.

Renal Impairment

No dosage adjustment is needed in patients with renal impairment.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
Anidulafungin is not dialyzable and may be administered without regard to the timing of hemodialysis. Supplemental dosing is not required.
 
Continuous renal replacement therapy
Anidulafungin is not dialyzable and may be administered without regard to the timing of hemodialysis. Supplemental dosing is not required.
 
Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO)
Lower anidulafungin exposures have been noted in patients supported by ECMO. Higher anidulafungin doses may be necessary in these patients to achieve adequate drug concentrations.
