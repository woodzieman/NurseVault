Drug Class: Cetuximab is a recombinant, human/mouse chimeric monoclonal antibody that binds specifically to the extracellular domain of the human epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), mediating antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) against certain human tumor types.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 400 mg/m2 IV for the initial dose then 250 mg/m2 IV once weekly for subsequent doses; OR 500 mg/m2 IV every 2 weeks.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): angina, cardiac arrest, heart failure, myocardial infarction, respiratory arrest, cough, dyspnea, pneumonitis, aseptic meningitis, infection, pharyngitis, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hyponatremia, anxiety, confusion, depression, headache, insomnia, peripheral neuropathy, arthralgia, bone pain, myopathy, asthenia, fatigue, malaise, anemia, leukopenia, lymphopenia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Cetuximab should be used with caution in patients with known murine protein hypersensitivity or hypersensitivity to any component of the product. Severe infusion-related reactions may occur despite the use of prophylactic antihistamines (see Adverse Reactions). Patient counseling: Advise patients to limit sun exposure during treatment and for 2 months after the last dose of cetuximab. Emetic risk: Low

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Cetuximab should be used with caution in patients with known murine protein hypersensitivity or hypersensitivity to any component of the product. Severe infusion-related reactions may occur despite the use of prophylactic antihistamines (see Adverse Reactions). Serious infusion reactions occurred with cetuximab administration in approximately 3% of patients in clinical trials; a fatal outcome was reported in less than 1 in 1000. Patients should be monitored for at least 1 hour after administration of cetuximab; longer observation periods may be required in patients who have experienced an infusion reaction. These reactions were characterized by the rapid onset of airway obstruction (acute bronchospasm, stridor, hoarseness), urticaria, and/or hypotension. Although the majority of infusion reactions occurred during the first infusion of cetuximab, caution must be exercised with every cetuximab infusion, as there were patients who experienced their first infusion reaction during later infusions. Adjustments to the infusion rate and continued antihistamine therapy are recommended if a mild to moderate infusion reaction occurs (see Administration and Dosage sections). Severe infusion reactions necessitate immediate interruption and discontinuation of therapy. Appropriate medical therapy including epinephrine, corticosteroids, intravenous antihistamines, bronchodilators, and oxygen should be available for use in the treatment of such a reaction.
 
In the event of acute onset or worsening of pulmonary symptoms, especially in patients with pre-existing pulmonary disease, cetuximab therapy should be interrupted and the etiology of these symptoms investigated. If interstitial lung disease is confirmed, cetuximab therapy should be discontinued. Interstitial lung disease and pulmonary edema has been reported in advanced colorectal cancer patients receiving cetuximab (see Adverse Reactions). Two patients with pre-existing fibrotic lung disease (pulmonary fibrosis) experienced acute exacerbation of their disease during therapy with cetuximab and irinotecan. The onset of symptoms occurred between the fourth and eleventh doses of therapy.
 
During clinical trials, cetuximab therapy was associated with dermatologic toxicities (see Adverse Reactions). Subsequent to the development of severe dermatologic toxicities, complications including S. aureus sepsis and skin abscesses requiring incision and drainage were reported. Patients developing dermatologic toxicities while receiving cetuximab should be monitored for the development of inflammatory sequelae or skin infection and appropriate treatment initiated. Dose modifications of any future dose of cetuximab should be instituted in the case of severe acneform rash (see Dosage). Treatment with topical and/or oral antibiotics should be considered; topical corticosteroids are not recommended. It is recommended that patients limit sunlight (UV) exposure by wearing sunscreen and hats while receiving cetuximab and for 2 months after the last dose, as UV exposure can exacerbate any skin reaction that may occur.
 
Cetuximab in combination with radiation therapy should be used with caution in head and neck cancer patients with a history of cardiac disease, specifically coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, or cardiac arrhythmias. Cardiopulmonary arrest and/or sudden death occurred in 2% of 208 patients with squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck treated with cetuximab and radiation therapy (see Adverse Reactions). Fatal events occurred within 1 to 43 days after the last cetuximab treatment. Although the etiology of these events is unknown, electrolyte imbalance may be a risk factor for serious cardiac events such as arrhythmia. Close monitoring of serum electrolytes for hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia, and hypokalemia is recommended during and for at least 8 weeks after cetuximab therapy. The safety of cetuximab in combination with radiation therapy and cisplatin for the treatment of head and neck cancer has not been established. Serious cardiotoxicity and death were observed in a single arm trial with cetuximab in combination with delayed, accelerated (concomitant boost) fractionation radiation therapy and cisplatin in patients with locally advanced head and neck cancer.
 
Cetuximab is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. Animal reproduction studies have not been conducted with cetuximab, and there are no adequate and well-controlled trials of cetuximab in pregnant women. It is not known whether cetuximab can cause fetal harm when administered to a pregnant woman or whether cetuximab can effect reproductive capacity. However, EGFR has been implicated in the control of prenatal development and may be essential for normal organogenesis, proliferation, and differentiation in the developing fetus. In addition, IgG1 is know to cross the placental barrier; therefore, cetuximab has the potential to be transmitted from the mother to the fetus. Cetuximab should only be used in a pregnant woman if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus. All patients should be counseled regarding the potential risks of cetuximab treatment to the developing fetus prior to initiation of therapy. Males and females need to use adequate contraception during cetuximab receipt and for 6 months after the last dose. If a patient becomes pregnant while receiving the drug, she should be apprised of the potential risks to the fetus and/or the potential risk for loss of pregnancy.
 
It is not known if cetuximab is secreted in human breast milk; however, IgG is secreted in human milk. The potential for absorption of cetuximab and harm to the infant after ingestion is unknown. It is advised that women avoid breast-feeding during cetuximab therapy and for 60 days after the last dose of cetuximab.
 
The safety and effectiveness of cetuximab in children have not been established.
 
Patients who participated in cetuximab advanced colorectal cancer clinical trials were required to have immunohistochemical evidence of positive EGFR expression using the DakoCytomation EGFr pharmDx test kit. Primary tumor or tumor from a metastatic site was tested. Response rate did not correlate with either the percentage or positive cells or the intensity of EGFR expression. Assessment for EGFR expression should be performed by a laboratory with demonstrated proficiency in the technology. Failure to properly process the assay can lead to unreliable results. Pretreatment assessment for evidence of EGFR expression is not required for patients with squamous cell cancer of the head and neck. Retrospective subset analyses of metastatic colorectal cancer trials have not shown a treatment benefit for cetuximab in patients whose tumors had KRAS mutations in codon 12 or 13. Use of cetuximab is not recommended for the treatment of colorectal cancer with these mutations.

    Headline
    alpha-gal syndrome, history of tick bites, infusion-related reactions
    General Information
    Serious and fatal infusion-related reactions can occur with cetuximab treatment; patients with a history of tick bites, red meat hypersensitivity, or alpha-gal syndrome (IgE antibodies against galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose) may be at increased risk for anaphylactic reactions. Premedicate with a histamine-1 (H1) receptor antagonist as recommended. Infusion reactions may occur during or several hours following completion of the infusion; most severe infusion reactions (90%) occurred with the first infusion, despite premedication with antihistamines. If a reaction occurs during the infusion, interrupt the infusion. Permanently discontinue cetuximab for serious infusion reactions (grade 3 or 4); the infusion may be resumed at a slower rate for grade 1 or 2 reactions. After each infusion is complete, monitor patients in a setting with resuscitation equipment and other agents necessary to treat anaphylaxis for at least 1 hour. If a patient requires treatment for an infusion reaction, monitor for more than 1 hour afterwards to confirm resolution.
    Headline
    cardiac arrest, cardiac arrhythmias, coronary artery disease, heart failure
    General Information
    Carefully consider the use of cetuximab for the treatment of squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck (SCCHN) in combination with radiation therapy or with fluorouracil and platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with a history of coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, or cardiac arrhythmias. Cardiac arrest or sudden death occurred in patients who received cetuximab in combination with radiation therapy or fluoropyrimidine and platinum-based chemotherapy. Monitor serum electrolytes including magnesium, potassium, and calcium, during cetuximab treatment and for at least 8 weeks after completion of therapy; replete electrolytes as necessary.
    Headline
    pulmonary disease
    General Information
    Use cetuximab with caution in patients who have a history of pre-existing pulmonary disease; severe pneumonitis/interstitial lung disease has been reported in patients treated with cetuximab. Monitor patients for new or worsening respiratory symptoms including fever, cough, or dyspnea. Immediately interrupt cetuximab therapy in patients with new or worsening respiratory symptoms and permanently discontinue therapy if no other potential causes are identified.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Cetuximab can cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy, based on its mechanism of action and data from animal studies. There are no available data on its use during human pregnancy. In mice, EGFR is critically important in reproductive and developmental processes including blastocyst implantation, placental development, and embryo-fetal/postnatal survival and development. Reduction or elimination of embryo-fetal or maternal EGFR signaling can prevent implantation, can cause embryo-fetal loss during various stages of gestation through effects on placental development, and can cause developmental anomalies and early death in surviving fetuses. Disruption or depletion of EGFR in animal models results in impaired placental, lung, cardiac, skin, and neural development. In an animal reproduction study, intravenous administration of cetuximab (1 to 4 times the recommended human dose based on body surface area) to pregnant cynomolgus monkeys during organogenesis resulted in an increased incidence of embryo-lethality and abortion. The American Society of Clinical Oncology provides additional information on the use of certain medications for the management of cancer during pregnancy.

People who may become pregnant should use effective contraception while taking cetuximab and for at least 2 months after the last dose. Based on animal studies, cetuximab may impair female fertility.

Interactions
Cholera Vaccine: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the live cholera vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to cholera bacteria after receiving the vaccine.
CISplatin: (Major) Cetuximab is not indicated for the treatment of squamous cell cancer of the head and neck (SCCHN) in combination with radiation and cisplatin due to the risk of increased serious adverse reactions. In a controlled study of patients with locally advanced SCCHN (n = 940), treatment with cetuximab in combination with radiation therapy and cisplatin increased the incidence of grade 3 and 4 mucositis, radiation recall syndrome, acneiform rash, cardiac events, and electrolyte disturbances compared to radiation and cisplatin alone. Myocardial ischemia occurred in 2% of patients in the cetuximab arm compared with 0.9% in the control arm; adverse reactions with fatal outcome were reported in 4% in the cetuximab arm and 3% in the control arm. The addition of cetuximab to radiation and cisplatin did not improve progression free survival.
Dengue Tetravalent Vaccine, Live: (Contraindicated) Avoid administration of the live dengue virus vaccine with immunosuppressive drug therapy and prior to immune recovery following treatment with immunosuppressive drug therapy. When feasible, administer indicated live virus vaccines at least 4 weeks before planned immunosuppression or wait until at least 3 months after discontinuation. The time to restoration of immune competence may be longer in some patients. Patients with altered immunocompetence may be at increased risk for severe adverse reactions due to uninhibited growth of the attenuated live virus. Additionally, vaccines may be less effective if administered during a period of altered immunocompetence.
SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) vaccines: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the SARS-CoV-2 virus vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to SARS-CoV-2 virus after receiving the vaccine.
SARS-CoV-2 Virus (COVID-19) mRNA Vaccine: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the SARS-CoV-2 virus vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to SARS-CoV-2 virus after receiving the vaccine.
SARS-CoV-2 Virus (COVID-19) Recombinant Spike Protein Nanoparticle Vaccine: (Moderate) Patients receiving immunosuppressant medications may have a diminished response to the SARS-CoV-2 virus vaccine. When feasible, administer indicated vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppressant medications. Counsel patients receiving immunosuppressant medications about the possibility of a diminished vaccine response and to continue to follow precautions to avoid exposure to SARS-CoV-2 virus after receiving the vaccine.
Sodium Iodide I-131: (Moderate) Monitor for bone marrow suppression during concomitant use of sodium iodide I-131 and antineoplastic agents. Concomitant use of bone marrow depressants may enhance the depression of the hematopoietic system caused by the use of large doses of sodium iodide I-131.
Tuberculin Purified Protein Derivative, PPD: (Moderate) Immunosuppressives may decrease the immunological response to tuberculin purified protein derivative, PPD. This suppressed reactivity can persist for up to 6 weeks after treatment discontinuation. Consider deferring the skin test until completion of the immunosuppressive therapy.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  In a randomized controlled trial in patients with squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck (SCCHN), cardiac arrest/respiratory arrest and/or sudden death occurred in 4/208 (2%) of patients treated with radiation therapy and cetuximab as compared to none of 212 patients treated with radiation therapy alone. Three patients with a history of coronary artery disease died at home, with myocardial infarction as the presumed cause of death. One of these patients had a cardiac arrhythmia, and one had congestive heart failure. Death occurred 27, 32, and 43 days after the last dose of cetuximab. One patient with no prior history of coronary artery disease died one day after the last dose cetuximab. Etiology of these events is unknown. Carefully consider use of cetuximab in combination with radiation therapy in head and neck cancer patients with a history of coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, or arrhythmias. The incidence of all infusion reactions (Grade 1—4) was 15% for head and neck patients treated with cetuximab and radiation therapy. In patients with colorectal cancer, infusion reactions occurred in 19% of patients receiving combination therapy with cetuximab plus irinotecan and in 21% of patients treated with cetuximab alone. Although these reactions usually occur with the initial dose, patients may experience their first severe infusion reaction during later infusions. Approximately 90% of severe infusion reactions were associated with the first infusion of cetuximab, despite the use of prophylactic antihistamines. Severe infusion reactions occurred in 3% (46/1485) of patients receiving cetuximab during clinical trials for colorectal cancer or head and neck cancer, rarely with fatal outcome (< 1/1000). Severe infusion reactions are characterized by rapid onset of airway/respiratory obstruction (bronchospasm, stridor, hoarseness, angioedema), urticaria, hypotension, hypertension, shock, loss of consciousness, myocardial infarction, and/or cardiac arrest. Severe infusion reactions require immediate interruption of the cetuximab infusion and permanent discontinuation of therapy. Among 25 patients who had received cetuximab in the past and who had a hypersensitivity reaction after cetuximab receipt, 17 had IgE antibodies against the drug before treatment whereas only 1 of 51 patients without a hypersensitivity reaction had IgE present. The IgE antibodies were specific for an oligosaccharide, galactose--1,3-galactose, which is present on the Fab portion of the cetuximab heavy chain. IgE antibodies against cetuximab were detected in several patients who never received cetuximab: 15 of 72 samples from control subjects in Tennessee, 3 of 49 samples from northern California, and 2 of 341 samples from Boston. Of the 8 patients who had received cetuximab in the past and had a hypersensitivity reaction but had negative results on the IgE assay, 7 had grade 1 or 2 reactions, and 1 had a grade 3 reaction. Five of the 8 patients were rechallenged; 1 had a second hypersensitivity reaction, and four completed treatment without further reactions. During clinical trials, mild to moderate reactions were managed by decreasing the infusion rate and continued use of antihistamines during subsequent doses. Appropriate medical therapy including epinephrine, corticosteroids, intravenous antihistamines, bronchodilators, and oxygen should be available for use in the event of such a reaction. An observation period of 1 hour is recommended after cetuximab infusion for all patients. Monitor patients in a setting with resuscitation equipment and other agents necessary to treat anaphylaxis. Description
  Text
  Cetuximab is a recombinant, human/mouse chimeric monoclonal antibody that binds specifically to the extracellular domain of the human epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), mediating antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) against certain human tumor types. It is indicated for the treatment of advanced head and neck cancer in combination with radiation therapy or platinum-based chemotherapy, or as monotherapy following progression after platinum-based therapy; for the treatment of advanced KRAS wild-type, EGFR-positive colorectal cancer (CRC) in combination with FOLFIRI or irinotecan, or as monotherapy in patients who have failed oxaliplatin- and irinotecan-based chemotherapy or are intolerant to irinotecan; and for the treatment of BRAF V600E mutation-positive metastatic CRC in combination with encorafenib. In vitro assays and in vivo animal studies have shown that cetuximab inhibits the growth and survival of tumor cells that express EGFR; no anti-tumor effects were observed in human tumor xenografts lacking EGFR expression. Induction of EGFR-mediated MAPK pathway activation has been identified as a mechanism of resistance to BRAF inhibitors in BRAF-mutant CRC; combinations of a BRAF inhibitor and EGFR inhibitors have overcome this resistance in nonclinical models. Confirmation of EGFR and Ras status, as well as BRAF V600E mutation status if applicable, is required prior to treatment for CRC; because EGFR expression has been detected in nearly all SCCHN tumor specimens, patients are not required to have immunohistochemical evidence of EGFR tumor expression prior to treatment. Treatment with cetuximab can result in severe infusion-related reactions as well as cardiopulmonary arrest and sudden death. Closely monitor serum electrolytes, including serum magnesium, potassium, and calcium, during and after treatment with cetuximab. Mucocutaneous adverse reactions and dermatologic toxicities are also common; patients should be instructed to limit sun exposure.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Cetuximab is a recombinant human/mouse chimeric monoclonal antibody that binds specifically to the extracellular domain of the human epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). It is composed of the Fv regions of a murine anti-EGFR antibody with human IgG1 heavy and kappa light chain constant regions, and is produced in a mammalian (murine myeloma) cell culture. The EGFR is a transmembrane glycoprotein that is a member of a subfamily of type 1 receptor tyrosine kinases including EGFR, HER2, HER3, and HER4. It is constitutively expressed in many normal epithelial tissues, including the skin and hair follicle, but is also detected in many human cancers including those of the head and neck, colon, and rectum.
Cetuximab binds to EGFR on both normal and tumor cells, competitively inhibiting the binding of epidermal growth factor (EGF) and other ligands such as transforming growth factor-alpha. In vitro assays and in vivo animal studies have shown that binding of cetuximab to the EGFR blocks phosphorylation and activation of receptor-associated kinases, resulting in inhibition of cell growth, induction of apoptosis, and decreased matrix metalloproteinase and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) production. Signal transduction through EGFR activates wild-type Ras proteins; however, in cells with activating Ras somatic mutations, the resulting mutant Ras proteins are continuously active regardless of EGFR regulation.
In vitro, cetuximab can mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) against certain human tumor types. In vitro assays and in vivo animal studies have shown that cetuximab inhibits the growth and survival of tumor cells that express EGFR; no anti-tumor effects of cetuximab were observed in human tumor xenografts lacking EGFR expression. The addition of cetuximab to radiation therapy or irinotecan in human tumor xenograft models in mice resulted in an increase in anti-tumor effects compared to radiation therapy or chemotherapy alone.
Induction of EGFR-mediated MAPK pathway activation has been identified as a mechanism of resistance to BRAF inhibitors in patients with BRAF-mutant colorectal cancer. In nonclinical models, combinations of a BRAF inhibitor with an EGFR inhibitor have been shown to overcome this resistance mechanism. Coadministration of cetuximab with encorafenib had an antitumor effect greater than either drug alone in a mouse model of colorectal cancer with mutated BRAF V600E.
