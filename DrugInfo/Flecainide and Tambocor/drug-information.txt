Drug Class: Flecainide is an oral, local anesthetic-type antiarrhythmic.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 300 mg/day PO for paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT) prophylaxis, atrial fibrillation, or atrial flutter; 400 mg/day PO for ventricular tachycardia prophylaxis.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Flecainide displays cross sensitivity to amide anesthetics. Patients with amide local anesthetic hypersensitivity should use flecainide with caution.
 
In general, flecainide should be used cautiously in patients with certain types of cardiac disease. Flecainide has proarrhythmic properties and can induce or worsen cardiac arrhythmias. Flecainide dosages should be reduced, or discontinued, in patients who exhibit prolonged QRS (> 180 ms) or prolonged PR (> 300 ms) intervals. Flecainide is contraindicated in patients with preexisting second- or third-degree AV block, or bifascicular or trifascicular bundle-branch block unless a pacemaker is in place that could sustain cardiac rhythm in the event of complete heart block. Although its effects on normal sinus node function are negligible, flecainide is contraindicated in patients with preexisting sick sinus syndrome or significant conduction delay because the drug can cause profound bradycardia or arrest in these patients. Flecainide can increase endocardial pacing thresholds, so it is not recommended for use in patients with nonprogrammable pacemakers unless pacing rescue is available. These effects on pacing threshold are reversible following cessation of therapy. Because flecainide has been shown to worsen ventricular tachycardia in some patients, flecainide should be used cautiously, if at all, in patients with these cardiac arrhythmias. Use of flecainide in chronic atrial fibrillation has not been adequately studied and is not recommended by the manufacturer, due to the reported risk of ventricular arrhythmias (about 10%) in this population.
 
Flecainide is contraindicated in patients with cardiogenic shock. Due to its potential negative inotropic effects and high risk of proarrhythmias, flecainide should be avoided in patients with congestive heart failure (CHF), acute myocardial infarction, or a history of myocardial infarction with ventricular dysfunction (LVEF < 30%). Serum drug concentration monitoring is suggested for patients with CHF (see Dosage, Therapeutic Drug Monitoring). QT prolongation and torsade de pointes have occurred during flecainide therapy. Therefore, flecainide should be avoided when possible in patients with congenital or acquired QT prolongation syndromes or in patients with a history of torsade de pointes.
 
Any electrolyte imbalance, such as hypokalemia or hyperkalemia, should be corrected before beginning therapy with any antiarrhythmic agent, including flecainide.
 
Hepatic disease and renal impairment (e.g., renal disease, renal failure) can alter the metabolism and/or excretion of flecainide, potentially resulting in drug accumulation and reduced dosage requirements. Approximately 30% of flecainide is excreted renally. In patients with hepatic or renal impairment, dosage should be adjusted at intervals that exceed 4 days. Serum drug concentration monitoring is required for patients with severe renal or hepatic disease, and may also be helpful for patients with moderate renal disease (see Dosage, Therapeutic Drug Monitoring). Elderly patients are more likely to have age-associated compromises in renal, hepatic, and cardiac function, and are at increased risk for adverse effects due to flecainide, including proarrhythmias. Flecainide elimination from plasma is somewhat slower in elderly subjects than in younger subjects; however, elderly patients have been safely treated with usual adult dosages.
 
Flecainide is classified in FDA pregnancy risk category C. Flecainide has been shown to have teratogenic effects in certain breeds of laboratory animals. Because there are no well-controlled pregnancy studies in humans, flecainide should only be used in pregnant women if the potential benefits justify the possible risk to the fetus.
 
Flecainide is excreted into breast milk in concentrations exceeding those in human plasma. If flecainide therapy is essential to the mother, an alternative to breast-feeding should be considered. Results from a multiple dose study conducted in mothers soon after delivery indicates that flecainide is excreted in human breast milk in concentrations as high as 4-fold (mean 2.5-fold) higher than maternal plasma levels. Assuming a maternal plasma level at upper end of the therapeutic range (1 mcg/ml), the estimated daily dose ingested by a nursing infant (assuming 700 ml of breast milk intake per 24 hours) would be < 3 mg.
 
Use flecainide with caution in children. The manufacturer recommends direct supervision by a cardiologist skilled in the treatment of pediatric arrhythmias. Small changes in dosage may lead to disproportionate increases in plasma levels in children and infants; dosage titration requires ECG evaluation and serum flecainide concentration monitoring.

    General Information
    Flecainide is contraindicated in individuals with cardiogenic shock or preexisting second or third-degree AV block or right bundle branch block when associated with a left hemiblock (bifascicular block), unless a pacemaker is present to sustain cardiac rhythm in the event of complete heart block. Avoid use of flecainide in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk. Correct preexisting hypokalemia before flecainide administration. Do not use flecainide in individuals with chronic atrial fibrillation. Flecainide has proarrhythmic properties and can cause new or worsened supraventricular or ventricular arrhythmias. It is uncertain if the risk of proarrhythmia with flecainide is exaggerated in individuals with chronic atrial fibrillation, high ventricular rate, and/or exercise. Individuals treated with flecainide for atrial flutter have been reported with 1:1 atrioventricular conduction due to slowing the atrial rate. A paradoxical increase in the ventricular rate also may occur in individuals with atrial fibrillation who receive flecainide. Concomitant negative chronotropic therapy, such as digoxin or beta-blockers, may lower the risk of this complication. Use flecainide with extreme caution in individuals with sick sinus syndrome because it may cause sinus bradycardia, sinus pause, or sinus arrest. Use flecainide with caution in individuals with pacemaker dependency, including permanent pacemakers or temporary pacing electrodes; do not use flecainide in individuals with existing poor thresholds or nonprogrammable pacemakers unless suitable pacing rescue is available. Determine the pacing threshold in individuals with pacemakers prior to initiating therapy with flecainide, again after 1 week of therapy, and at regular intervals thereafter. Flecainide can increase endocardial pacing thresholds and may suppress ventricular escape rhythms. Use flecainide with caution and monitor carefully in individuals with a history of heart failure or myocardial dysfunction; when reasonable, monitoring of flecainide plasma concentrations is recommended in individuals with heart failure. Flecainide has a negative inotropic effect and may cause or worsen heart failure, particularly in individuals with cardiomyopathy, preexisting severe heart failure (New York Heart Association Class III or IV) or low ejection fractions (less than 30%). Because of the proarrhythmic effects of flecainide, reserve use for individuals in whom the benefits of treatment outweigh the risks. The relatively high frequency of proarrhythmic events in individuals with sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) and serious underlying heart disease, and the need for careful titration and monitoring, requires that flecainide therapy be started in the hospital for individuals with sustained VT. Flecainide is not recommended in individuals with less severe ventricular arrhythmias even if the individuals are symptomatic. In the CAST clinical trial, subjects with asymptomatic non-life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias who had a myocardial infarction more than 6 days but less than 2 years prior and were treated with flecainide demonstrated an excessive rate of mortality or non-fatal cardiac arrest compared to placebo. The average duration of treatment with flecainide was 10 months. The applicability of the CAST results to other populations (e.g., those without recent myocardial infarction) is uncertain, but at present, it is prudent to consider the risks of Class IC agents, including flecainide, coupled with the lack of any evidence of improved survival, generally unacceptable in individuals without life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias, even if the individuals are experiencing unpleasant, but not life-threatening, symptoms or signs. Do not use flecainide in individuals with recent myocardial infarction.

    hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use flecainide in individuals with significant hepatic impairment or hepatic failure only if the potential benefits clearly outweigh the risks. If used, early and frequent flecainide plasma concentration monitoring is recommended to guide dosage; increase the dose very cautiously when plasma concentrations have plateaued (after more than 4 days). Flecainide elimination can be markedly slower in individuals with significant hepatic impairment.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    A reduced initial flecainide dose may be necessary in individuals with severe renal impairment or renal failure (CrCl 35 mL/minute/1.73 m2 or less). Frequent flecainide plasma concentration monitoring is required to guide dosage adjustments in individuals with severe renal impairment or renal failure and may also be useful in individuals with less severe renal impairment. Increase the flecainide dosage very cautiously when plasma concentrations have plateaued (after more than 4 days) in individuals with renal impairment or renal failure, observing the individual closely for signs of adverse cardiac effects or other toxicity. It may take longer than 4 days before a new steady-state flecainide plasma concentration is reached after a dosage change in individuals with renal impairment or renal failure.
    Headline
    hyperkalemia
    General Information
    Hyperkalemia may alter the effects of Class I antiarrhythmic drugs, including flecainide. Correct preexisting hyperkalemia before flecainide administration.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, infants, neonates
    General Information
    Initiate flecainide in the hospital with rhythm monitoring in neonates, infants, children, and adolescents. Any use of flecainide in pediatric populations should be directly supervised by a cardiologist who is skilled in the treatment of pediatric arrhythmias. In pediatric individuals with structural heart disease, flecainide has been associated with cardiac arrest and sudden death. In some children on higher doses, despite previously low flecainide plasma concentrations, the concentration has increased rapidly to far above therapeutic values while receiving the same dose. Small changes in dose may also lead to disproportionate increases in plasma concentrations. Obtain flecainide plasma trough concentrations and ECG at presumed steady-state either after initiation or change in flecainide dose, whether the dose was increased for lack of effectiveness, or increased growth of the individual. For the first year of flecainide therapy, whenever the child is seen for reasons of clinical follow-up, it is suggested that a flecainide plasma trough concentration and 12-lead ECG are obtained.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Flecainide was teratogenic and embryotoxic in New Zealand White rabbits at 30 to 35 mg/kg/day, but not in Dutch Belted rabbits at doses up to 30 mg/kg/day. No birth defects occurred in rats or mice, although delayed bone development was seen in rats at high doses. Because well-controlled pregnancy studies in humans are lacking, use flecainide during pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential fetal risk.

Interactions
Abiraterone: (Moderate) Monitor for an increase in flecainide-related adverse reactions, including QT prolongation, if coadministration with abiraterone is necessary. Flecainide is a CYP2D6 substrate and abiraterone is a moderate CYP2D6 inhibitor. Plasma concentrations of flecainide may increase, especially in extensive CYP2D6 metabolizers.
Acebutolol: (Moderate) Pharmacologically, beta-blockers, like acebutolol, cause AV nodal conduction depression and additive effects are possible when used in combination with flecainide. When used together, AV block can occur. During flecainide clinical trials, increased adverse events have not been reported in patients receiving combination therapy with beta-blockers and flecainide. However, patients should be monitored closely and the dose should be adjusted according to clinical response.
acetaZOLAMIDE: (Moderate) Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors can decrease the urinary excretion and enhance the clinical effects of flecainide. Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors increase the alkalinity of the urine, thereby increasing the amount of nonionized drug available for renal tubular reabsorption into the systemic circulation.
Adagrasib: (Major) Avoid concomitant use of adagrasib and flecainide due to the potential for increased flecainide exposure and additive risk for QT/QTc prolongation and torsade de pointes (TdP). If use is necessary, monitor for flecainide-related adverse effects and consider taking additional steps to minimize the risk for QT prolongation and TdP, such as electrolyte monitoring and repletion and ECG monitoring. Flecainide is a CYP2D6 substrate, adagrasib is a moderate CYP2D6 inhibitor, and both medications have been associated with QT interval prolongation.
Alfuzosin: (Major) Due to the potential for QT prolongation and torsade de pointes (TdP), caution is advised when administering alfuzosin with flecainide. Based on electrophysiology studies performed by the manufacturer, alfuzosin has a slight QT prolonging effect. The QT prolongation appeared less with alfuzosin 10 mg than with 40 mg. Flecainide, a Class IC antiarrhythmic, is also associated with a possible risk for QT prolongation and/or TdP; although, the increase in QT interval is largely due to prolongation of the QRS interval. Causality for TdP has not been established for flecainide.
Amiodarone: (Major) Consider an empiric 30%-50% flecainide dosage reduction if concomitant use with amiodarone is necessary. Additional flecainide concentration monitoring and further dosage adjustments may be required. Combination therapy should be reserved for patients who are unresponsive to a single agent. Concomitant use may increase flecainide exposure and the risk for flecainide-related adverse effects. Additionally, coadministration may increase the risk for QT/QTc prolongation and torsade de pointes (TdP). Flecainide is a CYP2D6 substrate, amiodarone is a weak CYP2D6 inhibitor, and both medications have been associated with QT/QTc prolongation and TdP. Concomitant use has been observed to increase flecainide levels by over 2-fold in some patients. Due to the extremely long half-life of amiodarone, a drug interaction is possible for days to weeks after drug discontinuation.
Amisulpride: (Major) Monitor ECGs for QT prolongation when amisulpride is administered with flecainide. Amisulpride causes dose- and concentration- dependent QT prolongation. Flecainide is a Class IC antiarrhythmic associated with a possible risk for QT prolongation and/or TdP; flecainide increases the QT interval, but largely due to prolongation of the QRS interval. Although causality for TdP has not been established for flecainide, patients receiving concurrent drugs that have the potential for QT prolongation may have an increased risk of developing proarrhythmias.

Description
  Text
  Flecainide is an oral, local anesthetic-type antiarrhythmic. It is classified as category IC, similar to encainide, propafenone, and moricizine, and is used to treat documented life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias. Flecainide is also indicated for conversion to and/or maintenance of sinus rhythm in patients with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and/or atrial flutter associated with disabling symptoms and without structural heart disease. It is approved for the prevention of various forms of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT) caused by both reentrant and non-reentrant mechanisms. As a result of the Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial (CAST), flecainide is not considered a first-line agent to suppress ventricular arrhythmias because of its propensity for fatal proarrhythmic effects. Class IC agents as a whole markedly depress cardiac conduction, which might explain their proarrhythmic effects. Flecainide should not be used in patients with left ventricular dysfunction or recent acute myocardial infarction. Flecainide was approved by the FDA in October 1985. Generic flecainide tablets were approved in July 2002.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  As a class, the IC agents have three primary electrophysiological effects. Potent inhibition of the fast sodium channels depresses the upstroke of the action potential, which is manifested as a decrease in the maximal rate of phase 0 depolarization. Secondly, the IC agents significantly slow His-Purkinje conduction and cause QRS widening. Finally, these agents shorten the action potential of Purkinje fibers without affecting the surrounding myocardial tissue.
Flecainide inhibits the fast sodium channels of the myocardial cell membrane, thereby increasing the recovery period after repolarization. The drug can inhibit extracellular calcium influx but only at high doses. Flecainide does not exhibit vagomimetic, vagolytic, or beta-adrenergic blocking properties. It slows intracardiac conduction and can slightly increase action potential duration in atrial and ventricular muscle. The PR, QRS, and QT intervals are usually prolonged.
 
Flecainide acts on the antegrade pathways of anomalous AV conduction, thereby terminating paroxysmal reentrant supraventricular tachycardias. In patients with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome, flecainide increases the refractoriness of the anterograde and/or retrograde pathways by decreasing conduction in these or accessory pathways. Although it may have mild to moderate negative intropic effects, flecainide generally has only minimal cardiovascular effects; blood pressure, heart rate, and left ventricular function are usually unchanged.

    Pediatrics
    Description
    Infants, Children, and Adolescents
In a pharmacokinetic study of pediatric patients receiving flecainide (n = 26), the elimination half-life was longer and the plasma clearance slower in infants and adolescents compared to children. The elimination half-life was approximately 11 to 12 hours in infants, 8 hours in children, and 11 to 12 hours in adolescents. The mean volume of distribution was 9.5 L/kg and did not vary with age.
 
Neonates
Based on limited data, the elimination half-life is prolonged in neonatal patients compared to older pediatric patients and may be as long as 29 hours at birth decreasing to 11 to 12 hours by 3 months of age.
    Name
    Geriatric
    Description
    Flecainide elimination from plasma is somewhat slower in elderly subjects compared to younger subjects.
  Text
  Flecainide is administered orally. It is 40% to 50% bound to plasma proteins, primarily alpha-1-acid glycoprotein (AGP). It appears to be well distributed, with a volume of distribution of approximately 7 L/kg. Although it is not known whether flecainide crosses the placenta, it does distribute into maternal breast milk. Recommended therapeutic serum concentrations are 0.2 to 1 mcg/mL. It is metabolized by dealkylation and conjugation to at least 5 metabolites that are excreted in the urine. Approximately 30% of the drug is excreted via renal pathways and 5% is excreted fecally. The terminal plasma half-life ranges from about 12 to 30 hours and the pharmacodynamic duration of activity is 1 to 2 days.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes: CYP2D6
Flecainide is primarily metabolized by CYP2D6. It has a narrow therapeutic range and caution is advised when administering with CYP2D6 inhibitors.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Use in patients with hepatic impairment only if benefits clearly outweigh the risks due to slower flecainide elimination. Frequent and early plasma drug monitoring is required for patients with severe hepatic disease. Adjust dosage at intervals of more than 4 days, since steady-state conditions may take longer to achieve in these patients.
  Renal Impairment
  Adult patients:
CrCl > 35 mL/min: No dosage adjustment is required.
CrCl <= 35 mL/min: Initially, 100 mg PO once daily or 50 mg PO twice daily. Frequent plasma concentration monitoring is required. Adjust dosage at intervals of more than 4 days, since steady-state conditions may take longer to achieve in these patients.
 
Pediatric patients: Specific recommendations for dosage adjustment in pediatric patients with renal impairment are not available.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
See dosage for patients with CrCl <= 35 mL/min. No dosage supplement is needed for hemodialysis. Hemodialysis removes about 1% of an oral dose of unchanged flecainide.
