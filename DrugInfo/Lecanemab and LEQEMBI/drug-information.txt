Drug Class: Lecanemab is an amyloid beta-directed antibody indicated for the treatment of Alzheimer's disease (AD).

    Headline
    amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, cerebral edema, intracerebral hemorrhage, superficial siderosis
    General Information
    Monoclonal antibodies directed against aggregated forms of beta amyloid, including lecanemab, can cause amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) and these are addressed in a boxed warning in the product label. ARIA can be fatal. These abnormalities may appear as ARIA with edema (ARIA-E), which can be observed on brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as cerebral edema or sulcal effusions. ARIA with hemosiderin deposition (ARIA-H), which includes microhemorrhage (small areas of intracranial bleeding) and superficial siderosis (hemosiderosis), may also occur. ARIA can occur spontaneously in those with Alzheimer's disease, particularly in individuals with MRI findings suggestive of cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), such as evidence of prior intracerebral hemorrhage, cerebral microhemorrhage, or cortical superficial siderosis. CAA has an increased risk for intracerebral hemorrhage. The baseline presence of at least 2 microhemorrhages or the presence of at least 1 area of superficial siderosis on MRI, which may be suggestive of CAA, have been identified as risk factors for ARIA. In clinical studies, individuals were excluded from enrollment for the presence of more than 4 microhemorrhages and additional findings suggestive of CAA (such as prior cerebral hemorrhage greater than 1 cm in diameter, superficial siderosis, or vasogenic edema) or other lesions (aneurysm, vascular malformation) that could potentially increase the risk of intracranial bleeding. ARIA-H associated with monoclonal antibodies directed against aggregated forms of beta amyloid generally occurs in association with an occurrence of ARIA-E. ARIA usually occur early in treatment and are often asymptomatic, although serious and life-threatening events, including seizure and status epilepticus, can rarely occur. When present, reported symptoms associated with ARIA may include headache, confusion, visual changes, dizziness, nausea, and gait difficulty. Focal neurologic deficits may also occur. Symptoms associated with ARIA typically resolve over time. In order to determine baseline status and monitor for ARIA, obtain a recent baseline brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) prior to initiating treatment and prior to the third, fifth, seventh, and fourteenth infusion. In general, the MRI should be performed within approximately 1 week before the scheduled infusion and reviewed prior to proceeding with the infusion. Enhanced clinical vigilance for ARIA is particularly recommended during the first 14 weeks of treatment, but monitor treated individuals throughout therapy. If ARIA occur, the severity of symptoms (if present) or severity of ARIA-E or -H may affect decisions to continue or suspend treatment. For individuals experiencing moderate or severe symptoms of ARIA-E, moderate or severe severity of ARIA-E, or mild to moderate severity of ARIA-H, suspend treatment until the MRI demonstrates radiographic resolution and symptoms (if present) resolve. Perform an additional follow-up MRI 2 to 4 months following initial identification to assess for resolution of ARIA. Likewise, suspend treatment in those who develop severe ARIA-H as shown on MRI until radiographic stabilization and symptoms (if present) resolve; use clinical judgment to decide to resume treatment or permanently discontinue lecanemab. The risk of experiencing ARIA, including symptomatic and serious ARIA, may also be related to the individual's genetics. In a clinical trial of lecanemab, the incidence of ARIA was higher in individuals who were apolipoprotein Eepsilon4 (ApoEepsilon4) homozygotes (45% on lecanemab vs 22% on placebo) than in heterozygotes (19% on lecanemab vs 9% on placebo) and noncarriers (13% on lecanemab vs 4% on placebo). Symptomatic ARIA occurred in 9% of ApoEepsilon4 homozygotes, 2% of heterozygotes, and 1% noncarriers. Serious ARIA events occurred in 3% of homozygotes and approximately 1% of heterozygotes and noncarriers. The recommendations for management of ARIA do not differ between ApoEepsilon4 carriers and noncarriers; however, testing for ApoEepsilon4 should be performed prior to initiation of treatment to educate individuals or caregivers of the risk of developing ARIA. Prescribers should inform individuals that if genotype testing is not performed they can still be treated with lecanemab; however, it cannot be determined if they are ApoEepsilon4 homozygotes and at higher risk for ARIA. In addition to ARIA, serious intracerebral hemorrhages greater than 1 cm in diameter have occurred in individuals treated with lecanemab. Fatal events of intracerebral hemorrhage have also been observed in individuals taking lecanemab. Because ARIA-E can cause focal neurological deficits that can mimic an ischemic stroke, treating clinicians should consider whether such symptoms could be due to ARIA-E before giving thrombolytic agents to individuals being treated with lecanemab. Consider the benefit of lecanemab for the treatment of Alzheimer's disease and the potential risk of serious adverse events associated with ARIA when deciding to initiate treatment with lecanemab. If an individual experiences symptoms suggestive of ARIA, perform clinical evaluation, including MRI scanning if indicated. Adjustments to treatment dosing depend on clinical symptoms and/or radiographic severity of ARIA.

    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use lecanemab with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of lecanemab in human milk, the effects on the breastfed infant, or the effects of the drug on milk production. Published data from other monoclonal antibodies generally indicate low passage of monoclonal antibodies into human milk and limited systemic exposure in the breastfed infant. The effects of this limited exposure are unknown. The developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding should be considered along with the clinical need for lecanemab and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed infant from lecanemab or from the underlying maternal condition.

    Headline
    General Information
    General Information
    The safety of subcutaneous lecanemab administration once weekly was evaluated in open-label studies in 424 subjects. This included 72 lecanemab-naive subjects and 49 subjects who received subcutaneous lecanemab 360 mg, and 273 subjects who received subcutaneous 500 mg for treatment continuation. The overall safety profile in these subjects were similar to that observed in subjects who received lecanemab by intravenous infusion during clinical trials.
    Headline
    chills, fever, hypertension, hypotension, infusion-related reactions, injection site reaction
    General Information
    In 2 double-blind, placebo-controlled trials of intravenous lecanemab, infusion-related reactions occurred in 20% to 26% of subjects receiving lecanemab infusions compared to 3% to 7% of subjects receiving placebo. Most of these reactions (75%; 178/237) occurred with the first infusion; the majority were mild (69%) or moderate (28%) in severity. Infusion-related reactions resulted in discontinuation in 1% to 2% of subjects treated with intravenous lecanemab. Symptoms of infusion-related reactions include fever, flu-like symptoms (e.g., fever, chills), nausea, vomiting, changes in blood pressure (hypotension or hypertension), and oxygen desaturation. Infusion-related reactions can occur during or after completion of the infusion. In the event of an infusion-related reaction during the infusion, the infusion rate may be reduced, or the infusion may be discontinued, and appropriate therapy initiated as clinically indicated. Consider prophylactic treatment with antihistamines, acetaminophen, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or corticosteroids prior to future infusions. Localized injection site reaction and systemic injection-related reactions were observed in subjects receiving subcutaneous lecanemab during clinical trials. Localized injection-related reactions included erythema, induration, swelling, heat, pain, pruritis, rash, ecchymosis, and hematoma. Recurrent localized injection-related reactions and/or delayed reactions, occurring up to several days after an injection, were also observed. Systemic injection-related reactions were observed less frequently, with symptoms of headache, fever, and fatigue. Severe localized injection-related reactions and localized injection-related reactions leading to discontinuation or interruption of subcutaneous lecanemab did occur.

    amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, cerebral edema, confusion, dizziness, gait disturbance, hemosiderosis, intracranial bleeding, seizures, visual impairment
    General Information
    In double-blind, placebo-controlled studies in individuals with Alzheimer's disease, 21% (191/989) of intravenous lecanemab recipients experienced amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) compared to 9% (84/897) with placebo. Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities that were edema-related (ARIA-E), which may be observed on MRI as cerebral edema or sulcal effusions, were observed in 13% (113/898) of intravenous lecanemab recipients compared to 2% (15/897) with placebo. In clinical studies, the majority of ARIA-E radiographic events occurred early in treatment (within the first 7 doses), although ARIA can occur at any time and there can be more than 1 episode. The maximum radiographic severity of ARIA-E was mild in 4% (37/898), moderate in 7% (66/898), and severe in 1% (9/898) of intravenous lecanemab recipients. Resolution on MRI occurred in 52% of ARIA-E individuals by 12 weeks, 81% by 17 weeks, and 100% overall after detection. Among intravenous lecanemab recipients, the rate of severe radiographic ARIA-E was highest in ApoEepsilon4 homozygotes (5%; 7/141), compared to heterozygotes (0.4%; 2/479) or noncarriers (0%; 0/278). ARIA associated with hemosiderin deposition (ARIA-H), which includes microhemorrhage (small areas of intracranial bleeding) and hemosiderosis, was reported in 17% (152/898) of intravenous lecanemab recipients compared to 9% (80/897) with placebo. In clinical studies, the maximum radiographic severity of ARIA-H microhemorrhage was mild in 9% (79/898), moderate in 2% (19/898), and severe in 3% (28/898) of intravenous lecanemab recipients; superficial siderosis was mild in 4% (38/898), moderate in 1% (8/898), and severe in 0.4% (4/898). Among intravenous lecanemab recipients, the rate of severe radiographic ARIA-H was highest in ApoEepsilon4 homozygotes (13.5%; 19/141) compared to heterozygotes (2.1%; 10/479) or noncarriers (1.1%; 3/278). There was no increase in isolated ARIA-H (i.e., ARIA-H in individuals who did not also experience ARIA-E) for intravenous lecanemab treatment compared to placebo. Serious intracerebral hemorrhages of greater than 1 cm have occurred in individuals treated with this class of medications and fatal events have been observed. Intracerebral hemorrhage greater than 1 cm in diameter was reported in 0.7% (6/898) of intravenous lecanemab treated subjects compared to 0.1% (1/897) with placebo. While ARIA is usually asymptomatic, serious and life-threatening events can occur, including seizures and status epilepticus. When present, symptoms associated with ARIA may include headache, confusion, visual impairment, dizziness, nausea, and gait disturbance. ARIA-E can cause focal neurologic deficits that can mimic ischemic stroke. Symptoms associated with ARIA usually resolve over time. In the placebo-controlled studies of intravenous lecanemab, symptomatic ARIA occurred in 3% (29/898) of intravenous lecanemab recipients; serious symptoms were reported in 0.7% (6/898). Clinical symptoms resolved in 79% (23/29) of these individuals during the period of observation. In general, the MRI should be performed within approximately 1 week before the scheduled infusion and reviewed prior to proceeding with the infusion. If an individual experiences symptoms suggestive of ARIA, perform clinical evaluation, including MRI scanning if indicated.     antibody formation
    General Information
    Antibody formation against lecanemab has been reported. However, because of limited data, there is insufficient information to characterize the effects of anti-lecanemab antibodies on pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, safety, or effectiveness of intravenous or subcutaneous lecanemab. During the 18-month treatment period in Study 2, 3.4% (30/883) of subjects treated with lecanemab 10 mg/kg intravenously every 2 weeks developed treatment-emergent anti-lecanemab antibodies, and 1.9% (17/885) developed neutralizing antibodies. Among lecanemab-treated subjects who developed anti-lecanemab antibodies, mean trough concentrations of lecanemab were lower at various time points compared to subjects who did not develop anti-lecanemab antibodies. Although there was no clear clinically meaningful impact of anti-drug antibodies (ADA) (including neutralizing antibodies) on the pharmacodynamics, safety, or efficacy in any individual receiving lecanemab over the treatment duration of 18 months, given the low incidence of ADA, the available data are too limited to conclusively assess the impact of ADAs on the safety and efficacy of lecanemab. Subjects who received 360 mg or 500 mg of subcutaneous lecanemab were also assessed for immunogenicity. The incidence of anti-lecanemab antibodies was 2% (1/49) after a median treatment duration of 83 days with subcutaneous dosing of 360 mg/week and 1.4% (3/211) after a median treatment duration of 85 days with subcutaneous dosing of 500 mg/week. In a substudy of lecanemab-naive subjects, the incidence of anti-lecanemab antibodies was 3.2% (2/63) after a median treatment duration of 18 months with subcutaneous lecanemab at exposures comparable to those achieved with intravenous lecanemab.

Description
  Text
  Lecanemab is an amyloid beta-directed antibody indicated for the treatment of Alzheimer's disease (AD). Treatment should be initiated in people with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or mild dementia stage of AD, the population in which treatment was initiated in clinical trials. There are no safety or effectiveness data on initiating treatment at earlier or later stages of the disease than were studied. Treatment may be initiated by intravenous infusion (Leqembi) or subcutaneous injection (Leqembi IQLIK). The subcutaneous formulation provides the first FDA-approved at-home starting treatment option for individuals with AD, allowing appropriately trained individuals or caregivers to administer treatment with care team guidance and supervision. After 18 months of the starting regimen, treatment may continue with the initial regimen or transition to an intravenous or subcutaneous maintenance regimen. The effectiveness of subcutaneous lecanemab is based on the established effectiveness of intravenous lecanemab, supported by comparable pharmacokinetic exposures and reductions in amyloid beta plaque concentrations. The results from Clarity AD were used to determine full FDA approval of lecanemab. The Clarity AD trial was a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 1,795 subjects (age: 50 to 90 years) with early AD and evidence of amyloid on PET or cerebrospinal fluid testing. Subjects were randomized to receive intravenous lecanemab every 2 weeks or placebo. Primary efficacy endpoint was the change in score on the CDR-SB from baseline at 18 months, while key secondary endpoints were change from baseline at 18 months in amyloid burden on PET, and scores on ADAS-Cog14, the ADCOMS, and the score on the Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study-Activities of Daily Living Scale for Mild Cognitive Impairment (ADCS-MCI-ADL). CSF and plasma biomarkers were also assessed. At the end of 18 months, the adjusted mean difference versus placebo in the CDR-SB score, ADCOMS, ADAS-Cog14, and ADCS-MCI-ADL were all statistically significant. A substudy of amyloid burden on PET (n = 698) found the mean amyloid concentration change from baseline was also statistically significant. After 18 months, subjects were transitioned to maintenance treatment, which was predicted to continue reduction in amyloid beta plaque concentrations and maintain reductions in plasma p-tau181. Lecanemab treatment may result in amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), which most commonly present as temporary swelling in areas of the brain that usually resolve over time and typically do not cause symptoms. For subjects who are symptomatic, typical ARIA symptoms may include headache, confusion, dizziness, vision changes, or nausea. Infusion-related reactions may occur with intravenous administration. Providers should encourage people to participate in real world data collection registries to help further the understanding of Alzheimer's disease and the impact of disease treatments. Providers and individuals can contact Eisai at 888-274-2378 for a list of currently enrolling programs. Additionally, the Alzheimer's Network for Treatment and Diagnostics (ALZ-NET) is a voluntary provider-enrolled registry that collects information on treatments for Alzheimer's disease, including lecanemab. Providers may obtain information about the registry at www.alz-net.org. The FDA approved lecanemab in January 2023 under the accelerated approval process based on clinical trials showing reduction in amyloid beta plaques; full FDA approval was granted in July 2023.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The accumulation of amyloid beta plaques in the brain is a defining pathophysiologic feature of Alzheimer's disease. Lecanemab is a humanized immunoglobulin gamma 1 (IgG1) monoclonal antibody directed against aggregated soluble and insoluble forms of amyloid beta, reducing amyloid beta plaques over time.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics

    Description
    Steady-state concentrations of lecanemab were reached after 6 weeks of 10 mg/kg IV infusion dosing every 2 weeks and systemic accumulation was 1.4-fold. The estimated mean steady state concentration (CV%) is estimated to be 156 mcg/mL (37.7) in people receiving 10 mg/kg once every 2 weeks, and 78.1 mcg/mL (37.7) in people receiving 10 mg/kg every 4 weeks. The Cmax and AUC of lecanemab increased dose proportionally in the dose range of 0.3 to 15 mg/kg following a single dose.
    Name
    Subcutaneous Route
    Description
    Following subcutaneous administration, the absolute bioavailability of 500 mg and 360 mg dosing was approximately 77.5% and 53%, respectively, and was not affected by the injection site (abdomen, upper thigh, or back of the upper arm). The estimated mean steady-state concentration (CV%) of 500 mg and 360 mg weekly subcutaneous lecanemab is 164 mcg/mL (45.3) and 83.1 mcg/mL (58.8), respectively. Using population pharmacokinetic modeling and simulation, initiation of therapy with the intravenous dosing regimen (10 mg/kg once every 2 weeks) or subcutaneous dosing regimen (500 mg once weekly) as well as maintenance of therapy with intravenous dosing regimen (10 mg/kg once every 4 weeks) or subcutaneous dosing regimen (360 mg once weekly) were predicted to have similar pharmacokinetic exposures.

Administration
  General Information
  For storage information, see the specific product information within the How Supplied section.
  Route Specific Administrations
    Administration Details
      Name
      Intravenous Administration
      Description
      Preparation of IV infusion:
Use aseptic technique when preparing. The injection solution must be further diluted as an infusion prior to administration.
Each vial is for single-use only and is preservative-free. Discard any unused portion.
Calculate the dose and total volume of lecanemab solution required, and the number of vials needed based on the patient's actual body weight and the recommended dose of 10 mg/kg. Each vial contains a lecanemab concentration of 100 mg/mL; more than 1 vial may be needed for a full dose. Select the correct number of vial(s) for the required dose.
Withdraw the required volume of lecanemab from the vial(s) and add to an infusion bag of 250 mL of 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection. Do not use any other intravenous diluents to prepare the infusion.
Gently invert the infusion bag to mix completely; do not shake.
Storage: After dilution, immediate use is recommended. If not administered immediately, store the prepared infusion in the refrigerator at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) for up to 4 hours, or at room temperature up to 30 degrees C (86 degrees F) for up to 4 hours. Do not freeze.
 
IV infusion Administration:
Prior to infusion, allow the prepared IV infusion solution to warm to room temperature. Inspect before administration. Do not use if it is discolored, opaque, or if foreign particles are seen. Do not shake.
Infuse intravenously over approximately 1 hour through an IV line containing a terminal low-protein binding 0.2 micron in-line filter.
Flush infusion line to ensure all lecanemab is administered.
Monitor for any signs or symptoms of an infusion-related reaction during the infusion and consider longer periods of observation if clinically indicated. Infusion rate may be reduced or infusion may be interrupted or discontinued, and appropriate therapy administered as clinically indicated. Consider pre-medication with antihistamines, acetaminophen, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or corticosteroids prior to future infusions.
Missed Dose: If a starting dosage or maintenance dosage infusion is missed, administer the next dose as soon as possible.

Subcutaneous Administration
Before injection, remove lecanemab from the refrigerator and leave at room temperature for 20 minutes. Do not use an external heat source to warm because this may damage the product.
Do not shake. Do not use if it looks damaged or has been dropped.
Sites for injection include the abdomen [at least 2 inches (5 cm) from the umbilicus], upper thigh, or upper arm (only with help from care team or a caregiver). Do not inject into moles, scars, bruises, tattoos, or into areas where the skin is red, hard, tender or injured. Do not inject through clothing.
After the cap is removed, place the autoinjector at a 90-degree angle flat to the skin. Push the autoinjector firmly against the skin to start the injection. The injection will take about 10 seconds and the indicator window will show when the injection is complete.
After the injection is complete, continue to hold the injector against the skin for 5 more seconds. Then, pull the injector straight up, so the needle cover can move into place to cover the needle.
Throw away the used autoinjector in an FDA-cleared sharps disposal container.
Monitor for signs or symptoms of an injection reaction.
Rotate injection sites with each injection and administer at least 1 inch from the previous injection.
Storage: Store autoinjector(s) in the original carton(s) in the refrigerator at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F). Do not freeze. If needed, the autoinjector(s) can be stored at room temperature up to 25 degrees C (77 degrees F) in the original carton for up to 14 days. Once stored at room temperature, do not return it to the refrigerator. Discard autoinjector(s) if these conditions are exceeded.
