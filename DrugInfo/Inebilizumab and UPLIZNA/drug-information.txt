  General Information
  General dosing information:
Perform hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection screening in all individuals before inebilizumab initiation. Inebilizumab is contraindicated in individuals with active HBV infection, confirmed by positive results for surface antigen [HBsAg] and anti-HBV tests. Consult liver disease experts before starting and during treatment with inebilizumab for individuals who are negative for HBsAg and positive for HB core antibody [HBcAb+] or are carriers of HBV [HBsAg+].
Evaluate for active tuberculosis and test for latent infection before initiating treatment with inebilizumab. Consult infectious disease experts before initiating treatment with inebilizumab for individuals with active tuberculosis or positive tuberculosis screening without a history of appropriate treatment.
Perform testing for quantitative serum immunoglobulins before starting inebilizumab treatment. Consult immunology experts before starting treatment with inebilizumab for individuals with low serum immunoglobulins.

    Headline
    history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use
    General Information
    Inebilizumab is contraindicated in individuals with a history of significant adverse event(s) associated with previous use, specifically life-threatening infusion-related reactions. Administration of inebilizumab requires an experienced clinician with access to appropriate medical support to manage potential severe reactions, including infusion reactions. To reduce the risk of an infusion reaction, administer premedication with a corticosteroid, an antihistamine, and an antipyretic. Monitor treated patients closely for at least 1 hour after the completion of the infusion. Management recommendations for infusion reactions depend on the type and severity of the reaction, which can range from mild to life-threatening. Permanently discontinue inebilizumab if a life-threatening or disabling infusion reaction occurs, and administer appropriate supportive treatment. For less severe reactions, management may include temporarily stopping the infusion, reducing the infusion rate, and/or administering symptomatic treatment.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    hypogammaglobulinemia
    General Information
    Perform testing for quantitative serum immunoglobulins before starting inebilizumab treatment. Consult immunology experts before starting treatment with inebilizumab for individuals with hypogammaglobulinemia (low serum immunoglobulins). A progressive and prolonged hypogammaglobulinemia or decline in the levels of total and individual immunoglobulins such as immunoglobulins G and M (IgG and IgM) may occur with inebilizumab treatment. Consider discontinuing therapy if a patient with low immunoglobulin G or M develops a serious opportunistic infection or recurrent infections, or if prolonged hypogammaglobulinemia requires treatment with intravenous immunoglobulins.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate data on the developmental risk associated with the use of inebilizumab during human pregnancy. Inebilizumab is a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody, and immunoglobulins are known to cross the placental barrier. Based on animal data, inebilizumab can cause fetal harm due to B-cell lymphopenia and reduce antibody response in offspring exposed to inebilizumab even after B-cell repletion. Transient peripheral B-cell depletion and lymphocytopenia have been reported in infants after maternal exposure to other B-cell depleting antibodies during pregnancy. B-cell concentrations in infants after maternal exposure to inebilizumab have not been studied in clinical trials, and the potential duration of B-cell depletion in such infants, and the impact of B-cell depletion on vaccine safety and effectiveness, is unknown. In animal studies, administration of inebilizumab to mice throughout organogenesis and lactation resulted in depletion of B cells and persistent reductions in immune function (even after repletion of B cells and lasting into adulthood) in offspring. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant individuals exposed to inebilizumab; information about the registry can be obtained at www.upliznapregnancyregistry.com or by calling 1-303-724-4644.
    Headline
    infection, tuberculosis
    General Information
    Delay inebilizumab initiation or continued treatment in individuals with an active infection until the infection has resolved. An increased risk of serious, life-threatening or fatal, bacterial, fungal, and new or reactivated viral infections has been observed with inebilizumab and other B-cell-depleting therapies. Inebilizumab treatment is contraindicated in people with active or untreated latent tuberculosis. Evaluate for active tuberculosis and test for latent infection before initiating treatment with inebilizumab. Consider anti-tuberculosis therapy before treatment initiation in individuals with a history of latent active tuberculosis in whom an adequate course of treatment cannot be confirmed, and for people with a negative test for latent tuberculosis but with risk factors for tuberculosis infection. Consult infectious disease experts before initiating treatment with inebilizumab for individuals with active tuberculosis or positive tuberculosis screening without a history of appropriate treatment.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Inebilizumab is associated with reproductive risk. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while receiving inebilizumab and for 6 months after the last infusion of inebilizumab. Treated patients who become pregnant or plan to become pregnant while taking inebilizumab should inform their health care provider.
    Headline
    hepatitis B infection
    General Information
    Inebilizumab is contraindicated in individuals with active hepatitis B infection, confirmed by positive results for surface antigen [HBsAg] and anti-hepatitis B virus (HBV) tests. Perform HBV screening in all individuals before initiation of inebilizumab treatment. Do not administer inebilizumab to individuals with active hepatitis. Consult liver disease experts before starting and during treatment with inebilizumab for individuals who are negative for HBsAg and positive for HB core antibody [HBcAb+] or are carriers of HBV [HBsAg+]. Fulminant hepatitis, hepatic failure, and death caused by HBV reactivation have occurred in people treated with B-cell depleting therapies. HBV reactivation was observed in an individual treated with inebilizumab during the generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) clinical trial.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Do not administer live or live-attenuated vaccines before confirming recovery of B-cell counts in neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero. Depletion of B-cells from inebilizumab in the exposed neonate or infant may increase the risks from live or live-attenuated vaccines. Non-live vaccines, as indicated, may be administered before recovery from B-cell and immunoglobulin concentration depletion; however, consider consultation with a qualified specialist to assess whether a protective immune response was mounted.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use inebilizumab with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of inebilizumab in human milk, the effects on a breastfed infant, or the effects on milk production. Inebilizumab is a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody, and human IgG is excreted in human milk. The potential for absorption of inebilizumab to lead to B-cell depletion in the breastfed infant is unknown. Consider the developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding along with the clinical need for inebilizumab and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed infant from inebilizumab or from the underlying maternal condition.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  Adverse Reactions
    Headline
    cough, cystitis, infection, influenza, pharyngitis, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy
    General Information
    An increased risk of serious, including life-threatening or fatal, bacterial, fungal, and new or reactivated viral, infections has been observed with other B-cell-depleting therapies. Delay inebilizumab administration in people with an active infection until the infection is resolved. During a randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial in subjects with neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), urinary tract infection (cystitis) was reported in 11% of inebilizumab-treated subjects (n = 161) compared to 10% of subjects given placebo (n = 52). Across both the randomized and open-label treatment, urinary tract infection (20%), naso-pharyngitis (13%), upper respiratory tract infection (8%), and influenza (7%) were among the most common infections reported in inebilizumab-treated subjects. In the immunoglobulin G4-related disease (IgG4-RD) randomized controlled and open-label periods, the most common infections reported by inebilizumab-treated subjects were upper respiratory tract infection (11%), naso-pharyngitis (10%), urinary tract infection (9%), and influenza (6%). During the IgG4-RD randomized controlled trial, infections reported among the 68 inebilizumab-treated subjects included urinary tract infection (12%), influenza, and pneumonia. During a randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial in subjects with generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG), urinary tract infection (cystitis) [7%], naso-pharyngitis (7%), and cough (7%) were observed. Fulminant hepatitis, hepatic failure, and death caused by HBV reactivation have occurred in people treated with B-cell depleting therapies. HBV reactivation was observed in an individual treated with inebilizumab during the gMG clinical trial and has also been observed in the postmarketing setting. People with chronic HBV infection were excluded from clinical trials. Also, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) is an opportunistic viral infection of the brain caused by the John Cunningham (JC) virus that typically only occurs in people who are immunocompromised, and that usually leads to death or severe disability. Although no confirmed cases of PML were identified in inebilizumab clinical trials, JC virus infection resulting in PML has been observed in subjects treated with other B-cell-depleting antibodies and other therapies that affect immune competence. In inebilizumab clinical trials, 1 subject died after the development of new brain lesions for which a definitive diagnosis could not be established, though the differential diagnosis included an atypical NMOSD relapse, PML, or acute disseminated encephalomyelitis. At the first sign or symptom suggestive of PML, withhold inebilizumab and perform an appropriate diagnostic evaluation. MRI findings may be apparent before clinical signs or symptoms. Typical symptoms associated with PML are diverse, progress over days to weeks, and include progressive weakness on 1 side of the body or clumsiness of limbs, disturbance of vision, and changes in thinking, memory, and orientation, leading to confusion and personality changes.

    Headline
    hypogammaglobulinemia, lymphopenia, neutropenia
    General Information
    Neutrophil counts between 1,000 and 1,500 cells/mcL were observed in 7% of neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) subjects treated with inebilizumab compared to 2% of subjects given placebo. Neutrophil counts between 500 and 1,000 cells/mcL were observed in 2% of inebilizumab-treated subjects compared to no subjects given placebo. At the end of the 6.5 month randomized, placebo-controlled trial period, 12% of inebilizumab-treated subjects (n = 161) had a neutrophil count below the limit of normal (neutropenia) compared to 4% of subjects given placebo (n = 52). During the 12 month randomized, controlled trial in immunoglobulin G4-related disease (IgG4-RD) subjects (n = 68), 8% of inebilizumab-treated subjects had neutrophil counts between 1,000 and 1,500 cells/mcL versus 3% of subjects in the placebo group. Neutrophil counts between 500 and 1,000 cells/mcL were observed in none of the inebilizumab-treated subjects and in 2% of subjects given placebo. Overall, neutropenia was reported in 6% of IgG4-RD subjects who received inebilizumab compared to 5% who received placebo. During the 26 week randomized, controlled period, neutrophil counts between 1,000 and 1,500 cells/mcL were observed in 3% of generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) subjects who received placebo compared to no subjects who received inebilizumab. Neutrophil counts between 500 and 1,000 cells/mcL were observed in 1% of subjects treated with inebilizumab compared to no subjects who received placebo. A reduction of lymphocyte counts below the limit of normal (lymphopenia) was observed more frequently in subjects treated with inebilizumab compared to placebo. Lymphopenia was observed in 5% of NMOSD subjects treated with inebilizumab compared to 4% of subjects given placebo at the end of the 6.5-month controlled period. During the 12 month randomized, controlled trial in IgG4-RD subjects, lymphopenia was observed in 42% of inebilizumab-treated subjects compared to 36% of subjects who received placebo. Overall, lymphopenia was reported in 19% of IgG4-RD subjects who received inebilizumab compared to 9% who received placebo. During the 26 week randomized, controlled period, a reduction in lymphocyte counts was observed in 35% of subjects with gMG treated with inebilizumab compared to 31% of subjects who received placebo. There may be a progressive and prolonged hypogammaglobulinemia or decline in the concentrations of total and individual immunoglobulins such as immunoglobulins G and M (IgG and IgM) with continued inebilizumab treatment. At the end of the 6.5 month randomized, placebo-controlled trial period in NMOSD subjects, the total immunoglobulin concentration was reduced by approximately 8% from baseline for inebilizumab-treated subjects compared to an increase of 6% in subjects given placebo. The mean decreases from baseline in immunoglobulin G (IgG) and immunoglobulin M (IgM) were approximately 4% and 32%, respectively, in inebilizumab-treated subjects, whereas IgG was increased by 6% and IgM was increased by approximately 13% in subjects given placebo. The proportion of subjects treated with inebilizumab who had IgG concentrations below the lower limit of normal at year 1 was 7% and at year 2 was 13%. The proportion of subjects treated with inebilizumab who had IgM concentrations below the lower limit of normal at year 1 was 31% and at year 2 was 42%. At the end of the 12 month randomized, controlled period in IgG4-RD subjects, the total immunoglobulin concentration was reduced by 12% from baseline for subjects treated with inebilizumab compared to an increase of 21% for subjects in the placebo group. The mean decreases from baseline in IgG and IgM were 9% and 32%, respectively, in inebilizumab-treated subjects compared to an increase in IgG and IgM of 26% and 3%, respectively, in placebo subjects. At the end of the 26 week randomized, controlled period, relative to baseline, trial in subjects with gMG, the total Ig concentration was reduced approximately 13% from baseline for subjects treated with inebilizumab as compared to an increase of 15% in subjects treated with placebo. The mean decreases from baseline in IgG and IgM were approximately 8% and 30%, respectively, in subjects treated with inebilizumab, whereas IgG was increased by 18% and IgM was increased by approximately 5% in placebo-treated subjects. The proportion of subjects treated with inebilizumab who had IgG and IgM concentrations below the lower limit of normal at the end of the 26 week randomized, controlled period was 29% and 16%, respectively, compared to 8% and 4%, respectively, in the placebo group. Monitor the concentrations of quantitative serum immunoglobulins during treatment with inebilizumab, especially in people with opportunistic or recurrent infections, and until B-cell repletion after discontinuation of therapy. Consider discontinuing inebilizumab therapy if an individual with low IgG or IgM develops a serious opportunistic infection or recurrent infections, or if prolonged hypogammaglobulinemia requires treatment with intravenous immunoglobulins.

Description
  Text
  Inebilizumab is an intravenous CD19-directed cytolytic humanized afucosylated IgG1 monoclonal antibody indicated for the treatment of neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) in adults who are anti-aquaporin-4 (AQP4) antibody-positive. The drug is also approved to treat adults with immunoglobulin G4-related disease (IgG4-RD). It is also indicated for the treatment of generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) in adults who are anti-acetylcholine receptor (AChR) or anti-muscle specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK) antibody-positive. The FDA has designated inebilizumab an orphan drug for NMOSD and gMG indications. Prior to starting treatment, screen potential drug recipients for hepatitis B virus and active or latent tuberculosis, test for quantitative serum immunoglobulins, and administer all immunizations according to guidelines (at least 4 weeks before for live or live-attenuated vaccines). Infusion-related reactions have been reported with inebilizumab. These reactions are most common with the first infusion but may also occur during subsequent infusions. Administration of inebilizumab requires an experienced clinician with access to appropriate medical support to manage potential severe reactions, such as serious infusion reactions. To reduce the risk of an infusion reaction, premedication with a corticosteroid, an antihistamine, and an antipyretic is recommended. Inebilizumab received initial FDA approval in 2020.
 
In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of subjects with NMOSD who were anti-AQP4 antibody-positive (n = 213) or anti-AQP4 antibody-negative (n = 17), the time to first adjudicated relapse was significantly longer in subjects treated with inebilizumab compared to placebo (relative risk reduction 73%; HR 0.272; p less than 0.0001). In the anti-AQP4 antibody-positive population, there was a 77.3% relative reduction (HR 0.227; p less than 0.0001). There was no evidence of a benefit in subjects who were anti-AQP4 antibody-negative. Compared to subjects given placebo, inebilizumab-treated subjects who were anti-AQP4 antibody-positive had reduced annualized rates of hospitalizations (0.11) vs. placebo (0.5).
 
The efficacy of inebilizumab in treated IgG4-RD was evaluated in a 52 week, randomized, double-blind trial involving 135 adults with newly diagnosed or recurrent IgG4-RD that required glucocorticoid treatment and who had a confirmed history of organ involvement at some point in the course of the disease. The time to the First Treated and Adjudicated Committee-determined IgG4-RD flare was significantly longer for the 68 subjects randomized to receive inebilizumab than for the 67 subjects in the placebo group. The number of subjects who experienced a flare during the 52 week period was lower in the inebilizumab group (n = 7 of 68) as compared to placebo (40 of 67). Inebilizumab reduced the risk of treated and AC-determined IgG4-RD flare by 87% (HR: 0.13; 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.28; p less than 0.0001).
 
The efficacy of inebilizumab in treated gMG was evaluated in a randomized, double-blind, multicenter, placebo-controlled trial. The treatment period was 52 weeks for subjects who were anti-AChR antibody-positive and 26 weeks for subjects who were anti-MuSK antibody-positive. A statistically significant difference favoring inebilizumab was observed in the mean change from baseline in Myasthenia Gravis-Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) total score (-4.2 points in the inebilizumab-treated group compared to -2.2 points for placebo, difference of -1.9, 95% CI: -2.9, -1; p less than 0.0001). A statistically significant difference favoring inebilizumab was observed in the mean change from baseline in Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG) total score in the overall population (-4.8 points in the inebilizumab -treated group compared to -2.3 for placebo, difference -2.5, 95% CI: -3.8, -1.2; p = 0.0002).

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The mechanism by which inebilizumab-cdon exerts its therapeutic effects in neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), immunoglobulin G4-related disease (IgG4-RD), and generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) is presumed to involve binding to CD19, a cell surface antigen presents on pre-B and mature B lymphocytes. Following cell surface binding to B lymphocytes, inebilizumab-cdon results in antibody-dependent cellular cytolysis.
 
Pharmacodynamics
Pharmacodynamics of inebilizumab were assessed using an assay for CD20+ B-cells, since the drug can interfere with the CD19+ B-cell assay. Treatment with inebilizumab reduces blood CD20+ B-cell counts by 8 days after drug administration. In 1 study, CD20+ B-cell counts were reduced below the lower limits of normal (LLN) by Week 4 in 100% of drug recipients and remained below LLN for 28 weeks after initiation of therapy in 94% of subjects. Another study showed similar effects on CD20+ B-cell counts, with counts being below LLN by Week 2 in all drug recipients and remaining below LLN in 85% of subjects for up to 52 weeks after starting treatment. In an additional study, CD20+ B-cell counts were reduced below the LLN by Week 4 in 99% and more drug recipients who were anti-acetylcholine receptor (AChR) or anti-muscle specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK) antibody-positive. B-cell counts remained below the LLN in 96% or more of drug recipients in the anti-AChR antibody-positive population for 52 weeks after initiation of treatment, and in more than 96% of drug recipients in the anti-MuSK antibody-positive population for 26 weeks after initiation of treatment.

  Text
  Inebilizumab is administered via intravenous infusion. Pharmacokinetics are biphasic after intravenous administration. The estimated typical central and peripheral Vd of inebilizumab was 2.95 L and 2.57 L, respectively. Inebilizumab is degraded by proteolytic enzymes distributed throughout the body. The estimated systemic clearance of the first-order elimination pathway is 0.19 L/day. The mean terminal half-life of inebilizumab is 18 days. At low concentrations, inebilizumab is likely subject to CD19-mediated clearance, which decreases with time because of B-cell depletion. Inebilizumab reduces CD20+ B-cell counts in blood by 8 days after infusion.

      Description
      Before every inebilizumab infusion, determine whether there is an active infection. In case of an active infection, delay inebilizumab administration until the infection resolves.
Administration of inebilizumab requires an experienced clinician with access to appropriate medical support to manage potential severe reactions, such as serious infusion reactions.
To reduce the risk of an infusion reaction, administer premedication with a corticosteroid (methylprednisolone 80 to 125 mg IV or equivalent) 30 minutes before the inebilizumab infusion and an antihistamine (diphenhydramine 25 to 50 mg PO or equivalent) and an antipyretic (acetaminophen 500 to 650 mg PO or equivalent) 30 to 60 minutes before the inebilizumab infusion.
Monitor patients closely for at least 1 hour after the completion of the infusion.
 
Dilution
Do not shake inebilizumab vials.
Withdraw 10 mL from each of the 3 inebilizumab vials contained in the carton.
Transfer a total of 30 mL into a 250 mL IV bag of 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection. Do not use other diluents.
Gently invert the infusion bag to mix the diluted solution. Do not shake the solution.
Administer the prepared solution immediately.
Storage: Discard any unused portion remaining in the vials. If not administered immediately, store the infusion solution for a maximum of 24 hours under refrigeration at 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F) or 4 hours at room temperature of 20 to 25 degrees C (68 to 77 degrees F). Do not freeze.
 
Intermittent IV Infusion
Ensure the prepared infusion solution is at room temperature before the start of the IV infusion.
Administer through an IV line containing a sterile, low-protein binding 0.2 or 0.22-micron in-line filter.
Administer the dose IV via an infusion pump at an increasing rate to completion over approximately 90 minutes. Infuse at 42 mL/hour for the first 30 minutes, 125 mL/hour for the next 30 minutes, and then 333 mL/hour until completion.
