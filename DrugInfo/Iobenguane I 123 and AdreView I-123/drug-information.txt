Drug Class: Iobenguane I 123 for injection (AdreView) is a radiopharmaceutical diagnostic agent used in the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma or neuroblastoma.

Routes: intravenous; Common adult doses: 10 mCi (370 MBq).

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): dizziness, flushing, headache, hematoma, injection site reaction, pruritus, rash

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Iobenguane is contraindicated in patients with hypersensitivity to iobenguane or iobenguane sulfate. Iobenguane I 123 is classified as FDA pregnancy category C.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Iobenguane is contraindicated in patients with hypersensitivity to iobenguane or iobenguane sulfate.
 
Iobenguane I 123 is classified as FDA pregnancy category C. Radiopharmaceuticals have the potential to cause fetal harm. It is not known if iobenguane I 123 causes fetal harm when administered to a pregnant woman. Iobenguane I 123 should be given to a pregnant woman only if clearly needed and the expected benefit outweighs the potential risk.
 
It is not known whether iobenguane is excreted into human breast milk; iodine 123 is excreted into breast milk. According to the manufacturer, because many drugs are excreted into human milk and there is potential for serious adverse reactions in nursing infants, a decision should be made whether to interrupt breast-feeding after administration of iobenguane I 123 or not to administer iobenguane I 123. Based on the physical half-life of iodine 123 (13.2 hours) nursing women may consider interrupting nursing for 6 days after iobenguane I 123 administration in order to minimize the risk to nursing infants. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally administered drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
The safety and efficacy of iobenguane I 123 have not been established in infants < 1 month old. Iobenguane I 123 contains benzyl alcohol at a concentration of 10.3 mg/ml. Benzyl alcohol has been associated with fatal “gasping syndrome” in premature neonates and infants of low birth weight. Exposure to excessive amounts of benzyl alcohol has been associated with hypertension and metabolic acidosis, particularly in neonates, and an increased incidence of kernicterus, particularly in small preterm infants. Observe infants for signs or symptoms of benzyl alcohol toxicity.
 
Iobenguane I 123 contains benzyl alcohol at a concentration of 10.3 mg/ml. Patients with benzyl alcohol hypersensitivity should receive iobenguane I 123 with caution. Additionally, benzyl alcohol has been associated with fatal “gasping syndrome” in premature neonates and infants of low birth weight. Exposure to excessive amounts of benzyl alcohol has been associated with hypertension and metabolic acidosis, particularly in neonates, and an increased incidence of kernicterus, particularly in small preterm infants. Observe infants for signs or symptoms of benzyl alcohol toxicity.
 
The safety and efficacy of iobenguane I 123 have not been established in patients with severe renal impairment and diagnostic evaluation may be limited in these patients. Iobenguane I 123 is cleared by glomerular filtration and is not dialyzable. Patients with severe renal impairment may be exposed to an increased radiation dose due to decreased elimination. Delayed iobenguane I 123 clearance may also decrease the quality of scintigraphic images by reducing target to background ratios.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Before iobenguane I 123 administration, assess individuals for a history of prior reactions to iodine, an iodine-containing contrast agent, or other products containing iodine. If the individual is known or strongly suspected to have hypersensitivity to iodine, an iodine-containing contrast agent, or other products containing iodine, base the decision to administer iobenguane I 123 upon an assessment of the expected benefits compared to the potential hypersensitivity risks. Ensure anaphylactic and hypersensitivity treatment measures are available before iobenguane I 123 administration.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Iobenguane I 123 is cleared by glomerular filtration and is not dialyzable. The radiation dose to individuals with severe renal impairment or renal failure may be increased due to the delayed elimination of the drug. Delayed iobenguane I 123 clearance may also reduce the target to background ratios and decrease the quality of scintigraphic images. These risks may limit the role of iobenguane I 123 in individuals with severe renal impairment. The safety and efficacy of iobenguane I 123 have not been established in individuals with severe renal impairment.
    Headline
    multiple system atrophy, parkinsonism
    General Information
    Individuals with conditions that affect the sympathetic nervous system, e.g., parkinsonism, such as Parkinson disease, or multiple system atrophy, may show decreased cardiac uptake of iobenguane I 123 independent of heart disease.
    Headline
    family history of thyroid cancer, personal history of thyroid cancer
    General Information
    Before administration of iobenguane I 123 to individuals at risk for thyroid accumulation of the drug, such as young individuals or those with a personal history of thyroid cancer or a family history of thyroid cancer, administer potassium iodide oral solution or Lugol's solution (equivalent to 100 mg iodide for adults, body-weight adjusted for children) or potassium perchlorate (400 mg for adults, body-weight adjusted for children) to block uptake of iodine 123 by the individual's thyroid. Administer the blocking agent at least 1 hour before the dose of iobenguane I 123. Individualize thyroid blockade; for example, the blockade may not be necessary for individuals who have undergone thyroidectomy or those with a very limited life expectancy. Failure to block thyroid uptake of iodine 123 may result in an increased long term risk for thyroid neoplasia.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, infants, neonates, premature neonates
    General Information
    Serious and fatal adverse reactions, including "gasping syndrome", can occur in neonates and infants treated with benzyl alcohol-preserved drugs, including iobenguane I 123. The "gasping syndrome" is characterized by central nervous system depression, metabolic acidosis, and gasping respirations. Additional adverse reactions include gradual neurological deterioration, seizures, intracranial hemorrhage, hematologic abnormalities, skin breakdown, hepatic and renal failure, hypotension, bradycardia, and cardiovascular collapse. Premature neonates and low birth weight infants may be more likely to develop these reactions because they may be less able to metabolize benzyl alcohol. When administering iobenguane I 123 to infants, consider the combined daily metabolic load of benzyl alcohol from all sources, including iobenguane I 123 (10.3 mg benzyl alcohol per mL) and other drugs containing benzyl alcohol. The minimum amount of benzyl alcohol at which serious adverse reactions may occur is not known. However, benzyl alcohol doses of 99 to 234 mg/kg/day produced high concentrations of benzyl alcohol and its metabolites in the blood and urine of premature neonates and infants in the neonatal intensive care unit who received medications containing benzyl alcohol as a preservative; benzyl alcohol blood concentrations were 0.61 to 1.378 mmol/L. Neonates, infants, children, and adolescents may also be at risk for thyroid accumulation of iobenguane I 123. Before administration of iobenguane I 123 to young individuals, administer potassium iodide oral solution or Lugol's solution (equivalent to 100 mg iodide for adults and body-weight adjusted for children) or potassium perchlorate (400 mg for adults and body-weight adjusted for children) to block uptake of iodine 123 by the individual's thyroid. Administer the blocking agent at least 1 hour before the dose of iobenguane I 123. Individualize thyroid blockade; for example, the blockade may not be necessary for individuals who have undergone thyroidectomy or those with a very limited life expectancy. Failure to block thyroid uptake of iodine 123 may result in an increased long term risk for thyroid neoplasia.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    All radiopharmaceuticals, including iobenguane I 123, have the potential to cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy depending on stage of fetal development and radiation dose. Radioactive iodine crosses the placenta and can permanently impair fetal thyroid function. Administer an appropriate thyroid blocking agent before the use of iobenguane I 123 in pregnant individuals to protect the patient and fetus from accumulation of I 123. Advise pregnant patients of the potential risk. No data are available on the use of iobenguane I 123 during human pregnancy to evaluate for a drug-associated risk of birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse pregnancy outcomes. Animal reproduction studies have not been conducted. Iobenguane I 123 contains 10.3 mg/mL of benzyl alcohol. Because benzyl alcohol is rapidly metabolized during pregnancy, benzyl alcohol exposure in the fetus is unlikely. However, adverse reactions have occurred in premature neonates and lower birth weight infants who received benzyl alcohol-containing drugs.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with iobenguane I 123. Verify pregnancy status prior to administration in those of reproductive potential.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding for at least 6 days (more than 10 physical half-lives) after iobenguane I 123 administration, to minimize radiation exposure to the breastfed child. Iobenguane I 123 is present in human milk. There are no data on the effects of the drug on the breastfed child or on milk production.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Iobenguane I 123 did not produce serious adverse reactions in clinical studies conducted in 251 patients. Adverse reactions that occurred in isolated cases (<= 2 patients) include dizziness, rash, pruritus, flushing, and injection site hemorrhage.
 
Iobenguane I 123 may cause release of norepinephrine from chromaffin granules resulting in transient hypertension; however, this effect was not observed in the clinical study. Ensure that emergency cardiac and anti-hypertensive treatments are available prior to treatment. Monitor blood pressure prior to and 30 minutes after iobenguane I 123 administration.
 
The long term risk for the secondary malignancy, thyroid neoplasia, may be increased by thyroid uptake of iodine 123. Administer thyroid blocking medications (e.g. SSKI, Lugol’s solution) prior to the administration of iobenguane I 123 (see Dosage).
 
Anaphylactoid reactions have occurred after the administration of iobenguane I 131. In patients who are known or suspected to have hypersensitivity to iodine, an iodine-containing contrast agent, or other products containing iodine, the decision to administer iobenguane I 123 should be based on an assessment of the benefits compared to the potential risks. Anaphylactic and hypersensitivity treatment measures should be available prior to the administration of iobenguane I 123.

Description
  Text
  Iobenguane I 123 for injection (AdreView) is a radiopharmaceutical diagnostic agent used in the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma or neuroblastoma. The drug is also indicated for use in patients with New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II/III heart failure and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <= 35% to help identify lower 1 and 2 year mortality risks. Iobenguane is taken up into adrenergically innervated tissues in a similar fashion as norepinephrine. The addition of the isotope iodine 123 allows gamma-scintigraphic images to be obtained in tissues where iobenguane I 123 has accumulated. Iodine 123 emits gamma radiation only. In December 2008, iobenguane I 123 received FDA approval for use in the detection of primary or metastatic pheochromocytoma or neuroblastoma as an adjunct to other diagnostic tests. In March 2013, the FDA approved its use to help identify lower 1 and 2 year mortality risks in patients with class II/III heart failure and LVEF <= 35%.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Iobenguane I 123 is a diagnostic radiopharmaceutical administered via intravenous injection. Iobenguane is structurally similar to norepinephrine and undergoes a similar uptake and accumulation pathway. Iobenguane is taken up by the norepinephrine transporter on adrenergic nerve terminals. As a result, iobenguane accumulates in tissues that have high adrenergic innervation such as the adrenal medulla, heart, salivary glands, liver, spleen, and lungs. Iobenguane is also taken up by tumors that are derived from the neural crest. Iobenguane I 123 is labeled with the isotope iodine 123, a gamma-radiation emitter. The addition of iodine 123 to iobenguane makes it possible to obtain scintigraphic images of the organs and tissues where the radiopharmaceutical has accumulated.

Pharmacokinetics
  Special Populations
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    Iobenguane is primarily excreted unchanged via glomerular filtration. Patients with severe renal impairment may have an increased radiation exposure and impaired imaging results. Iobenguane I 123 is not dialyzable.
  Text
  Iobenguane I 123 is administered as an intravenous injection. After administration, iobenguane I 123 is rapidly cleared from the blood stream and accumulates in adrenergically innervated tissues. Iobenguane I 123 retention is prolonged in tissues that are highly adrenergically innervated (e.g., heart, salivary glands, adrenal medulla). Iobenguane I 123 is a diagnostic radiopharmaceutical agent that contains a small amount of iobenguane (0.08 mg/mL) and is not expected to produce a pharmacodynamic effect related to iobenguane. The thyroid gland should be blocked prior to administration to reduce the amount of drug uptake and radiation exposure to this organ.
 
Iobenguane I 123 is excreted unchanged in the urine via glomerular filtration. Circulating iobenguane is cleared rapidly by the kidneys. Iobenguane in tissues is cleared more slowly as it is released back into the bloodstream. After administration, 70—90% of iobenguane is recovered unchanged in the urine within 4 days. The majority of remaining radioactivity recovered in the urine is in the form of the radioiodinated metabolite m-iodohippuric acid (MIHA) and free radioiodide. The enzymatic metabolism and pharmacologic activity of these metabolites is unclear. Iodine 123 decays to Te 123 by electron capture and has a physical half-life of 13.2 hours.

    Administration Details
      Name
      Intravenous Administration
      Description
      Use aseptic technique and a radiation shielding syringe during administration.
Administer as an intravenous injection over 1 to 2 minutes.
Normal saline flush may be administered after iobenguane I 123 to ensure complete delivery of drug.
To minimize radiation exposure to the bladder, encourage hydration before and after administration and frequent voiding for 48 hours after administration.
For pheochromocytoma and neuroblastoma: Obtain whole body planar scintigraphic images 18—30 hours after administration. If appropriate, single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) may be performed after planar scintigraphy.
For heart failure: Obtain anterior planar scintigraphic imagines of the chest 4 hours after administration. SPECT may be performed after planar imaging. For all imaging, a low-energy high-resolution collimator is recommended. The recommended matrix for planar images is 128x128. Position the camera to include the entire heart and as much of the upper chest as possible.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; however, it appears that no dosage adjustments are needed.
  Renal Impairment
  Safety and efficacy have not been established in patients with severe renal impairment. Iobenguane I 123 is eliminated via glomerular filtration; radiation exposure may be increased in patients with severe renal impairment due to delayed elimination of the drug. Scintigraphic image quality may also suffer as delayed iobenguane I 123 clearance may reduce the target to background ratios. Iobenguane I 123 is not dialyzable.
