        Dosage Description
        Safety and efficacy have been evaluated; however, glimepiride is not generally recommended in pediatric patients as a first line agent because of its adverse effects on body weight and hypoglycemia compared to metformin. In a manufacturer-sponsored clinical trial of pediatric patients 8 to 17 years, the overall mean treatment difference in A1C values between glimepiride and metformin was 0.2%, favoring metformin (95% confidence interval -0.3% to +0.6%); the trial did not meet its primary objective of showing a similar reduction in A1C compared to metformin. Glimepiride was initiated at 1 mg PO once daily, and then titrated up to 2, 4, or 8 mg/day (mean last dose 4 mg/day) through Week 12, targeting a self-monitored fasting fingerstick blood glucose less than 126 mg/dL. Hypoglycemic events were observed in 4% of pediatric patients treated with glimepiride vs. 1% of those treated with metformin. One patient in each treatment group experienced a severe hypoglycemic episode. Use of glimepiride vs. metformin has also been described in the literature, with similar outcomes, in pediatric patients 8 to 17 years. Glimepiride was initiated at a dose of 1 mg PO once daily in the morning and titrated as needed by doubling the dose at weeks 4, 8, and 12 according to blood glucose goals. Max dose: 8 mg/day PO. The mean final dose for glimepiride was 3.8 mg/day (range 1 to 8 mg/day PO). While both metformin and glimepiride were similarly effective in treating diabetes, weight gain occurred and hyperglycemia rates were higher in the glimepiride group.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Glimepiride should not be used in patients with a known sulfonylurea hypersensitivity.
 
It may be prudent to monitor patients with a known history of sulfonamide hypersensitivity for allergic-type reactions when initiating glimepiride. Although they contain a sulfonamide side chain, sulfonylureas and other nonantibiotic sulfonamides do not contain the N4 aromatic amine or the N1-substituent that are present in sulfonamide antibiotics and thought to be responsible for hypersensitivity-type adverse reactions; the risk of cross-sensitivity in patients taking a nonantibiotic sulfonamide that have a history of sulfonamide hypersensitivity is low and has been confirmed by recent, observational studies. However, several cases in the literature report of cross-sensitivity reactions to sulfonylureas in patients with a history of sulfonamide hypersensitivity. A 57 year-old man with a self-reported sulfonamide allergy (unknown offending agent) and stable on hydrochlorothiazide and glyburide experienced possible erythema multiforme, an acute inflammatory skin reaction, and throat swelling within 30 days after initiating celecoxib, which contains a sulfonamide side chain. Although the skin reaction resolved with celecoxib discontinuation, a similar reaction occurred when glyburide and hydrochlorothiazide therapies were re-introduced. In another case report, a 71 year-old man with multiple, documented drug allergies including Stevens-Johnson syndrome to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole experienced a rash after receiving furosemide and after receiving glyburide, both of which contain a sulfonamide side chain. It should be noted that this patient also had a history of several non-sulfonamide allergies; he subsequently received torsemide, which also contains a sulfonamide side chain, without problems. In general, patients with a history of hypersensitivity to any drug are predisposed for subsequent hypersensitivity reactions to other drugs. Because of this, patients with a history of sulfonamide hypersensitivity should be monitored for hypersensitivity reactions to other drugs, including sulfonylureas; however, treatment with a nonantibiotic sulfonamide may not need to be withheld in patients with a sulfonamide allergy as long as patients are monitored appropriately, especially if alternative therapies are not available.
 
Glimepiride should not be used in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus. Glimepiride is contraindicated in patients with diabetic ketoacidosis, with or without coma; this condition should be treated with insulin.
 
Severe hypoglycemia is possible with all sulfonylureas, including glimepiride. Proper patient selection and education are necessary to avoid hypoglycemic episodes. Elderly patients may be more susceptible to the hypoglycemic effects of glimepiride. Hypoglycemia can be more difficult to detect in the elderly and in patients with autonomic neuropathy. Patients with renal disease or hepatic disease are more sensitive to the hypoglycemic effects of glimepiride. The elderly and patients with hepatic disease, renal impairment, or renal failure should be initiated at the lower end of the adult dosage range for glimepiride (see Dosage). Other patients who might be susceptible to severe hypoglycemia include patients with malnourishment, thyroid disease, adrenal insufficiency, or pituitary insufficiency.
 
Patients stabilized on glimepiride can lose control of their blood glucose when exposed to stress such as fever, trauma, infection, or surgery. Glucose control can also diminish over time (secondary failure). Insulin therapy may be necessary alone or in combination with glimepiride for these cases.
 
There are conflicting studies regarding the possible cardiovascular risks associated with the use of oral sulfonylurea antidiabetic agents. The largest of the trials, the United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS), has demonstrated that intensive therapy with sulfonylureas does not increase the risk of myocardial infarction or diabetes-related death when compared to conventional therapy. In this trial, lowering blood glucose with sulfonylurea therapy did not significantly effect cardiovascular complications. A 16% reduction (not statistically significant) in the risk of combined fatal or nonfatal myocardial infarction and sudden death has been reported. In a follow-up study to the UKPDS, researchers found that after 10-years of resuming typical care, patients originally randomized to intensive therapy with sulfonylureas or insulin had a 15% relative reduction (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.74—0.97; p = 0.01) in the risk of myocardial infarction and a 13% relative decrease (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.76—0.96; p = 0.007) in the risk of death from any cause as compared to patients originally randomized to conventional therapy; it should be noted that these reductions in cardiovascular risks persisted even though HbA1c concentrations were similar in the 2 groups after 1 year of follow-up. In contrast, the University Group Diabetes Program (UGDP) has previously reported that the administration of oral sulfonylureas increases cardiovascular mortality compared with dietary management alone, or dietary management and insulin therapy. The UGDP study has been widely criticized for study limitations including a small sample size (i.e., 200 patients per treatment group). Despite the controversy regarding these findings, the results of the UGDP study serve as a basis for the manufacturers' warning of possible risk of cardiovascular mortality associated with the use of oral sulfonylureas.
 
In animal studies, glimepiride and other sulfonylureas have been associated with an increased risk of intrauterine fetal death. No adequate, well-controlled studies have been performed in women, and glimepiride has been designated pregnancy category C. In general, abnormal blood glucose concentrations during pregnancy increase the risk of congenital abnormalities. Thus, many practitioners recommend insulin to treat diabetes during pregnancy.
 
It is not known whether glimepiride is excreted in human milk, but other sulfonylureas are. Due to the risk of hypoglycemia in the infant, the manufacturer recommends that glimepiride be discontinued in breast-feeding mothers. If therapy other than diet and exercise is needed, insulin is recommended.
 
The safe and effective use of glimepiride has not been established in children. However, glimepiride (n = 135) vs. metformin (n = 137) was evaluated in an active-controlled, 24-week trial of children aged 8—17 years with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Glimepiride was initiated at a dosage of 1 mg/day PO and titrated to 2, 4, or 8 mg/day PO until a fasting blood glucose concentration of < 126 mg/dl was achieved. Metformin was dosed initially at 500 mg PO twice daily up to a maximum of 1000 mg PO twice daily. In the group of patients naive to antidiabetic therapy, glycosylated hemoglobin decreased by 1 % in patients treated with glimepiride versus 1.2% in patients treated with metformin; in the group of patients that were not naive to antidiabetic therapy, glycosylated hemoglobin changed by a 0.2% increase in patients treated with glimepiride vs. a 0.2% decrease in patients treated with metformin. As expected, hypoglycemic rates were higher in patients treated with glimepiride (4% for glimepiride vs. 1% for metformin). Adverse events in children treated with glimepiride were similar to those events reported in adult patients.
 
Patients with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency (G6PD deficiency) who take glimepiride may be at risk for hemolytic anemia; consider using a non-sulfonylurea alternative in these patients.

    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Use glimepiride with caution in people with renal impairment or renal failure. A reduced initial dosage of glimepiride is recommended for individuals with renal impairment or renal failure, followed by cautious dosage titration. If a sulfonylurea must be used, shorter-acting agents, such as glipizide or glimepiride, are preferred over glyburide in those with renal impairment; glipizide is generally considered the preferred agent.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    The Beers Criteria recommend avoidance of sulfonylureas as first or second-line monotherapy or add-on therapy in the geriatric adult unless there are substantial barriers to the use of safer and more effective agents. If a sulfonylurea is used, choose a short-acting agent (e.g., glipizide) over longer-acting agents (e.g., glyburide or glimepiride), which confer a higher risk of prolonged hypoglycemia. Sulfonylureas in general have a higher risk of cardiovascular (CV) events, all-cause mortality, and hypoglycemia than alternative agents. Sulfonylureas may also increase the risk of CV death and ischemic stroke. Use conservative initial, titration, and maintenance doses of glimepiride in older adults to help avoid hypoglycemic reactions, with close monitoring. Hypoglycemia can be more difficult to detect in the older adult.
    Headline
    obstetric delivery, pregnancy
    General Information
    Glimepiride is not recommended for use during pregnancy; the potential maternal benefits must be weighed against the potential risks to the fetus. Available data for glimepiride from a small number of published studies and postmarketing experience over decades have not identified any drug-associated risks for major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse maternal outcomes. However, fetotoxicity has been noted with glimepiride and other sulfonylureas in animal studies. The effect is perinatal and believed to be directly related to the pharmacologic (hypoglycemic) action. In studies in rats and rabbits, no teratogenic effects were found. Sulfonylureas like glimepiride are not likely to provide good glucose control in pregnant women who cannot be controlled on diet alone. If glimepiride is used during pregnancy, it should be discontinued at least 2 weeks prior to the expected obstetric delivery date and the neonate should be observed for symptoms of hypoglycemia and respiratory distress. In general, abnormal blood glucose concentrations during pregnancy increase the risk of congenital abnormalities. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the American Diabetes Association (ADA) continue to recommend human insulin as the standard of care in pregnant individuals with diabetes mellitus and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) requiring medical therapy; insulin does not cross the placenta.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use glimepiride with caution during breast-feeding. Breastfed infants of lactating individuals using glimepiride should be monitored for symptoms of hypoglycemia, such as poor feeding, increased fussiness, or lethargy. It is unknown whether glimepiride is excreted in human milk or the effects of glimepiride on milk production; other sulfonylureas are known to be excreted in human milk. Consider the developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding along with the clinical need for glimepiride and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed infant from glimepiride or the underlying maternal condition. If glimepiride is discontinued and blood glucose is not controlled on diet and exercise alone, insulin therapy may be considered. Other oral hypoglycemics may also be considered. Metformin monotherapy may be appropriate for some patients as available studies indicate low excretion in milk and that maternal use during breast-feeding is not expected to result in side effects to a healthy breastfed infant. Some experts recommend using metformin with caution if the patient is breast-feeding a newborn or a premature neonate with reduced renal function. Because acarbose has limited systemic absorption, which results in minimal maternal plasma concentrations, clinically significant exposure via breast milk is not expected; therefore, this agent may be an alternative if postprandial glucose control is needed. Glyburide may be a suitable alternative since it was not detected in the breast milk of lactating individuals who received single and multiple doses of glyburide. If any oral hypoglycemics are used during breast-feeding, the breastfed infant should be monitored for signs of hypoglycemia, such as increased fussiness or somnolence.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero at the time of delivery have developed prolonged severe hypoglycemia (4 to 10 days). This has been reported more frequently with the use of sulfonylurea agents with prolonged half-lives. If maternal glimepiride has been discontinued 2 weeks prior to delivery as recommended, this risk is reduced. If maternal glimepiride has not been discontinued, then exposed neonates should be monitored for hypoglycemia or other risks. Neonates exposed to sulfonylureas in utero may be at increased risk for neonatal intensive care admission and may develop respiratory distress, hypoglycemia, birth injury, and be large for gestational age.

Acebutolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Atenolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Labetalol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. Nebivolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Octreotide: (Moderate) Monitor patients receiving octreotide concomitantly with insulin or other antidiabetic agents for changes in glycemic control and adjust doses of these medications accordingly. Octreotide alters the balance between the counter-regulatory hormones of insulin, glucagon, and growth hormone, which may result in hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia. The hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia which occurs during octreotide acetate therapy is usually mild but may result in overt diabetes mellitus or necessitate dose changes in insulin or other hypoglycemic agents. In patients with concomitant type1 diabetes mellitus, octreotide is likely to affect glucose regulation, and insulin requirements may be reduced. Symptomatic hypoglycemia, which may be severe, has been reported in type 1 diabetic patients. In Type 2 diabetes patients with partially intact insulin reserves, octreotide administration may result in decreases in plasma insulin levels and hyperglycemia.

Vemurafenib: (Moderate) Concomitant use of vemurafenib and glimepiride may result in increased glimepiride concentrations. Vemurafenib is a CYP2C9 inhibitor and glimepiride is a CYP2C9 substrate. Monitor serum glucose concentrations if glimepiride is coadministered with CYP2C9 inhibitors. Dosage adjustments may be necessary.
Verteporfin: (Moderate) Use caution if coadministration of verteporfin with sulfonylureas is necessary due to the risk of increased photosensitivity. Verteporfin is a light-activated drug used in photodynamic therapy; all patients treated with verteporfin will be photosensitive. Concomitant use of other photosensitizing agents like sulfonylureas may increase the risk of a photosensitivity reaction.
Vitamin B Complex Supplements: (Moderate) Niacin (nicotinic acid) interferes with glucose metabolism and can result in hyperglycemia. Changes in glycemic control can usually be corrected through modification of hypoglycemic therapy. Monitor patients taking antidiabetic agents for changes in glycemic control if niacin (nicotinic acid) is added or deleted to the medication regimen. Dosage adjustments may be necessary.
Vonoprazan; Amoxicillin; Clarithromycin: (Moderate) The concomitant use of clarithromycin and antidiabetic agents can result in significant hypoglycemia. Careful monitoring of blood glucose is recommended.
Voriconazole: (Moderate) Voriconazole should be used cautiously with sulfonylureas. The combination of voriconazole and oral antidiabetic agents may result in severe hypoglycemia. Voriconazole may inhibit the metabolism of sulfonylureas. Blood glucose concentrations should be monitored and possible dose adjustments of hypoglycemics may need to be made.
Warfarin: (Moderate) Monitor blood glucose and INR during concomitant use of sulfonylureas and warfarin. Concomitant use may increase the risk of hypoglycemia and produce an unpredictable effect on warfarin response, potentially increasing the risk of bleeding or reducing warfarin efficacy. These effects may be related to alterations in protein binding, although the exact mechanism of interaction is unknown.
Zafirlukast: (Moderate) Glimepiride is metabolized by CYP2C9. It is possible for serum concentrations of glimepiride to rise when coadministered with drugs that inhibit CYP2C9 like zafirlukast. Monitor serum glucose concentrations if glimepiride is coadministered with zafirlukast. Dosage adjustments may be necessary.
Ziprasidone: (Moderate) Monitor blood glucose during concomitant atypical antipsychotic and sulfonylurea use. Atypical antipsychotic therapy may aggravate diabetes mellitus. Atypical antipsychotics have been associated with metabolic changes, including hyperglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar, hyperglycemic states, and diabetic coma. Possible mechanisms include atypical antipsychotic-induced insulin resistance or direct beta-cell inhibition.

  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  In general, therapy with glimepiride appears to be safe and well-tolerated; the profile of adverse reactions in pediatric patients is similar to that observed in adults. Adverse events that were reported in placebo-controlled trials in more than 1% of patients included dizziness (1.7% vs. 0.3% placebo), asthenia (1.6% vs. 1%), headache (1.5% vs. 1.4%), and nausea/vomiting (1.1% vs. 0%). These changes are believed to be due to changes in blood glucose and can increase or become pronounced during the initiation of therapy. Untreated diabetics can also experience blurred vision, and therapy might actually reduce this condition. In placebo-controlled trials of glimepiride, the incidence of blurred vision was 0.4% for glimepiride vs. 0.7% placebo. Hypoglycemia, which can be severe, can occur during glimepiride therapy and is manifested as hunger, pallor, nausea, fatigue, perspiration, headache, palpitations, numbness of the mouth, tingling in the fingers, tremors, muscle weakness, blurred vision, hypothermia, uncontrolled yawning, irritability, mental confusion, tachycardia, shallow breathing, or loss of consciousness. Hypoglycemia can be a result of excessive dosage, but it also could be due to other factors such as improper diet or excessive physical activity. In addition, sulfonylureas cross the placenta, and the newborns of women who have been receiving the drug have reportedly developed severe hypoglycemia.
 
Gastrointestinal effects that have occurred in fewer than 1% of patients treated with glimepiride in clinical trials include gastrointestinal pain (abdominal pain), nausea/vomiting and diarrhea. Elevated hepatic enzymes occur rarely. Isolated cases of cholestasis and jaundice and hepatitis have been reported rarely with sulfonylureas including glimepiride.
 
Adverse dermatological reactions have been reported in fewer than 1% of patients treated with glimepiride. These include pruritus, erythema, urticaria, and morbilliform or maculopapular rash. Porphyria cutanea tarda, photosensitivity, and allergic vasculitis have been reported with sulfonylureas including glimepiride.
 
Leukopenia, agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, aplastic anemia, and pancytopenia have been reported with sulfonylureas, including glimepiride. The incidence of these effects with glimepiride is not known.
 
Cases of hyponatremia have been reported with all sulfonylureas including glimepiride. The incidence appears to be greater in patients on other medications or with other diseases that cause hyponatremia or antidiuretic hormone secretion. SIADH secretion has been reported with other sulfonylureas, including glimepiride. It has been suggested that certain sulfonylureas may augment the antidiuretic action of ADH and/or increase its release.
 
Disulfiram reactions and hepatic porphyria reactions have been reported with sulfonylureas, including glimepiride.

    General Information
    The ability of many antidiabetic agents, including glimepiride, to effectively lower blood sugar may decrease over time; this process is called secondary failure. Since the mid-1950's and prior to the advent of alternative or add-on antidiabetic treatments, secondary failure rates due to sulfonylureas were estimated to be 21.8% in the first year of treatment and in up to 46.8% of individuals within 2 years. In contemporary use of antidiabetic treatments, secondary failure can be defined by the point at which the individual- 1) must add treatments or switch to another antihyperglycemic drug after 6 months of sulfonylurea monotherapy OR 2) has the first A1C measurement that exceeds 8% following monotherapy. Most individuals taking sulfonylurea monotherapy will switch or add antihyperglycemic drugs at some point in the first 7 years of treatment. Secondary failure of sulfonylurea therapy is inversely associated with the level of A1C achieved within the first year of monotherapy; 50% of those with A1C measurements of 7 to 7.9 will have laboratory progression within 24 months, whereas it can take 74 months or longer for progression in those individuals achieving A1C values of less than 7%. The exact reason for this phenomenon is not known, but may be due to the progression of the disease or decreased responsiveness to the drug. Secondary failure is not to be confused with primary failure, which occurs when the antidiabetic drug fails to control blood sugar when it is first given to the individual.

Description
  Text
  Glimepiride is an oral sulfonylurea. It is a member of the 'second-generation' of high potency sulfonylurea agents that include glyburide and glipizide. Compared to glyburide, glimepiride appears to have less propensity to cause hypoglycemia and is metabolized to metabolites that are weakly active; the pharmacokinetic properties make it an option in this class for the geriatric adult and those with renal dysfunction. Glimepiride is used as an adjunct to diet to lower blood glucose in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Sulfonylureas are not optimal antidiabetic agents for the pediatric population. In clinical trials of glimepiride in children and adolescents (8 to 17 years), primary objectives of showing a similar reduction in A1C compared to metformin were not met and more glimepiride-treated subjects experienced hypoglycemia and weight gain compared to metformin. First-line T2DM therapy depends on comorbidities, individual-centered treatment factors, and management needs. In individuals with T2DM with or at high risk for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), heart failure (HF), and/or chronic kidney disease (CKD), certain glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA's) or sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors with proven benefit are appropriate initial therapy, with or without metformin based on glycemic needs. In individuals with T2DM who do not have ASCVD/indicators of high-risk, HF, or CKD, sulfonylureas are an option that, as a class, generally reduce hemoglobin A1C by 1% to 2%. Disadvantages of sulfonylurea therapy include hypoglycemia (especially in the geriatric adult), weight gain, and frequent loss of efficacy over time; however, the weight gain is relatively modest in large cohort studies and the incidence of severe hypoglycemia is lower than with insulin. Of the second-generation agents, shorter-acting sulfonylureas, such as glipizide, are preferred in the geriatric adult and people with renal impairment over glyburide. Glimepiride also has favorable pharmacokinetics in these populations. Glimepiride was initially FDA approved in 1995.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Glimepiride lowers blood sugar by stimulating pancreatic islet cells, resulting in an increase in insulin secretion. Sulfonylureas are believed to bind to ATP-sensitive potassium-channel receptors on the pancreatic cell surface, thereby reducing potassium conductance and causing depolarization of the membrane. Depolarization stimulates calcium ion influx through voltage-sensitive calcium channels, raising intracellular concentrations of calcium ions, which induces the secretion, or exocytosis, of insulin. The drug is not effective in the absence of functioning beta-cells, as occurs in diabetes mellitus type 1, or when the number of viable beta-cells is low, as occurs in severe cases of diabetes mellitus type 2.
 
Prolonged administration of sulfonylureas also produces extrapancreatic effects that contribute to their hypoglycemic activity. These effects include reduction of basal hepatic glucose production and an enhanced peripheral sensitivity to insulin secondary to an increase in insulin receptors or to changes in the events that follow insulin-receptor binding. The relative importance of each of these actions to the overall therapeutic effect of the drug will vary among oral antidiabetic agents and from patient to patient, which might account for the variability in potency among these drugs. Glimepiride may enhance peripheral tissue insulin sensitivity to a greater degree in fatty tissue than in skeletal muscle.

    Renal Impairment
    Description
    The relative total clearance of glimepiride increased when kidney function was impaired (e.g., CrCl 10 to 60 mL/minute). Studies also demonstrated that the elimination of the 2 major metabolites (M1 and M2) was reduced in patients with renal impairment. In an animal model, M1 has about 1/3 the pharmacological activity of glimepiride in animal models; M2 is not active. In a small single-dose study was administered to patients with mild renal impairment (Group I, CrCl more than 50 mL/minute), patients with moderate renal impairment (Group II, CrCl 20 to 50 mL/minute) and patients with severe renal impairment (Group III, CrCl less than 20 mL/minute). Although glimepiride serum concentrations decreased with decreasing renal function, Group III had a 2.3-fold higher mean AUC for M1 and an 8.6-fold higher mean AUC for M2 compared to the mean AUCs in Group I. The apparent terminal half-life for glimepiride did not change, while the half-lives for M1 and M2 increased as renal function decreased. Mean urinary excretion of M1 plus M2 as a percentage of dose decreased from 44.4% for Group I to 21.9% for Group II and 9.3% for Group III.

  Text
  Glimepiride is administered orally. The pharmacokinetics of glimepiride does not differ between healthy subjects and patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. It is greater than 99.5% protein-bound. Glimepiride is completely oxidatively metabolized following oral administration. Two metabolites are formed, the cyclohexyl hydroxy methyl derivative (M1) and the carboxyl derivative (M2). The hepatic isoenzyme CYP2C9 has been shown to be involved in the biotransformation to M1. M1 is metabolized to M2 via an unknown pathway. M2 is not active. In an animal model, M1 has about 1/3 the pharmacological activity of glimepiride; the clinical significance of M1 on glucose-lowering effects is not clear. Glimepiride is excreted in the urine (60%) and feces (40%), predominantly in the form of M1 and M2. The metabolites M1 and M2 accounted for about 70% (ratio of M1 to M2 was 1:3) of the dose recovered in feces. No parent drug was recovered from urine or feces. The half-life of glimepiride is 5 hours initially, but increases to roughly 9 hours after multiple dosing. Glimepiride does not accumulate in serum following multiple dosing. Clearance of glimepiride after oral administration does not change over the 1 mg to 8 mg dose range, indicating linear pharmacokinetics.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP450) isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2C9
Sulfonylureas are substrates of the CYP2C9 isoenzyme; thus, coadministration with CYP2C9 inhibitors or inducers will increase or decrease, respectively, sulfonylurea concentrations.
