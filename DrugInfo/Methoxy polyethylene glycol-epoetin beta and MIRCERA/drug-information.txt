Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Evaluate the iron status before and during treatment. Administer supplemental iron when the serum ferritin is less than 100 mcg/L or when serum transferrin saturation is less than 20%.
Correct or exclude other causes of anemia such as vitamin deficiency, metabolic or chronic inflammatory conditions, and bleeding before initiating methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta (MPG-epoetin beta).
For lack or loss of hemoglobin response to MPG-epoetin beta, search for causative factors.
Conditions that may interfere with response to erythropoietin-stimulating agents (ESAs) include bleeding, infection, inflammation, malignancy, malnutrition, hypothyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, vitamin B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, iron deficiency, hematological disease (e.g., hemolytic anemia, sickle cell disease, thalassemia, refractory anemia), and bone marrow disorders, such as myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS).
If possible, correct the etiology such as vitamin B12, folate, or iron deficiency. In the absence of another etiology, evaluate for pure red cell aplasia (PRCA), including testing for the presence of antibodies to erythropoietin.
Monitor hemoglobin (Hgb) weekly until stable and sufficient to minimize the need for red blood cell (RBC) transfusions.
MPG-epoetin beta has not been shown to improve symptoms, physical functioning, or health-related quality of life.
MPG-epoetin beta is not indicated for use:
In the treatment of anemia due to cancer chemotherapy.
As a substitute for RBC transfusions in individuals who require immediate correction of anemia.
In adults with chronic kidney disease not on dialysis, initiate when the Hgb is less than 10 g/dL, the rate of Hgb decline indicates the likelihood of requiring an RBC transfusion, and reducing the risk of alloimmunization and/or other RBC transfusion-related risks is a goal.
In adults with chronic kidney disease on dialysis, initiate when the Hgb concentration is less than 10 g/dL
For all children with chronic kidney disease, base the Hgb concentration at which to initiate therapy on individual consideration of potential benefits (e.g., improvement in quality of life, school attendance/performance, and avoidance of transfusion) and potential harms.
In pediatric populations, a target Hgb concentration of 11 to 12 g/dL is recommended.
In individuals in whom all correctable causes for lack or loss of hemoglobin response have been maximally treated but who remain hyporesponsive, ESA therapy may be continued cautiously at doses up to 4 times the initial dose to prevent a further decline in hemoglobin concentration. Red blood cell transfusions can be used to prevent or treat anemia-related symptoms and signs.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Cases of pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) and of severe anemia, with or without other cytopenias, associated with neutralizing antibodies to erythropoietin have been reported in patients treated with erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs). This has been reported predominantly in patients with chronic kidney failure receiving an ESA by subcutaneous administration. Intravenous administration of MPG-epoetin beta is the preferred route of administration, as it may decrease the chance of anti-erythropoietin antibody formation. Any patient who develops a sudden loss of response to MPG-epoetin beta, accompanied by severe anemia and low reticulocyte count, should be evaluated for the etiology of loss of effect, including the presence of binding and neutralizing antibodies to erythropoietin. If anti-erythropoietin antibody-associated anemia is suspected, withhold MPG-epoetin beta. Contact Roche (800—526—6367) to perform assays for binding and neutralizing antibodies. MPG-epoetin beta should be permanently discontinued in patients with antibody-mediated anemia. Patients should not be switched to other erythropoietic proteins, as antibodies may cross-react.
 
MPG-epoetin beta is contraindicated in patients with a known hypersensitivity to MPG-epoetin beta. In addition, injectable solutions of MPG-epoetin beta in vials and prefilled syringes are formulated in an aqueous solution containing mannitol. Patients with mannitol hypersensitivity should not use MPG-epoetin beta.
 
Patients receiving dialysis who are marginally dialyzed may require adjustments in their dialysis prescription. Treatment with MPG-epoetin beta may result in an increase in red blood cells and a decrease in plasma volume, which could decrease the efficiency of dialysis. Administration of MPG-epoetin beta by the IV route is recommended for patients receiving hemodialysis, as the IV route may be less immunogenic as compared with the SC route. Also, a higher maintenance dose of MPG-epoetin beta may be needed for patients on dialysis as compared with the dose needed for patients not on dialysis. Patients who are not receiving dialysis may be more responsive to the effects of MPG-epoetin beta; judicious monitoring of blood pressure, hemoglobin, renal function, and fluid electrolyte balance is advised.
 
The risk for a blood clot such as deep venous thrombosis or pulmonary embolus may be higher for patients undergoing surgery. Blood clots can also form in the hemodialysis vascular access such as arteriovenous fistulas. The safety and efficacy of MPG-epoetin beta have not been established for reduction in the need for allogeneic RBC transfusion in the peri-surgical setting. In this conditions, clinical trials of ESAs have shown risks for thrombotic events and/or mortality.
 
In clinical trials involving similar ESAs, but not specifically MPG-epoetin beta, ESAs have been shown to increase the risk of death and serious cardiovascular and thromboembolic events (e.g. myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, hemodialysis vascular access thrombosis) in patients with renal failure or renal impairment treated to a target hemoglobin concentration greater than 12 g/dl. ESA doses should be adjusted to the lowest possible dose sufficient to maintain hemoglobin concentrations between 10—12 g/dl and prevent the need for red blood cell transfusions (see Dosage). Hemoglobin increases of greater than 1 g/dl in a 2 week period may also contribute to the incidence of these adverse effects. In addition, patients with chronic renal failure who do not respond to treatment with an ESA may be at an even higher risk for these effects. Increased mortality was also observed in patients receiving an ESA to reduce the need for red blood cell transfusion when undergoing coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG). The majority of deaths were secondary to thrombotic events. Furthermore, in a study of patients not receiving prophylactic anticoagulation undergoing orthopedic procedures receiving an ESA to reduce dependence on red blood cell transfusion, thrombotic events were more frequent in the arm receiving the ESA. Additional caution should be exercised when administering an ESA to patients with pre-existing cardiac disease or thromboembolic disease. The safety and efficacy of MPG-epoetin-beta have not been established for use among patients with anemia due to cancer chemotherapy or for reduction in the need for allogeneic RBC transfusion in the peri-surgical setting. In these conditions, clinical trials of ESAs have shown risks for thrombotic events and/or mortality. Because the risk of thromboembolism is increased in many populations, the anticipated benefits of MPG-epoetin beta therapy in any patient with a history of thromboembolic disease should be weighed against the potential risks. NOTE: For more information, see adverse reactions section.
 
MPG-epoetin beta should not be used to treat patients with neoplastic disease or chemotherapy-induced anemia. A dose-ranging study of MPG-epoetin beta in patients undergoing chemotherapy for non-small cell lung cancer was terminated early due to significantly more deaths in the patients receiving MPG-epoetin beta compared to another ESA. Further information on the nature of these deaths is currently unavailable. In clinical trials with similar ESAs, but not specifically MPG-epoetin beta, patients with certain types of neoplastic disease (i.e. breast, head and neck, non-small cell lung, cervical, and lymphoid malignancies) experienced an increased incidence of thrombotic events, tumor progression, and/or death when receiving ESAs. These events occurred predominantly in patients treated to a target hemoglobin concentration >12 g/dl. However, the potential risk for tumor progression and increased mortality in patients treated to target hemoglobin concentrations 1 g/dl in any 2 week period). Hypertensive encephalopathy and seizures have occurred in patients treated with MPG-epoetin beta. Caution should be exercised when treating patients with a history of a seizure disorder. Blood pressure and premonitory neurologic symptoms should be monitored closely during therapy, with special attention in the first several months of treatment (see Adverse Reactions). Reduce or withhold the MPG-epoetin beta dose if blood pressure is difficult to control by pharmacologic or dietary measures.
 
Virtually all patients receiving MPG-epoetin beta therapy will require iron supplementation to achieve and maintain optimal erythropoietic response. Patients with iron-deficiency anemia should be treated with appropriate iron supplementation to correct the deficiency prior to initiation of MPG-epoetin beta. Concurrent iron-deficiency may develop in patients receiving treatment with MPG-epoetin beta, as iron stores are utilized in the production of red blood cells. Supplemental iron therapy should be given to patients with serum ferritin < 100 mcg/L or serum transferrin saturation < 20%. MPG-epoetin beta is not indicated in patients with iron-deficiency anemia or anemias due to acute or chronic blood loss. It is also not indicated for the immediate correction of severe anemia; MPG-epoetin beta therapy should not replace the need for an RBC transfusion in patients with emergent needs.
 
Conditions that may interfere with response to MPG-epoetin beta include infection, inflammation, malignancy, hemolytic anemia, occult blood loss, severe aluminum toxicity, osteitis fibrosis cystica, vitamin B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, hematological disease (e.g. thalassemia, refractory anemia or myelodysplastic disorders), or bone marrow fibrosis. Initiate a search for the cause of a lack of a hemoglobin response or failure to maintain a hemoglobin response with MPG-epoetin beta. If possible, correct the etiology such as vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. In the absence of another etiology, the patient should be evaluated for evidence of PRCA, including tests for the presence of antibodies to erythropoietin (see Adverse Reactions).
 
The safety and efficacy of epoetin alfa have not been established in patients with sickle cell disease or coagulopathy (i.e., hypercoagulable states).
 
MPG-epoetin beta is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C and should be used during pregnancy only when the benefits of treatment outweigh the risks. No adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women exist. The possibility of pregnancy and contraceptive considerations should be discussed with the patient.
 
It is not known if MPG-epoetin beta is excreted into breast milk. Due to the high molecular weight of approximately 60,000 daltons, it is likely that MPG-epoetin beta distributes poorly into breast milk. Although MPG-epoetin beta is similar to an endogenous hormone already present in breast milk, the unknown risk of administering MPG-epoetin beta to a breast-feeding woman should be weighed against the known benefit of continued breast-feeding.
 
Do not administer MPG-epoetin beta to children. The safety and efficacy of MPG-epoetin beta have not been established in this population.

    red cell aplasia
    General Information
    Methoxy polyethylene glycol (MPG)-epoetin beta is contraindicated in individuals with pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) that begins after treatment with an erythropoietin protein medication. Cases of PRCA and severe anemia, with or without other cytopenias associated with neutralizing antibodies to erythropoietin, have been reported in individuals treated with MPG-epoetin beta, predominantly in individuals with chronic kidney disease receiving an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) by subcutaneous administration.
    Headline
    cardiovascular disease, coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG), mortality, orthopedic surgery, thromboembolism
    General Information
    In controlled trials, subjects with chronic kidney disease experienced greater risks for mortality, serious adverse cardiovascular reactions, and stroke when administered erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) to target a hemoglobin concentration greater than 11 g/dL. No trial has identified a hemoglobin target concentration, ESA dose, or dosing strategy that does not increase these risks. Use the lowest ESA dose sufficient to reduce the need for red blood cell transfusions. For individuals with chronic kidney disease either on or off dialysis, a hemoglobin less than 10 g/dL is advised before methoxy polyethylene glycol (MPG)-epoetin beta initiation. In controlled clinical trials of subjects with chronic kidney disease comparing higher hemoglobin targets (13 to 14 g/dL) to lower targets (9 to 11.3 g/dL), ESAs increased the risk of death, myocardial infarction, stroke, congestive heart failure, thrombosis of vascular access, and other thromboembolism in the higher target groups. Using ESAs to target a hemoglobin concentration greater than 11 g/dL increases the risk of serious adverse cardiovascular reactions and has not been shown to provide additional benefit. Use ESAs with caution in individuals with coexistent cardiovascular disease and stroke. Individuals with chronic kidney disease and an insufficient hemoglobin response to ESA therapy may be at even greater risk for cardiovascular reactions and mortality than other populations. A rate of hemoglobin rise of greater than 1 g/dL over 2 weeks may contribute to these risks; a dose reduction is warranted. Additionally, ESAs increased the risk of death in adult subjects undergoing coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG) and the risk of deep venous thrombosis in individuals undergoing orthopedic surgery in controlled clinical trials. However, guidelines suggest it is reasonable to use ESAs with iron supplementation several days before cardiac surgery to increase red cell mass in individuals who have preoperative anemia, refuse blood cell transfusion, or are deemed high-risk for postoperative anemia; studies have reported no adverse effects associated with short-term ESA pretreatment.

    General Information

Advise individuals about the importance of compliance with antihypertensive therapy and dietary restrictions.

Methoxy polyethylene glycol (MPG)-epoetin beta is not indicated and is not recommended for the treatment of anemia due to cancer chemotherapy.     General Information
    Advise individuals with a history of seizure disorder to contact their health care provider for premonitory symptoms or a change in seizure frequency. Methoxy polyethylene glycol (MPG)-epoetin beta increases the risk of seizures in individuals with chronic kidney disease. During the first several months after MPG-epoetin beta initiation, closely monitor for premonitory neurologic symptoms.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    MPG-epoetin beta should be used during pregnancy only when the benefits of treatment outweighs any risk to the fetus. Available data from a small number of published case reports and postmarketing experience is insufficient to identify a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. Chronic kidney disease is associated with maternal and embryo-fetal risks. Animal studies have shown bone malformation and reduced fetal weights at doses 17-fold or more of the recommended human dose.
    Headline

    General Information

Although MPG-epoetin beta is similar to an endogenous hormone already present in breast milk, the unknown risk of administering MPG-epoetin beta to a breast-feeding woman should be weighed against the known benefit of continued breast-feeding. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.

Interactions
Lenalidomide: (Moderate) Monitor for signs and symptoms of thromboembolic events if concomitant use of lenalidomide and an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) is required. Both medications have been associated with venous thromboembolism and concomitant use may increase the risk of thrombosis.
Thalidomide: (Moderate) Monitor for signs and symptoms of thromboembolic events if concomitant use of thalidomide and an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) is required. Both medications have been associated with venous thromboembolism and concomitant use may increase the risk of thrombosis.
Vadadustat: (Major) Avoid concomitant use of vadadustat and erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESA), such as methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta. If ESA rescue therapy with methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta is necessary, interrupt vadadustat treatment. Resume vadadustat at the prior dose or with a dose that is 150 mg more than the prior dose when hemoglobin concentrations are 10 g/dL or more and 14 days have elapsed after the last dose of methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta. Concomitant use may result in a rapid rise in hemoglobin concentrations or an increase in hemoglobin above the target concentration, which may increase the risk for thrombotic vascular events.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Serious fatal and non-fatal cardiovascular and thromboembolic events have occurred during treatment with erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs). In patients with renal failure, an increase in mortality has been observed, particularly in patients treated to a target hemoglobin >12 g/dl. Events include myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalization for congestive heart failure, venous thromboembolism, and hemodialysis vascular access thrombosis. Patients with an insufficient hemoglobin response to ESA treatment may be at an even higher risk for cardiovascular events and mortality than other patients. Attempts should be made to maintain hemoglobin levels between 10—12 g/dl with the lowest dose necessary to prevent red blood cell transfusion (see Dosage). Patients who experience a hemoglobin rise of >1 g/dl in 2 weeks or less may be at an increased risk for cardiovascular and/or thromboembolic events. Treatment should be withheld and/or dose adjustments made to mitigate rapid hemoglobin response.Pure red cell aplasia and severe anemia, with or without cytopenias, have been associated with neutralizing antibody formation in patients receiving ESAs. Antibody formation occurs primarily in patients receiving ESAs by subcutaneous administration. Any patient who develops a sudden loss of response to MPG-epoetin beta accompanied by severe anemia and low reticulocyte count should be evaluated for the etiology of the altered response. If anti-erythropoietin antibody-associated anemia is suspected, withhold MPG-epoetin beta and other erythropoietic proteins. Serum samples should be obtained at least a month after the last MPG-epoetin beta administration. Contact Roche (800—526—6367) to perform assays for binding and neutralizing antibodies. Neutralizing antibodies to erythropoietin were not detected in patients receiving MPG-epoetin beta in clinical trials. Testing for antibodies may be influenced by many factors (i.e. assay methodology, sample handling, timing of sample, etc.) and comparisons should not be drawn between the incidence of neutralizing antibodies observed with MPG-epoetin beta and other ESAs. Patients who are found to have anti-erythropoietin antibody formation should not receive any further treatment with MPG-epoetin beta or any other ESA.MPG-epoetin beta caused hypertension in 13% of patients in clinical trials. Hypertension may occur in previously normotensive individuals. In studies of patients with renal failure, both dialysis-dependent and dialysis-independent, intensification of antihypertensive therapy was required in approximately 27% of patients. MPG-epoetin beta is contraindicated for use by patients with uncontrolled hypertension. Cases of hypertensive encephalopathy and seizures have been reported. Blood pressure and premonitory neurologic symptoms should be monitored closely during therapy, with special attention in the first several months of treatment. Patients who become resistant to pharmacologic or dietary measures to control their hypertension should have therapy with MPG-epoetin beta withheld or the dose reduced until the hypertension is controlled. Special care should be given to monitor and control blood pressure during therapy, especially in patients with a history of cardiovascular disease or hypertension. Patients who are not receiving dialysis may be more responsive to the effects of MPG-epoetin beta; judicious monitoring of blood pressure, hemoglobin, renal function, and fluid electrolyte balance is advised. The relationship between rate of rise of hemoglobin and seizures is uncertain. MPG-epoetin beta should be held and/or the dose decreased when the hemoglobin increase is greater than 1 g/dl in any 2 week period (see Dosage).In clinical studies, thrombocytopenia (defined as at least one post-baseline platelet count < 100,000/L) was observed in approximately 7% of patients. Also, the average platelet count decreased approximately 7%; most patients maintained a normal platelet count. The decrease in platelet count was seen upon initiation of MPG-epoetin beta treatment, and the levels remained stable thereafter. The mechanism through which MPG-epoetin beta causes thrombocytopenia is unclear. Carefully monitor platelet counts after initiation of therapy.Serious allergic reactions consisting of sinus tachycardia, pruritus, and rash (unspecified) have been reported in patients receiving MPG-epoetin beta. If a serious or anaphylactic reaction occurs with MPG-epoetin beta therapy, supportive measures should be taken, and therapy should be immediately and permanently discontinued. A less serious, injection site reaction has also been seen in patients receiving MPG-epoetin beta. Erythema, swelling, and itching may occur. Description
  Text
  Methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta (MPG-epoetin beta), also called continuous erythropoietin receptor activator, is a long-acting synthetic erythropoietin protein approved for the treatment of anemia associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD) in adult patients on or off dialysis and pediatric patients 3 months and older on or off hemodialysis who are converting from another erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) after their hemoglobin concentration was stabilized with an ESA. Studies in adult patients with CKD who were either dialysis-dependent or dialysis-independent have shown similar efficacy in achieving target hemoglobin (Hgb) concentrations and reducing red blood cell transfusion dependence as comparator ESAs. In an open-label, multiple dose, dose finding pediatric study (n = 36, ages 5 to 17 years), the mean change in Hgb concentration from baseline was -0.15 g/dL (95% CI:-0.49 to 0.2) for patients receiving MPG-epoetin beta using the recommended dose conversion factor. Supportive efficacy results showed that 75% of pediatric patients maintained Hgb values within +/- 1 g/dL of baseline and 81% maintained Hgb values within 10 to 12 g/dL when using the conversion factor to calculate the MPG-epoetin beta dose. The primary advantage of MPG-epoetin beta vs. other ESAs is that it can be dosed less frequently (up to every 4 weeks) due to its extremely long half-life. MPG-epoetin beta should only be used in patients with CKD and should not be used to treat cancer-related anemia. In adult patients, ESAs have been shown to increase the risk of death, myocardial infarction, stroke, venous thromboembolism, thrombosis of vascular access, and tumor progression or recurrence. MPG-epoetin beta was FDA-approved in 2007.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta (MPG-epoetin beta) is a synthetic, continuous erythropoietin-receptor activator. Endogenous erythropoietin is a primary growth factor in the generation of red blood cells. Erythropoietin is produced primarily in the kidneys and responds to hypoxic conditions to stimulate erythroid progenitor cells to increase red blood cell production. Synthesis of endogenous erythropoietin is frequently impaired in patients with renal failure. MPG-epoetin beta contains a methoxy polyethylene-glycol polymer attached to recombinant human erythropoietin at either the N-terminal amino or the epsilon-amino group of any lysine. As a result of this structural modification, MPG-epoetin beta remains in the circulation much longer than endogenous erythropoietin (approximately 134 hours vs. 7 to 8 hours). It also binds the erythropoietin-receptor much slower and dissociates at a much faster rate, which helps prevent internalization and degradation of the molecule, thus keeping it biologically active. Prolonged biologic activity allows for dosing intervals of every 2 to 4 weeks.
