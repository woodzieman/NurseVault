Drug Class: Chlorhexidine gluconate is an antimicrobial and antiseptic agent used for disinfection of the skin and mucous membranes.

Routes: topical, dental/oral rinse; Common adult doses: Maximum dosage information not available.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): edema, erythema, gingivitis, hypoesthesia, oral ulceration, paresthesias, parotitis, sialadenitis, anaphylactic shock, anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema, dyspnea, rash, skin irritation, urticaria, wheezing

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: It is not known if chlorhexidine is excreted into breast milk. In a case report, a woman who was breast-feeding sprayed chlorhexidine on her breasts to prevent mastitis.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  It is not known if chlorhexidine is excreted into breast milk. In a case report, a woman who was breast-feeding sprayed chlorhexidine on her breasts to prevent mastitis. Her 2-day old infant developed bradycardia following breast feeding. The bradycardia episodes resolved when the chlorhexidine was discontinued.Severe chlorhexidine hypersensitivity reactions have been reported. Anaphylaxis has been reported following skin disinfection with 0.05—1% chlorhexidine. Treatment with oxygen, epinephrine, corticosteroids, antihistamines and cardiorespiratory resuscitation may be required. In addition, anaphylactic reactions have been reported with chlorhexidine gel used intra-urethrally as a lubricant on urinary catheters and chlorhexidine-impregnated intravenous catheters. As of March 1998, the FDA has not received any reports of immediate systemic hypersensitivity reactions related to central venous catheters in the United States. Hypersensitivity reactions to chlorhexidine oral rinse are rare; however, patients sensitive to the topical antiseptic may be sensitive to the oral rinse as well.Patients with dental work such as anterior tooth restorations (front-tooth fillings) or dentures may develop excessive and permanent discoloration of these areas after using chlorhexidine oral rinse. Replacement of fillings and appliances may be needed for cosmetic reasons. If natural stain cannot be removed from these appliances, consider avoiding treatment with chlorhexidine oral rinse due to increased risk of permanent staining. Anterior tooth restorations with rough surfaces or margins are at increased risk for staining and discretion should be used in these patients.The use of chlorhexidine in the management of patients with some types of dental disease should be carefully considered. Chlorhexidine oral rinse may reduce the signs and symptoms of periodontitis. The presence or absence of gingival inflammation and bleeding should not be used as indicators of periodontitis in patients treated with chlorhexidine oral rinse. Chlorhexidine dental implants have not been studied in the treatment of acute abscessed periodontal pockets and are not recommended. Management of these patients should include evaluation of underlying medical problems such as cancer, diabetes and immunocompromised status.When using topical solutions containing alcohol, including chlorhexidine, prior to surgery, be aware that the solution gives off flammable vapors. Do not drape or use ignition source such as cautery or laser until solution is completely dry (minimum of 3 minutes on hairless skin). Avoid applying solution into hairy areas because the solution may take much longer to dry or may not dry completely. Do not allow the solution to pool and remove wet materials from prep area.Following ocular exposure of chlorhexidine topical antiseptic, serious and permanent eye injury has occurred during surgery when chlorhexidine enters and remains in the eye. Ocular effects include corneal edema, severe corneal endothelium damage requiring penetrating keratoplasty, iris atrophy, anterior chamber applanation (or flattening), and increased intraocular pressure. Inadvertent ocular exposure of chlorhexidine is characterized by streak formation in the anterior chamber.Safety for use of chlorhexidine during pregnancy has not been established. Use only if clearly indicated and when the potential benefits are greater than the potential risks to the fetus.In the presence of tympanic membrane perforation, take precautions to prevent exposure of inner ear tissues to chlorhexidine. Hearing loss and deafness may occur when chlorhexidine becomes in contact with the middle ear.Chlorhexidine topical antiseptic should not be used in patients with skin disease or wounds involving more than the superficial layers of skin, or for repeated cleansing of large body areas except when it is necessary to reduce the bacteria population of the skin. Do not use under an occlusive dressing. Additionally, chlorhexidine should not be used to cleanse the skin prior to lumbar puncture; contact with the meninges should be avoided.Rarely, infections including abscesses and cellulitis, have been reported with the adjunctive use of PerioChip® after scaling and root planing. Infection has been reported after scaling and root planing alone. Management of patients with periodontal disease should include consideration of potentially contributing medical disorders, such as cancer, diabetes, and immunocompromised status.Although subjects aged 65 years and over were included in clinical studies of chlorhexidine, there were not sufficient numbers of these subjects to determine whether they respond differently from younger subjects. Other reported clinical experience has not identified differences in responses between the elderly and younger patients. Overall differences in safety or effectiveness have not been identified between elderly and younger patients.Chlorhexidine should not be used in premature or low-birth weight neonates or in infants less than 2 months of age because of the potential for excessive skin irritation and increased drug absorption.

    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    The use of chlorhexidine in the management of individuals with certain types of periodontal disease requires careful consideration. In those with coexisting gingivitis and periodontitis, the presence or absence of gingival inflammation following treatment with chlorhexidine oral rinse should not be used as a primary indicator of underlying periodontitis, as the effect of chlorhexidine oral rinse on periodontitis has not been established.
    Headline
    infants, neonates, premature neonates
    General Information
    Use topical chlorhexidine with caution in premature neonates, neonates, and infants younger than 2 months due to the potential for skin irritation or chemical burns. The Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) list includes topical chlorhexidine as a potentially inappropriate medication for use in premature neonates and neonates and use should be avoided. If avoidance is not possible, use chlorhexidine with caution and appropriate monitoring.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of chlorhexidine use during human pregnancy to identify a drug-associated risk. Animal reproduction studies have not demonstrated evidence of fetal harm. Use during pregnancy only if clearly needed. Chlorhexidine has been used vaginally or topically on the abdomen or perineum prior to delivery to prevent infection.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use chlorhexidine with caution during breast-feeding. Topical use in the mouth or on areas of skin other than the nipple is not likely to cause adverse effects in the breast-fed child.  It is not known if chlorhexidine is excreted into breast milk. However, chlorhexidine is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract even after oral use; hence, the presence of the drug in human milk is unlikely. In studies using chlorhexidine vaginally prior to delivery to prevent mother-to-child transmission of HIV, no adverse events were reported in any breast-fed infant. In one case report, a breast-feeding individual used chlorhexidine spray (430 mcg/spray) on their chest to prevent mastitis starting with the third feed when the child was 12 hours old. After 48 hours, the child developed bradycardia. Chlorhexidine was discontinued, and bradycardia became less severe and less frequent. By day 6, the bradycardia had resolved. However, in a study of 200 lactating individuals who received chest sprays of 0.2% chlorhexidine in alcohol before and after each feeding, there were no reported side effects in any breast-fed infant.

Interactions
Disulfiram: (Minor) Some chlorhexidine oral rinses contain ethanol in significant percentages. Although chlorhexidine is poorly absorbed from the GI tract and the products are intended for oral topical rinse and not for systemic ingestion, there is a potential for interaction with disulfiram when such products are swallowed.
Keratinocytes; Fibroblasts; Collagen: (Major) Avoid the use of chlorhexidine gluconate solution on the wound following application of keratinocytes and dermal fibroblast collagen. Administering these drugs together may reduce wound repair and regeneration. Chlorhexidine gluconate has been shown to be toxic to keratinocytes and human dermal fibroblasts.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Chlorhexidine oral rinse may cause tooth discoloration and staining of the dorsum of the tongue, dentures or other mouth appliances, and tooth restorations (fillings) with rough surfaces or margins. Staining may be seen beginning after 1 week of therapy. After 6 months of treatment with chlorhexidine oral rinse, approximately 50% of patients had a measurable increase in tooth stain with approximately 10% having heavy stains. Staining was more severe in patients with heavier areas of unremoved plaque. The stain can be removed from most tooth surfaces by conventional dental cleaning techniques. Staining to tooth restorations may be permanent and may necessitate replacement. Chewing sugar-free gum for 20 minutes after brushing and rinsing with chlorhexidine oral rinse may decrease the amount of staining without affecting chlorhexidine efficacy in gingivitis.Patients treated with chlorhexidine oral rinse may experience taste alterations or a metallic taste. This usually decreases with time and will resolve completely once treatment with chlorhexidine is discontinued. Permanent taste alteration has not been reported.Increased calculus (tartar) formation has been reported with the use of chlorhexidine oral rinse. It is recommended to remove calculus deposits by dental prophylaxis every 6 months, especially during treatment with chlorhexidine oral rinse. An increase in supragingival calculus was noted in chlorhexidine users. Whether chlorhexidine increases subgingival calculus is not known.Parotitis (parotid duct obstruction) resulting in swelling of glands on the side of the face and neck and mouth irritation (superficial desquamation lesions) have been reported mainly in children ages 10—18 years following use of chlorhexidine oral rinse. The lesions are usually painless and transient. In addition, tongue tip irritation has been reported.Small children (10 kg or 22 lbs.) who accidentally drink more than 4 ounces of chlorhexidine oral solution may show signs of ethanol intoxication and nausea/vomiting. Medical attention and symptomatic treatment are recommended.Local and allergic reactions have been reported with chlorhexidine. Allergic reactions are rare with chlorhexidine oral rinse. Anaphylaxis has been reported following skin disinfection with 0.05%—1% chlorhexidine topical antiseptic, chlorhexidine gel and chlorhexidine-impregnated intravenous catheters. Symptoms have included bronchospasm, cough, dyspnea, pruritus, nasal congestion, vesicular rash, shortness of breath, urticaria and wheezing. Patients sensitive to skin antiseptics containing chlorhexidine may also be sensitive to the oral rinse. Skin irritation and sensitization have occurred especially in the genital area. Severe contact dermatitis and hypersensitivity reactions have been reported in neonates following use of chlorhexidine-impregnated patches to secure central venous catheters.Mild to moderate dental pain and sensitivity may occur during the first week following placement of the chlorhexidine dental implant. This usually resolves spontaneously and is less with repeated treatments. Toothache including gingival or mouth pain, tenderness, aching, throbbing, soreness, discomfort or sensitivity has been reported in half the patients treated with chlorhexidine dental implants.Hearing loss and deafness may occur if chlorhexidine penetrates the middle ear. In the presence of a perforated eardrum, take precautions to prevent exposure of inner ear tissues to chlorhexidine.Rarely, chlorhexidine has been reported to cause photosensitivity.Moderate to severe ocular irritation can occur following accidental eye exposure to chlorhexidine topical antiseptics, including Hibiclens®. Ocular effects included corneal edema, severe corneal endothelium damage requiring penetrating keratoplasty, iris atrophy, anterior chamber applanation (or flattening), and increased intraocular pressure. Inadvertent chlorhexidine ocular exposure is characterized by streak formation in the anterior chamber.Rarely, infections including abscesses and cellulitis, have been reported with the adjunctive use of PerioChip® after scaling and root planing. Infection has been reported after scaling and root planing alone.

    edema, erythema, gingivitis, glossitis, hypoesthesia, oral ulceration, paresthesias, parotitis, sialadenitis, stomatitis, xerostomia
    General Information
    Parotid gland swelling (parotitis), inflammation of the salivary glands (sialadenitis), minor oral mucosa irritation, and superficial desquamation of the oral mucosa have been reported with chlorhexidine gluconate oral rinse. Tongue tip irritation has also been reported. Other oral mucosal adverse events reported in less than 1% of patients include aphthous ulcer, grossly obvious gingivitis, trauma, oral ulceration, erythema, desquamation, coated tongue, keratinization, geographic tongue, mucocele, and short frenum. Postmarketing reports have included cases of stomatitis, gingivitis, glossitis, ulcer, xerostomia, glossal edema, mucosal hypoesthesia, and mucosal paresthesias.

    Serious and sometimes fatal hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylactoid reactions and anaphylactic shock, have been reported rarely with the use of chlorhexidine gluconate-containing products, including prescription and over-the-counter skin antiseptics, dental preparations, and medical devices (e.g., dressings, intravenous lines). Reactions may occur within minutes of exposure and can present with wheezing, dyspnea, angioedema, rash, or urticaria (hives). Local skin irritation has also been reported.

Description
  Text
  Chlorhexidine gluconate is an antimicrobial and antiseptic agent used for disinfection of the skin and mucous membranes. The oral rinse may be used for the treatment of gingivitis, denture stomatitis, and aphthous ulcer. Chlorhexidine oral rinse is also used to prevent and treat oral mucositis or stomatitis in individuals receiving cytoreductive chemotherapy. Topical formulations of chlorhexidine gluconate are indicated for use as a surgical scrub, health care personnel hand wash, patient preoperative skin preparation, skin wound cleanser, and general skin cleanser. Chlorhexidine gluconate exhibits antimicrobial activity against a broad range of gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, fungi, and certain viruses. Chlorhexidine gluconate demonstrates persistent antimicrobial activity on the skin, rapid bactericidal effects, and minimal systemic absorption following topical application. However, serious hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis, have been reported in association with chlorhexidine-containing topical products and medical devices.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Chlorhexidine, a divalent cationic biguanide molecule, exerts a concentration-dependent antimicrobial action by binding to negatively charged sites on bacterial cell membranes and strongly adhering to phosphate-containing molecules on the bacterial cell surface. At low concentrations (0.02% to 0.06%), chlorhexidine exerts bacteristatic activity by penetrating and damaging the bacterial cell wall, causing disruption in the cell wall integrity leading to loss of osmoregulation, outflow of cytoplasmic components (e.g., potassium, phosphorus), and inhibition of cytoplasmic enzymes, which, ultimately, inhibits cellular respiration. At high concentrations (0.12% or more), chlorhexidine exerts bacteriocidal activity by causing cytoplasmic coagulation, precipitation of cytoplasmic components (e.g., adenosine triphosphate, nucleic acids), and complete disruption of membrane integrity.
 
Susceptibility
Chlorhexidine has a broad antimicrobial spectrum with activity against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, fungi, and viruses. In dental applications, chlorhexidine implants have been associated with significant reductions in Porphyromonas (Bacteroides) gingivalis, Prevotella (Bacteroides) intermedia, Bacteroides forsythus, and Campylobacter rectus (Wolinella recta) following implant placement, and long-term use of chlorhexidine oral rinse for up to six months has resulted in a 54 to 97% reduction in selected aerobic and anaerobic bacteria in dental plaque. Clinical studies have not demonstrated significant bacterial resistance or opportunistic overgrowth during or following chlorhexidine therapy.

    Description
    Following oral rinsing, chlorhexidine is adsorbed onto the surfaces of teeth, dental plaque, and oral mucosa and therefore interferes with bacterial adhesion, allowing substantivity for up to 12 hours. Approximately 30% of chlorhexidine gluconate remains within the oral cavity. Chlorhexidine is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract.     Topical Route
    Description
    After skin cleaning with chlorhexidine gluconate, skin concentrations and germicidal effects may persist for up to 24 hours after application. There is no evidence that chlorhexidine is absorbed through intact skin. Chlorhexidine activity is pH dependent, with an optimal ph of 5.5 to 7, and activity may be reduced in the presence of organic compounds, such as blood and pus.
  Text
  Chlorhexidine is applied topically to the skin or is used as an oral rinse; it should never be ingested or used in the eye. No specific data regarding the distribution and metabolism of chlorhexidine are available. Chlorhexidine gluconate is eliminated primarily via the feces (approximately 90%), with less than 1% excreted in the urine.

Surgical Scrub Brush/Sponge

Clean under nails with nail pick provided. Nails should be maintained with a 1 mm free edge.Wet sponge and squeeze to work up lather.

Dry thoroughly. Discard brush-sponge.
 
Topical Solution
Surgical hand scrub
Wet hands and forearms with warm water.
Scrub for 3 minutes with approximately 5 mL of product and a wet sponge. Pay close attention to nails, cuticles, and interdigital spaces. A separate nail cleaner may be used.
Rinse thoroughly.
Wash for an additional 3 minutes with approximately 5 mL of product and rinse under running water.
Dry thoroughly.
Health care personnel hand wash
Wet hands with water.
Dispense approximately 5 mL of product into cupped hands and wash in a vigorous manner for 15 seconds.
Rinse and dry thoroughly.
Patient preoperative skin preparation
Apply liberally to surgical site and swab for at least 2 minutes.
Dry with a sterile towel.
Repeat swabbing process for an additional 2 minutes, and dry with a sterile towel.
Skin wound and general skin cleansing or MRSA decolonization
Thoroughly rinse area to be cleansed with water.
Apply the minimum amount of product necessary to cover the skin or wound area and wash gently.
Rinse again thoroughly.
For no-rinse solution, may add 4% chlorhexidine solution to a basin of water and dilute 1:1 to yield a 2% solution.
 
Topical Pad (cloth)
Opening package/Preparation
Open the package by holding the top with one hand and lifting the backside with the other. Grasp the top flap and pull downward to tear it away and expose the foam. Alternatively, use sterile scissors to cut off the end seal of the package.
Transfer contents onto prep table, avoiding contact between cloths and outside of package to reduce risk of cloth contamination.
Use the first cloth to prepare the skin area indicated for a moist or dry site, making certain to keep the second cloth where it will not be contaminated. Use second cloth to prepare larger areas.
Application
Dry surgical sites (such as abdomen or arm):
Use 1 cloth to cleanse each 161 cm area (approximately 5 x 5 inches) of skin to be prepared.
Vigorously scrub skin back and forth for 3 minutes, completely wetting treatment area, then discard.
Allow area to air dry for one (1) minute. Do not rinse.
Moist surgical sites (such as inguinal fold):
Use 1 cloth to cleanse each 65 cm area (approximately 2 x 5 inches) of skin to be prepared.
Vigorously scrub skin back and forth for 3 minutes, completely wetting treatment area, then discard.
Allow area to air dry for one (1) minute. Do not rinse.
Discard each cloth after a single use after package has been opened, discard any unused cloths.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Chlorhexidine is poorly absorbed from the GI tract following inadvertent ingestion; excretion is primarily through the feces. Therefore, no dosage adjustments are necessary.
  Renal Impairment
  Chlorhexidine is poorly absorbed from the GI tract following inadvertent ingestion; excretion is primarily through the feces with < 1% excreted in urine. Therefore, no dosage adjustments are necessary.
