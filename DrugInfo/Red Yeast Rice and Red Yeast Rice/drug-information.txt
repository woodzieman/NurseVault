Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Red Yeast Rice should not be used in during pregnancy or breast-feeding since the key constituent of some marketed products, mevinolin (see lovastatin monograph), is classified by the FDA as a category X product.Red Yeast Rice products are intended for use only in healthy adults. Since Red Yeast Rice products contain active components with HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor activity (i.e., mevinolin, in some marketed products, which is structurally similar to lovastatin) and have not been widely evaluated for safety as an FDA-approved drug, they should not be used in children or in patients with hepatic disease (including cholestasis, hepatic encephalopathy, hepatitis, jaundice, or unexplained elevations of hepatic enzymes), organ transplant, surgery , infection, or alcoholism. Since Red Yeast Rice is purported to have a similar active component to lovastatin, it should be avoided in conditions of decreased renal perfusion because kidney failure is possible if drug-induced rhabdomyolysis occurs. Predisposing conditions include renal disease or renal insufficiency, hypotension, acute infection, endocrine disease, electrolyte imbalance, uncontrolled seizure disorder, major surgery, and trauma. Data are lacking for use of Red Yeast Rice in patients with renal impairment or renal failure; use is not recommended. Some medications decrease 'statin' metabolism and may lead to myopathy (and complications such as rhabdomyolysis), such medications should also be avoided with Red Yeast Rice (see Drug Interactions).

    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Red yeast rice should generally not be used in during pregnancy due to the lack of safety data for use during pregnancy, and, the variability in dietary supplement quality and purity that may occur in marketed products. The key active constituent of red yeast rice is monacolin K, a weak reversible inhibitor of 3-HMG-coenzyme A reductase. This agent has similar actions to prescription statins. Experts recommend that most patients discontinue statin therapy during pregnancy due to the risks to the fetus; however, in certain high-risk patients, a prescription statin may be continued during pregnancy.

Erythromycin: (Contraindicated) The concurrent use of erythromycin is not recommended during lovastatin therapy. Erythromycin potently inhibits the metabolism of certain statins via the CYP3A4 isoenzyme and increase the risk of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis. Several case reports have described the development of rhabdomyolysis after erythromycin was added to a drug regimen containing lovastatin. Symptoms improved after erythromycin was discontinued. According to the manufacturer, if no alternative to a short course of erythromycin therapy is available, brief interruption of lovastatin therapy should be considered. There are no known adverse effects with short-term discontinuation of statins. Other HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors which are significant CYP3A4 substrates include atorvastatin and cerivastatin. Coadministration of atorvastatin with erythromycin increases atorvastatin plasma concentrations by about 40%. Since pravastatin and rosuvastatin are not substantially metabolized and fluvastatin is a minor CYP3A4 substrate (20%), these statins are less likely to be significantly affected by CYP3A4 inhibitors such as erythromycin. Coadministration of a single dose of rosuvastatin (80 mg) with erythromycin results in a 20% decrease in the AUC of rosuvastatin. Erythromycin (500 mg, single dose) does not affect steady state plasma levels of fluvastatin when administered 40 mg once daily. Since compounds in red yeast rice claim to have HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor activity, red yeast rice should not be used in combination with erythromycin. In general, erythromycin should be used with caution in patients receiving HMG CoA-reductase inhibitors; patients should be monitored closely for signs and symptoms of myopathy and/or rhabdomyolysis.

Description
  Text
  Red Yeast Rice (Monascus purpureus), also called Went yeast, is a naturally-derived, oral dietary supplement that is made by fermenting rice with Monascus purpureus. It is marketed to help maintain healthy cholesterol levels. The active constituent has weak activity that is similar to the HMG-CoA-reductase inhibitors (statins), but the dietary supplement is not as potent at reducing total cholesterol or low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels. Experts note use of this supplement may have utility to improve lifestyle factors for low-risk patients with mild to moderate hypercholesterolemia, particularly in those people who cannot take statins or other LDL-cholesterol-lowering therapies. Cardiovascular (CV) risk reduction data are not robust for red yeast rice use, and thus use is not recommended in patients with higher CV risk. Since the action is similar to the statins, red yeast rice use is recommended to be avoided during pregnancy or breast-feeding. 70480
NOTE: In the U.S., herbal products and dietary supplements are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for dietary supplements as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since supplements are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for these products and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The key active constituent of red yeast rice (Went Yeast or Monascus purpureus) is monacolin K, a weak reversible inhibitor of 3-HMG-coenzyme A reductase. This agent has similar actions to prescription statins. Per clinical studies, daily consumption causes a reduction in low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-cholesterol plasma levels up to 15% to 25% within 6 to 8 weeks. The decrease in LDL-cholesterol is accompanied by a proportional decrease in total and non-high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, plasma apolipoprotein B, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein.

  Text
  Red yeast rice is administered orally. An active constituent is a weak natural HMG-CoA-reductase inhibitor called monacolin K. Pharmacokinetic data for red yeast rice products are scant. Monacolins content in these products is usually around 0.4% w/w, of which about 90% is represented as monacolin K (also known as mevinolin). Thus, a 600 mg dosage of red yeast rice contains roughly 2.4 mg of monacolin K. Monacolin K is reported to be chemically identical to lovastatin, which is metabolized by the liver.
 
Affected Cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4
Monacolin K is reported to be extensively metabolized by CYP3A4, thus certain CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducers may cause changes in monacolin K plasma concentrations. For this reason, the concomitant use of CYP 3A4 inhibitors such as grapefruit juice and some medications may increase the risk of myotoxicity or rhabdomyolysis due to red yeast rice, mainly when used at doses corresponding to 10 mg/day of monacolin K or higher.
