Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Potassium iodide should be used with extreme caution in patients with iodine hypersensitivity. Patients at an increased risk of developing adverse effects caused by iodine include those with hypocomplementemic vasculitis, goiter, or autoimmune thyroid disease.
 
Potassium iodide is classified as pregnancy category D. It crosses the placenta in amounts sufficient enough to cause fetal goiter and/or hypothyroidism. Prolonged use during pregnancy is not advised, however, potassium iodide has been used short term (e.g., 10 days) to manage labor-induced thyrotoxic crisis and as treatment prior to thyroidectomy in pregnant women.
 
Potassium iodide is excreted into breast milk. Rash or thyroid suppression can occur in the nursing infant, however, the American Academy of Pediatrics does not consider breast-feeding a contraindication.
 
Potassium iodide should be used with caution in patients with sulfite hypersensitivity and/or asthma because some formulations of this drug contain sodium bisulfite. A higher frequency of sensitivity reactions occur in asthmatic patients compared to nonasthmatic patients. Potassium iodide should be used cautiously in patients with acute bronchitis.
 
Potassium iodide should be used cautiously in patients with renal impairment. Due to impaired renal filtering of electrolytes, an increase in serum potassium can occur. Potassium iodide can also exacerbate pre-existing hyperkalemia or myotonia congenita (Thomsen's disease). Serum potassium and potential signs and symptoms of potassium toxicity should be monitored. Potassium iodide should also be used with extreme caution in the following circumstances: acute dehydration, muscle cramps, adrenal insufficiency, and cardiac disease.
 
Potassium iodide should be used with extreme caution in patients with tuberculosis because pulmonary irritation and increased secretions may ensue. If possible, potassium iodide should be avoided in this patient population.
 
Potassium iodide should be used with caution in patients with acne vulgaris. The product can produce an acneiform rash or aggravate existing acne.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Do not use in people who are allergic to iodine.
    Headline
    acute bronchitis, cardiac disease, hyperthyroidism, myotonia congenita, primary adrenal insufficiency, renal failure, renal impairment, tuberculosis
    General Information
    Use potassium iodide with caution in individuals with primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease), cardiac disease, hyperthyroidism, myotonia congenita, tuberculosis, acute bronchitis, or renal impairment, including renal failure.
    Headline
    dermatitis herpetiformis, hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis, thyroid nodule
    General Information
    Avoid use of potassium iodide in patients with dermatitis herpetiformis, hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis, or thyroid nodule in the setting of cardiac disease.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Potassium iodide can cause fetal harm, abnormal thyroid function, and fetal goiter if administered during human pregnancy. Potassium iodide therapy is used during pregnancy when medically necessary for thyroid protection or hyperthyroid emergencies; use as an expectorant is not advised. Although prolonged use for thyroid disease during pregnancy is not advised, potassium iodide has been used short term (e.g., 10 days) to manage labor-induced thyrotoxic crisis and as treatment prior to thyroidectomy in pregnant individuals. Patients should not self-supplement with potassium iodide during pregnancy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use potassium iodide with caution during breast-feeding. It is excreted into breast milk. Rash and thyroid suppression can occur in the breastfed infant. Potassium iodide therapy would only be used in a lactating individual when medically necessary for thyroid protection or hyperthyroid emergencies; use as an expectorant is not advised. Breast-feeding individuals should take potassium iodide if they are contaminated with radioactive iodine and are advised to do so by their governmental agencies, according to current guidelines for radiation emergencies. The normal amount of potassium iodide present in human milk is not sufficient to protect an infant who has been exposed to radioactive Iodine-131. Thus, in an environmental exposure, the breastfed child must receive supplemental potassium iodide to block uptake of radiation in their thyroid gland.

Description
  Text
  Potassium iodide (KI) is an oral compound comprised of roughly 76% iodine and 23% potassium by weight. Clinically, iodine has been used as a therapeutic agent since the 1800s, most notably in the treatment of thyroid disease. Potassium iodide is used as an adjunct to other antithyroid agents in the treatment of hyperthyroidism and thyrotoxicosis, and preoperatively to induce thyroid involution. The drug is also useful as a protectant of the thyroid gland following radiation exposure. Certain potassium iodide formulations (e.g., Iostat, ThyroSafe, ThyroShield), received FDA clearance for thyroid protection against radioactive iodine exposure (e.g., in the event of a nuclear plant accident or 'dirty bomb' containing radioactive iodine, I-131). These formulations may be distributed to high-risk individuals by the government or public health officials and are also approved for OTC sale to the general public. The U.S. government actively distributes stockpiles of KI tablets to those States that house nuclear reactors. Clinicians should note that FDA-approved potassium iodide products only provide protection for the thyroid gland from radioiodines, and do not provide protection against other types of radiation exposure. Potassium iodide (KI) appears useful for dermatologic conditions; the drug has demonstrated efficacy for cutaneous and lymphocutaneous sporotrichosis and is used in the tropics to treat entomophthoramycosis caused by the fungi Basidiobolus and Conidiobolus. The drug is also used off-label as a second line agent for various inflammatory dermatoses, including erythema nodosum, subacute nodular migratory panniculitis, nodular vasculitis, erythema multiforme, and Sweet's syndrome. Although potassium iodide was commonly used historically as a respiratory expectorant, the drug is no longer recommended for this purpose due to a lack of evidence for clinical efficacy and the potential for thyroid dysfunction or other side effects. Commercial products containing potassium iodide have been marketed for medical use since 1939.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: Potassium iodide (KI) supplies iodine systemically. The pharmacology of potassium iodide is not completely understood, despite years of use in clinical medicine for various medical conditions.
•Effects on the thyroid gland: Iodides are usually used in the treatment of hyperthyroidism to prepare patients for thyroidectomy. Iodides are also used along with antithyroid drugs and symptomatic treatments for the acute treatment of thyrotoxic crisis. By inhibiting thyroid hormone synthesis and release, thyroid gland vascularity is reduced, thyroid gland tissue becomes firmer, thyroid cell proliferation is reduced, follicular colloid reaccumulates, and bound iodine levels increase. The response of the hyperthyroid patient to iodide treatment is usually rapid, with symptomatic changes noted within 24 hours, although maximal effects may not be apparent until 2 weeks later. Although the effects may be noted for several weeks, iodides do not usually control the disease indefinitely, which is the reason why patients often need other measures (e.g., thyroidectomy) for treatment. The effects of iodides on the thyroid gland are complex, and can be dependent on the baseline thyroid status of the patient. Euthyroid patients or patients with certain underlying thyroid disorders may be at risk for iodine-induced hypothyroidism if they are exposed to large amounts of iodine systemically.
•Effects as a thyroid protectant prior to/following exposure to radioiodines: During a nuclear accident, various iodine isotopes are produced, including 131I, a radioactive iodine that concentrates in the thyroid gland. 131I has an 8-day half-life, which means that 99% of the radioactivity will decay in roughly 56 days. Potassium iodide (KI) is considered a safe and effective means by which to prevent radioiodine uptake by the thyroid gland and to minimize the risk of radiation-induced thyroid cancer. The administration of KI saturates the thyroid gland with iodine, minimizing the uptake of the radioactive isotope. For optimal protection against inhaled radioiodines following a radiation emergency, KI should be administered immediately coincident with the passage of the radioactive cloud, although KI may still have a substantial protective effect if taken 3 to 4 hours after the exposure.
•Effects in inflammatory dermatoses: The precise actions by which potassium iodide is therapeutically beneficial for various dermatologic conditions are not known. It appears that the drug may have some effects on inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis, actions which might be beneficial in certain inflammatory dermatoses that display increased neutrophil activation in early stages of the disease. In dermatoses caused by yeast and fungi, potassium iodide appears to act as an antifungal, although it is not clear if the drug is directly fungicidal or works by enhancing the tissue and immune response to the infection.

  Text
  Potassium iodide is administered orally. It demonstrates significant extracellular distribution, with most of the drug accumulating in the thyroid gland. Potassium iodide distributes into breast milk and crosses the placenta in amounts sufficient enough to cause fetal harm (see Contraindications). Therapeutic effects from KI usually are observed within 24 hours after administration, with maximum effectiveness occurring after 10—15 days of therapy. Potassium iodide is excreted renally.
