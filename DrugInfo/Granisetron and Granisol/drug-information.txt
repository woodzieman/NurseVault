Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Granisetron should be avoided in patients with known or suspected granisetron hypersensitivity or to any inactive ingredients of the product. Granisetron should be used with caution in patients with ondansetron hypersensitivity, palonosetron hypersensitivity, dolasetron hypersensitivity, or hypersensitivity to similar drugs; cross sensitivity may be seen between these agents. Furthermore, it has been hypothesized that antagonism at serotonin (5HT) receptors, and the subsequent increased concentrations of serotonin, increase the risk of developing bronchospasm and/or vasoconstriction. There have been several reports of anaphylactic/anaphylactoid reactions associated with the use of drugs in this class. It is not clear at this time if these reactions are due to the use of the serotonin antagonist alone or due to a drug interaction between the serotonin antagonist and a chemotherapeutic agent. Nevertheless, clinicians should not overlook this possibility.
 
Granisetron injection is formulated with benzyl alcohol, and should be avoided in patients with benzyl alcohol hypersensitivity. Granisetron injection should be avoided in neonates. Exposure to large amounts of benzyl alcohol has been associated with 'gasping syndrome,' a potentially fatal condition characterized by metabolic acidosis and CNS, respiratory, circulatory, and renal dysfunction.
 
Granisetron is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category B. Animal studies have revealed no teratogenic effects. It is not known if granisetron crosses the placenta; however, due to its low molecular weight, passage across the placental barrier is expected. Granisetron injection contains benzyl alcohol which may cross the placenta resulting in untoward adverse events in the fetus. Granisetron should be used during pregnancy only if the benefit to the mother outweighs the potential risk to the fetus.
 
It is not known whether granisetron is excreted in human milk. However, due to its low molecular weight, transfer into breast milk should be expected. Caution should be exercised when administering granisetron to a breast-feeding woman.
 
Safety and effectiveness in children under 2 years of age have not been established. Furthermore, safety and effectiveness of granisetron injection have not been established in pediatric patients for the prevention or treatment of postoperative nausea or vomiting.
 
During clinical trials, 713 patients 65 years or older received granisetron injection, and 325 received granisetron tablets. Effectiveness and safety were similar in patients of various ages.
 
Data demonstrate that ECG interval changes are a class effect of the 5-HT(3) receptor antagonists. However, theoretical concern regarding cardiovascular adverse events with these agents is not supported by clinical experience. The clinically significant benefits of these agents appear to outweigh the theoretically small risk of meaningful cardiovascular events; however, the risks and benefits should be determined for each individual patient.
 
The use of granisetron in patients following abdominal surgery or in patients with chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting may mask a progressive ileus, GI obstruction, and/or gastric distension. These patients should be appropriately monitored.
 
Patients with hepatic disease, hepatitis, and elevated hepatic enzymes should be observed closely while receiving granisetron since the primary route of metabolism is via hepatic pathways. Lower doses of granisetron may be necessary in patients with hepatic disease.
 
Granisetron may be degraded by direct natural or artificial sunlight. Due to a potential skin reaction, if there is a risk of sunlight (UV) exposure, patients should cover the patch with clothing during the period of wear and for 10 days after its removal.

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in individuals with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Extended-release granisetron (Sustol) is contraindicated in individuals with ondansetron hypersensitivity, palonosetron hypersensitivity or dolasetron hypersensitivity. Use regular-release granisetron with caution in individuals with hypersensitivity to other serotonin antagonists or similar drugs; cross sensitivity may be seen between these agents. Hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis, may occur in granisetron-treated individuals with prior serotonin antagonist hypersensitivity. These reactions may occur up to 7 days or longer after extended-release administration, may be prolonged, and require patient counseling, prompt medication intervention, and monitoring until resolution.
    Headline
    constipation
    General Information
    The use of granisetron in individuals after abdominal surgery or in individuals with chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting may mask a progressive ileus, GI obstruction, and/or gastric distension. Monitor individuals for the development of constipation and decreased bowel activity, particularly in individuals with risk factors for GI obstruction, and consider optimizing bowel regimens. In individuals receiving extended-release granisetron, the drug may be released over at least 5 to 7 days.
    Headline
    bradycardia, cardiomyopathy, congenital long QT syndrome, coronary artery disease, females, geriatric, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, QT prolongation
    General Information
    Use granisetron with caution in people with baseline QT prolongation or who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, congenital long QT syndrome, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk.
    Headline
    conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature
    General Information
    Precautions should be taken when exposure of the granisetron transdermal patch to heat is expected. Individuals should avoid conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature, such as prolonged exposure to heat near the application site (e.g., a heating pad, ambient temperature increase, electric blanket, hot baths, etc.), as systemic plasma concentrations will increase during heat exposure.
    Headline
    bleeding disorder, neutropenia
    General Information
    Use caution if treatment with extended-release granisetron injection is necessary in individuals with neutropenia or a bleeding disorder, including individuals receiving concomitant anticoagulant therapy or antiplatelet therapy, as there is an increased risk for serious or severe injection site reactions (ISRs). Infections (e.g., abscess, cellulitis with gangrene), prolonged bleeding, severe bruising (e.g., greater than 4 cm bruise), hematomas, nodules, pain, and tenderness have been reported at the injection site during clinical trials and/or postmarketing. ISRs may occur up to 2 weeks or more after administration. Bleeding, in some cases prolonged, has also been reported and required emergency management in 1 subject. For ongoing ISRs, administer granisetron at a site away from the affected area. Consider treatment discontinuation for severe or persistent ISRs.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available published data and postmarketing reports with granisetron use during human pregnancy have not identified a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. Use granisetron during pregnancy only if the benefit to the pregnant person outweighs the potential risk to the fetus. No fetal harm has been observed in animals given granisetron doses much higher than the maximum recommended human dose, based on body surface area, for all formulations. In addition, no fetal harm was observed after exposure to the polymer vehicle present in granisetron extended-release injection.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use granisetron with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of granisetron in human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effects on milk production. Due to lack of data, an alternative agent such as ondansetron, may be preferred.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Granisetron is well tolerated, even at doses approaching 300 mcg/kg. Clinical trials comparing granisetron and ondansetron have shown that the frequency and severity of adverse reactions is similar between the two agents. According to the manufacturer, the most frequent adverse reactions associated with oral therapy include headache (21%), constipation (18%), asthenia (14%), diarrhea (8%), and abdominal pain (6%). Similar events and incidences have been reported with granisetron intravenous injection. Most IV granisetron studies reporting adverse events are designed without placebo controls, making the evaluation of adverse events difficult. In one study involving 184 patients receiving granisetron 5—40 mcg/kg IV, the most frequently reported adverse events were headache (20%), diarrhea (11%), constipation (7%), fever (7%), drowsiness (6%), and dyspepsia (5%). For both oral and IV therapy, headache is the most frequently and most consistently reported adverse event. Reported adverse events may also be attributed to concomitant use of chemotherapeutic treatment regimens. The side effects of granisetron do not appear to be dose-related.
 
Less frequent adverse effects included dizziness (5%), insomnia (5%), anxiety (2%), agitation ( 2 times the upper limit of normal) occurred in roughly 5—6% of patients receiving oral granisetron. Incidences for injectable granisetron were slightly lower, however these frequencies were not significantly different from those seen with comparators.
 
As a class, 5-HT3-receptor antagonists have been associated with some cardiovascular adverse effects, mainly hypertension (1—2%) and, rarely, angina pectoris, asymptomatic arrhythmias (e.g., atrial fibrillation, sinus bradycardia), ECG abnormalities, syncope, varying degrees of AV block, and ventricular ectopy (including non-sustained tachycardia).
 
Symptoms suggestive of, but not diagnostic for, extrapyramidal symptoms have been observed with other 5-HT3 receptor antagonists, and one case was reported in a patient treated with granisetron tablets. Clinicians should be aware of this class effect and be vigilant for any dystonic reaction observed in patients receiving granisetron.
 
It has been hypothesized that antagonism at serotonin (5HT) receptors and the subsequent increased concentrations of serotonin increase the risk of developing bronchospasm and/or vasoconstriction. It has been reported that the FDA has received approximately 24 reports of anaphylactic/anaphylactoid reactions to ondansetron injection. Laryngospasm, shock, cardiac arrest, and respiratory arrest have been reported during allergic reactions in patients receiving injectable ondansetron. Manifestations in granisetron-treated patients have included shortness of breath, dyspnea, hypotension, and/or urticaria.
 
Skin irritation and other skin reactions may occur with application of the transdermal system. Skin reactions in clinical trials were comparable to placebo and were generally mild. If severe reactions or generalized reactions occur, the patch must be removed.
 
In a clinical study conducted in Japan, the types of adverse events reported for granisetron injection differed from those reported in US studies. The adverse events that occurred in > 2% included fever (50—56%), sputum increase (1.7—2.7%), and dermatitis (<= 2.7%).

In a randomized, single-blind, positive (moxifloxacin)- and placebo-controlled parallel study of healthy patients treated with the granisetron patch (Sancuso, n = 60) or IV granisetron (10 mcg/kg, n = 60), the baseline corrected QTcF for Sancuso was below 10 msec suggesting no effects on QT prolongation. Hypertension was reported in 2% of patients who received granisetron IV (2 to 160 mcg/kg over single or multiple days) and 1% of patients who received oral tablets prior to emetogenic chemotherapy in clinical trials. Hypotension and atrial fibrillation were reported rarely with IV and oral use. Other cardiovascular adverse effects that occurred rarely with IV granisetron in clinical trials include ECG abnormalities and other arrhythmias (e.g., bradycardia, AV block, ventricular ectopy including non-sustained tachycardia). Angina pectoris and syncope were reported rarely with oral granisetron in clinical trials. Syncope and atrial fibrillation were each reported in less than 3% of patients who received subcutaneous extended-release granisetron in clinical trials (n = 924). Bradycardia, chest pain (unspecified), palpitations, and sick sinus syndrome have been identified in post-marketing use of the granisetron patch (Sancuso).

Description
  Text
  Granisetron is an oral, parenteral, and transdermal serotonin-3 (5HT3) receptor antagonist. Granisetron is most often used for the prevention of radiation- or chemotherapy-induced nausea and/or vomiting (CINV); its efficacy has been demonstrated alone and in combination with dexamethasone. Granisetron is similar to ondansetron in efficacy and adverse effects. IV granisetron is additionally indicated for the prevention of post-operative nausea and vomiting (PONV) in adults. A granisetron transdermal system is used for the prevention of CINV in adults receiving moderately emetogenic chemotherapy (MEC) and/or HEC regimens of up to 5 days duration. An extended-release injection for subcutaneous administration (Sustol) is used in adults for the prevention of acute CINV prior to both MEC and HEC; it is also approved for the prevention of delayed CINV prior to MEC only. Intravenous granisetron is FDA-approved for use in pediatric patients 2 years and older to prevent radiation and chemotherapy-induced emesis. Other formulations of granisetron (oral, transdermal, and extended-release subcutaneous injection) are not FDA-approved for pediatric patients, although oral granisetron has been used off-label. The American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guidelines recommend that adult patients who are treated with moderate to high-emetic-risk chemotherapy agents should be offered a 3-drug combination of a 5-HT3 receptor antagonist, a neurokinin 1 (NK1) receptor antagonist, and dexamethasone; olanzapine is also added to the 3-drug combination during use of high-emetic-risk agents. Children receiving moderate to high-emetic-risk agents should be offered a 2-drug combination of a 5-HT3 receptor antagonist and dexamethasone; aprepitant is also added to the 2-drug combination during use of high-emetic-risk agents.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Granisetron may have central and peripheral actions. Granisetron selectively blocks type 3 serotonin (5-HT3) receptors. 5-HT3 receptors are found centrally in the chemoreceptor trigger zone and peripherally at vagal nerve terminals in the intestines. Whether the action of granisetron is mediated centrally, peripherally, or a combination of both remains to be determined. Its affinity for 5-HT3 receptors is 4,000 to 40,000 times higher than for other serotonin receptors. Emesis during chemotherapy appears to be associated with the release of serotonin from enterochromaffin cells in the small intestine, stimulating 5-HT3 receptors. This evokes vagal afferent discharge, inducing vomiting. Blocking these nerve endings in the intestines prevents signals to the central nervous system. Granisetron may antagonize the effects of serotonin on the cholinergic neurons of the colon, slowing colonic transit time.

    Subcutaneous Route
    Description
    In healthy subjects, extended-release granisetron is released from the polymer and remains detectable in plasma for 7 days post-dose. A mean concentration of 3.5 ng/mL (range, 0 to 14 ng/mL) was observed at 5 days post-dose. After a single subcutaneous injection (10 mg), the Cmax was 9.8 ng/mL (+/- 4.8 ng/mL) after abdominal administration (n = 113) and 10.8 ng/mL (+/- 4.6 ng/mL) when administered in the upper arm (n = 113); the mean AUC was 680 ng*hour/mL (+/- 362 ng*hour/mL) and 720 ng*hour/mL (+/- 366 ng*hour/mL), respectively.     Name
    Topical Route
    Description
    After transdermal application, granisetron crosses intact skin into systemic circulation by passive diffusion. After a 7-day application of the granisetron patch in healthy subjects (n = 24), a Cmax of 5 ng/mL (CV, 170%) was reached at approximately 48 hours (range, 24 to 168 hours) after patch application; the mean AUC0 to 168 was 527 ng x hour/mL (CV, 173%). The mean plasma concentration at 24 hours after the second patch application was 1.5-told higher due to residual granisetron from the first patch. However, at 48 hours after the second patch was applied, the difference in plasma concentrations decreased (1.3-fold increase compared to the first patch).

  Text
  Granisetron is administered orally, intravenously, subcutaneously, and transdermally. Granisetron distributes freely between plasma and erythrocytes; approximately 65% of the drug is protein bound. There is considerable interpatient variability in the clearance. Approximately 12% of an intravenous dose is eliminated unchanged in the urine in 48 hours; the remainder of a dose is excreted as metabolites in the urine (49%) and the feces (34%).
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes: CYP1A1, CYP3A4
Granisetron is metabolized by CYP1A1 and CYP3A4. Granisetron metabolism involves N-demethylation and aromatic ring oxidation followed by conjugation. Animal studies indicate that the metabolites may have pharmacologic activity. Granisetron does not induce or inhibit the cytochrome P450 enzyme system.
