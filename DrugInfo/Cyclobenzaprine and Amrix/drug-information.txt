Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Cyclobenzaprine is structurally related to the tricyclic antidepressant amitriptyline. In addition to the listed precautions for cyclobenzaprine, precautions relevant to the tricyclic antidepressants should be considered. Tricyclic drugs are known to produce an allergic response in some patients. There appears to be cross-sensitivity, and caution should be used when changing from one of these chemically-related agents to another. Alternative therapy to cyclobenzaprine should be considered in those patients with tricyclic antidepressant hypersensitivity.
 
Because of a potential risk of cardiac events, the manufacturer contraindicates the use of cyclobenzaprine in patients with hyperthyroidism.
 
Cyclobenzaprine, like the tricyclic antidepressants, should not be given to patients who are in the acute recovery phase following acute myocardial infarction; this could cause re-infarction or sudden death. Do not administer cyclobenzaprine to patients with QT prolongation or familial histories of long-QT syndromes or in those patients with cardiac conduction defects (e.g., cardiac arrhythmias, AV block, bundle-branch block or congestive heart failure). Use cyclobenzaprine with caution in patients with any other cardiac disease (past history of myocardial infarction), because of the alterations in ECG patterns or heart rhythm that may occur. Although the risk of cardiovascular adverse events is higher after acute overdose, patients with cardiovascular disease should be closely monitored. Many adverse cardiovascular effects are associated with the use of tricyclic-related drugs.
 
Because of the drug's chemical similarity to tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), use cyclobenzaprine with caution in patients being treated for psychological illness. Cyclobenzaprine is not an effective treatment for depression. Cyclobenzaprine is contraindicated for concomitant use in patients receiving MAOI therapy (see Drug Interactions). It is unclear if cyclobenzaprine, like the TCAs, can transform depression into mania or hypomania in predisposed individuals (e.g., some patients with bipolar disorder). Also use cyclobenzaprine with caution in patients with psychotic disorders (e.g., schizophrenia).
 
Use cyclobenzaprine with caution in patients with a seizure disorder. Tricyclic drugs can lower the seizure threshold. If seizures occur during therapy with cyclobenzaprine, discontinue the drug.
 
Cyclobenzaprine can induce significant sedation, particularly during the initiation of treatment. Patients should use caution when driving or operating machinery until they are aware of the effects of the medication. Use with caution in patients with a history of alcoholism or who may use alcohol or other sedative medications because the depressant effects on the CNS can be potentiated. Decreased mental alertness can occur.
 
Cyclobenzaprine possesses anticholinergic activity, which may cause GI and other side effects. This drug should not be used in patients with paralytic ileus. Patients with increased intraocular pressure, closed-angle glaucoma, prostatic hypertrophy, or urinary retention should receive cyclobenzaprine with caution. The anticholinergic effects of cyclobenzaprine may make the eyes dry, which may cause discomfort for wearers of contact lenses. The use of lubricating drops may be necessary. The anticholinergic effects of cyclobenzaprine may be additive with other anticholinergic medications. Anticholinergic effects appear most frequently and cause the greatest morbidity in elderly patients. Cyclobenzaprine is generally not recommended for use in patients 65 years of age and older. The manufacturer recommends that cyclobenzaprine be used in the elderly only if clearly needed. If needed, initiate cyclobenzaprine therapy with 5 mg doses and titrate cautiously in the elderly (see Dosage); less frequent dosing may be needed. The plasma concentrations of cyclobenzaprine are elevated 1.7-fold in elderly patients relative to younger patients. In addition, elderly patients are at higher risk for adverse CNS (e.g. hallucinations, confusion) and cardiac events, potentially leading to falls or other sequelae. Because of reduced clearance and a greater than 40% increase in cyclobenzaprine AUC, the use of cyclobenzaprine extended-release capsules in the elderly patients is not recommended.
 
Like other tricyclic drugs, cyclobenzaprine may rarely cause liver dysfunction. Cyclobenzaprine should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease. Cyclobenzaprine is extensive metabolized by the liver to glucuronides. Peak plasma concentrations and AUC of cyclobenzaprine are nearly doubled in patients with hepatic impairment (15 of 16 patients studied had mild hepatic impairment). Cyclobenzaprine is not recommended for patients with moderate to severe hepatic impairment due to insufficient data. In patients with mild hepatic impairment, initiate therapy with 5 mg doses and titrate cautiously (see Dosage); less frequent dosing may be needed.
 
Cyclobenzaprine has not been found effective in the treatment of spasticity associated with cerebral or spinal cord disease, or in children with cerebral palsy. Safety and efficacy of cyclobenzaprine in children under the age of 15 years have not been established.
 
Studies for determining safe use of cyclobenzaprine during pregnancy have not been performed. Although the drug is classed as FDA pregnancy risk category B, it is closely chemically related to the tricyclic antidepressants. Animal studies of cyclobenzaprine have not noted an increased incidence of harm to the fetus. Patients should be told about the potential risks to the neonate; cyclobenzaprine therapy should be used during pregnancy only when clearly needed.
 
Use caution when administering cyclobenzaprine during breast-feeding. Cyclobenzaprine is closely chemically related to the tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., amitriptyline). Distribution of the drug into breast milk has not been established, but tricyclic drugs are known to be found in breast milk. Tricyclic drugs are generally listed by the American Academy of Pediatrics as drugs whose effect on the nursing infants are not known but may be of concern, particularly with prolonged exposure.
 
Cyclobenzaprine lowers the seizure threshold. Because of a potential increased risk of seizures, cyclobenzaprine should not be used during intrathecal radiographic contrast administration. Cyclobenzaprine therapy should be discontinued 48 hours before and not restarted until at least 24 hours after myelography.
 
Patients may be more prone to sunburn during therapy with cyclobenzaprine. Suitable precautions should be taken prior to sunlight (UV) exposure, such as using sunscreens and protective clothing.

    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Cyclobenzaprine is structurally related to tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., amitriptyline) and there is a potential for cross-sensitivity among tricyclic agents. Alternative therapy to cyclobenzaprine should be considered in people with tricyclic antidepressant hypersensitivity, particularly if the reaction was severe or life-threatening.

    Advise cyclobenzaprine recipients against driving or operating machinery or activities requiring coordination and concentration until the effects of cyclobenzaprine are known. Cyclobenzaprine can cause sedation, which may interfere with everyday activity. Use cyclobenzaprine with caution in combination with ethanol ingestion or other CNS depressants.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class A, Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Immediate-release oral cyclobenzaprine tablets and sublingual cyclobenzaprine tablets are not recommended for use in individuals with moderate (Child-Pugh class B) to severe (hepatic failure or Child-Pugh class C) hepatic impairment. A dosage adjustment is recommended in individuals with mild hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A).  Extended-release cyclobenzaprine is not recommended for use in individuals with mild, moderate, or severe hepatic impairment, including hepatic failure. Cyclobenzaprine is extensively metabolized by the liver.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    The Beers Criteria identifies skeletal muscle relaxants, such as cyclobenzaprine, as potentially inappropriate for geriatric adults. This is due to poor tolerance, potential anticholinergic effects, sedation, increased fracture risk, and questionable effectiveness at dosages that older adults can tolerate. Cyclobenzaprine exposure is significantly increased in geriatric adults. Only use cyclobenzaprine in the geriatric adult if clearly needed. If used, initiate at the lowest adult dosage and titrate slowly.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There is limited data on the use of cyclobenzaprine during human pregnancy to inform a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. Based on animal data, cyclobenzaprine may cause fetal harm. Advise pregnant individuals of the potential risk to the fetus. An increased incidence of neural tube defects (splayed vertebral arches and spina bifida occulta) was observed when pregnant rabbits were treated with oral cyclobenzaprine during embryogenesis at 30 mg/kg/day (approximately 0.2 times the maximum recommended sublingual human dose [MRHD] of 5.6 mg/day on an AUC basis) in the absence of maternal toxicity. Comparatively, no adverse embryo-fetal effects were reported after oral administration of cyclobenzaprine doses up to 20 mg/kg/day in mice and rabbits during organogenesis (approximately 3 and 15 times the extended-release capsule MRHD of 30 mg/day, respectively, on a mg/m2 basis). Maternal toxicity (decreased body weight gain) was observed in mice at the highest tested dose. In rats, decreased pup body weight and survival were noted at cyclobenzaprine doses more than or equal to 10 mg/kg/day (approximately 0.8 times the sublingual MRHD on an AUC basis and approximately 3 times extended-release capsule MRHD on a mg/m2 basis) when administered orally during pregnancy and lactation. Product labeling for cyclobenzaprine sublingual tablets requests that pregnancies be reported to the Tonix Medicines, Inc., Adverse Event reporting line at 1-888-869-7622.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with cyclobenzaprine. Neural tube defects were observed in animal studies at clinically relevant doses. Because neural tube development occurs early in pregnancy, often before pregnancy is recognized, FDA-approved labeling for the sublingual tablets recommends pregnancy testing prior to treatment initiation to exclude use of cyclobenzaprine during the first trimester of pregnancy. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking cyclobenzaprine and for 2 weeks after the final dose.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use cyclobenzaprine with caution during breast-feeding. A small number of published cases report the transfer of cyclobenzaprine into human milk in low amounts, but these data cannot be confirmed. There are no data on the effects of cyclobenzaprine on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. Consider the developmental and health benefits of breast-feeding along with the clinical need for cyclobenzaprine and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from cyclobenzaprine or the patient's underlying condition.

  General Information
  Adverse reactions most commonly reported with cyclobenzaprine use, resulting from CNS depression and/or anticholinergic response, are drowsiness (39%), xerostomia (27%), and dizziness (11%). Reactions that were reported in between 1% and 3% of patients taking cyclobenzaprine include: fatigue/tiredness, asthenia, nausea/vomiting, constipation, dyspepsia, dysgeusia, blurred vision, headache, nervousness, and confusion. Other less frequently reported side effects include: malaise, sinus tachycardia, cardiac arrhythmias, potential for QT prolongation at higher doses or overdosage based on similar structure to tricyclic antidepressants , a case report of torsades de pointes with concurrent droperidol , vasodilation, palpitation, hypotension, vomiting, diarrhea, GI pain, gastritis, thirst, flatulence, swollen tongue, abnormal liver-function tests, cholestatic jaundice, angioedema, pruritus, urticaria, rash, ataxia, vertigo, tremors, seizures (convulsions), muscle twitching, disorientation, hallucinations, excitement, paresthesia, diplopia, anxiety, depression, sweating, and tinnitus. Elderly patients are at higher risk for adverse CNS (e.g. hallucinations, confusion) and cardiac events, potentially leading to falls or other sequelae.

Description
  Text
  Cyclobenzaprine is a skeletal muscle relaxant with a tricyclic structure that is closely related to amitriptyline. Cyclobenzaprine is approved for the relief of muscle spasm associated with acute and painful musculoskeletal conditions. Sublingual cyclobenzaprine (Tonmya) is indicated for fibromyalgia. Cyclobenzaprine is not effective for the treatment of muscle spasm due to central nervous system disease (e.g., cerebral palsy, spinal cord disease) and has not been found effective in the treatment of children with cerebral palsy. Cyclobenzaprine was first approved by the FDA in 1977.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Cyclobenzaprine is structurally related to tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs). Pharmacological studies in animals demonstrated a similarity between the effects of cyclobenzaprine and TCAs, including reserpine antagonism, norepinephrine potentiation, potent peripheral and central anticholinergic effects, and sedation. Cyclobenzaprine relieves skeletal muscle spasm of local origin without interfering with muscle function. It acts primarily within the central nervous system (CNS) at the brain stem as opposed to the spinal cord level, although an overlapping action on the latter may contribute to its overall skeletal muscle relaxant. It is ineffective in muscle spasm due to CNS disease. Cyclobenzaprine does not directly act on the neuromuscular junction or skeletal muscle. Evidence suggests that the net effect of cyclobenzaprine is a reduction of tonic somatic motor activity, influencing both gamma and alpha motor systems. The mechanism of action of cyclobenzaprine for the treatment of fibromyalgia is unknown. In vitro pharmacology studies demonstrate functional antagonism of 5-HT2A, alpha 1-adrenergic, H1-histaminergic, and M1-muscarinic acetylcholine receptors.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Immediate-release tablets
Cyclobenzaprine is well absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract following oral administration. Estimates of mean oral bioavailability range from 33% to 55%. Cyclobenzaprine exhibits linear pharmacokinetics over the dose range 2.5 to 10 mg. The drug accumulates when dosed 3 times a day, reaching steady-state within 3 to 4 days at plasma concentrations about 4-fold higher than after a single dose. At steady-state, healthy adults receiving 10 mg 3 times a day, Cmax was 25.9 nanogram/mL (range, 12.8 to 46.1 nanogram/mL), and AUC over an 8 hour dosing interval was 177 nanogram x hour/mL (range, 80 to 319 nanogram x hour/mL).
 
Sublingual tablets (e.g., Tonmya)
Following single-dose sublingual cyclobenzaprine administration of 2.8 mg and 5.6 mg in healthy adults, Cmax and AUC increased in an approximately dose-proportional manner. In a multiple-dose study of 5.6 mg sublingual cyclobenzaprine administered once daily for 20 days in healthy adults, approximately 2.5-fold accumulation of plasma cyclobenzaprine exposure was observed at steady-state. Tmax was approximately 4.3 hours for both dosages. A food effect study using a single dose of 5.6 mg demonstrated an approximate 10% decrease in Cmax with no effect on AUC when administered with food relative to a fasting state. These changes are not clinically significant.
 
Extended-release capsules (e.g., Amrix)
When the extended-release capsules were given to 15 healthy adults, Cmax and AUC increased in a dose-proportional manner from 15 mg to 30 mg; Tmax was 7 to 8 hours for both doses. When the contents of the extended-release capsules were administered by sprinkling on applesauce, it was found to be bioequivalent to the same dose administered as an intact capsule. A food effect study using a single dose of 30 mg extended-release capsule demonstrated a statistically significant increase in bioavailability when the capsule was given with food relative to a fasting state. Cmax increased by 35% and AUC by 20%, and Tmax was not affected. Cyclobenzaprine in plasma was first detectable in both the fed and fasted states at 1.5 hours.
  Special Populations
    Name
    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    In a pharmacokinetic study of immediate-release cyclobenzaprine tablets in 15 subjects with mild hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh Class A) and 1 subject with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh Class B), both AUC and Cmax were approximately double the values seen in subjects with normal hepatic function. The pharmacokinetics of cyclobenzaprine in people with moderate hepatic impairment (1 subject only) and severe hepatic impairment is not known.
    Name
    Geriatric
    Description
    The plasma concentration of cyclobenzaprine is elevated in geriatric subjects. AUC concentrations of cyclobenzaprine are 1.7 higher in geriatric adults compared to younger adults. Geriatric male subjects had the highest observed mean increase, approximately 2.4-fold (198.3 nanogram x hour/mL, range 155.6 to 255.3 vs. 83.2 nanogram x hour/mL, range 41.1 to 142.5 for younger males) while concentrations in geriatric females were increased to a much lesser extent, approximately 1.2-fold (143.8 nanogram x hour/mL, range 96.1 to 196.3 vs.115.9 nanogram x hour/mL, range 36.1 to 182.9 for younger females). After cyclobenzaprine extended-release capsules dosing, the AUC was increased by 40% and the half-life of cyclobenzaprine was prolonged in geriatric subjects greater than 65 years compared to younger adult subjects 18 to 45 years (50 hours vs. 32 hours, respectively). There were no notable differences in Cmax or Tmax in this study.
  Text
  Cyclobenzaprine is administered orally or sublingually. Cyclobenzaprine is highly bound to plasma proteins. It is extensively metabolized and undergoes enterohepatic recirculation. CYP1A2, CYP2D6 (to a lesser extent), and CYP3A4 are primarily responsible for the N-demethylation pathway of cyclobenzaprine metabolism (1 of the oxidative pathways). It is excreted primarily as glucuronides via the kidney. The plasma clearance is 0.7 L/minute. The half-life ranges from 8 to 37 hours.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP1A2, CYP2D6, CYP3A4
Cyclobenzaprine is a substrate of CYP1A2, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4.
