Drug Class: Recombinant glucagon is identical to native glucagon.

Routes: nasal, injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: Dependent on route of administration and indication for therapy.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): asthenia, coma, dizziness, flushing, headache, hyperglycemia, hypoglycemia, pallor, hypertension, hypotension, sinus tachycardia, bullous rash, necrolytic migratory erythema, pruritus, cough, dysgeusia, epistaxis, nasal congestion, parosmia, rhinorrhea, sneezing, throat irritation

In an Overdose: It is also used as a second-line agent as a cardiac stimulant in beta-adrenergic blocker overdose to help treat bradycardia; high dose infusions have been necessary for adjunctive treatment of these cases. While also used for calcium channel blocker overdose in some studies, the evidence is lacking for a survival benefit with its use.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Glucagon is contraindicated in patients with a known hypersensitivity to glucagon or any other constituent of the formulations. Glucagon is contraindicated in patients who have been diagnosed with insulinoma and/or pheochromocytoma.

        Dosage Description
        The dose ranges from 0.2 mg to 2 mg depending on the diagnostic technique and the route of glucagon administration. The usual diagnostic dose for relaxation of the stomach, duodenal bulb, duodenum, and small bowel is 0.2 mg to 0.5 mg IV OR 1 mg IM; the usual dose to relax the colon is 0.5 mg to 0.75 mg IV OR 1 mg to 2 mg IM. After the end of the diagnostic procedure, give oral carbohydrates to patients who have been fasting, if this is compatible with the diagnostic procedure applied. Because the stomach and colon are less sensitive to glucagon's effects, one manufacturer recommends the 2 mg IM dose to inhibit movement of these organs. 1 mg IM has an approximate onset of action of 8 to 10 minutes and a duration of 12 to 27 minutes. 2 mg IM has an approximate onset of action of 4 to 7 minutes and a duration of 21 to 32 minutes. If examining the colon, administer IM approximately 10 minutes prior to the procedure.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Glucagon is contraindicated in patients with a known hypersensitivity to glucagon or any other constituent of the formulations.
 
Glucagon is contraindicated in patients who have been diagnosed with insulinoma and/or pheochromocytoma. The administration of glucagon can cause these tumors to release catecholamines and insulin, respectively. In patients with pheochromocytoma, stimulation of catecholamine release by exogenous glucagon can cause a substantial increase in blood pressure. In patients with insulinomas, hypoglycemia can occur due to excessive insulin release from the tumor. Use cautiously in patients suspected of having pheochromocytoma or insulinoma.
 
Glucagon is active in patients with normal liver stores of glycogen. Glucagon is not effective in treating hypoglycemia secondary to depleted glycogen stores, as occurs with malnutrition, starvation, adrenal insufficiency, or chronic hypoglycemia.
 
Since glucagon increases the force of contraction, it should be used cautiously in patients with known cardiac disease or coronary artery disease.
 
Because glucagon depletes glycogen stores, patients should be given supplemental carbohydrates as soon as possible, especially children and adolescents. The safe and effective use of glucagon as a diagnostic aid in children has not been established.
 
Glucagon has been classified pregnancy category B. Animal studies have not revealed any teratogenic effects associated with glucagon. Limited human and animal data both indicate that glucagon does not cross the placenta. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women; however limited clinical data in humans have revealed that embryo and/or fetal risk from glucagon administered during pregnancy is very low or even negligible. Only doses of up to 1 mg have been reported in pregnant women. Because appropriate care of pregnant women is essential, pregnant women suffering from severe hypoglycemia should be administered glucagon when clinically indicated without concern for fetal safety; withholding therapy may place the fetus at greater risk than the minimal, if any, risk of glucagon exposure.
 
It is not known if glucagon is excreted in human milk. Because many drugs are excreted in human milk, caution should be exercised when glucagon is administered to breast-feeding women. No clinical studies have been performed in nursing mothers, however, glucagon is a peptide and is not absorbed intact from the GI tract. Therefore, it would be unlikely to have any effect on the infant if ingested by the infant via breast milk. Further, glucagon has a high molecular weight and a short plasma half-life thus limiting amounts excreted into human milk and available to the nursing infant.

    Headline
    pheochromocytoma
    General Information
    Glucagon is contraindicated in patients with pheochromocytoma because glucagon may stimulate the release of catecholamines from the tumor. If the patient develops a substantial increase in blood pressure and a previously undiagnosed pheochromocytoma is suspected, 5 to 10 mg of phentolamine mesylate, administered intravenously, has been shown to be effective in lowering blood pressure for the short time that control would be needed.
    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    Glucagon is effective in treating hypoglycemia only if sufficient hepatic glycogen stores are present. Individuals in states of starvation (e.g., anorexia nervosa), malnutrition, adrenal insufficiency, or chronic hypoglycemia may not have adequate concentrations of hepatic glycogen for glucagon administration to be effective. Treat individuals with these conditions with glucose. After the end of a diagnostic procedure where glucagon has been administered, administer oral carbohydrates to individuals who have been fasting, if this is compatible with the diagnostic procedure applied.
    Headline
    General Information
    Headline
    glucagonoma, insulinoma
    General Information
    Glucagon is contraindicated in patients who have been diagnosed with or suspected of having an insulinoma. In patients with insulinoma, glucagon may produce an initial increase in blood glucose; however, glucagon administration may directly or indirectly (through an initial rise in blood glucose) stimulate exaggerated insulin release from the insulinoma. Any patient developing symptoms of hypoglycemia after a dose of glucagon should be given glucose orally or intravenously, whichever is most appropriate. Glucagon is contraindicated in patients with glucagonoma when used as a diagnostic aid. Test patients suspected of having glucagonoma for blood levels of glucagon prior to diagnostic use, and monitor for changes in blood glucose levels during treatment. If a patient develops symptoms of hypoglycemia after a diagnostic dosage, administer glucose orally or intravenously.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Inform patients that hypoglycemia has occurred when glucagon has been used for diagnostic purposes. Inform patients of the symptoms of hypoglycemia and how to treat it. Advise patients to avoid activities requiring coordination and concentration, such as driving or operating machinery, until ingesting a meal. Advise patients to inform their health care provider if hypoglycemia occurs so that treatment may be given if necessary. When used as a diagnostic aid in patients with diabetes mellitus, glucagon may cause hyperglycemia; monitor such patients for changes in blood glucose levels during treatment.
    Headline
    cardiac disease
    General Information
    Glucagon may increase myocardial oxygen demand, blood pressure, and pulse rate which may be life-threatening in patients with cardiac disease or coronary artery disease. Cardiac monitoring is recommended in patients with cardiac disease during use of glucagon as a diagnostic aid, and an increase in blood pressure and pulse rate may require therapy.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data from case reports and a few observational studies over decades of glucagon use during pregnancy have not identified a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. Glucagon does not cross the human placental barrier. Pregnant individuals suffering from severe hypoglycemia should be administered glucagon when clinically indicated without concern for fetal safety; withholding therapy may place the fetus at a greater risk than the minimal, if any, risk of glucagon exposure. Animal studies have not revealed any teratogenic effects associated with glucagon.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Glucagon is compatible with breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of glucagon in human milk, its effects on the breast-fed child, or its effects on milk production. However, glucagon is a peptide and intact glucagon is not absorbed from the GI tract. Therefore, even if the breast-fed child ingested glucagon it would be unlikely to have any effect on them. Additionally, glucagon has a short plasma half-life, thus limiting the amounts available to the breast-fed child.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Adverse reactions are minimal during glucagon use. The most frequent adverse reaction is nausea/vomiting, which is dose related. Doses above 1 mg or doses administered by rapid injection (less than 1 minute) produce a higher incidence of nausea and vomiting than lower doses. Nausea and vomiting may also be caused by hypoglycemia, rather than the drug.Generalized allergic reactions, including urticaria, lightheadedness, and dizziness have occurred in patients receiving glucagon. The exact incidence of such reactions is not known. Anaphylactoid reactions, including respiratory distress, appear to be infrequent. Hypotension, as an anaphylactoid-related reaction to glucagon, is rare. However, hypotension has been reported up to 2 hours after administration in patients receiving glucagon as premedication for upper GI endoscopy procedures. Glucagon exerts positive inotropic and chronotropic effect and may therefore cause transient sinus tachycardia and hypertension following administration; increases in blood pressure and pulse rate may require therapy in patients with pheochromocytoma or coronary artery disease. Because glucagon is a cardiac stimulant, it can theoretically cause chest pain in patients with angina. The actual incidence of this reaction is not known, but appears to be rare.

Description
  Text
  Recombinant glucagon is identical to native glucagon. The recombinant injection products are expressed in either a Saccharoyces cervisiae (e.g., GlucaGen) or Escherichia coli strain (e.g., Glucagon, Lilly), with subsequent purification. Glucagon nasal powder (Baqsimi) and glucagon injection (Gvoke) are also identical to native glucagon. Native human glucagon is a hormone synthesized by the alpha-2 cells of the pancreatic islets of Langerhans and acts to increase blood glucose. Glucagon is used in the emergency treatment of severe hypoglycemia in patients with diabetes mellitus. Other uses for glucagon are as a diagnostic aid in radiologic and endoscopic examination of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract; it is approved in adults for this purpose and is used off-label in pediatric patients for diagnostic use. It is also used as a second-line agent as a cardiac stimulant in beta-adrenergic blocker overdose to help treat bradycardia; high dose infusions have been necessary for adjunctive treatment of these cases. While also used for calcium channel blocker overdose in some studies, the evidence is lacking for a survival benefit with its use.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Glucagon injections produce the same pharmacologic effects as endogenous glucagon. Native human glucagon is a hormone synthesized by the alpha-2 cells of the pancreatic islets of Langerhans and acts to increase blood glucose. Glucagon increases blood glucose concentration by activating hepatic glucagon receptors, thereby stimulating glycogen breakdown and release of glucose from the liver. Hepatic stores of glycogen are necessary for glucagon to produce an antihypoglycemic effect. As a result, blood glucose levels are increased within minutes of glucagon administration. Blood glucose concentration increase within 10 minutes of injection, with maximal blood glucose levels occurring 30 to 45 minutes after injection, depending on the route of administration. The duration of hyperglycemic action after intravenous or intramuscular injection is 60 to 90 minutes.
 
Extra hepatic effects of glucagon include relaxation of the smooth muscle of the stomach, duodenum, small bowel, and colon. The exact mechanism by which glucagon exerts its effects on GI smooth muscle are unknown.
