Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General dosing and discontinuation recommendations
The risks and benefits of ongoing benzodiazepine use for treatment of anxiety should be assessed every 3 months or as clinically indicated.
Those who are candidates for discontinuation should be assessed for risks of withdrawal prior to stopping the medication; individuals who are likely to be physically dependent on the benzodiazepine should be tapered off the medication rather than having an abrupt discontinuation. While most tapers can be managed outpatient, patients at imminent risk of harm related to continued use (e.g., drug interactions, falls, or overdose), those with concomitant physical or mental health conditions that may complicate the safe tapering, and those with current or anticipated severe or complicated withdrawals may require inpatient care.
Tapering strategies should be patient-specific and may be adjusted as needed to address withdrawal symptoms. Initial dose reductions of 5% are recommended, with an assessment of tolerability made. In general, the pace of benzodiazepine tapering should include dose reductions of 5% to 10% every 2 to 4 weeks and should not exceed 25% every 2 weeks.
Individuals at risk of physical dependence, such as those taking a higher dose for more than a year, may require a conservative taper of 5% to 10% every 6 to 8 weeks or slower, depending on symptoms. Those with lower risks of physical dependence or who tolerate the initial dose reduction may be able to reduce the dose by 10% to 25% every 2 to 4 weeks.
If withdrawal symptoms occur and interfere with the taper (e.g., sleep difficulty or anxiety), the taper may be paused or further slowed. Adjunctive medications may also be considered.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Chlordiazepoxide is contraindicated in any patient with a known or suspected hypersensitivity to chlordiazepoxide or with sensitivity to any component of the formulation. Patients with a benzodiazepine hypersensitivity to other benzodiazepines may experience cross-sensitivity to chlordiazepoxide.
 
Intraarterial administration of injectable chlordiazepoxide is not recommended due to the possibility of arteriospasm and resultant gangrene.
 
Occasionally, pre-existing depression may emerge or worsen with the use of benzodiazepines. Chlordiazepoxide should be administered cautiously and prescribed in the smallest possible amount to patients with suicidal ideation or a history of suicide attempt. Chlordiazepoxide should be used cautiously in patients with bipolar disorder because mania and hypomania have been reported in conjunction with the use of benzodiazepines in depressive disorders.
 
In general, chlordiazepoxide should not be administered parenterally to patients with acute ethanol intoxication because the drug can worsen CNS depression. If indicated, parenteral chlordiazepoxide may be used to control agitation in acute alcohol withdrawal with a switch to oral chlordiazepoxide as soon as feasible. Use of oral or parenteral chlordiazepoxide should be avoided in patients with coma, head trauma, or shock also due to worsening CNS depression.
 
Many benzodiazepines are contraindicated in patients with acute closed-angle glaucoma. However, the manufacturers of chlordiazepoxide do not specifically contraindicate use of the drug in any type of glaucoma. The mechanistic rational for this contraindication has been questioned, as benzodiazepines do not have antimuscarinic activity and do not raise intraocular pressure. Benzodiazepines may be used in patients with open-angle glaucoma who are receiving appropriate therapy.
 
Chlordiazepoxide can cause physical and psychological dependence, and should be used with extreme caution in patients with known, suspected or a history of substance abuse. Generally, benzodiazepines should be prescribed for short periods (2—4 weeks) with continued reevaluation of the need for treatment. Tolerance (or tachyphylaxis) may develop to the sedative effects of benzodiazepines. Patients should be questioned about the need for escalating doses, and the clinician may need to intervene to prevent further tolerance or increased risk for addiction. Abrupt discontinuation of chlordiazepoxide after prolonged use should be avoided. Abrupt discontinuation of benzodiazepine therapy has been reported to cause withdrawal symptoms, especially following high dose or prolonged therapy (see Adverse Effects). However, benzodiazepine dependence can occur following administration of therapeutic doses for as few as 1—2 weeks, and withdrawal symptoms may be seen following the discontinuation of therapy. Patients with a history of a seizure disorder or who are taking other drugs that lower the seizure threshold (i.e., tricyclic antidepressants, phenothiazines) should not be withdrawn abruptly from benzodiazepines due to the risk of precipitating seizures; status epilepticus has also been reported. Benzodiazepines should be withdrawn slowly, using a gradual dosage-tapering schedule. During withdrawal, the greatest risk of seizure appears to be during the first 24 to 72 hours. Clinicians should be aware that the use of flumazenil may increase the risk of seizures, particularly in long-term users of benzodiazepines and in cyclic antidepressant overdoses (see Flumazenil monograph).
 
Chlordiazepoxide should be used with caution in patients with a neuromuscular disease, such as muscular dystrophy, myotonia, or myasthenia gravis as these conditions can be exacerbated. Patients with late stage Parkinson's disease may experience worsening of their psychosis or impaired cognition with administration of benzodiazepines. Benzodiazepines may also cause incoordination or paradoxical reactions that may worsen symptoms of Parkinson's disease. Use cautiously in patients with organic brain syndromes (e.g., dementia) or other types of psychosis. Paradoxical reactions, including acute rage, stimulation and excitement have been reported in adult psychiatric patients using chlordiazepoxide.
 
Chlordiazepoxide should be used with extreme caution in patients with respiratory depression, pulmonary disease such as severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), or sleep apnea because the drug can exacerbate ventilatory failure.
 
Due to CNS depression, patients should be cautioned against driving or operating machinery until they know how chlordiazepoxide may affect them. Some patients may experience excessive sedation and impaired ability to perform tasks. Increased CNS effects may be seen with concurrent use of chlordiazepoxide and other CNS depressant agents (see Drug Interactions). Anterograde amnesia has been reported with benzodiazepines.
 
The administration of chlordiazepoxide can exacerbate acute intermittent porphyria, so the drug should be used with caution in patients with this condition.
 
Chlordiazepoxide is classified as pregnancy category D because it could cause harm to the fetus when administered to pregnant women. Positive evidence of human fetal risk exists based on investigational, marketing, or human studies. There is rarely sufficient urgency to justify the use of minor tranquilizers during pregnancy. Chlordiazepoxide should not be administered during the first trimester of pregnancy. Chlordiazepoxide is not recommended for use in obstetrical procedures, labor, or obstetric delivery, including cesarean section. Neonatal flaccidity has been reported in an infant whose mother was using benzodiazepines during pregnancy.
 
Chlordiazepoxide is distributed into breast milk and can cause sedation, feeding difficulties, and weight loss in the nursing infant. The use of chlordiazepoxide during breast-feeding is generally not recommended.
 
Chlordiazepoxide should be administered cautiously to patients with severe hepatic disease because the elimination half-life of the drug can be prolonged, possibly resulting in toxicity. The liver is utilized for metabolism of chlordiazepoxide. Patients with hepatic disease should receive reduced initial dosages and be closely monitored for tolerability. Additionally, chlordiazepoxide metabolites may accumulate leading to severe and prolonged stupor in patients with cirrhosis or hepatitis.
 
Patients with renal impairment should be carefully monitored during prolonged treatment with benzodiazepines to avoid the adverse reactions that occur from accumulation. In patients with renal failure, it is suggested to reduce the dose of chlordiazepoxide by at least 50%.
 
The clearance and/or elimination of many drugs are reduced in the elderly. Delayed elimination can either intensify or prolong the actions of adverse reactions of the chlordiazepoxide. Benzodiazepines have been associated with an increased incidence of falls in the elderly. The impairment of cognitive and motor function may be more marked in this patient group and lower initial dosage is recommended together with close monitoring (see Dosage). The concomitant use of other psychotropic agents should be approached with caution in the elderly. Federal OBRA regulations recommend maximum dosage limits for chlordiazepoxide when used as an anxiolytic or hypnotic in the nursing home resident (see dosage).
 
The safe and effective use of chlordiazepoxide in children under 6 years old has not been established and is not recommended in this age group. Children are generally more sensitive to the CNS effects of the benzodiazepines. Paradoxical reactions, including acute rage, stimulation and excitement have been reported in hyperactive aggressive pediatric patients and should be observed for if chlordiazepoxide therapy is instituted.

    Headline
    coadministration with certain medications, pulmonary disease, respiratory insufficiency
    General Information
    Deaths have been reported in people with severe pulmonary disease after initiation of chlordiazepoxide; closely monitor patients with impaired respiratory function (respiratory insufficiency). If signs and symptoms of respiratory depression, hypoventilation, or apnea occur, discontinue chlordiazepoxide. Chlordiazepoxide coadministration with certain medications, such as opioids, may result in serious adverse effects, including profound sedation, CNS depression, respiratory depression, coma, and death. The concomitant use of these medications has demonstrated an increased risk of drug-related mortality compared to the use of opioids alone. The combined use of opioids and benzodiazepines should be reserved for patients in whom alternative treatment options are inadequate. If the decision is made to prescribe chlordiazepoxide concomitantly with opioids, the lowest effective dose should be prescribed for the shortest duration of use, and patients should be followed closely for signs and symptoms of respiratory depression and sedation. Advise patients and caregivers about the risks of respiratory depression and sedation when chlordiazepoxide is used with opioids. Due to the risks associated with concomitant use, the ASAM clinical practice guidelines suggest re-evaluation of the risks and benefits of continued benzodiazepine prescribing in patients taking opioids at least every 3 months or with every related clinical encounter or prescription renewal, whichever is more frequent.
    Headline
    porphyria
    General Information
    Chlordiazepoxide may have a porphyrogenic effect and should be used cautiously in patients with porphyria. There are isolated reports associating chlordiazepoxide with exacerbation of porphyria.
    Headline
    abrupt discontinuation, increased risk for benzodiazepine withdrawal, substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    The use of benzodiazepines, including chlordiazepoxide, exposes users to serious adverse events, including risks of abuse, misuse, and addiction, which can lead to overdose or death. Abuse and misuse of benzodiazepines commonly involve concomitant use of other medications, alcohol, and/or illicit substances, which can increase the frequency of serious adverse outcomes. Before prescribing and throughout treatment, assess each patient's risk for abuse, misuse, and addiction using a standardized screening tool. Counsel patients about the risks and proper use of chlordiazepoxide and prescribe the lowest effective dose to reduce excess exposure and advise patients on proper disposal of unused medication. Minimize the concomitant use of CNS depressants or other substances associated with abuse, misuse, and addiction (e.g., opioid analgesics, stimulants). If a substance abuse disorder is suspected, evaluate the patient and institute or refer them for early treatment. Chronic, continued use of benzodiazepines may also lead to physical benzodiazepine dependence. The risk of dependence increases with longer treatment duration and higher daily doses and may lead to an increased risk for benzodiazepine withdrawal upon discontinuation. Abrupt discontinuation or rapid dose reduction can precipitate acute withdrawal reactions, which can be life-threatening. Patients with a history of a seizure disorder should not be withdrawn abruptly from benzodiazepines due to the risk of precipitating seizures; status epilepticus has also been reported. Clinicians should be aware that the use of flumazenil may increase the risk of seizures, particularly in long-term users of benzodiazepines. To reduce the risk of withdrawal reactions, use a gradual, patient-specific taper to reduce the dose or discontinue chlordiazepoxide. In some cases, patients using benzodiazepines have developed a protracted withdrawal syndrome with symptoms lasting weeks to more than 12 months.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Chlordiazepoxide has the potential to impair judgment, thinking, or motor skills. Individuals should use caution when driving or operating machinery or participating in other activities requiring coordination and concentration until they know how chlordiazepoxide may affect them. Some patients may experience excessive sedation and impaired ability to perform tasks. Given the primary CNS effects of chlordiazepoxide, use caution during coadministration with other CNS depressants. Alcohol may further impair cognitive and motor skills and can increase the risk of serious CNS or respiratory depression and alcohol should be avoided during chlordiazepoxide treatment.
    Headline
    myasthenia gravis
    General Information
    Chlordiazepoxide should be used with caution in patients with myasthenia gravis, particularly at higher doses; these patients may be more sensitive to the CNS and respiratory effects of the benzodiazepines.
    Headline
    hepatic disease
    General Information
    The usual precautions in treating people with impaired hepatic function should be observed when chlordiazepoxide is prescribed. Use chlordiazepoxide with caution in people with hepatic disease due to the extensive metabolism of the drug in the liver and the production of active metabolites; the elimination half-life of the drug can be prolonged. For example, chlordiazepoxide metabolites may accumulate leading to severe and prolonged stupor in patients with hepatic cirrhosis. Patients with hepatic disease should receive reduced initial dosages and be closely monitored for sedation and tolerability.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Use chlordiazepoxide with caution in those with renal impairment; while chlordiazepoxide is extensively metabolized, some of the metabolites are active and metabolites are excreted in the urine. In renal failure (eGFR less than 10 mL/minute), it is suggested to reduce the dosage of chlordiazepoxide by at least 50%. Monitor these patients closely for excessive sedation or other adverse effects.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Chlordiazepoxide should be used cautiously in geriatric adults since they are more sensitive to the effects of benzodiazepines; lower initial doses and slower titration are recommended. Because chlordiazepoxide can cause drowsiness and a decreased level of consciousness, there is a higher risk of falls, particularly in the elderly, with the potential for subsequent severe injuries. Due to its long half-life and the availability of safer alternatives, chlordiazepoxide is not a drug of choice for most indications in the older adult. The Beers Criteria state that benzodiazepines are potentially inappropriate medications in geriatric adults with a history of delirium, dementia, or a history of falls or fractures; benzodiazepines increase the risk of cognitive impairment, delirium, falls, fractures, and motor vehicle accidents in older adults and avoidance is generally recommended, with some exceptions for severe generalized anxiety disorder and other approved indications. If a benzodiazepine must be used, consider reducing the use of other CNS-active medications and implement other strategies to reduce fall risk. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates anxiolytic and sedative/hypnotic use in long-term care facilities. When a drug is used to induce sleep, treat a sleep disorder, manage behavior, stabilize mood, or treat a psychiatric disorder, tapering as outlined in the OBRA guidelines should be attempted unless clinically contraindicated. Dosages and durations of treatment used should align with prescribing labels, published literature recommendations, and expert guidelines.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Published data from observational studies of benzodiazepine use during pregnancy have not established a drug-associated risk of major birth defects. Early studies suggested an increased risk of congenital malformations with chlordiazepoxide and diazepam use. However, more recent case-control and cohort studies of benzodiazepine use during pregnancy, adjusted for confounding exposures to other substances, have not confirmed these findings. Benzodiazepines cross the placenta. Neonates exposed to benzodiazepines during late pregnancy, including during labor and delivery, may experience symptoms of sedation. Those with prolonged exposure during late pregnancy may experience withdrawal after delivery. The incidence, time to onset, and duration of sedation and withdrawal are multifactorial (e.g., duration of use, drug lipophilicity, placental disposition, degree of accumulation in neonatal tissues). Chlordiazepoxide is not a preferred benzodiazepine to use during pregnancy because of its long duration of action and active metabolites. Individuals who become pregnant or intend to become pregnant while taking chlordiazepoxide should be advised to discuss the possibility of discontinuing the drug with their physician. If a benzodiazepine is required during pregnancy, avoid first trimester administration if possible, consider short-acting agents, limit treatment to the shortest possible duration and lowest effective dose, and discontinue the drug well before delivery.  There is a pregnancy registry that monitors pregnancy outcomes in women exposed to chlordiazepoxide or other psychiatric medications during pregnancy. Healthcare providers are encouraged to register patients by calling the National Pregnancy Registry for Psychiatric Medications at 1-866-961-2388 or visiting online at https://womensmentalhealth.org/research/pregnancyregistry/.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Monitor neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero for symptoms of withdrawal, such as hyperreflexia, irritability, restlessness, tremors, inconsolable crying, and feeding difficulties. In neonates exposed during late pregnancy, including during labor and delivery, monitor for sedation, including respiratory depression, lethargy, and hypotonia. If symptoms are present, manage accordingly.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Avoid the use of chlordiazepoxide during breast-feeding. Chlordiazepoxide and its metabolites are excreted into human milk in moderate concentrations. Based on its long half-life and active metabolites, other benzodiazepines are preferred in lactation. Monitor children exposed to benzodiazepines through breast milk for excessive sedation, slowed breathing rate, poor feeding, and inadequate weight gain. Withdrawal may occur in those with long-term exposure to the drug. This may occur with cessation of breast-feeding or cessation of the medication in the breast-feeding person.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Tolerance (or tachyphylaxis) may develop to the sedative effects of benzodiazepines, including chlordiazepoxide. Tolerance to the anxiolytic effects of benzodiazepines has also been reported. If tolerance develops, clinicians should carefully assess the need for escalating doses. A temporary reduction in dosage may be beneficial in some cases to reverse the effects of tachyphylaxis.Prolonged use of benzodiazepines can produce physiological dependence with or without psychological dependence. The risk of dependence increases with duration of treatment and in those with a history of alcohol or drug abuse. Dependence can occur following routine use of therapeutic doses for as few as 1—2 weeks, particularly during treatment with short-acting benzodiazepines. Extreme caution is advised when considering the use of chlordiazepoxide in patients with a known or suspected history of substance abuse.Anterograde amnesia can occur following administration of therapeutic doses of benzodiazepines, such as chlordiazepoxide. The risk for developing anterograde amnesia increases at higher doses and with concurrent ingestion of alcohol.The most frequent adverse effects of chlordiazepoxide therapy are likely to be extensions of the drug's CNS-depressant effects, e.g., drowsiness or lightheadedness. The incidence and severity of these effects will vary with the dose used and condition being treated. Tolerance to these adverse effects may develop over time.Paradoxical stimulation can occur secondary to benzodiazepine therapy. Signs of CNS-stimulation are generally seen early in benzodiazepine therapy. This is of particular significance in psychiatric patients who may be receiving other CNS-depressants, and have underlying behavioral problems. Hyperactive children may also show signs of paradoxical stimulation. Chlordiazepoxide should be withdrawn if there is any evidence of agitation, rage, irritability, aggressiveness or hostility.Chlordiazepoxide has been in use for many years and discontinuation of therapy due to adverse events is rare. Elderly and debilitated patients are more likely to suffer from adverse effects, such as drowsiness, ataxia, and confusion. Other adverse events associated with oral therapy have included syncope; minor menstrual irregularity; nausea/vomiting; constipation; extrapyramidal symptoms such as dyskinesia, akathisia, and dystonic reaction (e.g., torticollis, opisthotonos, trismus, and oculogyric crisis); tremor; libido increase or libido decrease. Changes in EEG patterns have been noted. In addition, use of parenteral chlordiazepoxide has been associated with blurred vision, hypotension and an injection site reaction (pain from intramuscular injection). There have been occasional reports of blood dyscrasias, jaundice and hepatic dysfunction. Chlordiazepoxide-induced purpura has been reported in one case. Patients receiving long-term therapy with chlordiazepoxide should undergo periodic blood counts and liver function tests.Physiological dependence on chlordiazepoxide is evidenced by manifestation of withdrawal symptoms. The severity of symptoms is related to the dosage level and duration of therapy. At therapeutic dosage levels abrupt discontinuation of chlordiazepoxide can lead to withdrawal symptoms such as dysphoria and insomnia. At higher doses and after prolonged use abrupt discontinuation may lead to more severe symptoms such as seizures, tremor, abdominal and muscle cramps, vomiting and sweating. If discontinuation of therapy is indicated, the dose of chlordiazepoxide should be reduced slowly and cautiously. Patients with a history of drug or alcohol abuse are more likely to become dependent on chlordiazepoxide and require careful monitoring.Chlordiazepoxide is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category D. Many benzodiazepines have been associated with teratogenesis. Use of benzodiazepines during pregnancy, particularly in the first trimester, generally increases the risk of congenital malformations and decreases viability.

    General Information
    Prolonged use of benzodiazepines can produce physiological dependence with or without psychological dependence. The risk of psychological dependence increases in those with a history of alcohol or substance abuse. Abuse and misuse of benzodiazepines commonly involve concomitant use of other medications, alcohol, and/or illicit substances, which is associated with an increased frequency of serious adverse outcomes, including respiratory depression, overdose, and death. Avoid or minimize concomitant use of CNS depressants or other medications associated with addiction or abuse. Clinically significant physiological dependence may occur with continued use of benzodiazepines. The risks of dependence and withdrawal increase with longer treatment duration and higher daily dose. Abrupt discontinuation or rapid dosage reduction of benzodiazepines after continued use may precipitate acute withdrawal reactions, which can be life-threatening. Withdrawal symptoms may range from mild dysphoria and insomnia to a major withdrawal syndrome including abdominal and muscle cramps, vomiting, sweating, tremors, and seizures. In some cases, benzodiazepine users have developed a protracted withdrawal syndrome with withdrawal symptoms lasting weeks to more than 12 months. Use a gradual, patient-specific taper to lower the risk of withdrawal reactions when reducing the dose or discontinuing therapy. If significant withdrawal symptoms occur, the rate of reduction may need to be slowed or paused. Adjunctive medications may also be considered to help mitigate withdrawal effects.

Description
  Text
  Chlordiazepoxide is a long-acting benzodiazepine. It is used to treat anxiety disorders or for the short-term relief of anxiety symptoms, alcohol withdrawal symptoms, and preoperative apprehension and anxiety. However, the drug is rarely used for these purposes as it has been largely replaced by newer agents. If used for anxiety, chlordiazepoxide should generally be prescribed for short periods only due to the potential for dependence and cognitive impairment, and the availability of safer alternatives. The ASAM guidelines recommend re-evaluating the risks and benefits of ongoing benzodiazepine use at least every 3 months; for patients who are candidates for discontinuation, an individualized taper strategy should be used that considers dose, duration, and frequency of use. Chlordiazepoxide also has been used for many years in the management of alcohol withdrawal and can be one of the preferred agents in patients with alcohol withdrawal exhibiting severe symptoms. Longer-acting agents such as chlordiazepoxide can accumulate and lead to oversedation and respiratory depression, particularly in geriatric adults. As with all benzodiazepines, product labels carry a boxed warning about the risk of fatal respiratory depression particularly when used with opioids, as well as risks for substance abuse, misuse and addiction, and withdrawal reactions with discontinuation. Chlordiazepoxide was initially FDA-approved in 1961.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Benzodiazepines act at the level of the limbic, thalamic, and hypothalamic regions of the CNS and can produce any level of CNS depression required including sedation, hypnosis, skeletal muscle relaxation, and anticonvulsant activity. Recent evidence indicates that benzodiazepines exert their effects through enhancement of the gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-benzodiazepine receptor complex. GABA is an inhibitory neurotransmitter that exerts its effects at specific receptor subtypes designated GABA-A and GABA-B. GABA-A is the primary receptor subtype in the CNS and is thought to be involved in the actions of anxiolytics and sedatives.
 
Specific benzodiazepine receptor subtypes are thought to be coupled to GABA-A receptors. Three types of BNZ receptors are located in the CNS and other tissues; the BNZ1 receptors are located in the cerebellum and cerebral cortex, the BNZ2 receptors in the cerebral cortex and spinal cord, and the BNZ3 receptors in peripheral tissues. Activation of the BNZ1 receptor is thought to mediate sleep while the BNZ2 receptor affects muscle relaxation, anticonvulsant activity, motor coordination, and memory. Benzodiazepines bind nonspecifically to BNZ1 and BNZ2 which ultimately enhances the effects of GABA. Unlike barbiturates which augment GABA responses by increasing the length of time that chloride channels are open, benzodiazepines enhance the effects of GABA by increasing GABA affinity for the GABA receptor. Binding of GABA to the site opens the chloride channel resulting in a hyperpolarized cell membrane that prevents further excitation of the cell.
 
Benzodiazepines alleviate insomnia by decreasing the latency to sleep and increasing sleep continuity and total sleep time through their effects on GABA.

  Text
  Chlordiazepoxide is administered orally or parenterally by the intramuscular or intravenous route. Chlordiazepoxide distributes throughout the body and is about 96% protein-bound. It crosses the placenta and is distributed into breast milk.
 
Chlordiazepoxide undergoes oxidative metabolism in the liver, producing several active compounds including demoxepam, desmethylchlordiazepoxide, desmethyldiazepam, and oxazepam. Chlordiazepoxide has a long half-life (5—30 hours) compared with other benzodiazepines, and the half-lives of its metabolites range from 14—100 hours. The risk of accumulation is high during periods of repeated dosing. Unchanged drug and its active metabolites are excreted in the urine, but the elimination is slower than that of other benzodiazepines because the metabolites can still be present in the blood for several weeks after the initial dosing interval.

  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments are not available; use lower initial dosages and monitor closely for oversedation or other adverse effects. Use in patients with significant hepatic disease with extreme caution; there is a high risk of drug accumulation with repeated dosing.

  eGFR 10 mL/minute or more: Use with caution. It is advisable to start with low initial dosage and adjust to response and tolerance in patients with renal impairment.
