Drug Class: Lincomycin is a lincosamide antibiotic, structurally related to clindamycin.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 8 g/day IV. 1.2 g/day IM or more for severe infections.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): pruritus ani, c. difficile-associated diarrhea, candidiasis, pseudomembranous colitis, superinfection, vaginitis, agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, pancytopenia, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (ttp), azotemia, oliguria, proteinuria, dizziness, drowsiness, headache

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Lincomycin should not be used in patients with known lincomycin hypersensitivity. Because some cross-sensitivity may occur, patients with clindamycin hypersensitivity should not be treated with lincomycin.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Lincomycin should not be used in patients with known lincomycin hypersensitivity. Because some cross-sensitivity may occur, patients with clindamycin hypersensitivity should not be treated with lincomycin. Use the drug with caution in patients with asthma or a significant history of allergy (atopy).
 
This drug does not treat viral infection (e.g., common cold). Prescribing lincomycin in the absence of a proven or strongly suspected bacterial infection or a prophylactic indication is unlikely to provide benefit to the patient and increases the risk of the development of drug-resistant bacteria. Patients should be told to complete the full course of treatment, even if they feel better earlier.
 
While some penetration into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) occurs, lincomycin should not be used in the treatment of meningitis as the drug may not adequately diffuse into the CSF.
 
Lincomycin can cause the overgrowth of non susceptible bacteria resulting in superinfection, particularly yeast and fungal infection. Should superinfection occur, appropriate measures should be taken.
 
Almost all antibacterial agents have been associated with pseudomembranous colitis (antibiotic-associated colitis) which may range in severity from mild to life-threatening. In the colon, overgrowth of Clostridia may exist when normal flora is altered subsequent to antibacterial administration. The toxin produced by Clostridium difficile is a primary cause of pseudomembranous colitis. It is known that systemic use of antibiotics predisposes patients to development of pseudomembranous colitis. Consideration should be given to the diagnosis of pseudomembranous colitis in patients presenting with diarrhea following antibacterial administration. Because lincomycin has been associated with severe colitis which may end fatally, therapy should be reserved for serious infections where less toxic antimicrobials are inappropriate. Do not use for nonbacterial infections such as most upper respiratory tract infections. Systemic antibiotics should be prescribed with caution to patients with inflammatory bowel disease such as ulcerative colitis. If diarrhea develops during therapy, the drug should be discontinued. Following diagnosis of pseudomembranous colitis, therapeutic measures should be instituted. In milder cases, the colitis may respond to discontinuation of the offending agent. In moderate to severe cases, fluids and electrolytes, protein supplementation, and treatment with an antibacterial effective against Clostridium difficile may be warranted. Practitioners should be aware that diarrhea, colitis, and pseudomembranous colitis have been observed to begin up to several weeks following discontinuation of systemic antibiotic therapy.
 
Patients with benzyl alcohol hypersensitivity should not be administered intravenous lincomycin because the injection contains benzyl alcohol as a preservative. Lincomycin injection should not be used in infants less than 1 month of age (i.e., neonates). Benzyl alcohol has been associated with a fatal 'gasping syndrome' in premature infants. Safety and effectiveness in neonates have not been established.
 
Lincomycin injection must not be administered by intravenous injection bolus. The injection is only to be used for intramuscular administration or may be delivered by slow intravenous infusion after appropriate dilution. The infusion should be administered over at least 60 minutes (see Administration) and at the recommended concentrations to limit the possibility of serious cardiopulmonary side effects. Patients with a history of cardiac arrhythmias who are receiving lincomycin intravenous infusions may require close monitoring with the initiation of therapy.
 
Lincomycin should be used with caution in patients with renal disease. Patients with renal dysfunction can experience plasma concentrations two to threefold higher than patients with normal kidney function. Dosage reductions are needed in patients with severe renal impairment or renal failure (see Dosage). Serum concentration monitoring during high-dose lincomycin therapy may be needed.
 
Lincomycin should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease. The half-life of the drug is prolonged in these patients and toxicity can occur. Although it would be wise to use lower doses in patients with hepatic dysfunction, specific recommendations for dosage adjustments are not available. Liver function tests (LFTs) should be monitored regularly during therapy, particularly in patients with severe hepatic disease. Serum concentration monitoring during high-dose lincomycin therapy may be needed.
 
Lincomycin is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category B when administered by any route. Placental concentrations are roughly 25% of maternal serum concentrations; however, animal studies have not revealed evidence of fetal harm and data do not support an association of lincomycin with congenital defects in humans. Parenteral lincomycin has been used as prophylactic therapy for Caesarean section. Experience in 345 obstetrical patients receiving this drug revealed no ill effects related to pregnancy.
 
Lincomycin is excreted into human breast milk at detectable concentrations (0.5—2.4 mcg/ml) after maternal administration by the oral or parenteral routes. Because of the potential for adverse reactions in the infant, the decision to discontinue the drug during breast-feeding should be made, taking into account the importance of the drug to the mother. Three potential problems from lincomycin exposure via breast-milk that may occur in the infant are alterations in gut flora, direct effects from the drug, or interference with culture results in the infant should a clinical diagnosis of fever or infection be required.
 
Clinical studies for lincomycin did not include sufficient numbers of elderly patients 65 years of age and older to determine whether they respond differently from younger subjects, however, patients greater than 65 years of age are more likely to suffer from renal or liver disease, and may be more likely to develop antibiotic-associated diarrhea or tolerate diarrhea less well. Lincomycin should be prescribed with caution in these patients.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Lincomycin is also contraindicated in patients with clindamycin hypersensitivity.
    Headline
    C. difficile-associated diarrhea, GI disease
    General Information
    Lincomycin has been associated with C. difficile-associated diarrhea (CDAD). Use lincomycin with caution in people with a history of GI disease, particularly colitis.
    Headline
    infants, neonates
    General Information
    Lincomycin injection contains benzyl alcohol as a preservative. Benzyl alcohol has been associated with serious adverse events including gasping syndrome and death in pediatric patients. Premature neonates and low-birth weight infants may be more likely to develop toxicity.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Reduce lincomycin dose and monitor serum lincomycin concentrations during high-dose therapy in patients with severe renal impairment or renal failure. Periodically monitor renal function tests during prolonged therapy with lincomycin.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Dose lincomycin cautiously and monitor serum lincomycin concentrations during high-dose therapy in patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure. Periodically monitor liver function tests during prolonged therapy with lincomycin.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    When lincomycin is indicated in geriatric patients, they should be carefully monitored for change in bowel frequency. Review of experience to date suggests that a subgroup of older adults with associated severe illness may tolerate diarrhea less well.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on the use of lincomycin during pregnancy are insufficient to evaluate a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. Use lincomycin during pregnancy only if clearly needed. Parenteral formulations of lincomycin contain benzyl alcohol, which crosses the placenta and has been associated with neonatal gasping syndrome. Lincomycin crosses the placental barrier, with cord serum concentrations of approximately 25% of maternal serum concentrations; no substantial accumulation occurs in the amniotic fluid. Data from 345 pregnant patients receiving lincomycin showed no adverse effects relating to pregnancy. There was no evidence of teratogenicity in rats given oral lincomycin at doses up to 6 times the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) based on body surface area during the period of major organogenesis. There were also no adverse effects on offspring survival from birth to weaning in rats given oral lincomycin at doses up to 1.2 times the MRHD based on body surface area.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use lincomycin with caution during breast-feeding. Lincomycin is excreted in human milk in small amounts, with reported concentrations between 0.5 to 2.4 mcg/mL. There are no data evaluating the effect of lincomycin on the breast-fed child or its effect on milk production. Due to limited exposure via human milk and poor oral bioavailability, lincomycin is unlikely to affect the breast-fed child. Monitor the breast-fed child for diarrhea; modification of gut flora is possible. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for lincomycin, and potential adverse effects on the breast-fed child from the medication or from the patient's maternal medical condition.

Desogestrel: Ethinyl Estradiol; Ferrous Bisglycinate: (Moderate) It would be prudent to recommend alternative or additional contraception when oral contraceptives (OCs) are used in conjunction with antibiotics. It was previously thought that antibiotics may decrease the effectiveness of OCs containing estrogens due to stimulation of metabolism or a reduction in enterohepatic circulation via changes in GI flora. One retrospective study reviewed the literature to determine the effects of oral antibiotics on the pharmacokinetics of contraceptive estrogens and progestins, and also examined clinical studies in which the incidence of pregnancy with OCs and antibiotics was reported. It was concluded that the antibiotics ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin, doxycycline, metronidazole, ofloxacin, roxithromycin, temafloxacin, and tetracycline did not alter plasma concentrations of OCs. Antituberculous drugs (e.g., rifampin) were the only agents associated with OC failure and pregnancy. Based on the study results, these authors recommended that back-up contraception may not be necessary if OCs are used reliably during oral antibiotic use. Another review concurred with these data, but noted that individual patients have been identified who experienced significant decreases in plasma concentrations of combined OC components and who appeared to ovulate; the agents most often associated with these changes were rifampin, tetracyclines, and penicillin derivatives. These authors concluded that because females most at risk for OC failure or noncompliance may not be easily identified and the true incidence of such events may be under-reported, and given the serious consequence of unwanted pregnancy, that recommending an additional method of contraception during short-term antibiotic use may be justified. During long-term antibiotic administration, the risk for drug interaction with OCs is less clear, but alternative or additional contraception may be advisable in selected circumstances. Data regarding progestin-only contraceptives or for newer combined contraceptive deliveries (e.g., patches, rings) are not available.

It was previously thought that antibiotics may decrease the effectiveness of OCs containing estrogens due to stimulation of metabolism or a reduction in enterohepatic circulation via changes in GI flora. One retrospective study reviewed the literature to determine the effects of oral antibiotics on the pharmacokinetics of contraceptive estrogens and progestins, and also examined clinical studies in which the incidence of pregnancy with OCs and antibiotics was reported. It was concluded that the antibiotics ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin, doxycycline, metronidazole, ofloxacin, roxithromycin, temafloxacin, and tetracycline did not alter plasma concentrations of OCs. Antituberculous drugs (e.g., rifampin) were the only agents associated with OC failure and pregnancy. Based on the study results, these authors recommended that back-up contraception may not be necessary if OCs are used reliably during oral antibiotic use. Another review concurred with these data, but noted that individual patients have been identified who experienced significant decreases in plasma concentrations of combined OC components and who appeared to ovulate; the agents most often associated with these changes were rifampin, tetracyclines, and penicillin derivatives. These authors concluded that because females most at risk for OC failure or noncompliance may not be easily identified and the true incidence of such events may be under-reported, and given the serious consequence of unwanted pregnancy, that recommending an additional method of contraception during short-term antibiotic use may be justified. During long-term antibiotic administration, the risk for drug interaction with OCs is less clear, but alternative or additional contraception may be advisable in selected circumstances. Data regarding progestin-only contraceptives or for newer combined contraceptive deliveries (e.g., patches, rings) are not available.

Another review concurred with these data, but noted that individual patients have been identified who experienced significant decreases in plasma concentrations of combined OC components and who appeared to ovulate; the agents most often associated with these changes were rifampin, tetracyclines, and penicillin derivatives. These authors concluded that because females most at risk for OC failure or noncompliance may not be easily identified and the true incidence of such events may be under-reported, and given the serious consequence of unwanted pregnancy, that recommending an additional method of contraception during short-term antibiotic use may be justified. During long-term antibiotic administration, the risk for drug interaction with OCs is less clear, but alternative or additional contraception may be advisable in selected circumstances. Data regarding progestin-only contraceptives or for newer combined contraceptive deliveries (e.g., patches, rings) are not available.

  General Information
  Nausea/vomiting, glossitis, stomatitis, and pruritus ani can result from lincomycin therapy. Most symptoms resolve within a few days to weeks after discontinuing the drug. Description
  Text
  Lincomycin is a lincosamide antibiotic, structurally related to clindamycin. Lincomycin was isolated in 1962 from a soil organism, Streptomyces lincolnensis. Further chemical alteration resulted in the production of clindamycin, an antibiotic with greater antimicrobial activity; there is no advantage to using lincomycin over clindamycin, and lincomycin is rarely used clinically today. Lincomycin is indicated for the treatment of serious staphylococcal, pneumococcal, and streptococcal infections in penicillin-allergic patients when treatment with less toxic agents (e.g., erythromycin) is not appropriate. Lincomycin should be avoided in patients with an allergy to clindamycin. Complete bacterial cross resistance has been seen with clindamycin; however, partial cross resistance may also be encountered with lincomycin and agents in the macrolide class. Susceptibility testing is recommended. As with other antibiotics, lincomycin has been associated with severe or fatal pseudomembranous colitis. Lincomycin was FDA-approved in 1964.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Lincomycin binds to the 50 S ribosomal subunits of the bacteria and inhibits protein synthesis, eventually resulting in inhibition of bacterial cell growth or bacterial death. Antibacterial activity primarily results from inhibition of peptide bond formation. Lincomycin is either bacteriostatic or bactericidal depending on its concentration at the site of infection and the specific susceptibility of the organism being treated. Complete bacterial cross resistance has been seen with clindamycin. Resistance is most often due to methylation of specific nucleotides in the 23S RNA of the 50S ribosomal unit, which can determine cross resistance to macrolides and streptogramins B. Susceptibility testing using the D-zone test or other appropriate method is recommended for macrolide-resistant organisms to determine inducible resistance.

  Text
  Lincomycin is administered via the intravenous and intramuscular routes. Lincomycin is 77% to 82% protein bound.
 
Significant lincomycin concentrations have been demonstrated in the majority of body tissues. Lincomycin appears to diffuse into cerebral spinal fluid (CSF); however, the CSF concentrations appear inadequate for the treatment of meningitis.
