Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Varicella virus vaccine live is preservative-free, but it does contain trace amounts of neomycin from the manufacturing process and should not be given to anyone with a history of immediate-type neomycin hypersensitivity (e.g., anaphylactoid reactions). Patients with a history of hypersensitivity to any component of the vaccine, including gelatin hypersensitivity, should also not receive varicella virus vaccine live. With any biologic product, the prescriber or health care professional should take precautions to prevent allergic reactions. The health care professional should have immediate availability of epinephrine (1:1000) injection and other agents used in the treatment of severe anaphylaxis in the event of a serious allergic reaction to varicella virus vaccine live. Prior to the administration of the vaccine, the health care personnel should inform the parent, guardian, or responsible adult of the benefits and risks to the patient. This should include the provision of the varicella virus vaccine live information statement from the manufacturer. The responsible adult should report any adverse reaction following vaccine administration to the health care provider. The US Department of Health and Human Services has established a Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) to accept all reports of suspected adverse events after the administration of any vaccine. This includes, but is not limited to, the reporting of events required by the National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act of 1986. The toll-free number for VAERS is 800—822—7967.
 
Intravenous administration of varicella virus vaccine live is not recommended. Varicella virus vaccine live is for subcutaneous administration only. Current evidence indicates that inadvertent intramuscular administration of the vaccine does not increase side effects and produces adequate short-term immune responses; revaccination is not recommended. The duration of immunity following inadvertent intramuscular administration is less clear.
 
Any febrile respiratory illness such as a respiratory infection or other active infection associated with a fever is a contraindication for varicella virus vaccine live. Further, patients with active, untreated tuberculosis should not be given varicella virus vaccine live, as there are no data on the effects of the safety of vaccination in these patients.
 
Recommendations and precautions for patients with immunosuppression are complex, but the varicella virus vaccine live is contraindicated in any person with a primary immunodeficiency state, an acquired immunodeficiency state, a hypogammaglobulinemic or dysgammaglobulinemic state (hypogammaglobulinemia). A family history of congenital or hereditary immunodeficiency is also a contraindication for varicella virus vaccine live unless the immune competence of the potential vaccine recipient is demonstrated. If a varicella-zoster-like rash develops after vaccination in a healthy person, the person should avoid contact with varicella-susceptible persons who are at high risk of severe complications such as patients who are immunocompromised while the rash is present. Immunocompromised persons who are vaccinated are more likely to get severe varicella-like rashes and may transmit the varicella-zoster virus to susceptible persons. Patients with impaired humoral immunity (e.g., IgA deficiency, hypogammaglobulinemia) may be vaccinated according to current CDC guidelines. However, patients receiving blood transfusions or immunoglobulin therapy may need to have alterations in their immunization schedule (see Drug Interactions). Some children with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection may be vaccinated with varicella virus vaccine live. In a study of HIV-infected children 1—8 years of age with a range of CD4 counts of 875—2132 cells/mm3, about half of children had evidence of varicella-zoster virus immunity 1 year after receiving 2 vaccine doses administered 3 months apart. Of 18 vaccine recipients who had no evidence of varicella-zoster virus-specific immunity at 1 year, 7 developed detectable antibody after a third vaccine dose. Vaccination should be considered for HIV-infected children with age-specific CD4+ T-lymphocyte percentages >15%; weigh risks versus benefit. Routine serologic testing to determine the varicella-zoster virus serologic status of HIV-infected adults is not recommended. No studies have been performed to evaluate the vaccine in HIV-infected adolescents or adults, but varicella vaccination may be considered in HIV-seropositive/varicella-zoster virus-seronegative adults and adolescents with CD4+ counts of at least 200 cells/mcl. If vaccination results in disease due to vaccine virus, therapy with acyclovir may be appropriate. Patients infected with HIV who have met diagnostic criteria of acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) should not receive the vaccine; the vaccine is contraindicated for patients with immunocompromising conditions or HIV infection with a CD4+ T lymphocyte count of < 200 cells/mcl. Varicella virus vaccine live should also not be administered to those with cellular immune deficiency or those patients on immunosuppressive therapy such as high doses of corticosteroid therapy. Patients who are not otherwise immunocompromised and are using topical or inhaled corticosteroids or oral doses for corticosteroid replacement may receive varicella virus vaccine live. Consult current CDC recommendations for vaccination of other immunosuppressed patients. Any neoplastic disease that causes bone marrow suppression or affects the lymphatic systems contraindicates the use of varicella virus vaccine live. Patients with leukemia, lymphoma, or any blood dyscrasia not in remission should not receive the vaccine, including those currently receiving radiation therapy or induction chemotherapy. Patients with leukemia in remission may be able to receive the vaccine under certain circumstances; current CDC guidelines should be consulted. NOTE: Children and adolescents with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) in remission can receive vaccination under an investigational protocol if specific criteria are met (Contact: Bio-Pharm Clinical Services, Inc., 4 Valley Square, Blue Bell, PA 19422 call: 215—283—0897).
 
Varicella virus vaccine live has been classified as FDA pregnancy category C. No animal reproductive studies have been undertaken, and it is not known if the vaccine has any teratogenic or mutagenic properties. The manufacturer recommends that varicella virus vaccine live not be administered during pregnancy or within the three months before becoming pregnant. Premenopausal females who have achieved menarche and who are sexually active should be counseled on the recommendation for the use of adequate contraception for 3 months following each dose of varicella virus vaccine live. The CDC recommends avoidance of vaccination of women who are pregnant or might become pregnant within 4 weeks of receiving the vaccine, assessment of pregnant women for evidence of varicella immunity, and administration of dose 1 of the varicella vaccine upon completion or termination of pregnancy and before discharge from the health-care facility to women who do not have evidence of immunity; administer dose number two 4—8 weeks after dose one. The manufacturer of Varivax® has established a pregnancy registry to monitor maternal-fetal outcomes of pregnant women inadvertently administered the varicella virus vaccine live. For information about the registry, call 800—986—8999.
 
Varicella virus vaccine live should be used with caution in breast-feeding women. Whether the attenuated varicella virus in varicella virus vaccine is excreted in human milk is not known; therefore whether the infant could be infected is not known. The CDC has noted that most live vaccines have not been demonstrated to be secreted in breast milk; however, attenuated rubella virus vaccine has been detected in breast milk and has produced asymptomatic infection in an infant. Varicella vaccine virus live may be considered for a nursing mother with caution.
 
At this time, no clinical data are available on the efficacy and safety of varicella vaccine live in infants < 12 months of age; vaccination of infants is not recommended.
 
Varicella-specific humoral and cellular immune responses to varicella virus vaccine live do not appear to be different in healthy elderly adults versus younger adult populations. Susceptible elderly patients may be considered for vaccination if indicated according to current CDC recommendations.

    General Information

    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Use of this vaccine is contraindicated in individuals with a neomycin or gelatin hypersensitivity; the vaccines contain a residual amount of neomycin and gelatin from the manufacturing process.
    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    The decision to administer or to delay vaccination with the varicella virus vaccine, live because of current or recent febrile illness depends on the severity of symptoms and on the etiology of the disease. Consider delaying vaccinations during the course of a moderate or severe acute illness with or without fever and administer after the acute phase of illness has resolved. All vaccines can be administered to people with mild illnesses. Vaccinate people with moderate or severe febrile illness as soon as they have recovered from the acute phase of the illness.
    Headline
    fever, tuberculosis
    General Information
    Varicella virus vaccine, live is contraindicated in individuals with active, untreated tuberculosis. It is also contraindicated in individuals with an active febrile illness with fever greater than 101.3 degrees F (greater than 38.5 degrees C).
    Headline
    immunosuppression
    General Information
    Recommendations and precautions for individuals with specific immunosuppressive conditions are complex; however, live attenuated varicella virus vaccine is contraindicated in persons with significant immunosuppression, including primary immunodeficiency disorders such as severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), IgA deficiency, hypogammaglobulinemia, agammaglobulinemia, dysgammaglobulinemia, clinically significant cellular immune deficiency, blood dyscrasias, leukemia, lymphoma, or other malignancies affecting the bone marrow or lymphatic system, as well as in individuals receiving chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or high-dose immunosuppressive therapy, including prolonged high-dose corticosteroids, because vaccination may result in extensive vaccine-associated rash or disseminated disease. A family history of congenital or hereditary immunodeficiency is also a contraindication unless immune competence has been demonstrated in the potential vaccine recipient. Corticosteroid therapy generally is not considered significant immunosuppression when it is short term for less than 2 weeks; low to moderate dose, defined as less than 2 mg/kg/day or 20 mg/day of prednisone or equivalent in persons weighing more than 10 kg; alternate-day therapy with short-acting preparations; physiologic replacement therapy; or topical, inhaled, ophthalmic, or locally injected corticosteroid therapy. Vaccination should be delayed until at least 1 month after discontinuation of prolonged high-dose corticosteroid therapy. In patients with leukemia, lymphoma, or other malignancies, live-virus vaccination may be considered when the disease is in remission and chemotherapy or radiation therapy has been discontinued for at least 3 months. Vaccine recipients who develop a post-vaccination rash should avoid close contact for 6 weeks with susceptible high-risk individuals, including immunocompromised persons, pregnant people without evidence of immunity, newborn infants of mothers without evidence of immunity, and infants born before 28 weeks gestation regardless of maternal immunity; when such contact is unavoidable, the potential risk of transmission of vaccine virus should be weighed against the risk of acquiring and transmitting natural varicella virus. In individuals living with HIV, the varicella virus vaccine may be recommended for those without severe immunosuppression. Adults and adolescents with CD4 counts below 200 cells/mm3 should not receive the live attenuated varicella virus vaccine. The vaccine is not recommended for children aged 1 to 5 years with CD4 counts below 500 cells/mm3 or CD4 percentages below 15% or children aged 6 to 12 years with CD4 counts below 200 cells/mm3 or CD4 percentages below 15%.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Varicella virus vaccine, live is contraindicated during pregnancy. Wild-type varicella virus infection during pregnancy can cause fetal harm, including varicella syndrome; however, available pregnancy-registry data have not demonstrated an increased risk of miscarriage, major birth defects, or congenital varicella syndrome following inadvertent administration of the vaccine during pregnancy or shortly before conception. In the manufacturer's pregnancy registry, 905 pregnancies had known outcomes, including 819 live-born infants; major birth defects occurred at rates consistent with estimated background rates, and no abnormalities consistent with congenital varicella syndrome were identified. FDA-approved labeling recommends that varicella virus vaccine, live not be administered during pregnancy or to individuals planning to become pregnant within the next 3 months. Because transmission of the vaccine virus may rarely occur, pregnant individuals without a documented history of chickenpox or laboratory evidence of prior infection should avoid contact with anyone who received the varicella virus vaccine, live within the past 6 weeks. The CDC recommends that varicella vaccine not be administered during pregnancy. Pregnant individuals should be assessed for evidence of varicella immunity, and nonpregnant individuals who receive the vaccine should avoid becoming pregnant for 1 month after each dose. Individuals without evidence of immunity should receive the first dose after completion or termination of pregnancy and before discharge from the healthcare facility; the second dose should be administered 4 to 8 weeks later.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with varicella virus vaccine, live. Wild type varicella virus infection is known to cause fetal harm. FDA-approved labeling recommends avoiding pregnancy for 3 months after administration of varicella vaccine, live. The CDC recommends avoiding pregnancy for 4 weeks after receiving the vaccine.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use varicella virus vaccine, live with caution during breast-feeding. It is not known whether varicella virus vaccine, live is excreted in human milk. Postpartum vaccination of individuals without evidence of varicella immunity need not be delayed because of breast-feeding, and individuals vaccinated postpartum may continue to breast-feed. According to the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), most vaccines, including varicella vaccine, may be administered to lactating individuals without adversely affecting the safety of breast-feeding for the vaccine recipient or the breastfed child. Varicella vaccine virus has not been detected in human milk. In one small study, vaccine virus was not detected in 217 human milk samples collected after vaccination or in specimens obtained from the infants, and none of the infants seroconverted. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of potential vaccine exposure in the child, and the risk of not vaccinating the lactating individual.

Interactions
Abatacept: (Contraindicated) If possible, administer all needed vaccines before abatacept initiation. Live vaccines should not be given concurrently with abatacept or within 3 months of its discontinuation. The immune response of the immunocompromised patient to vaccines may be decreased and adjusted doses or boosters that are more frequent may be required. The immune response to an inactive vaccine may still be suboptimal. Live virus vaccines may induce the illness they are intended to prevent and are contraindicated for use during immunosuppressive treatment. If immunization is necessary, choose an alternative to live vaccination, or, consider a delay or change in the immunization schedule. Practitioners should refer to the most recent CDC guidelines regarding vaccination of patients who are receiving drugs that adversely affect the immune system.
Abrocitinib: (Contraindicated) Avoid administration of live virus vaccines with immunosuppressive drug therapy and prior to immune recovery following treatment with immunosuppressive drug therapy. When feasible, administer indicated live virus vaccines at least four weeks before planned immunosuppression or wait until at least three months after discontinuation. The time to restoration of immune competence may be longer in some patients. Patients with altered immunocompetence may be at increased risk for severe adverse reactions due to uninhibited growth of the attenuated live virus. Additionally, vaccines may be less effective if administered during a period of altered immunocompetence.
Acetaminophen; Aspirin, ASA; Caffeine: (Major) No adverse events associated with the use of salicylates after varicella vaccination have been reported. However, the manufacturer of varicella virus vaccine live recommends the avoidance of salicylates or aspirin, ASA use for 6 weeks after vaccination. Reye's syndrome, which exclusively affects children under 15 years old, has been associated with aspirin use following active varicella infection. Vaccination with close clinical monitoring is recommended for children who require therapeutic aspirin, ASA therapy; according to the CDC the use of attenuated, live varicella virus vaccine is thought to present less risk than natural varicella disease to such children.

Acyclovir: (Major) If possible, discontinue acyclovir at least 24 hours before administration of the varicella-zoster virus vaccine, live. In patients with HIV, guidelines recommend waiting at least 72 hours after the last dose of acyclovir before administering varicella vaccines. Also, do not administer acyclovir for at least 14 days after vaccination. Concurrent administration of any of the varicella-zoster virus vaccines (Zostavax, Varivax, ProQuad) with antiviral medications known to be effective against varicella zoster virus has not been evaluated. Therefore, when possible, a washout period between the use of the antiviral medication and the vaccines is recommended. Acyclovir has a relatively short serum half-life and is quickly cleared from the body. Refer to the most recent Center for Disease control guidance if concurrent use is necessary.
Adalimumab: (Contraindicated) Do not administer live vaccines to adalimumab recipients; no data are available regarding the risk of secondary transmission of infection by live vaccines in patients receiving adalimumab. Before initiation of adalimumab therapy, consider completion of all age appropriate vaccinations per current immunization guidelines. Adalimumab recipients may receive inactivated vaccines, but the immune response to vaccines or toxoids may be decreased.
Albuterol; Budesonide: (Contraindicated) Avoid the administration of live virus vaccines with high-dose corticosteroid therapy and for at least 1 month following treatment.   Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Serious adverse reactions following administration of varicella virus vaccine live are rare. The only reactions of a causal nature occurring more frequently than in placebo-treated children or adults are fever; injection site reaction (symptoms such as pain/soreness, erythema, induration, rash (unspecified), swelling, pruritus); and a vesicular rash (varicella-like). In uncontrolled trials involving healthy children receiving a single dose, injection site reaction was reported in 19.3%, fever >= 102 degrees F (39 degrees C) in 14.7%, a generalized vesicular rash in 3.8%, and a local (injection site) vesicular rash in 3.4%. Most varicella-like rashes consist of a median of = 100 degrees F (37.7 degrees C), was reported at 10.2% vs 9.5% after first and second doses respectively. A generalized vesicular rash (varicella-like) was reported by 5.5% after the first dose vs 0.9% after the second doses. A localized injection site vesicular rash occurred in 3% after the first dose and 1% after the second dose. Decreased appetite, headache, mild insomnia, myalgia, and uveitis have also been reported rarely in children and adults. Urticaria has been reported secondary to the gelatin component of the vaccine in patients allergic to gelatin protein on rare occasions.
 
Data on potential adverse events reported to the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) are now available. From March 1995 to July 1998, 9.7 million doses of varicella virus vaccine live were distributed in the US. Most doses were administered to children. Adverse events were reported in 6,580 patients, 4% of these adverse events were serious. The most frequently reported adverse event reported to the system was rash (unspecified), mostly described as a vesicular rash, at a rate of 37/100,000 doses distributed. Most rash events occurring within 2 weeks of vaccination have been attributed to wild-type varicella-zoster virus. Reports of serious adverse events, without regard to causality, have included ataxia, bell palsy, encephalitis, erythema multiforme, herpes zoster, neuropathy including Guillain-Barre syndrome, paresthesias, pharyngitis, Henoch-Shonlein purpura, secondary infections such as cellulitis, impetigo or pneumonia, febrile-related seizures, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, thrombocytopenia, and transverse myelitis. At least three cases of anaphylactic shock have been reported within 10 minutes of vaccination. Of the 14 deaths reported to VAERS, 8 had definite other explanations for death, 3 had other plausible explanations for death, and 3 cases had insufficient data to determine causality. While VAERS data are limited by the underreporting of events and the unknown sensitivity of the reporting system, it appears that serious adverse events following varicella virus vaccine administration occur at a lower rate than serious events following natural varicella infection.
 
Reye's syndrome has been associated with natural varicella infection. Children and adolescents affected had usually received salicylates. Patients in clinical studies of varicella virus vaccine live were advised not to use salicylates for 6 weeks after vaccination. There were no reports of Reye's syndrome during clinical trials.
 
The following adverse events were reported in uncontrolled clinical trials in >= 1% children after a single dose and may or may not have a causal relationship to varicella virus vaccine (decreasing order of frequency): upper respiratory illness, cough, irritability/nervousness, fatigue, disturbed sleep, diarrhea, anorexia ( defined as loss of appetite), vomiting, otitis, diaper rash/contact rash, headache, malaise, abdominal pain, other rash, nausea, eye complaints, chills, lymphadenopathy, myalgia, lower respiratory illness, allergic reactions (including allergic rash, urticaria), stiff neck, heat rash/prickly heat, arthralgia, eczema/dry skin/dermatitis, constipation, and pruritus. Pneumonitis has occurred in = 1% adults in uncontrolled clinical trials have been similar.
 
Varicella virus vaccine does not appear to increase the risk of ischemic stroke in children. Ischemic stroke has been associated with primary varicella zoster virus infections, and a few case reports have suggested a possible association between varicella virus vaccine and ischemic stroke. However, a large retrospective cohort study of administrative data from 3.25 million children ages 11 months to 17 years found no association between stroke and the vaccine. The incidence of stroke was low in both groups: 0.003% of 1.14 million vaccinated children and 0.008% of 2.1 million unvaccinated children. Of note, because the vaccine was not readily available until the latter part of the study, the mean age of the unvaccinated group was significantly higher than that of the vaccinated group (7.9 years vs. 1.9 years). Analyses were based on coded diagnoses (i.e. ICD-9-CM diagnosis codes) from organizations that participate in the Vaccine Safety DataLink project; diagnoses were not confirmed by medical chart review. The risk window analyzed was the 12-month period after varicella virus vaccine receipt. The authors also found no association between varicella virus vaccine administration and encephalitis during this same time period; risk of meningitis was not investigated.

Description
  Text
  Varicella virus vaccine, live is indicated for active immunization for the prevention of varicella (chickenpox) caused by varicella-zoster virus. Varicella virus vaccine, live is a live, attenuated viral vaccine containing the Oka/Merck strain of the virus. The vaccine induces both humoral and cell-mediated immune responses to varicella-zoster virus, although the relative contribution of each to protect against varicella has not been established. In a placebo-controlled clinical trial conducted with a prior formulation, a single dose provided 96% to 100% protection against varicella over a 2-year period; in longer-term clinical follow-up, breakthrough varicella was generally mild, with most affected vaccine recipients having fewer than 50 lesions. Although the precise duration of protection is unknown, long-term efficacy studies have demonstrated continued protection for up to 10 years after vaccination. As a live, attenuated vaccine, varicella virus vaccine is contraindicated during pregnancy and in individuals who are immunodeficient or immunosuppressed due to disease or medical therapy. After the introduction of the U.S. varicella vaccination program in 1995, varicella cases declined by more than 97%; the current annual disease burden is estimated at fewer than 150,000 cases, 1,400 hospitalizations, and 30 deaths. Over the first 25 years of the U.S. vaccination program, an estimated 91 million varicella cases, 238,000 hospitalizations, and almost 2,000 deaths were prevented, with substantial associated health care and societal savings. Varicella virus vaccine, live was FDA-approved in 1995.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Vaccination with varicella virus vaccine, live produces a detectable IgG antibody humoral immune response in a high proportion of individuals and is well tolerated. Vaccinated patients also have a cell-mediated immune response, consisting of an expression of varicella-zoster specific activation of both CD4 T-helper and CD8 T-lymphocytes. The significance of humoral immunity and cell-mediated immunity in providing protection against chickenpox is unknown, but patients who do not seroconvert after vaccination still receive some protection from infection via cellular immunity. Vaccination appears to prevent severe disease even in patients who do not seroconvert. The severity of breakthrough chickenpox does not appear to be affected by the length of time since immunization; cases seen 8 years post-varicella virus vaccination still remain mild. Vaccine postmarketing surveillance suggests there may be a lower incidence rate of herpes zoster after varicella virus vaccination, live than occurs after natural varicella infection, but longer epidemiologic surveillance is needed to support these findings.

Pharmacokinetics
  Special Populations
    Name
    Pediatrics
    Description
    Children
Of children 1 to 12 years of age with a negative history of varicella who received 2 doses of varicella virus vaccine, live given 3 months apart, 2.2% developed varicella more than 42 days after vaccination during a 10-year observation period, whereas 7.5% of children who received 1 dose developed varicella greater than 42 days after vaccination. Most cases of varicella reported in recipients of 1 or 2 doses of vaccine were mild. The estimated vaccine efficacy for the 10-year observation period was 94% for 1 dose and 98% for 2 doses. Similar percentages of children 12 to 14 months, 15 to 17 months, and 18 to 23 months of age achieved a postvaccination varicella-zoster virus antibody concentration of at least 5 gpELISA units/mL. VZV antibody titers of at least 5 gpELISA unites/mL have been associated with a lower rate of breakthrough varicella compared with titers below 5 gpELISA unites/mL. Geometric mean titers were similar for the 3 age groups. Antibody concentrations were obtained 6 weeks after the single 0.5 mL dose.
  Text
  The varicella virus vaccine, live is administered subcutaneously or intramuscularly. A single dose administered to patients 13 years and older induced seroconversion in 75% of vaccine recipients after 4 weeks. A second dose administered 4 weeks after the first dose increased the seroconversion rate to 99%. In another study, adults and adolescents were administered 2 vaccinations 8 weeks apart. The seroconversion rates were 94% at 6 weeks after the first dose and 99% 6 weeks after the second. The duration of protection is unknown; however, long-term efficacy studies have demonstrated continued protection for up to 10 years after vaccination. A boost in antibody concentrations has been observed following exposure to wild-type varicella, which may contribute to the apparent long-term persistence of protection.

Administration
  General Information
  For storage information, see the specific product information within the How Supplied section.
Verify immunization status in the state or local immunization information system in addition to the health care electronic record. If this vaccine has been previously given, question the patient, parent, or guardian about any symptoms or signs of an adverse reaction.
Advise individuals on the benefits and risks of the vaccine. Provide a copy of the Vaccine Information Statement (VIS).
Record manufacturer, lot number, administration date, and the name and address of the person administering the vaccine.
Ensure supportive care, including epinephrine 1 mg/mL injection, is readily accessible in the event of a clinically significant hypersensitivity reaction.
Complete a Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) report if adverse events have been identified. The VAERS toll-free information line is 1-800-822-7967 and email address is info@vaers.org.
