Indication Specific Dosing

For the treatment of generalized anxiety disorder† (GAD), or for nutritional supplementation† for relief of stress† or anxiety†

Oral dosage
Adults


300 to 600 mg (root extract standardized to 5% withanolides) PO, given in divided doses, has been provisionally recommended for monotherapy or adjunctive treatment for generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) by selected guidelines. Data are limited. 225 to 600 mg/day PO, given in divided doses, has been studied in small clinical trials for use as an adaptogen for stress or anxiety/restlessness/nervousness.  


For the treatment of insomnia†

Oral dosage
Adults


600 mg/day PO, either given as 300 mg PO twice daily or 600 mg PO once daily in the evening, has been suggested as the most effective dosage based on evidence available. Improvements in latency of sleep onset, sleep efficiency, and sleep quality were noted versus placebo in one study. Data are limited; doses in small clinical trials included 120 to 600 mg/day, usually given in divided doses twice daily.  


Contraindications And Precaution
Drug Interactions

The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.

Hypersensitivity

This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Ashwagandha belongs to the nightshade (Solanaceae) plant family; some individuals are allergic to or have sensitivity to this plant family.

hepatic disease


Use ashwagandha with caution in individuals with hepatic disease.   Although rare, there have been a number of cases of liver injury due to ashwagandha supplements, although causality is not definitively established.


thyroid disease


Use ashwagandha with caution in individuals with thyroid disease, as the herb appears to alter thyroid hormone production. People with hyperthyroidism or those taking thyroid hormone replacement therapy for hypothyroidism should consult their care team before adding ashwagandha to their regimen. Cases of thyroiditis and hyperthyroidism have been reported in association with ashwagandha use.


autoimmune disease


Use ashwagandha with caution in people with autoimmune disease. Both immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive effects of ashwagandha have been reported. Ashwagandha may exacerbate autoimmune conditions or interfere with treatment.


prostate cancer


Because ashwagandha may increase testosterone levels, use ashwagandha with caution in individuals with hormone-sensitive prostate cancer.


activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery


In some individuals, ashwagandha may cause drowsiness. Individuals should use caution with activities requiring coordination and concentration, including driving or operating machinery, until they are aware of how this dietary supplement affects them.


planned procedure


Ashwagandha is not recommended for people who are about to have a planned procedure (e.g., surgery) and should be halted 2 weeks prior to  planned surgical procedures when possible, since the potential interactions with anesthetics or other sedating agents are unknown.


pregnancy


Avoid the use of ashwagandha during pregnancy. There are no human or animal data on ashwagandha use during pregnancy to evaluate for a risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. Large doses of ashwagandha are considered unsafe during pregnancy as the herb may have abortifacient properties in high doses.


breast-feeding


Avoid breast-feeding while taking ashwagandha. There are no data on the presence of ashwagandha in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. In Ayurvedic medicine, ashwagandha is sometimes used as a galactagogue, but no scientifically valid clinical trials support this use. Counsel lactating individuals on modifiable factors that affect milk production, rather than recommending a galactagogue.

Interactions

Drug interaction data for herbal and dietary supplements are limited. Advise patients to avoid products lacking good scientific evidence as use may reduce the efficacy of prescription medications or increase the risk for adverse effects.

Adverse Reaction
diarrhea, drowsiness, dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting


Drowsiness, dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea have been reported with clinical use of ashwagandha.


pruritus, rash, urticaria


Ashwagandha belongs to the Solanaceae (nightshade) family of plants, which may cause rare allergic reactions or sensitivity in some people. Allergic reactions may include rash, pruritus, urticaria, and mild respiratory distress. Serious hypersensitivity reactions to nightshades, such as anaphylaxis, may occur in some individuals. Nightshade sensitivity may include gastrointestinal distress or inflammation.


cholestasis, elevated hepatic enzymes, hepatitis, hepatotoxicity, hyperbilirubinemia, jaundice


Hepatotoxicity associated with ashwagandha use has been reported, with presentations including elevated hepatic enzymes, hyperbilirubinemia, hepatitis, cholestasis, cholestatic pruritus, and jaundice. If there is evidence of ashwagandha-induced liver dysfunction, the herb should be discontinued.


autoimmune disease, hyperthyroidism, thyroiditis, thyrotoxicosis


Hyperthyroidism associated with ashwagandha use has been reported, with rare cases including presentations including thyrotoxicosis and supraventricular tachycardia. Thyroiditis has also been reported. In the endocrine system, the withanolides in ashwagandha appear to stimulate the HPA axis, stimulating the production of T4 and T3, which can lead to thyroid hormonal imbalance in those with thyroid disease. Ashwagandha has some immune function-modulating effects and stimulates the immune system; it is proposed that ashwagandha could exacerbate autoimmune disease.


adrenocortical insufficiency


Adrenocortical insufficiency associated with ashwagandha was reported in one case, with the presentation including hyponatremia, nausea, headache, dizziness, and confusion.


Description

Ashwagandha, also known botanically as Withania somnifera, is an evergreen shrub cultivated in tropical and subtropical areas of Asia, Africa, and Europe. Ashwagandha is also sometimes known as winter cherry or Indian ginseng, although the plant does not belong to the ginseng family. Ashwagandha has been used for centuries in the traditional Ayurvedic medicine system of India as an adaptogen. Most commercial products contain dried or liquid extracts from the ashwagandha root, although some contain root and leaf extracts. The principal bioactive constituents are withanolides, steroidal lactones believed to underlie most of the herb's pharmacological activity. Proposed mechanisms include modulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity. Most marketed ashwagandha dietary supplements are promoted to reduce stress and anxiety. Clinical trials, most using standardized root extracts, have explored ashwagandha for conditions including anxiety, stress, and insomnia, with several small trials reporting improvement in these measures; however, evidence remains limited by small sample sizes and methodologic inconsistency, and no health condition currently has well-established efficacy data supporting its use. Cases of hepatotoxicity have been reported in individuals using ashwagandha-containing products, though causality has not been definitively established. If unexplained liver-related symptoms are reported during use, consider the possibility of liver injury. Ashwagandha may also affect endocrine function and have immunomodulatory activity. In the U.S., ashwagandha is regulated as an herbal dietary supplement and is not FDA-approved for any medical use.
 
NOTE: In the U.S., herbal products and dietary supplements are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for dietary supplements as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since supplements are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for these products and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action

The pharmacologically active constituents and precise mechanisms of action of ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) have not been fully established. Ashwagandha root contains multiple potentially bioactive constituents, including withanolides (steroidal lactones) and alkaloids. Constituent profiles vary among chemotypes and preparations; therefore, pharmacologic effects observed with a specific extract or isolated constituent may not apply to all ashwagandha products.
 
The CNS effects of ashwagandha may involve GABAergic and cholinergic signaling and modulation of physiologic stress-response pathways, according to preclinical evidence. GABAergic activity has been demonstrated with ashwagandha root extracts in experimental models, although the constituents responsible and the specific receptor-level mechanisms have not been established, and findings have varied among preparations. Preclinical studies also suggest that ashwagandha may attenuate stress-associated responses, including changes in adrenocortical activity.
 
Preclinical studies suggest that ashwagandha has anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective effects. Reported mechanisms include modulation of inflammatory signaling and cytokine production, effects on immune-cell activity, enhancement of endogenous antioxidant defenses, and protection against oxidative and neuronal injury. Both immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive effects have been reported, suggesting that immune effects may vary with the constituent, preparation, and experimental model. The clinical relevance of these effects has not been established.

Pharmacokinetics

Ashwagandha is administered orally. Human pharmacokinetic data for ashwagandha are limited and are primarily derived from single-dose studies of standardized root extracts in healthy adults. These studies evaluated specific withanolides and withanosides rather than the complete phytochemical mixture. Pharmacokinetic profiles differ among constituents and extract formulations. The volume of distribution varies based on the measured withanolides and withanosides. Sominone, a bioactive metabolite of withanoside IV, has been detected in plasma following oral administration of a standardized ashwagandha root extract. Various alkaloids of ashwagandha are metabolized by the liver via cytochrome P450 enzymes, which can form reactive metabolites that can cause oxidative stress in hepatic cells. Certain withanolides and metabolites have also been detected in urine following oral administration of a standardized root-and-leaf extract; urinary recovery of the quantified compounds was generally low. In single-dose studies, the half-lives of the measured withanolides and withanosides were approximately 2 to 5 hours. Half-life and systemic exposure may vary substantially according to the constituent and extract formulation.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2B6, CYP3A4, CYP2D6
In vitro studies have reported inhibition of CYP2B6 and induction or modulation of CYP3A4 by ashwagandha root extracts. While some in vitro studies found ashwagandha might induce CYP1A2, other investigations have found no effect. One review proposed a possibility of inhibition of CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 by ashwagandha as a possible mechanism for potential drug interactions, but evidence is not robust.

Route-Specific Pharmacokinetics
Oral Route

Following oral administration of standardized ashwagandha root extracts, measurable withanolides and withanosides reached peak plasma concentrations at approximately 1 to 2 hours. Cmax and AUC varied according to the constituent and extract formulation.
