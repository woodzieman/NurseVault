  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  Contraindications And Precautions
    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    alcohol use disorder, fever, hypovolemia, ketogenic diet, pancreatitis, surgery
    General Information
    Withhold empagliflozin, if possible, in temporary clinical situations that could predispose patients to ketoacidosis such as underinsulinization due to insulin dose reduction or missed insulin doses, acute fever, reduced caloric intake, ketogenic diet, surgery, hypovolemia, alcohol use disorder, and pancreatitis. For patients who undergo scheduled surgery, empagliflozin should be temporarily discontinued at least 3 days before surgery. Resume empagliflozin when the patient is clinically stable and has resumed oral intake. Reports of ketoacidosis, a serious, life-threatening condition requiring urgent hospitalization, have been identified in postmarketing surveillance in people with type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus receiving sodium glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, including empagliflozin. Fatal cases of ketoacidosis have been reported. The risk of ketoacidosis may be greater with higher SGLT2 doses. Consider ketone monitoring in people at risk for ketoacidosis if indicated by the clinical situation.
    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    Empagliflozin is not recommended for use to improve glycemic control in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus and should not be used for the treatment of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Empagliflozin significantly increases the risk of DKA in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus.
Empagliflozin is not recommended for the treatment of chronic kidney disease in persons with polycystic kidney disease (i.e., autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease) or persons requiring or with a recent history of intravenous immunosuppressive therapy or more than prednisone 45 mg or equivalent for kidney disease; empagliflozin is not expected to be effective in these populations.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    Assess renal function and volume status at baseline and as clinically indicated during empagliflozin therapy. Correct hypovolemia before initiation. Empagliflozin can cause intravascular volume depletion which may manifest as symptomatic hypotension or acute transient changes in creatinine; persons with renal impairment (i.e., eGFR less than 60 mL/minute/1.73 m2), dehydration, older adults, and those receiving loop diuretics may be at increased risk for volume depletion or hypotension. Use of empagliflozin for glycemic control in persons without established cardiovascular (CV) disease or CV risk factors is not recommended when eGFR is less than 30 mL/minute/1.73 m2. However, guidelines recommend use of empagliflozin in all patients with an eGFR 20 mL/minute/1.73 m2 or greater, T2DM, and CKD for CV and renal protection, regardless of glycemia or presence of albuminuria. The guidelines further state that while empagliflozin should not be initiated in these patients if the eGFR is less than 20 mL/minute/1.73 m2, it may be continued if tolerated for CV and renal protection until dialysis is initiated. Safety and efficacy studies with empagliflozin did not enroll persons with eGFR less than 20 mL/minute/1.73 m2 or renal failure requiring dialysis. Discontinuation of empagliflozin was not required for worsening of eGFR to less than 20 mL/minute/1.73 m2 or initiation of dialysis after study enrollment. Randomized clinical outcome trials of advanced kidney disease or high cardiovascular disease risk with normal kidney function have not shown that sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors promote acute kidney injury.
    Headline
    diabetic foot ulcer, lower limb amputation, peripheral neuropathy, peripheral vascular disease
    General Information
    Empagliflozin is associated with an increase in risk of lower limb amputation. Lower limb infections, gangrene, diabetic foot ulcer, and ischemia were the most common precipitating medical events leading to an amputation. The risk of amputation was highest in patients with a baseline history of prior amputation, peripheral vascular disease, or peripheral neuropathy. Monitor empagliflozin recipients for infection (including osteomyelitis) of the legs and feet, and instruct them to notify their care team immediately if they notice any new pain or tenderness, unusual sensations, skin color changes, sores or ulcers, or infection in their legs or feet. Daily self-examination of their feet is recommended, and a health care professional should routinely perform a complete foot exam.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    The Beers Criteria states that SGLT2 inhibitors should be used with caution in geriatric adults. Older adults may be at increased risk of urogenital infections, particularly women in the first month of treatment. An increased risk of euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) has also been seen in older adults. Empagliflozin is expected to have diminished glycemic efficacy in geriatric adults with renal impairment. In glycemic control clinical trials for type 2 diabetes mellitus, higher incidences of adverse reactions related to volume depletion and reduced renal function were reported in geriatric adults. The risk of urinary tract infections was increased in empagliflozin-treated adults who were 75 years and older. In clinical trials of patients with chronic kidney disease or heart failure, no overall differences in safety and effectiveness of empagliflozin have been observed between older and younger adults.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of empagliflozin use during pregnancy, and use is not recommended during the second and third trimesters of human pregnancy. Based on results from animal studies, empagliflozin may affect renal development and maturation. Rat studies demonstrate that empagliflozin crosses the placenta and reaches fetal tissues. In rat and rabbit studies, empagliflozin was not teratogenic at 48-times and 128-times, respectively, the maximum clinical human dose of 25 mg. However, at higher doses (139 to 154 times the 25 mg maximum human clinical dose), maternal and fetal toxicity, including malformations of limb bones, was evident. Maternal toxicity did not occur in pre- and postnatal development studies, starting at gestation day 6 through to lactation day 20 (weaning) at approximately 16 times the 25 mg maximum clinical dose in pregnant rats; reduced body weight was observed in the offspring. During pregnancy, consider appropriate alternative therapies, especially during the second and third trimesters. The potential risks to human kidney development are of concern. Empagliflozin should be used during pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus. Poorly controlled diabetes during pregnancy also increases fetal risk. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the American Diabetes Association (ADA) continue to recommend human insulin as the standard of care in pregnant individuals with diabetes mellitus and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) requiring medical therapy; insulin does not cross the placenta.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Avoid empagliflozin while breast-feeding due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in the breastfed infant. Empagliflozin is secreted in the milk of lactating rats reaching levels 5 times higher than that in maternal plasma; it is not known if empagliflozin is excreted into human milk. Since human kidney maturation occurs in utero and during the first 2 years of life when lactational exposure may occur, there may be risk to the developing human kidney. If empagliflozin is discontinued and blood glucose is not controlled on diet and exercise alone, insulin therapy should be considered. Other oral hypoglycemics may be considered. Metformin monotherapy may be appropriate for some patients as available studies indicate low excretion in milk and that maternal use during breast-feeding is not expected to result in side effects to a healthy breastfed infant. Some experts recommend using metformin with caution if the patient is breast-feeding a newborn or a premature neonate with reduced renal function. Because acarbose has limited systemic absorption, which results in minimal maternal plasma concentrations, clinically significant exposure via breast milk is not expected; therefore, this agent may be an alternative if postprandial glucose control is needed. Glyburide may be a suitable alternative since it was not detected in the breast milk of lactating women who received single and multiple doses of glyburide. If any oral hypoglycemics are used during breast-feeding, the breastfed infant should be monitored for signs of hypoglycemia, such as increased fussiness or somnolence.
    Headline
    genitourinary tract infection
    General Information
    People with a history of chronic or recurrent genitourinary tract infection are more likely to develop genitourinary infections when using empagliflozin or other sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. Monitor treated individuals for signs and symptoms of genitourinary infections (e.g., urinary tract infection, genital mycotic infection), and treat promptly, if indicated. Serious genitourinary infections, including urosepsis, pyelonephritis, and necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum (Fournier's gangrene), have occurred in individuals with and without diabetes mellitus receiving SGLT-2 inhibitors. Some cases have resulted in hospitalization, multiple surgical procedures, or death. If necrotizing fasciitis is suspected, discontinue the SGLT-2 inhibitor and promptly institute appropriate medical and/or surgical intervention.

Interactions
Acebutolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Atenolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. (Moderate) Thiazide diuretics can decrease insulin sensitivity thereby leading to glucose intolerance and hyperglycemia. Diuretic-induced hypokalemia may also lead to hyperglycemia. Because of this, a potential pharmacodynamic interaction exists between thiazide diuretics and antidiabetic agents. It appears that the effects of thiazide diuretics on glycemic control are dose-related and low doses can be instituted without deleterious effects on glycemic control. In addition, diuretics reduce the risk of stroke and cardiovascular disease in patients with diabetes. Esmolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Patients with diabetes should inform their health care professionals of their intent to ingest garlic dietary supplements. Some patients may require adjustment to their hypoglycemic medications over time. One study stated that additional garlic supplementation (0.05 to 1.5 grams PO per day) contributed to improved blood glucose control in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus within 1 to 2 weeks, and had positive effects on total cholesterol and high/low density lipoprotein regulation over time. More controlled trials are needed to discern if garlic has an effect on blood glucose in patients with diabetes. When garlic is used in foods or as a seasoning, or at doses of 50 mg/day or less, it is unlikely that blood glucose levels are affected to any clinically significant degree.

Labetalol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Octreotide: (Moderate) Monitor patients receiving octreotide concomitantly with insulin or other antidiabetic agents for changes in glycemic control and adjust doses of these medications accordingly. Octreotide alters the balance between the counter-regulatory hormones of insulin, glucagon, and growth hormone, which may result in hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia. The hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia which occurs during octreotide acetate therapy is usually mild but may result in overt diabetes mellitus or necessitate dose changes in insulin or other hypoglycemic agents. In patients with concomitant type1 diabetes mellitus, octreotide is likely to affect glucose regulation, and insulin requirements may be reduced. Symptomatic hypoglycemia, which may be severe, has been reported in type 1 diabetic patients. In Type 2 diabetes patients with partially intact insulin reserves, octreotide administration may result in decreases in plasma insulin levels and hyperglycemia.

Pindolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

    diabetic ketoacidosis
    General Information
    Reports of ketoacidosis, a serious, life-threatening condition requiring urgent hospitalization have been identified in clinical trials and postmarketing surveillance in patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus receiving sodium glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, including empagliflozin. Fatal cases of ketoacidosis have been reported in patients taking empagliflozin. The risk of ketoacidosis may be greater with higher doses. Empagliflozin significantly increases the risk of diabetic ketoacidosis in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus. In many of the postmarketing reports, and particularly in patients with type 1 diabetes, the presence of ketoacidosis was not immediately recognized, and the institution of treatment was delayed because presenting blood glucose levels were below those typically expected for DKA (often less than 250 mg/dL). Signs and symptoms at presentation were consistent with dehydration and severe metabolic acidosis and included nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, generalized malaise, and shortness of breath. In some but not all cases, factors predisposing to ketoacidosis such as acute febrile illness, infection, reduced caloric intake, ketogenic diet, surgery, reduction in dose of exogenous insulin or discontinuation of exogenous insulin or insulin secretagogue, volume depletion, or alcohol abuse were identified. Before initiating empagliflozin, consider factors in the patient history that may predispose to ketoacidosis including pancreatic insulin deficiency, caloric restriction, volume depletion, and alcohol abuse. Risk factors for ketoacidosis also include patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and pancreatic disorders such as, a history of pancreatitis, pancreatic insufficiency, or pancreatic surgery. Ketoacidosis and glucosuria may persist longer than typically expected. Urinary glucose excretion persists for 3 days after discontinuing empagliflozin; however there have been postmarketing reports of ketoacidosis and glucosuria lasting greater than 6 days and some up to 2 weeks after discontinuation of SGLT2 inhibitors. Based on the clinical situation, consider ketone monitoring in persons with type 1 diabetes mellitus and in others at risk for ketoacidosis. Patients treated with empagliflozin who present with signs and symptoms consistent with dehydration and severe metabolic acidosis should be assessed for ketoacidosis regardless of presenting blood glucose levels, as ketoacidosis may be present even if blood glucose levels are less than 250 mg/dL. If ketoacidosis is suspected, discontinue empagliflozin, evaluate the patient, and institute prompt treatment. Treatment of ketoacidosis may require insulin, fluid and carbohydrate replacement. Monitor patients for resolution of ketoacidosis before restarting empagliflozin. Withhold empagliflozin, if possible, in temporary clinical situations that could predispose patients to ketoacidosis. For patients who undergo scheduled surgery, empagliflozin should be temporarily discontinued at least 3 days before surgery. Resume empagliflozin when the patient is clinically stable and has resumed oral intake. Educate patients on the signs and symptoms of ketoacidosis and instruct patients to discontinue this product and seek medical attention immediately if signs and symptoms occur.

    necrotizing fasciitis, tissue necrosis
    General Information
    Empagliflozin and other sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have been associated with a serious, rare, and life-threatening infection, called necrotizing fasciitis (tissue necrosis) of the perineum, also referred to as Fournier's gangrene. In August 2018, the FDA announced that over 5 years, 12 cases of Fournier's gangrene have been identified in patients taking a SGLT2 inhibitor; these cases were identified in the medical literature and there may be additional unreported cases. Although most cases of Fournier's gangrene have previously been reported in men, the 12 cases included 7 men and 5 women aged 38 to 78 years. The average time to onset of Fournier's gangrene after drug initiation was 9.2 months (range 7 days to 25 months). All 12 patients required surgical debridement, 5 of which required more than 1 surgery and 1 required skin grafting. The clinical course for 4 patients was complicated by diabetic ketoacidosis, acute kidney injury, and septic shock, prolonging their hospitalizations or leading to death. Two patients were transferred to a rehabilitation hospital. The SGLT2 inhibitor was discontinued in 8 cases; 1 patient died and information on drug continuation or discontinuation was not included in 3 cases. Because diabetes is a known risk factor for Fournier's gangrene, the FDA also examined several other classes of antidiabetic agents to help assess whether the cases of Fournier's gangrene with the SGLT2 inhibitors are more likely to be associated with the underlying condition of diabetes as opposed to the drug. Only 6 cases of Fournier's gangrene (all in men) were identified in the review of other antidiabetic drug classes over a period of more than 30 years. Counsel patients to promptly seek medical attention if they experience any symptoms of tenderness, erythema, or swelling in the genital or perineal area, fever, or malaise. If Fournier's gangrene is suspected, discontinue empagliflozin and institute prompt treatment with antibiotics and surgical debridement, if appropriate. Closely monitor blood glucose levels, and provide appropriate alternative therapy for glycemic control.

Description
  Text
  Empagliflozin is an oral sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor indicated to improve glycemic control in adults and pediatric individuals 10 years of age and older with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) as an adjunct to diet and exercise, to reduce the risk of cardiovascular (CV) death in adults with T2DM and established CV disease, to reduce the risk of CV death and hospitalization for heart failure (HF) in adults with HF, and to reduce the risk of sustained decline in eGFR, end-stage kidney disease, CV death, and hospitalization in adults with chronic kidney disease (CKD) at risk of progression. Empagliflozin works by blocking the reabsorption of glucose and sodium by the kidney, which results in increased glucose excretion, reduced blood glucose concentrations, natriuresis, and osmotic diuresis. Empagliflozin significantly reduced the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE): CV death, the first occurrence of non-fatal myocardial infarction, or non-fatal stroke) by 14% relative to placebo (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.74 to 0.99) in persons with T2DM and established, stable, atherosclerotic CV disease. Empagliflozin also significantly reduced the risk of hospitalization for heart failure compared with placebo. In the EMPEROR-Reduced trial, empagliflozin significantly reduced the combined risk of death, hospitalization for heart failure, or an emergent/urgent need for intravenous treatment of heart failure in subjects with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction of 40% or less (NYHA class II to IV). Empagliflozin significantly reduced the total number of hospitalizations for heart failure and total number of either inindividual or outindividual visits related to worsening of heart failure. In the Emperor-Preserved trial, empagliflozin significantly reduced the combined risk of CV death or hospitalization for heart failure, the number of total (first and recurrent) hospitalizations for heart failure or CV reason, and worsening heart failure events, defined as CV death, hospitalization for heart failure, or an emergent/urgent need for intravenous treatment of heart failure in subjects with heart failure with an ejection fraction of more than 40% (NYHA class II to IV). Empagliflozin also reduced the total number of hospitalizations for heart failure that required intensive care or vasopressor/positive inotropic drug therapy. Empagliflozin-treated subjects were less likely to require outindividual intensification of diuretics and were 20% to 50% more likely to have a better NYHA class for at least 2 years. The reduction in total hospitalizations was similar in empagliflozin subjects with an ejection fraction of more than 40% to less than 50% and those with an ejection fraction of 50% to less than 60%; an attenuated response was observed in subjects with an ejection fraction more than 60%. In the EMPA-KIDNEY trial, empagliflozin was superior to placebo in reducing the risk of the primary composite endpoint of sustained 40% or more eGFR decline, sustained eGFR less than 10 mL/minute/1.73 m2, progression to end-stage kidney disease, or CV or renal death. Progression of kidney disease or CV or renal death occurred in 13.1% of subjects in the empagliflozin group compared to 16.9% of subjects in the placebo group (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.82; p less than 0.001). The rate of first and recurrent hospitalizations from any cause was also lower in the empagliflozin group compared to placebo (24.8 vs. 29.2 hospitalizations per 100 individual-years; HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.78 to 0.95; p = 0.003). First-line T2DM therapy depends on comorbidities, individual-centered treatment factors, and management needs. In adults with T2DM and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or indicators of high ASCVD risk, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) and/or SGLT2 inhibitor with proven CV benefit (e.g., canagliflozin, empagliflozin), should be initiated as a first-line therapy independent of A1C goal or other antihyperglycemic treatments, including metformin. In adults with T2DM and HF, an SGLT2 inhibitor with proven benefit (e.g., canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, and ertugliflozin) should be initiated as first-line therapy to reduce hospitalizations for HF. In adults with T2DM and CKD, an SGLT2 inhibitor with primary evidence (e.g., canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) of reducing CKD progression should be initiated as first-line therapy; however, the glycemic benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors are reduced at eGFR less than 45 mL/minute per 1.73 m2. In individuals with T2DM who do not have ASCVD/indicators of high-risk, HF, or CKD and who need to minimize hypoglycemia and/or promote weight loss, SGLT2 inhibitors are generally recommended as a second or third-line option as add-on to metformin therapy as they have a high efficacy for glucose lowering and an intermediate efficacy for weight loss. Empaglflozin was initially FDA-approved in 2014.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Empagliflozin is an inhibitor of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2), the transporter responsible for reabsorbing the majority of glucose filtered by the tubular lumen in the kidney. SGLT2 is expressed in the proximal renal tubules. By inhibiting SGLT2, empagliflozin reduces reabsorption of filtered glucose and lowers the renal threshold for glucose (RTG), and thereby increases urinary glucose excretion, improving blood glucose control. This urinary glucose excretion with empagliflozin also results in increased urinary volume. Following the administration of empagliflozin 10 mg and 25 mg orally once daily, increases in the amount of glucose excreted in the urine were observed immediately in adult patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and were maintained at the end of a 4-week treatment period averaging at approximately 64 grams/day and 78 grams/day, respectively.
 
Through inhibition of glucose and sodium reabsorption in the proximal renal tubule, SGLT2 inhibitors exert osmotic diuretic and natriuretic effects causing plasma volume contraction and reducing both systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Though the exact mechanism for the beneficial cardiorenal effects seen in clinical studies of SGLT2 inhibitor therapy is not fully known, it is believed to be, in part, related to glycosuria and natriuresis; these effects lead to uricosuria and reduction in plasma uric acid, and a reduction in plasma volume, lowering of cardiac preload, and reduced arterial pressure and stiffness possibly resulting in afterload reduction. Empagliflozin's reduction of sodium reabsorption and increased delivery of sodium to the distal tubule may influence many physiological functions including increasing tubuloglomerular feedback and reducing intraglomerular pressure which may contribute to long-term preservation of kidney function. Additionally, empagliflozin's reduction in fluid volume lowers the pre-and afterload of the heart and down regulates sympathetic activity which may help reduce the risk of cardiovascular death. Additional proposed mechanisms may involve increased circulating concentrations of beta-hydroxybutyrate, a ketone body and an improved source of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which may result in an increase in cardiac efficiency; increased erythropoietin concentrations and related cardioprotective effects; elevated hemoglobin concentrations leading to improved myocardium oxygenation; inhibition of the myocardial sodium-hydrogen exchanger resulting in increased mitochondrial calcium, reduced oxidative stress, and improved mitochondrial function; potential reduction in sympathetic nervous system activation; enhancing endothelium-dependent vasodilation through increased endothelial nitric oxide synthetase; and decreasing intraglomerular pressure which is believed to be mediated by increased tubuloglomerular feedback.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Following oral administration, peak plasma concentrations (Cmax) of empagliflozin occurred at 1.5 hours post-dose. Thereafter, plasma concentrations declined in a biphasic manner with a rapid distribution phase and a relatively slow terminal phase. The steady state mean plasma AUC and Cmax were 1870 nmol/hour/L and 259 nmol/L, respectively, with empagliflozin 10 mg/day. The steady state mean plasma AUC and Cmax were 4740 nmol/hour/L and 687 nmol/L, respectively, with 25 mg/day empagliflozin. Systemic exposure of empagliflozin increased in a dose proportional manner within the therapeutic dose range. The single-dose and steady-state pharmacokinetic parameters of empagliflozin were similar, suggesting linear pharmacokinetics with respect to time. Administration of a high-fat and high-calorie meal prior to empagliflozin administration resulted in slightly lower exposure; AUC decreased by approximately 16% and Cmax decreased by approximately 37%, compared to fasted conditions; however, the observed effect of food on empagliflozin was not considered clinically relevant. Empagliflozin may be administered with or without food.

  Empagliflozin is administered orally. The apparent steady-state volume of distribution was estimated to be 73.8 L based on a population pharmacokinetic analysis. Empagliflozin is approximately 86.2% protein bound. No major metabolites of empagliflozin were detected in human plasma. In vitro studies suggest that the primary route of metabolism of empagliflozin in humans is glucuronidation by the uridine 5'-diphospho-glucuronosyltransferases UGT2B7, UGT1A3, UGT1A8, and UGT1A9. The apparent terminal elimination half-life was estimated to be 12.4 hours and apparent oral clearance was 10.6 L/hour based on population pharmacokinetic analysis. Following once-daily dosing, up to 22% accumulation, with respect to plasma AUC, was observed at steady-state, which was consistent with empagliflozin half-life. Following a dose of empagliflozin, 54.4% and 41.2% total radioactivity is excreted in urine and feces, respectively. The majority of drug-related radioactivity recovered in feces was unchanged parent drug and approximately half of drug-related radioactivity excreted in urine was unchanged parent drug.
 
Affected Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes and drug transporters: None
Based on in vitro data, empagliflozin does not inhibit, inactivate, or induce CYP450 isoforms. Empagliflozin also does not inhibit UGT1A1, UGT1A3, UGT1A8, UGT1A9, or UGT2B7. No effect of empagliflozin is anticipated on concomitantly administered drugs that are substrates of the major CYP450 isoforms or UGT1A1, UGT1A3, UGT1A8, UGT1A9, or UGT2B7. The effect of UGT induction on empagliflozin exposure has not been evaluated. Empagliflozin is a substrate for P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), but it does not inhibit these efflux transporters at therapeutic doses. Empagliflozin is considered unlikely to cause interactions with drugs that are P-gp substrates. Empagliflozin is a substrate of the human uptake transporters OAT3, OATP1B1, and OATP1B3, but not OAT1 and OCT2. Empagliflozin does not inhibit any of these human uptake transporters at clinically relevant plasma concentrations and, therefore, no effect of empagliflozin is anticipated on concomitantly administered drugs that are substrates of these uptake transporters.
