Drug Class: Varicella-zoster immune globulin is an intramuscularly administered preparation of purified human immune globulin G (IgG) containing antibodies to varicella-zoster virus (anti-VZV).

Routes: intramuscular; Common adult doses: 625 International units/dose IM.

Indication Specific Dosing
  General Information
  General Dosing Information
Both healthy and immunocompromised individuals with verified histories of varicella (except hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT) recipients) may be considered immune. HCT recipients should be considered nonimmune regardless of previous varicella disease or varicella vaccination in themselves or their donors. However, HCT recipients in whom varicella or herpes zoster develops after transplantation may be considered immune. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the groups listed below are at risk for severe disease and complications and should receive varicella-zoster immune globulin after exposure to varicella or herpes zoster. The FDA-approved product labeling also includes infants younger than 1 year as a high-risk group. Individuals receiving monthly high-dose intravenous immune globulin (IVIG 400 mg/kg or more) are likely protected and generally do not require varicella-zoster immune globulin if the most recent IVIG dose was administered within 3 weeks before exposure.
Pregnant individuals without evidence of immunity
Immunocompromised individuals without evidence of immunity, including those with primary or acquired immunodeficiency disorders, hematologic or other neoplastic disease, or receipt of immunosuppressive therapy (e.g., prednisone 2 mg/kg/day or more or 20 mg/day or more). Varicella-zoster immune globulin is not indicated for individuals who received 2 doses of varicella vaccine before subsequently becoming immunocompromised due to disease or treatment.
In children with HIV, varicella-zoster immune globulin is recommended after substantial exposure to varicella or herpes zoster for those with HIV stage 1 or 2 who lack evidence of immunity to varicella and for those with HIV stage 3 regardless of prior immunity status.
Preterm neonates born before 28 weeks gestation or with a birth weight of 1,000 g or less who are exposed during hospitalization for prematurity, regardless of maternal immunity.
Preterm neonates born at 28 weeks gestation or later who are exposed during hospitalization for prematurity and whose mothers lack evidence of immunity to varicella.
Neonates whose mothers develop signs and symptoms of varicella from 5 days before through 2 days after delivery.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Varicella-zoster immune globulin, VZIG should not be given by intravenous administration. Intravenous administration of VZIG significantly increases the risk of developing a reaction with features similar to anaphylaxis, including hypotension. VZIG should only be administered intramuscularly.
 
Varicella-zoster immune globulin, VZIG contains human immunoglobulin and thimerosal. The manufacturer states that VZIG should not be administered to patients with a history of immediate-type human immune globulin hypersensitivity. VZIG should not be administered to patients with a history of thimerosal hypersensitivity. True allergic reactions to human immune globulin products occur rarely; however, the benefits of VZIG should be weighed against the potential risk of severe hypersensitivity reactions. The health care professional should have immediate availability of epinephrine (1:1000) injection and other agents used in the treatment of severe anaphylaxis in the event of a serious allergic reaction to VZIG.
 
Patients with IgA deficiency often develop antibodies against IgA and are more likely to have anaphylactic or immune-mediated adverse reactions to pooled immunoglobulin products. VZIG should only be administered to these individuals if the benefits of prophylaxis outweigh the potential risks of the product.
 
As with other products derived from or purified with human blood components, the possibility of transmission of viral or bacterial infections exists in patients receiving VZIG. Screening plasma donors for prior exposure to certain viruses, testing for the presence of viruses, and inactivating and/or reducing viruses has reduced the risk of transmition of an infectious agent from VZIG. The manufacturing processes are designed to reduce the risk of transmitting viral infection; however, none of the processes are completely effective. There is also the possibility that unknown infectious agents may be present in this product.
 
Intramuscular (IM) injections of varicella-zoster immune globulin, VZIG can cause injury at the injection site and should be used cautiously in patients with thrombocytopenia or coagulopathy (e.g., hemophilia). However, in patients meeting criteria for VZIG administration, the benefit of VZIG outweighs the risk associated with IM administration. The CDC recommends that intramuscular (IM) vaccines should be given with a fine needle (23 gauge or smaller), and firm pressure should be applied over the site for at least 2 minutes. If possible, schedule the IM injections shortly after antihemophilia or similar therapy to minimize the risk of a hematoma.
 
Varicella-zoster immune globulin, VZIG is an FDA pregnancy category C drug. The manufacturer recommends that VZIG should be used during pregnancy only when clearly needed. Studies concerning its effects on the fetus have not been done; however, VZIG and other immunoglobulins have been used safely during pregnancy in observational studies. The CDC does not currently recommend the use of VZIG for prevention of intrauterine varicella infection due to a lack of evidence of impact on fetal outcomes (e.g., congenital varicella syndrome or neonatal varicella). However, the use of VZIG may be appropriate to prevent or lessen the risk of varicella zoster associated complications (e.g., pneumonia) in the pregnant woman, particularly those with concomitant immunosuppressed status, even though VZIG administration has little impact on the development of varicella viremia itself. The need for post-exposure prophylaxis of varicella should be determined for each individual based on the current CDC guidelines for adults. Appropriate screening of varicella immune status using a rapid test, such as the latex agglutination test, prior to administering VZIG may avoid unneccessary medication expense and exposure to the pregnant patient and the fetus.
 
The safety of the use of varicella-zoster immune globulin, VZIG has not been established during lactation. VZIG should be used cautiously in women who are breast-feeding because it is not known if VZIG is excreted in breast milk.
 
The immune globulins present in VZIG may interfere with live virus vaccination, such as measles, mumps or rubella (MMR). Live virus vaccinations should be delayed until 5 months after VZIG administration; it may be necessary to revaccinate persons who receive VZIG shortly after receiving live virus vaccinations.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The most common adverse reaction following VZIG administration is an injection site reaction which creates local discomfort, pain, local erythema, and/or swelling in approximately 1% of recipients. Less frequent adverse events occur in roughly 0.2% of patients and include headache, malaise, myalgia, nausea/vomiting, rash (unspecified), mild fever, or mild dyspnea. Severe events, such as anaphylactoid reactions and angioedema, are reported to be very rare, occuring in less than 0.1% of patients who receive VZIG.

Description
  Text
  Varicella-zoster immune globulin is an intramuscularly administered preparation of purified human immune globulin G (IgG) containing antibodies to varicella-zoster virus (anti-VZV). It is indicated for passive immunity in high-risk individuals after exposure to varicella (i.e., post-exposure prophylaxis). High-risk individuals for which the immune globulin is indicated include immunocompromised children and adults, newborns of mothers with varicella shortly before or after delivery, premature infants, neonates and infants younger than 1 year of age, adults without evidence of immunity, and pregnant women. Varicella-zoster immune globulin is intended to reduce the severity of varicella infections; however, there is no convincing evidence that post-exposure prophylaxis reduces the incidence of infection or alters disease progression after the appearance of varicella rash (i.e., established infection). To provide the greatest effectiveness, the immune globulin should be administered as soon as possible after exposure to the virus, ideally within 96 hours (can be given up to 10 days after exposure). Varicella-zoster immune globulin may be obtained through a drug expanded access program by contacting FFF Enterprises at 1-800-843-7477 or ASD Healthcare at 1-800-746-6273.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Varicella-zoster immune globulin is a preparation of purified human immune globulin G (IgG) containing antibodies to varicella-zoster virus (anti-VZV) and, thus, provides passive immunization for non-immune individuals exposed to the varicella-zoster virus. The product may reduce the severity of varicella infections. No convincing evidence exists that the product reduces the incidence of chickenpox infection after exposure to the virus or that established varicella infections can be modified with the product. Of note, varicella-zoster immune globulin might extend the incubation period of the virus from 10 to 21 days to 28 days or more.
