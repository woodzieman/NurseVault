    Headline
    cardiac accessory conduction pathway syndrome, hypotension, second degree AV Mobitz II block or higher degree of AV block, second-degree AV block, sinus node dysfunction, syncope
    General Information
    Etripamil is contraindicated in individuals with cardiac accessory conduction pathway syndrome [e.g., Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome, Lown-Ganong-Levine syndrome, manifest pre-excitation (delta wave) on a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG)], sick sinus syndrome without a permanent pacemaker, or second degree AV Mobitz II block or higher degree of AV block. Because of effects on blood pressure, heart rate, and cardiac conduction, etripamil may cause dizziness and/or syncope, especially in individuals with a history of syncope and high-grade AV block (i.e., second-degree AV block or third-degree AV block), sinus node dysfunction, or those with a history of syncope during an episode of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT). Monitor individuals with a history of hypotension or those at increased risk for hemodynamic instability appropriately when initiating etripamil; clinically significant hypotension occurred in a small percentage of subjects during etripamil test dosing prior to randomization in clinical trials, which precluded further study participation.

    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no available data on the use of etripamil during pregnancy to inform a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. Reproductive studies conducted with intravenous etripamil in pregnant rats and rabbits during organogenesis did not show any evidence of fetal harm or malformations in rats at doses up to 0.375 mg/kg/day, approximately 3 times the maximum concentration (Cmax) and 0.4 times the AUC at the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD), and in rabbits at doses up to 0.1 mg/kg/day, approximately equivalent to the Cmax and 10 times the AUC at the MRHD. Abortion was observed in 1 rabbit at the high dose of 0.1 mg/kg/day, a dose that caused maternal toxicity. In a pre- and post-natal study in rats, no adverse effects on development were observed with intravenous etripamil doses up to 0.374 mg/kg/day. However, post-implantation loss was noted at 0.374 mg/kg/day, a dose that also caused significant maternal toxicity, including mortality, transient adverse clinical signs, and body weight reduction.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    There are no data on the presence of etripamil in human or animal milk; however, verapamil, a structurally related compound, is known to be present in human milk. There are also no data on the effects of etripamil on the breastfed infant or on milk production. Because the presence of etripamil in breast milk has not been characterized and there is the potential for adverse reactions in the breastfed infant including hypotension and bradycardia, advise lactating individuals to interrupt breast-feeding and pump and discard milk for 12 hours (approximately 5 terminal half-lives) after treatment with etripamil.

    General Information
    The majority of treatment-related adverse reactions reported with etripamil in clinical studies have been related to local reactions to, at, or near the nasal administration site, including the nose, throat, and eyes, and occurred within 24 hours of etripamil administration. These local reactions include nasal discomfort (23% to 28%), nasal congestion (12% to 14%), throat irritation (6% to 7%), oropharyngeal pain, lacrimation, rhinorrhea (10% to 12%), epistaxis (6% to 7%), upper-airway cough syndrome, and sneezing. Treatment-related adverse reactions occurred at similar rates with first vs. repeat etripamil doses and decreased across subsequently treated paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT) episodes, suggesting acclimation to local, administration site side effects and improved tolerance with repeated use. Most adverse events were localized to the administration site, mild or moderate, and transient, resolving without intervention.

Description
  Text
  Etripamil is a nondihydropyridine calcium channel blocker, specifically an L-type calcium influx inhibitor (slow channel blocker or calcium ion antagonist), indicated for the conversion of acute symptomatic episodes of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT) to sinus rhythm. Etripamil is formulated as a fast-acting nasal spray for self-administration as soon as possible after PSVT symptom onset, allowing individuals to treat PSVT outside a health care setting and potentially reducing the need for additional medical interventions. Because of effects on blood pressure, heart rate, and cardiac conduction, etripamil may cause dizziness and/or syncope. The majority of treatment-related adverse reactions reported with etripamil in clinical studies have been related to local reactions to, at, or near the nasal administration site and were mild or moderate, transient, and resolved without intervention. Etripamil was FDA-approved in December 2025.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Etripamil is a nondihydropyridine calcium channel blocker, specifically an L-type calcium influx inhibitor (slow channel blocker or calcium ion antagonist), that exerts its pharmacologic effect by modulating the influx of ionic calcium across the cell membrane of atrioventricular (AV) nodal cells, arterial smooth muscles, and contractile myocardial cells. Similar to verapamil, etripamil slows AV nodal conduction and prolongs the AV nodal refractory period by blocking the slow inward calcium influx through L-type calcium channels. By interrupting reentry at the AV node, etripamil can restore sinus rhythm in individuals with paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT). Atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia (AVNRT) is the most common cause of PSVT and involves 2 reentry pathways, the slow and fast pathways, in and around the AV node. Etripamil selectively affects the slow pathway bridge sharing voltages in the surrounding coronary sinus and fast pathway region. A high-density mapping of the slow pathway in an individual with AVNRT at 3 minutes after intranasal etripamil administration showed significant voltage loss in the slow pathway, similar to post-ablation.

  Text
  Etripamil is administered intranasally. Etripamil plasma protein binding is approximately 50%. Etripamil has a mean apparent Vd of approximately 2,200 to 3,500 L. Etripamil is primarily metabolized by blood esterases and hepatic metabolism, primarily by CYP3A4 and CYP3A5. Etripamil contains a methyl ester which renders it metabolically sensitive to bloodborne esterases, and it is rapidly transformed to the inactive metabolite MSP-2030. Etripamil metabolic pathways include hydrolysis, demethylation, N-dealkylation, and secondary oxidation, glucuronidation, and taurine conjugation. After a single dose of radiolabeled intranasal etripamil 70 mg was administered to healthy subjects, approximately 29% of the dose was recovered in urine (less than 0.05% unchanged), 26% was recovered in feces (less than 0.05% unchanged), and the remainder was recovered on nose and face tissues. The average etripamil concentration fell by approximately 60% of its peak value (Cmax) at 25 minutes and 80% of the Cmax by 60 minutes after dosing; subsequently, concentrations decrease at a slower rate. Etripamil has a half-life of approximately 2.5 hours.
Pharmacodynamics
Etripamil prolonged the PR interval by 8% to 10% approximately 5 minutes after administration of 70 mg intranasally. This PR interval prolongation begins to decline shortly after the etripamil Cmax and falls below the 8% to 10% threshold between 25 and 50 minutes. Etripamil mean maximum change from baseline PR interval was approximately 13% after a single 70 mg dose and 20% after two 70 mg doses. In subjects with induced supraventricular tachycardia, a single intranasal etripamil administration was associated with a maximal systolic blood pressure reduction of 21 mmHg with etripamil 140 mg, 17 mmHg with etripamil 105 mg, and 3 mmHg with etripamil 70 mg; no reduction in blood pressure was observed with etripamil 35 mg.
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2C9, CYP2D6, CYP3A4, CYP3A5, P-gp, MATE1, OATP1B1
Etripamil is a CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 substrate and inhibits CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4. Etripamil is also a substrate of P-gp and OATP1B1 and inhibits P-gp and MATE1.

  Route Specific Administrations
    Administration Details
      Name
      Intranasal Administration
      Description
      Nasal Spray
Each etripamil device delivers 2 sprays for a total dose of 70 mg.
Do not test, prime, or press the device plunger before ready to deliver a dose.
Administer etripamil as soon as possible after paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT) symptom onset.
Open the lid and remove 1 etripamil nasal spray device from the carrying case.
With the individual seated, hold the nasal spray device in an upright position with the thumb on the bottom of the plunger and the tip between the index and middle fingers.
Keep the head straight and insert the tip of the nasal spray device into 1 nostril until the index and middle fingers are touching the bottom of the nose.
Using the thumb, press the plunger firmly and quickly all the way up.
Advise the individual to breathe normally and avoid inhaling deeply.
Release the plunger and remove the nasal spray device from the first nostril.
Insert the same nasal spray device into the second nostril and spray right away.
Have the individual remain seated and keep head straight for 10 minutes.
When medicine drips out, wipe the nose, but do not blow the nose for 10 minutes.
If a full initial dose (i.e., 2 sprays, 1 in each nostril) is not administered due to device malfunction or misuse, wait at least 10 minutes before self-administering a second dose, if needed.
Discard etripamil device after use. Do not re-use the etripamil device.
If symptoms persist after 10 minutes, repeat dose with a new device.
If symptoms have not improved within 20 minutes after the second dose, individuals or caregivers should contact their health care provider or seek emergency medical help right away.
