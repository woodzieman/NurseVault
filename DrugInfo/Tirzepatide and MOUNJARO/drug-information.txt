Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  Contraindications And Precautions
    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    medullary thyroid carcinoma, multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2, thyroid C-cell tumors
    General Information
    Tirzepatide is contraindicated in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or in people with multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2). Tirzepatide has been shown to cause dose-dependent and treatment duration-dependent increase in the incidence of thyroid C-cell tumors (adenomas and carcinomas) in animal studies. It is unknown whether tirzepatide causes thyroid C-cell tumors, including MTC, in humans. It is not known whether monitoring serum calcitonin or performing thyroid ultrasounds will diminish human risk of thyroid C-cell tumors. Tirzepatide recipients should be counseled on the risk and symptoms of thyroid tumors (e.g. a mass in the neck, dysphagia, dyspnea or persistent hoarseness). Although routine monitoring of serum calcitonin is of uncertain value during tirzepatide treatment, if serum calcitonin is measured and found to be elevated, the individual should be referred to an endocrinologist for further evaluation.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Anaphylaxis and angioedema have been reported with GLP-1 receptor agonists. Use tirzepatide with caution in people with a history of angioedema or anaphylaxis with a GLP-1 receptor agonist because it is unknown whether such patients will be predisposed to these reactions with tirzepatide.
    Headline
    gastroparesis
    General Information
    Tirzepatide slows gastric emptying, consistent with the actions of other GLP-1 receptor agonists, and has been associated with gastrointestinal (GI) adverse reactions that are sometimes severe. Tirzepatide is not recommended in individuals with severe gastroparesis.
    Headline
    diabetic retinopathy
    General Information
    Monitor for visual changes in people with a history of diabetic retinopathy. Temporary worsening of diabetic retinopathy has been reported with rapid improvement in glucose control. Inform individuals to contact their care team if changes in vision are experienced during treatment.
    Headline
    pancreatitis
    General Information
    Use tirzepatide with caution in people with a history of pancreatitis. After initiation of tirzepatide, observe people carefully for signs and symptoms of pancreatitis (which may include persistent or severe abdominal pain (sometimes radiating to the back) and which may or may not be accompanied by nausea or vomiting). Acute pancreatitis, including fatal and non-fatal hemorrhagic or necrotizing pancreatitis, has been observed in individuals treated with GLP-1 receptor agonists. If pancreatitis is suspected, discontinue tirzepatide and initiate appropriate management.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Avoid the use of tirzepatide during pregnancy. Counsel individuals on the potential risk to the fetus. Weight loss offers no potential benefit to a pregnant person and may cause fetal harm. Available human pregnancy data are insufficient to determine a drug-associated risk for major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse pregnancy outcomes. However, animal studies suggest fetal harm. In pregnant animals given tirzepatide during organogenesis, fetal growth reductions (rats and rabbits) and fetal abnormalities (rats) occurred at clinically relevant exposures based on AUC. These effects coincided with tirzepatide's pharmacological effects on maternal weight and food consumption. A pregnancy exposure registry is available for Zepbound-exposed pregnancies. Contact the manufacturer at 1-800-545-5979 for more information. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommend human insulin as the standard of care for pregnant individuals with diabetes or gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) requiring medical therapy; insulin does not cross the placenta.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with tirzepatide. Based on animal reproduction studies, tirzepatide may cause fetal harm. For those who may become pregnant, contraception is recommended during treatment with tirzepatide and other glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA). Tirzepatide may reduce the effectiveness of oral hormonal contraceptives by delaying gastric emptying, especially after treatment initiation and dose escalations. Patients should use a non-oral contraceptive or add a barrier method for 4 weeks after starting tirzepatide and for 4 weeks after each dose increase. Discontinue GLP-1 RA therapy before a planned pregnancy. Advise individuals that several months may be needed before conception to allow for drug elimination and, for those with diabetes, transition to a therapy appropriate for use during pregnancy.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use tirzepatide with caution during breast-feeding. In a single-dose clinical lactation study, the concentration of tirzepatide in breast milk was found to be either undetectable or low compared to the maternal administered dose. The developmental and health benefits of breastfeeding should be considered along with the pregnant individual's clinical need for tirzepatide and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed infant from tirzepatide or from the underlying maternal condition. Following subcutaneous administration of a single 5 mg dose to 11 healthy lactating adult females, the concentration of tirzepatide in breast milk was found to be undetectable (limit of detection in breast milk 4 nanogram/mL) in 164/171 samples assayed. The cumulative amount of tirzepatide detected in the remaining 7 breast milk samples over the 28-day sampling window was equivalent to less than 0.02% of the maternal administered dose, with the last measurable concentrations occurring 5 days post-dose. The AUC of tirzepatide in breast milk could not be calculated, due to insufficient quantifiable concentrations. There are no available data on the effects of tirzepatide on the breastfed infant or on milk production. If tirzepatide for the management of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is discontinued and blood glucose is not controlled on diet and exercise alone, the clinician may consider insulin therapy. Oral hypoglycemics may also be considered. Metformin monotherapy may be appropriate for some patients as available studies indicate low excretion in milk and that maternal use during breast-feeding is not expected to result in side effects to a healthy nursing infant. Some experts recommend using metformin with caution if the patient is breastfeeding a newborn or a premature neonate with reduced renal function. Because acarbose has limited systemic absorption, which results in minimal maternal plasma concentrations, clinically significant exposure via breast milk is not expected; therefore, this agent may be an alternative if postprandial glucose control is needed. Glyburide may be a suitable alternative since it was not detected in the breast milk of lactating women who received single and multiple doses of glyburide. If any oral hypoglycemics are used during breast-feeding, the nursing infant should be monitored for signs of hypoglycemia, such as increased fussiness or somnolence.

Acebutolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Atenolol: (Moderate) Increased frequency of blood glucose monitoring may be required when a beta blocker is given with antidiabetic agents. Since beta blockers inhibit the release of catecholamines, these medications may hide symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, and blood pressure changes. Other symptoms, like headache, dizziness, nervousness, mood changes, or hunger are not blunted. Beta-blockers also exert complex actions on the body's ability to regulate blood glucose. Some beta-blockers, particularly non-selective beta-blockers such as propranolol, have been noted to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia and a delay in recovery of blood glucose to normal levels. Hyperglycemia has been reported as well and is possibly due to beta-2 receptor blockade in the beta cells of the pancreas. A selective beta-blocker may be preferred in patients with diabetes mellitus, if appropriate for the patient's condition. Selective beta-blockers, such as atenolol or metoprolol, do not appear to potentiate insulin-induced hypoglycemia. While beta-blockers may have negative effects on glycemic control, they reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke in patients with diabetes and their use should not be avoided in patients with compelling indications for beta-blocker therapy when no other contraindications are present.

Description
  Text
  Tirzepatide is a subcutaneously administered, once-weekly, dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist. Tirzepatide subcutaneous injection (Mounjaro) is indicated as an adjunct to diet and exercise to improve glycemic control in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and to reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular (CV) events (MACE; CV death, non-fatal myocardial infarction (MI), or non-fatal stroke) in people with T2DM who are at high risk for these events. Adults with T2DM in the SURPASS trials achieved average A1C reductions between 1.8% and 2.1% for tirzepatide 5 mg and between 1.7% and 2.4% for both tirzepatide 10 mg and tirzepatide 15 mg. Adults given the maximum recommended dose of 15 mg lowered their A1C by 0.5% more than semaglutide 1 mg, 0.9% more than insulin degludec, and 1% more than insulin glargine (p less than 0.001 for all 3 comparisons). The efficacy of tirzepatide for diabetes in pediatric individuals 10 years and older was evaluated in a 30-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, with a 22-week open-label extension, that randomized subjects (n = 99) with inadequate glycemic control on metformin (69%), basal insulin (8%), or both (23%) to receive tirzepatide 5 mg, 10 mg, or placebo once weekly as add-on therapy. Tirzepatide treatment resulted in statistically significant reductions in A1C compared to placebo. Additionally, greater reductions in fasting serum glucose and body mass index (BMI) were observed compared to placebo in tirzepatide-treated subjects. In the Phase 3, randomized, double-blind, active-comparator CV outcomes trial (SURPASS-CVOT), 13,299 subjects with type 2 diabetes mellitus and established CV disease received tirzepatide (Mounjaro) or dulaglutide. Tirzepatide (Mounjaro) was noninferior to dulaglutide for MACE-3 (CV death, nonfatal MI, or nonfatal stroke; HR 0.92 [95.3% CI, 0.83, 1.01]); superiority was not established. A separate product, tirzepatide subcutaneous injection (Zepbound), is indicated in combination with a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity to reduce excess body weight and maintain weight reduction long term in adults with obesity or adults with overweight in the presence of at least 1 weight-related comorbid condition. This formulation is also indicated for the treatment of moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) in adults with obesity. Approval for weight loss was supported by 2 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials of adults with obesity or who were overweight with at least 1 weight-related condition. These studies measured weight reduction after 72 weeks of treatment (at least 52 weeks at maintenance dose) of 2,519 adults who received either 5 mg, 10 mg or 15 mg of tirzepatide once weekly and a total of 958 adults who received once-weekly placebo injections. In both trials, after 72 weeks of treatment, adults who received tirzepatide at all 3 dose concentrations experienced a statistically significant reduction in body weight compared to those who received placebo, and greater proportions of adults who received tirzepatide achieved at least 5% weight reduction compared to placebo. In study 1, adults without diabetes who were obese (BMI 30 kg/m2 or greater) or overweight (BMI 27 to less than 30 kg/m2) with at least 1 weight-related comorbid condition had an average body weight of 231 pounds (105 kg) and average BMI of 38 kg/m2 at the start of the trial; those randomized to receive tirzepatide 15 mg once weekly lost on average 18% of their body weight compared to those randomized to placebo. In study 2, adults with a BMI of 27 kg/m2 or greater and T2DM had an average body weight of 222 pounds (101 kg) and an average BMI of 36 kg/m2; those randomized to receive tirzepatide 15 mg once weekly lost on average 12% of their body weight compared to those randomized to placebo. Approval for OSA was based on results of 2 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials that included a total of 469 adults with moderate to severe OSA and obesity. The first study enrolled 234 adults who were unable or unwilling to use positive airway pressure (PAP) therapy. The second study enrolled 235 adults who were on PAP therapy but were instructed to suspend PAP for 7 days prior to assessment. In both studies, they were randomized to receive tirzepatide or placebo for 52 weeks, with the tirzepatide dose escalating over a period of up to 20 weeks to a maximum tolerated dose of 10 mg or 15 mg subcutaneously once weekly. Treatment with tirzepatide for 52 weeks resulted in statistically significant reduction in Apnea-Hypopnea Index scores from baseline when compared to placebo treatment, with greater proportions of individuals on tirzepatide achieving remission or mild non-symptomatic OSA compared to placebo. These changes were irrespective of numerous factors, including baseline BMI or baseline OSA severity. In both studies, adults treated with tirzepatide showed improvement in sleep-related impairment compared to those on placebo. They also achieved a greater reduction in systolic blood pressure and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein concentrations compared to placebo. First-line T2DM therapy depends on comorbidities, individual-centered treatment factors, and management needs. In adults with T2DM and established atherosclerotic CV disease (ASCVD) or indicators of high ASCVD risk, sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2 inhibitor) and/or GLP-1 RA with proven CV benefit (e.g. dulaglutide, liraglutide, and semaglutide), should be initiated as a first-line therapy independent of A1C goal or other antihyperglycemic treatments, including metformin. Certain GLP-1-based therapies (tirzepatide and semaglutide) have proven benefits in individuals with heart failure (HF) and obesity, making them alternative treatment options in individuals with T2DM with HF who cannot tolerate an SGLT2 inhibitor. Tirzepatide may delay chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression, but sufficient evidence is lacking. Evidence from CV outcome trials indicate that dulaglutide, semaglutide, and liraglutide provide renal benefits, mainly through reductions in albuminuria. Notably, semaglutide has also been shown to slow CKD progression, positioning these agents as alternative options for individuals with T2DM and established CKD who are unable to use SGLT2 inhibitors. Tirzepatide may also have potential benefit in individuals with T2DM and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). In individuals with T2DM who do not have ASCVD/indicators of high-risk, HF, or CKD and who need to minimize hypoglycemia and/or promote weight loss, GLP-1-based therapy is generally recommended as a second-line option as add-on to metformin therapy. Tirzepatide has a very high glucose-lowering efficacy and efficacy for weight loss. For people requiring an injectable medication, GLP-1-based therapy is preferred to insulin due to similar or even better efficacy in A1C reduction, lower risk of hypoglycemia, and reductions in body weight. Obesity treatment should be individualized and focused on the prevention, mitigation, and treatment of obesity-related complications and diseases. Tirzepatide is a preferred anti-obesity medication across multiple complication-centric care categories. It is a first-line option in people with obesity or overweight who have concomitant T2DM, hypertension, CKD, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), MASH, or OSA. Tirzepatide is also a first-line option for prevention of prediabetes/metabolic syndrome/diabetes in these individuals. Tirzepatide was initially FDA approved in 2022.
 
NOTE: The FDA has established a "green list" import alert to help stop potentially dangerous GLP-1 RA active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) from unverified foreign sources from entering the U.S. market. This is part of the agency's decisive steps to safeguard consumers from illegal GLP-1 RA active ingredients imported from overseas to ensure safety and a secure drug supply chain. To protect people who use compounded versions of GLP-1 RAs, the green list will include GLP-1 APIs from facilities the agency has inspected or evaluated that appear to be in compliance with the FDA's rigorous standards applicable to all APIs manufactured in the U.S. APIs from other sources are subject to detention without physical examination. The agency will continue to work with state regulators, monitor the market, and take enforcement actions as necessary to prevent unsafe or fraudulent GLP-1 drugs from reaching U.S. consumers.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Tirzepatide is a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist. It is a 39-amino-acid modified peptide with a C20 fatty diacid moiety that enables albumin binding and prolongs the half-life. Tirzepatide selectively binds to and activates both the GIP and GLP-1 receptors, the targets for native GIP and GLP-1. Tirzepatide enhances first- and second-phase insulin secretion, and reduces glucagon levels, both in a glucose dependent manner. Tirzepatide also promotes weight loss. GLP-1 is a physiological regulator of appetite and caloric intake. Nonclinical studies suggest the addition of GIP may further contribute to the regulation of food intake.

  Text
  Tirzepatide is given via subcutaneous administration. Steady-state plasma concentrations were achieved after 4 weeks of once weekly administration. Tirzepatide exposure increases in a dose-proportional manner. The mean apparent steady-state Vd after subcutaneous administration in people with overweight or obesity is approximately 9.7 L, while the Vd in people with obstructive sleep apnea and obesity is approximately 11.8 L. Tirzepatide is 99% bound to plasma albumin. The apparent population mean clearance is 0.06 L/hour. Tirzepatide is metabolized by proteolytic cleavage of the peptide backbone, beta-oxidation of the C20 fatty diacid moiety, and amide hydrolysis. Tirzepatide metabolites are primarily excreted via urine and feces. Intact tirzepatide is not observed in urine or feces. The elimination half-life is approximately 5 to 6 days.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: none

      General Dosing Information
Tirzepatide (Mounjaro and Zepbound) is available in pre-filled, disposable, single-dose pens or vials, or single-patient-use multi-dose vials or KwikPen each containing 4 doses. Ensure the correct dose of the pen or vial is chosen for the dose to be administered.

Instruct patients/caregivers on proper injection techniques. For Mounjaro KwikPen, self-administration is not recommended for pediatric patients. Adequate oral and written instructions on the use should be supplied before a patient or caregiver administers a dose. People who are blind or have vision problems should not use injection pens (single-dose pen or single-patient-use KwikPen) without help from a person trained to use the pens.
Administer once every 7 days (once weekly) on the same day each week; the dose can be administered at any time of day, with or without meals.
Missed dose: If 1 dose is missed, instruct patients to administer tirzepatide as soon as possible within 4 days (96 hours) after the missed dose. If more than 4 days have passed, skip the missed dose and administer the next dose on the regularly scheduled day. In each case, patients can then resume their regular once weekly dosing schedule. The day of weekly administration can be changed, if necessary, as long as the time between the two doses is at least 3 days (72 hours).
 
 
Subcutaneous Single-Dose Pen (e.g., Mounjaro and Zepbound)
Remove the pen from the refrigerator.
Wash and dry hands before use.
The dose of tirzepatide is already set on the pen; each pen is for one-time use only.
Ensure that the pen is locked; once ready to inject, pull the gray base cap straight off and throw it away in the trash. Do not put the gray base cap back on. Do not touch the needle.
Place the clear base flat against the skin at the injection site. Unlock by turning the lock ring.
Inject subcutaneously into the thigh, abdomen, or have another person inject in the back of upper arm by pressing and holding the purple injection button for up to 10 seconds. Listen for the first click which indicates the injection has started, and then the second click which indicates the injection has completed. You will know the injection is complete when the gray plunger is visible.
Dispose of the used pen in an appropriate sharps container after each injection.
Rotate administration sites with each injection to prevent lipodystrophy; do not inject into areas of lipodystrophy or localized cutaneous amyloidosis. Do not inject where the skin is tender, bruised, scaly or hard, or into scars or damaged skin.
Storage: Store in the refrigerator at 36 to 46 degrees F (2 to 8 degrees C). If needed, each single-dose pen can be stored unrefrigerated at temperatures not to exceed 86 degrees F (30 degrees C) for up to a total of 21 days. If single-dose pen is stored at room temperature, it should not be returned to the refrigerator. Discard single-dose pen after a total of 21 days at room temperature.

Subcutaneous Single-Patient-Use KwikPen (e.g., Mounjaro and Zepbound)
General Dosing Information
For Mounjaro KwikPen, self-administration is not recommended for pediatric patients.
Each KwikPen contains additional volume to allow for device priming.
The KwikPen will prevent the patient from dialing a full dose after 4 weekly doses are administered. Do not inject leftover medication. Do not transfer from the KwikPen into a separate syringe.
 
Preparation Prior to Injection
Remove the KwikPen from the refrigerator.
Wash and dry hands before use.
Pull the cap straight off and wipe the red inner seal with an alcohol swab. If the cap cannot be removed, gently twist the cap back and forth, and then pull straight off.
Select a new KwikPen needle, then pull off the paper tab from the outer needle shield.
Push the capped needle straight onto the KwikPen and twist on until it is tight.
Pull off the outer needle shield and keep it as this will be reused. Pull off the inner needle shield and dispose in household trash.
 
Priming the KwikPen
Prime the KwikPen before each weekly dose to remove air from the cartridge and ensure that the KwikPen is working correctly.
Slowly turn the dose knob until you hear 2 clicks and the extended line is shown in the dose window. This is the prime position and can be corrected by turning the dose knob in either direction until the prime position lines up to the dose indicator.
Hold the KwikPen with the needle pointing up and tap the cartridge holder gently to collect air bubbles at the top.
Release some medicine into the air by pushing the dose knob in until it stops. Slowly count to 5 while holding the dose knob. The '0' icon must be shown in the dose window. Do not inject into body. The KwikPen has been primed if a small amount (a drop) of medication comes out of the tip of the needle.
If no medication comes out, repeat the process no more than 2 additional times.
If no medication comes out after 2 additional attempts, change the needle and repeat the process no more than 1 additional time.
If no medication comes out after the third attempt, contact Lilly at 1-800-545-5979.
 
Administration
Turn the dose knob all the way until it stops and the '1' icon is in the dose window (this will be the weekly dose). Do not count clicks as the dose is selected.
If the KwikPen prevents the patient from turning the dose knob, discard the KwikPen, including unused medication. Do not attempt to inject the leftover medication.
Inject subcutaneously into the thigh, abdomen, or have another person inject in the back of upper arm by pushing the dose knob until it stops. Slowly count to 5 while holding the dose knob. The '0' icon must be shown in the dose window before removing the needle to ensure the full dose has been administered. The patient may notice the plunger moving with each injection.
If the dose knob is difficult to push, the patient may need to try the following troubleshooting methods:
Push the dose knob more slowly, as this will make it easier to inject.
If the needle may be blocked, replace the needle and re-prime the KwikPen.
If dust, food, or liquid maybe inside the KwikPen, discard the KwikPen and obtain a new pen.
Pull the needle out and confirm the '0' icon is in the dose window. A drop of medication on the needle tip is normal and will not affect dose. If the patient sees blood after removing the needle, lightly press the injection site with gauze or a cotton ball. Do not rub the injection site.
If no '0' icon appears in the dose window, insert the needle back into the skin subcutaneously to finish injection. Do not redial dose.
If the patient does not think the full dose was received, do not start over or repeat the injection.
Carefully replace the outer needle shield. Unscrew the capped needle and dispose of the needle in a sharps container after each injection. Do not store KwikPen with the needle attached to prevent leaking, blocking the needle, and air from entering the pen.
Replace the KwikPen cap. Do not store the KwikPen without the cap attached.
Dispose of the used KwikPen in household trash or sharps container after the needle has been removed.
Rotate administration sites with each injection to prevent lipodystrophy. You may use the same area, but ensure a different injection site in that area is chosen. Do not inject into areas of lipodystrophy or localized cutaneous amyloidosis. Do not inject where the skin is tender, bruised, scaly or hard, or into scars or damaged skin.
