Drug Class: Zolpidem is a non-benzodiazepine sedative-hypnotic used for the treatment of insomnia in adults.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: Immediate-release tablets (e.g., Ambien): 10 mg/night PO.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): bone fractures, confusion, dizziness, drowsiness, fatigue, headache, impaired cognition, intracranial bleeding, lethargy, psychomotor impairment, amnesia, complex sleep-related behaviors, memory impairment, somnambulism, abnormal dreams, agitation, anxiety, delirium, euphoria, hallucinations, insomnia, nightmares, anaphylactic shock, anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema, laryngeal edema, acne vulgaris, bullous rash, erythema, flushing

In an Overdose: Post-marketing experience with overdose of zolpidem, either alone or in combination with other CNS depressants, has included reports of impaired consciousness, coma, cardiovascular and/or respiratory compromise, and fatalities. These patients may experience suicidal ideation and be more likely to intentionally overdose on medications.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: This medication should be avoided in those with a hypersensitivity to zolpidem or any ingredient in the product. Reactions including anaphylaxis or angioedema may occur with sedative-hypnotics, and may become evident as early as the initial dose.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  This medication should be avoided in those with a hypersensitivity to zolpidem or any ingredient in the product. Reactions including anaphylaxis or angioedema may occur with sedative-hypnotics, and may become evident as early as the initial dose. Patients should be instructed on the appropriate action in the event of an allergic reaction. Treatment with zolpidem should not be reinitiated in patients who experience angioedema after administration of the drug.
 
Zolpidem has a rapid onset of action and should only be administered immediately prior to retiring. Patients should be instructed to avoid driving or operating machinery or performing other tasks requiring mental alertness after taking their dosage. Sedative-hypnotic medications have the potential to cause sleep-related behaviors such as sleep-driving, a state of driving after ingestion of a sedative-hypnotic while not fully awake and having no memory of the event. Other sleep-related behaviors may include making phone calls or eating while asleep. The exact incidences among various sedative products are unknown; however patients should be informed of the risks prior to receiving any medication from this class. Next-day residual effects are not common with zolpidem. Anterograde amnesia may be particularly evident at doses above 10 mg/day. Additive effects may occur with alcohol or other CNS-depressant medications. Patients should not drink alcoholic beverages and should avoid ingestion of other alcohol-containing products while taking zolpidem. Patients should also avoid acute or chronic ethanol intoxication while taking this medication. Lower initial dosages of zolpidem should be considered in patients taking other CNS-depressant therapies concurrently. Mental status changes have been reported to occur with the use of sedatives-hypnotics, including the emergence of bizarre behaviors. While these are relatively infrequent with zolpidem, any emergence of changes in thinking or behavior should be evaluated. Sleep disturbances may indicate an underlying physical or psychiatric problem. The failure of insomnia to remit after 7—10 days of zolpidem treatment may indicate the presence of a primary medical or psychiatric illness; therefore, evaluation for a potential co-morbid diagnosis is recommended at that time.
 
Immediate-release zolpidem is recommended for use in the short-term treatment of insomnia. No such recommendation exists in the package labeling for extended-release zolpidem, although cautious use is prudent when prescribing any hypnotic. Problems associated with abrupt discontinuation of hypnotic drugs are more likely to occur following chronic therapy. There is no reliable data documenting the occurrence of withdrawal symptoms following discontinuation of zolpidem, but evidence of fatigue, nausea/vomiting, flushing, lightheadedness, inconsolable crying, stomach cramps, panic attack, nervousness, and abdominal discomfort may constitute a withdrawal syndrome. Rare post-marketing reports of abuse, dependence, and withdrawal have been received. Withdrawal of some hypnotics also precipitates a rebound insomnia. If therapy is continued for more than 2 weeks the possibility of a withdrawal syndrome should be considered and abrupt discontinuation of therapy avoided. The possibility of physical and psychological dependence to zolpidem requires close monitoring. Zolpidem should be used cautiously in patients with a history of alcoholism or substance abuse.
 
Zolpidem, like all CNS depressants, should be used cautiously in patients with symptoms of depression. These patients may have suicidal ideation tendencies and may be more likely to intentionally overdose on medications. In addition, worsening of depression may occur during zolpidem administration. Suicidal thoughts and actions, including completed suicides, have been reported in association with the use of sedative/hypnotics. Zolpidem should be prescribed in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management to reduce the risk of overdose.
 
Elderly and/or debilitated patients may be more sensitive to the effects of zolpidem. In 3-week controlled trials, the adverse event profile of zolpidem extended-release was similar in elderly and younger populations. However, the impairment of cognitive and motor function may be more marked in the elderly and a lower initial dosage is recommended together with close monitoring (see Dosage). Elderly patients who receive > 5 mg/day of zolpidem immediate-release or > 6.25 mg/day of zolpidem extended-release may be at risk for falls or mental status changes. Federal OBRA regulations recommend maximum dosage limits for hypnotic use in the nursing home resident (see Dosage). During studies evaluating sleep after discontinuation of zolpidem, elderly patients reported impaired sleep on the first post-treatment night after using doses above 5 mg/day. No objective evidence of rebound insomnia was observed at recommended doses.
 
Zolpidem should be administered cautiously to patients with hepatic disease because the elimination half-life of the drug can be prolonged, with possible resultant toxicity. Patients with hepatic disease should receive a lower initial dosage to avoid adverse CNS reactions (see Dosage).
 
All formulations of zolpidem are classified as FDA pregnancy category C. Animal studies have shown no evidence of teratogenicity from zolpidem administration with any dosage form; however, maternal sedation and less weight gain have occurred in some studies. The no effect dose for fetal or maternal effects is roughly 4—5 times the maximal recommended human dose (MRHD) based on mg/m2. One case report has documented the presence of zolpidem in human umbilical cord blood. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women. Animal study outcomes are not always predictive of human response; lack of information on the effects of zolpidem in pregnant women precludes the use of zolpidem immediate-release unless clearly needed. Zolpidem extended-release should be used during pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus. There is some risk that babies born to mothers taking sedative/hypnotics may suffer withdrawal symptoms and possible neonatal flaccidity. Zolpidem has no established use during labor or obstetric delivery.
 
Small amounts of zolpidem are excreted into breast milk (i.e., 0.004 and 0.019% of a dose) after a maternal dosage of 20 mg, which exceeds the normal recommended daily dosage. One study in rats indicated that zolpidem could inhibit the secretion of breast-milk; however, the no-effect dose was found to be roughly 6-times the maximal recommended human dose (MRHD) based on mg/m2. The effects of zolpidem exposure on the breast-feeding infant have not been evaluated. The manufacturer of zolpidem advises caution when administering zolpidem to nursing mothers ; however, the American Academy of Pediatrics has generally considered zolpidem compatible with lactation based on the available data. Lactating women should avoid breast-feeding their infants at times of peak drug concentrations, and observe the infant for any indications of adverse events, like sedation, increased crying, poor feeding, or irritability.
 
The safety and efficacy of zolpidem in children has not been established. Results of a controlled 8-week study for the treatment of insomnia in 201 children aged 6—17 years with attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) indicated that zolpidem was not effective compared to placebo. Ten patients discontinued treatment due to an adverse effect. Treatment-emergent adverse effects included dizziness, headache, and hallucinations.
 
Post-market reports indicate that respiratory insufficiency or oxygen desaturation may occur in some patients treated with zolpidem, mostly in patients with pre-existing pulmonary disease. Zolpidem should be used with caution in patients with pre-existing respiratory depression, such as severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), sleep apnea, or myasthenia gravis to avoid the risk of depressing ventilatory function.
 
Although evidence to date does not indicate any need to use lower doses of zolpidem in patients with renal impairment or renal failure, as a general precaution these patients should be closely monitored clinically.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. While zolpidem belongs to the nonbenzodiazepine, benzodiazepine receptor agonist (NBRA) class, the chemical structures of the agents in this class are distinct. Cross-sensitivity to one NBRA does not imply cross-sensitivity will occur to another agent in the class.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery, drug-induced complex sleep-related behaviors, females
    General Information
    Zolpidem and other non-benzodiazepine receptor agonists (NBRAs) are contraindicated in people with a history of drug-induced complex sleep-related behaviors. Sedative-hypnotics, especially NBRAs like zolpidem, can cause complex sleep-related behaviors (e.g., sleep-driving, eating, sexual activity, or making phone calls) with no memory of these events. These effects can occur with or without concomitant use of alcohol or other CNS depressants. Rarely, serious injuries or death have occurred. Patients should discontinue zolpidem immediately and contact their healthcare provider if such episodes occur. Care teams and patients are encouraged to report adverse events to the FDA MedWatch Safety Information and Adverse Event Reporting Program. Zolpidem has rapid onset and CNS depressant effects, and should be taken immediately before bed, with at least 7 to 8 hours remaining for sleep when used for insomnia or 4 hours for treatment of middle-of-the-night awakenings. Patients should avoid driving or operating machinery, or engaging in activities requiring coordination and concentration for at least 8 hours after immediate-release formulations and impairment may last up to 24 hours following ingestion of an extended-release formulation. The risk for next-day impairment is increased if zolpidem is taken without adherence to recommended hours for sleep or at doses higher than recommended. Anterograde amnesia may occur and can be particularly evident at zolpidem doses above 10 mg/day. Use with alcohol or other CNS depressants increases the risk of impairment and adverse effects, and patients should avoid alcoholic beverages. Consider lower doses of zolpidem when other CNS depressants are prescribed concurrently. Due to gender differences, lower initial zolpidem doses are recommended for females, as they may have higher plasma levels the next morning, increasing impairment risk.
    Headline
    respiratory insufficiency
    General Information
    Sedative-hypnotics have the potential to suppress respiratory drive, and caution is advised when using zolpidem in people with compromised respiratory function (respiratory insufficiency). Reports of respiratory insufficiency in people receiving zolpidem, many of whom had pre-existing respiratory impairment, have occurred. The risk of respiratory depression should be considered before using zolpidem in people with respiratory insufficiency, including severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), sleep apnea, myasthenia gravis, or individuals with concomitant opioid use.
    Headline
    substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    Zolpidem should be used with caution in people with a history of a substance abuse disorder. People with a history of addiction or abuse of drugs or alcohol are at increased risk for misuse, abuse, or addiction and should be monitored carefully when receiving zolpidem or any hypnotic. Additionally, people taking zolpidem are at risk of physical and/or psychological dependence and this risk increases with dose and duration of treatment. People with dependence on zolpidem may experience withdrawal signs and symptoms following abrupt discontinuation. To minimize these risks, zolpidem should be used at the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration of treatment possible, and reassess the patient periodically before continuing treatment for an extended period of time.
    Headline
    depression, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Worsening of depression and suicidal ideation, including completed suicides, have been reported during the use of hypnotics, primarily in people with pre-existing depression. Immediately evaluate patients with worsening depression, emergent suicidal ideation (suicidal thoughts or actions), or other new or worsening adverse behaviors during treatment with zolpidem. A variety of abnormal thinking and behavioral changes have been reported to occur in association with the use of sedative/hypnotics, including zolpidem. Some of these changes included decreased inhibition (e.g., aggressiveness and extroversion that seemed out of character), bizarre behavior, agitation and depersonalization. Visual and auditory hallucinations have been reported. Amnesia and other neuropsychiatric symptoms may occur unpredictably. Some adverse effects of zolpidem appear to be dose-related; use of the lowest effective dose may mitigate these effects. Because intentional overdose is more common in people with pre-existing depression, zolpidem should be prescribed in the smallest quantity consistent with good patient management to reduce the risk of overdose.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class A, Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Zolpidem dosage adjustments are recommended in people with mild (Child-Pugh class A) or moderate (Child-Pugh class B) hepatic impairment due to reduced clearance and subsequently increased systemic exposure. Use is not recommended in severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure; increased zolpidem exposure in this population may also increase the risk of precipitating hepatic encephalopathy.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Geriatric adults are more sensitive to the effects of zolpidem, necessitating lower initial doses and close monitoring. Since zolpidem can cause drowsiness and impairs consciousness, there is an increased risk of falls and severe injuries in older adults. Specifically, doses exceeding 5 mg/day of immediate-release zolpidem or 6.25 mg/day of extended-release zolpidem may heighten the risk of falls and mental status changes. Discontinuing zolpidem, especially at higher doses, can lead to rebound insomnia; however, at recommended doses, no objective evidence of rebound insomnia has been observed. During studies, older adults reported impaired sleep on the first night after stopping zolpidem doses above 5 mg/day. The Beers Criteria state that zolpidem and other nonbenzodiazepine, benzodiazepine-receptor agonists (NBRAs), are potentially inappropriate for use in geriatric adults. NBRAs may cause adverse effects similar to benzodiazepines, including falls, fractures, and delirium, which are particularly concerning in older adults. Their use is associated with increased emergency department visits, hospitalizations, and motor vehicle crashes, with minimal benefit in improving sleep latency and duration. Avoid NBRA use in individuals with dementia or cognitive impairment, or those at high risk of delirium. Additionally, avoid NBRAs in geriatric adults with a history of falls or fractures unless no safer alternatives are available. If an NBRA must be prescribed, consider reducing other CNS-active medications and employing strategies to mitigate fall risk. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates the use of sedative/hypnotic agents for residents of long-term care facilities (LTCFs); when a drug is being used to induce sleep or treat a sleep disorder, the facility should attempt periodic tapering of the medication as stated in the OBRA guidelines or provide documentation of continued medical necessity.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    The risk of zolpidem use during pregnancy should be carefully weighed against the risk of untreated sleep disorders in the pregnant person. Available data from observational studies, birth registries, and case reports on the use of zolpidem during human pregnancy have not reported a clear association with the drug and major birth defects; however, hypnotic benzodiazepine receptor agonist (HBRA) use in early pregnancy may be associated with a risk for nonteratogenic effects. The HBRAs, including zolpidem, have been shown to cross the human placenta. In a study examining data from the Swedish Medical Birth Registry, the birth outcomes for 603 infants who were exposed to zolpidem in early pregnancy were compared to nonexposed infants. In this study, the use of zolpidem was not associated with an increased risk for congenital malformations. There were 4 cases of intestinal malformations reported in infants exposed to HBRAs; 2 of these cases were in infants exposed to zolpidem. The authors noted the presence of several potential confounders, such as exposure to other medications, smoking status, and maternal age, that may have contributed to these malformations. Published studies of other hypnotic benzodiazepine receptor agonists (HBRAs) like zolpidem do not indicate an increased risk of congenital malformations at typical doses. However, one study has reported neural tube defects in an infant following high-dose exposure to zolpidem in the first trimester of pregnancy. In a systematic review and meta-analysis developed to assess for risks associated with use of HBRAs in pregnancy, the authors noted that pregnancy exposure to HBRAs was not associated with an increased risk of congenital malformations but was associated with increased risks of preterm birth, low birth weight, and being small for gestational age compared to unexposed infants. Similarly, a population-based cohort study determined that HBRA use in early pregnancy may be associated with an increased risk of infants being born small for gestational age, even after controlling for numerous confounding variables. Additional studies are needed to determine the true risk and incidence of these effects.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use zolpidem with caution during breast-feeding. Zolpidem is excreted into breast milk, and there are reports of excess sedation in infants exposed to zolpidem through breast milk. Monitor the exposed infants for excess sedation, hypotonia, poor weight gain, and respiratory depression. To minimize infant drug exposure, it has been suggested that patients interrupt breast-feeding, and pump and discard breast milk during treatment and for 23 hours (about 5 half-lives) after zolpidem administration. Expert opinion is divided, with some experts recommending against zolpidem use while breast-feeding, while others suggest zolpidem could be used safely in older breast-fed infants that do not breast-feed overnight after the maternal dose. In one small study of breast-feeding people (n = 5) who received a single 20 mg dose of zolpidem on postpartum day 3 or 4, the mean milk to maternal plasma concentration ratio was 0.13 (range, 0.11 to 0.18) three hours after drug administration. The amount of zolpidem measured in the breast milk samples represented 0.004% to 0.019% of the maternal administered dose. No detectable zolpidem was measured in subsequent milk samples taken 13 and 16 hours after the dose. Breast-feeding was discontinued for 24 hours after drug administration; therefore, infant exposure was not assessed. The transfer of zaleplon, an alternative sedative, into breast milk has also been assessed; however, the effects of zaleplon exposure on the breast-feeding infant have not been formally evaluated.
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Monitor exposed neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero during the third trimester for signs and symptoms of respiratory depression and sedation, and manage accordingly. Withdrawal symptoms may also be possible during the postnatal period. A limited number of exposed neonatal cases of moderate to severe respiratory depression requiring aspiration, artificial ventilation, or intratracheal intubation have been reported. The majority of neonates recovered within hours to a few weeks after birth once treated. Neonatal flaccidity has also been reported in infants born to individuals who have received sedatives during pregnancy.

Expert opinion is divided, with some experts recommending against zolpidem use while breast-feeding, while others suggest zolpidem could be used safely in older breast-fed infants that do not breast-feed overnight after the maternal dose. In one small study of breast-feeding people (n = 5) who received a single 20 mg dose of zolpidem on postpartum day 3 or 4, the mean milk to maternal plasma concentration ratio was 0.13 (range, 0.11 to 0.18) three hours after drug administration. The amount of zolpidem measured in the breast milk samples represented 0.004% to 0.019% of the maternal administered dose. No detectable zolpidem was measured in subsequent milk samples taken 13 and 16 hours after the dose. Breast-feeding was discontinued for 24 hours after drug administration; therefore, infant exposure was not assessed. The transfer of zaleplon, an alternative sedative, into breast milk has also been assessed; however, the effects of zaleplon exposure on the breast-feeding infant have not been formally evaluated.

  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Most of the adverse effects associated with zolpidem therapy are dose-dependent and CNS-related. The most frequently reported are headache, daytime drowsiness, dizziness, and a 'drugged feeling', although in one study of elderly volunteers, headache was reported by equal numbers of subjects taking placebo as those taking 20 mg doses of zolpidem. According to the manufacturer the following CNS side effects were reported in an incidence >= 1% over placebo with short-term (up to 10 days) treatment at dosages up to 10 mg/day: headache (1%), dizziness (1%), and daytime drowsiness (2%). During long-term (roughly 1 month) treatment with doses up to 10 mg/day, the most common adverse events occurring more commonly than with placebo were dizziness (5% vs. 1%) and daytime drowsiness (8% vs. 5%) or 'drugged feelings' (3% vs. 0%). Confusion has been reported in roughly 1.2% of patients with approximately 75% of those cases occurring in the elderly >= 70 years of age, particularly in those elderly patients receiving > 10 mg/day. One analysis of patients receiving zolpidem in clinical trials showed that 93% of those who experienced a fall were at least 70 years of age and 82% of those patients were receiving more than 10 mg/day. During a controlled 8-week study for the treatment of insomnia in 201 children aged 6—17 years with attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), side effects which occurred more frequently with zolpidem than placebo included dizziness (23.5% vs. 1.5%) and headache (12.5% vs. 9.2%).
 
After zolpidem dosages of <= 10 mg, decreases in next morning recall may occasionally occur of information presented during the times of peak medication effect (i.e., 90 minutes post-dose). Roughly 1% of patients may experience amnesia of this type at normally prescribed dosages. Anterograde amnesia is more likely to occur following dosages exceeding 10 mg/day. No patients receiving placebo in clinical trials reported amnesia. Somnambulism (sleep walking) has been reported rarely in patients taking zolpidem. Sleep walking in conjunction with binge eating and amnesia of the eating event has also been reported anecdotally; clinical reports are unpublished and unsubstantiated. Other sleep-related behaviors may occur such as sleep-driving, a state of driving after ingestion of a sedative-hypnotic while not fully awake and having no memory of the event. Patients should be informed of the risks prior to receiving any medication from the sedative-hypnotic class.
 
Symptoms of CNS stimulation including euphoria, agitation, anxiety, nightmares, and hyperactivity are infrequent (<=1%) paradoxical effects that can occur with zolpidem therapy. If there is evidence of CNS stimulation, zolpidem should be discontinued. Other thought or behavioral changes include decreased inhibition (e.g., aggressiveness or extroversion), bizarre behavior (unspecified), depersonalization, and auditory/visual hallucinations. Visual hallucinations may also occur when zolpidem is prescribed concurrently with certain antidepressant-type medications. Sporadic case reports of such interactions are reported in the literature, with durations of hallucinations lasting from 30 minutes to 7 hours after zolpidem administration. Dosage reduction should be considered if hallucinations occur, or zolpidem therapy may need to be discontinued. Hallucinations were reported during a controlled 8-week study of 201 children aged 6—17 years with insomnia and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), occurring in 7.4% of those receiving zolpidem versus none in the placebo group. During clinical trials in adults, hallucinations were reported in less than 1% of patients.
 
Other adverse events not previously listed that occurred in at least 1% of zolpidem-treated patients in long-term trials and at an incidence higher than placebo are included in the following list with reference vs. placebo. Autonomic nervous system: Xerostomia (3% vs. 1%). Body as a whole: Allergy, not specified (4% vs. 1%), back pain (3% vs. 2%), flu-like symptoms/malaise (2% vs. 0%), chest pain (unspecified) (1% vs. 0%). Cardiovascular: palpitations (2% vs. 0%). CNS: abnormal dreams (1% vs. 0%), lethargy (3% vs. 1%), depression (2% vs. 1%), lightheadedness (2% vs. 1%), and sleep disorder (1% vs. 0%). Respiratory system: sinusitis (4% vs. 2%), pharyngitis (3% vs. 1%). Skin: rash (unspecified) (2% vs. 1%).
 
During clinical trials with zolpidem, oxygen desaturation occurred, mostly in patients with pre-existing pulmonary disease. During postmarketing use, respiratory insufficiency (e.g., respiratory depression) has been reported, primarily in patients with pre-existing respiratory impairment. Zolpidem should be used with caution in patients with pre-existing respiratory depression, such as severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or sleep apnea, to avoid the risk of depressing ventilatory function.
 
Anaphylactoid reactions (e.g., angioedema) may occur with sedative-hypnotics, such as zolpidem, and may become evident as early as the initial dose. Airway obstruction resulting from angioedema of the tongue, glottis, or larynx may be fatal. Other symptoms suggestive of anaphylaxis may include dyspnea, throat closing, and nausea/vomiting. Patients should be instructed on the appropriate action in the event of an allergic reaction. Treatment with zolpidem should not be re-initiated in patients who experience angioedema.
 
Gastrointestinal adverse events resulting from zolpidem use appear to be dose related. Most commonly observed were constipation (2% vs. 1%) and diarrhea (1% zolpidem vs. 0%placebo) with dosages up to 10 mg/day PO. Abdominal pain, anorexia, dyspepsia, and nausea/vomiting have been reported, but the incidence is similar to that of placebo in clinical trials.
 
Zolpidem was studied in 3660 patients during pre-marketing clinical trials. During clinical trials, several events were recorded by investigators using terminology of their own choosing, and without regard to causality. Such events were coded as those that occurred frequently (1/100 patients), infrequently (1/1000 patients) or rarely (< 1/1000 patients). Frequent events, without regard to causality, included the following, listed by organ system: Body as a whole: arthralgia, asthenia, myalgia. CNS and peripheral nervous system: ataxia, confusion, euphoria, insomnia, vertigo. Gastrointestinal: hiccups. Respiratory: upper respiratory infection. Special senses: diplopia, visual impairment (unspecified). Urogenital: urinary tract infection. Infrequent and rare events are described in the manufacturer's product labeling.
 
Sedatives and hypnotics have been implicated in producing signs of withdrawal following abrupt discontinuation of treatment. Reliable determinations regarding possible drug dependency on zolpidem have not been undertaken. There is some evidence that symptoms of fatigue, nausea/vomiting, flushing, lightheadedness, inconsolable crying, stomach cramps, panic attack, nervousness, and abdominal discomfort that may constitute a withdrawal syndrome. Drug dependency may be related to dosage and duration of treatment. A dosage-tapering schedule may be adopted if drug dependency is suspected. Withdrawal of some hypnotics can precipitate a rebound insomnia and elderly patients may be more susceptible to sleep impairment following discontinuation of therapy. Rebound insomnia occurs rarely following discontinuation of zolpidem after continuous administration for <= 28 days.
 
In an open-label study of the effects of chronic daily administration of sublingual zolpidem (n = 60), one patient developed transient sublingual erythema, and one patient developed transient paresthesias of the tongue. The effects of chronic administration of zolpidem lingual spray have not been evaluated; however, single doses of this formulation showed no sign of oral irritation in studies of healthy volunteers.
 
Post-marketing experience with overdose of zolpidem, either alone or in combination with other CNS depressants, has included reports of impaired consciousness, coma, cardiovascular and/or respiratory compromise, and fatalities.

Description
  Text
  Zolpidem is a non-benzodiazepine sedative-hypnotic used for the treatment of insomnia in adults. Zolpidem is available in various orally administered formulations (e.g., Ambien, Ambien CR) for difficulty with sleep initiation; one formulation (Intermezzo) was granted FDA approval for difficulty falling back to sleep following middle-of-the-night awakenings. Zolpidem products for sleep initiation have a rapid onset of action and should be taken immediately before retiring. When used for middle-of-the-night awakenings, there should be at least 4 hours of bedtime remaining before the planned time of waking. Zolpidem can cause next-day psychomotor impairment. Unlike benzodiazepines, there is a lack of myorelaxant, anxiolytic, and anticonvulsant effects with zolpidem at therapeutic doses. Some sedative-hypnotics, including zolpidem, have been associated with complex sleep-related behaviors (e.g., making phone calls, engaging in sexual activity, eating, or driving while not fully awake). All sedative-hypnotics have the potential to cause sleep-related behaviors; therefore, patients should be informed of the risks prior to receiving any medication from this class. The Veterans Affairs/Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guidelines for management of chronic insomnia suggest zolpidem may be considered for short-term use in adults who are candidates for pharmacotherapy, but also indicate patients must be specifically counseled regarding the risk of complex sleep-related behaviors. While the drug has been studied in pediatric patients (6 to 17 years of age), zolpidem was not shown effective at treating pediatric insomnia and adverse events were frequent. The FDA approved zolpidem in December 1992.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Zolpidem is a gamma-aminobutyric acid-A (GABA-A) receptor positive modulator thought to exert its therapeutic effect in the treatment of insomnia through binding at the omega-1 subunit of the GABA-A receptor site. Binding at the omega-1 subunit increases the frequency of chloride channel opening, which results in the inhibition of neuronal excitation. Zolpidem is chemically unrelated to benzodiazepines, which non-selectively interact with omega-receptor subtypes at the GABA-A receptor site. Zolpidem binds to the omega-1 subunit with greater affinity than the omega-2 or omega-3 subunits and has no appreciable binding to omega-5, which may explain its lack of myorelaxant, anxiolytic, and anticonvulsant effects compared to benzodiazepines. Sleep studies in humans indicate that zolpidem normally preserves deep sleep (stages 3 and 4) and that changes in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep are minor and inconsistent. Anterograde amnesia appears to be more likely with dosages higher than 10 mg. In one study of zolpidem 10 mg or 20 mg, a significant decrease in next morning recall of information was observed during the times of peak medication effect (i.e., 90 minutes post-dose). Signs and symptoms of withdrawal may occur with rapid dose reduction or abrupt discontinuation. Zolpidem has no appreciable binding affinity for dopaminergic (D2), serotonergic (5-HT2), adrenergic, histaminic, or muscarinic receptors.

    Oral Route
    Description

Peak medication effects of the immediate-release tablet occur within 90 minutes of a single oral dosage. In single-dose studies in subjects administered 5 mg and 10 mg zolpidem, the mean peak concentrations (Cmax) were 59 (range: 29 to 113) and 121 (range: 58 to 272) ng/mL, respectively, occurring at a mean time (Tmax) of 1.6 hours for both strengths. The presence of food reduces the amount of absorption and increases the time taken to achieve maximum concentration, delaying sleep onset. Therefore, all formulations of zolpidem should be taken on an empty stomach versus after a meal.
Immediate-release capsules: Following a single dose administration of 7.5 mg zolpidem capsules and 10 mg zolpidem tablets to healthy volunteers in fasting conditions, the Cmax and AUC of zolpidem capsules and tablets were similar. Mean zolpidem Cmax and AUC decreased by 39% and 8%, respectively, with a high-fat, high-calorie meal compared to fasted conditions following administration of zolpidem tartrate capsules in healthy volunteers. The median time to reach zolpidem Tmax was prolonged from 1 hour to 2.8 hours post dose in the presence of food. Therefore, zolpidem capsules should not be taken with or immediately after a meal.
Extended-release (biphasic) tablets (e.g., Ambien CR): Extended-release zolpidem exhibits biphasic absorption characteristics through a coated 2-layer tablet with rapid initial absorption from the GI tract through the first tablet layer similar to zolpidem immediate-release, followed by extended plasma concentrations beyond 3 hours after administration by release of drug through the second layer. Similar to the immediate-release dosage form, zolpidem extended-release should be taken on an empty stomach for faster absorption and sleep-onset. Data show that the risk for next-morning impairment is higher for the extended-release than the immediate-release formulation.
 
Sublingual Route
Sublingual tablets (e.g., Edluar): Zolpidem sublingual tablets are bioequivalent to the immediate-release tablets with regard to Cmax and AUC. The mean peak plasma concentration occurs at a median time of 82 minutes (range: 30 to 180 minutes) after administration. When the drug is administered within 20 minutes after a high fat meal, the mean AUC and Cmax are decreased by 20% and 31%, respectively, compared to administration while fasting. The median Tmax is prolonged by 28%. Therefore, for faster sleep onset, zolpidem sublingual tablets should not be taken with or immediately after a meal.
Sublingual tablets for middle-of-the-night awakenings (e.g., Intermezzo): These sublingual tablets disintegrate in the sublingual cavity after administration. The mean Tmax is about 35 to 75 minutes. The average Cmax and AUC are higher in women than men. Administration with food decreases the overall Cmax and AUC by 42% and 19% respectively, and increases the Tmax to nearly 3 hours. Therefore, administration with or immediately after a meal is not recommended.

    Hepatic Impairment
    Description
    The pharmacokinetic parameters of zolpidem are affected by hepatic function. In patients with chronic cirrhosis, the immediate-release oral zolpidem Cmax and AUC were 2 and 5 times higher, respectively, than in normal controls and the half-life was also significantly increased to an average of 9.9 hours (range 4.1 to 25.8 hours), necessitating dosage reductions in the hepatically impaired. Dosage adjustments are also recommended for other zolpidem dosage forms depending on the degree of hepatic impairment.
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    The pharmacokinetic parameters of zolpidem are not appreciably altered in patients with renal impairment. Zolpidem is not removed by hemodialysis; however, the drug does not appear to accumulate in patients with end-stage renal disease with short-term durations (i.e., less than 2 to 3 weeks) of administration.
    Name
    Geriatric
    Description
    The pharmacokinetic parameters of zolpidem (both immediate- and extended-release) are altered with advanced age. The mean half-life of both products increased to roughly 3 hours in elderly patients. In comparative trials between younger and older adult subjects receiving zolpidem immediate-release tablets, significant elevations in both Cmax and AUC have been noted. In a pharmacokinetic study of 1.75 mg and 3.5 mg doses of sublingual zolpidem (Intermezzo), the plasma Cmax and AUC following the 3.5 mg dose were 34% and 30% higher, respectively, in elderly subjects compared to non-elderly subjects. The Cmax and AUC following the 1.75 mg dose in elderly subjects were higher than in non-elderly subjects receiving the same dose but lower than the Cmax and AUC in non-elderly subjects receiving the 3.5 mg dose. Zolpidem dosage reductions are recommended for the various dosage forms in the geriatric adult.
    Name
    Gender Differences
    Description
    Zolpidem blood concentrations of greater than 50 ng/mL appear to be capable of impairing driving to a level that increases the risk of a motor vehicle accident. Because adult females eliminate zolpidem at a slower rate than adult males, lower initial dosages of zolpidem are recommended in adult females. In simulation and laboratory studies, blood levels more than 50 ng/mL were observed in approximately 15% of women and 3% of men 8 hours after a 10 mg dose of immediate-release zolpidem. Three measurements in women and one in men were 90 ng/mL or higher. Data show that the risk for next-morning impairment is highest for both male and female patients taking the extended-release formulation (i.e., Ambien CR). In pharmacokinetic trials assessing the 12.5 mg dose of extended-release zolpidem, about 33% of women and 25% of men had blood levels more than 50 ng/mL approximately 8 hours after a dose. About 5% of patients had blood levels of 100 ng/mL or higher. In studies of the 6.25 mg extended-release dose, about 15% of adult women and 5% of adult men had blood levels more than 50 ng/mL compared to about 10% of elderly men and women. The Cmax and AUC of sublingually administered zolpidem (Intermezzo) were about 45% higher in adult females compared to males receiving the same dose. In addition, women cleared Intermezzo from the body at a lower rate than men.
  Text
  Zolpidem is administered orally as an immediate-release tablet, immediate-release capsule, controlled-release tablet, and sublingual tablet. It is about 92% bound to plasma protein. Hepatic metabolism through CYP isoenzymes produces inactive metabolites that are primarily excreted in the urine. Immediate-release zolpidem has not been shown to accumulate in young adults with administration up to 2 weeks. The mean elimination half-life of the immediate-release tablets is 2.6 hours (range 1.4 to 4.5 hours) for the 5-mg dose and 2.5 hours (ran: 1.4 to 3.8 hours) for the 10-mg tablets in patients with normal hepatic and renal function. Mean elimination half-life of the 7.5-mg dose of zolpidem immediate-release capsules is 3.7 hours (range: 1.2 to 8.3 hours) under fasting conditions. The half-life and plasma protein binding are similar between the extended-release and immediate-release tablet formulations. The mean elimination half-life of the Edluar sublingual tablet is 2.85 hours (range: 1.57 to 6.73 hours) and 2.65 hours (range: 1.75 to 3.77 hours) after a single dose of 5 or 10 mg, respectively, in healthy adult volunteers. The elimination half-life of a single 3.5 mg dose of the Intermezzo sublingual tablet for middle-of-the-night awakenings is about 2.5 hours (range: 1.4 to 3.6 hours).
Blood concentrations of zolpidem above 50 ng/mL appear to be capable of impairing driving to a level that increases the risk of a motor vehicle accident. Data show that the risk for next-morning impairment may be higher for the extended-release than the immediate-release formulation.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP450) isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, CYP2C19
Based on in vitro data, approximately 61% of zolpidem is metabolized by CYP3A4, with minor metabolism pathways by CYP2C9 (22%), CYP1A2 (14%), CYP2D6 (less than 3%), and CYP2C19 (less than 3%). Consider a lower dose of zolpidem when a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor and zolpidem are given together. Use of a potent CYP3A4 inducer in combination with zolpidem may decrease the efficacy of zolpidem and is not recommended.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Immediate-release tablets (e.g., Ambien):
-mild to moderate hepatic impairment: 5 mg/night PO if needed.
-severe hepatic impairment: Avoid use as it may contribute to encephalopathy.
 
Immediate-release capsules:
-mild to moderate hepatic impairment: Avoid use, as dose adjustments are not possible with available strengths.
-severe hepatic impairment: Avoid use as it may contribute to hepatic encephalopathy.
 
Extended-release tablets (e.g., Ambien CR):
-mild to moderate hepatic impairment: 6.25 mg/night PO if needed.
-severe hepatic impairment: Avoid use as it may contribute to encephalopathy.
 
Sublingual tablets (e.g., Edluar):
-mild to moderate hepatic impairment: 5 mg/night sublingual if needed.
-severe hepatic impairment: Avoid use as it may contribute to encephalopathy.
 
Sublingual tablets for middle-of-the night awakening (e.g., Intermezzo):
-mild to moderate hepatic impairment: 1.75 mg sublingual once per night as needed.
-severe hepatic impairment: Avoid use as it may contribute to encephalopathy.
  Renal Impairment
  It appears that no dosage adjustments are needed. Evidence to date does not indicate a need to use lower doses of zolpidem; however, as a general precaution patients with renal impairment should be closely monitored.
 
Intermittent hemodialysis
Zolpidem is not removed by hemodialysis. Based on limited study in end-stage renal disease patients undergoing hemodialysis 3 times each week, it appears that no dosage accumulation occurs after 14 or 21 days of daily zolpidem administration. However, close clinical monitoring is recommended as a general precaution.
