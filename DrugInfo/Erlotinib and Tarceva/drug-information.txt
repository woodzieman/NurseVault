Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Interstitial pulmonary disease, consisting of interstitial pneumonia, pneumonitis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and bronchiolitis obliterans has been associated with the use of erlotinib with an overall incidence of 0.6%. Symptoms start from 5 days to more than 9 months (median 47 days) after initiating erlotinib therapy. Most of the cases are associated with confounding or contributing factors such as concomitant/prior chemotherapy, prior radiotherapy, pre-existing parenchymal lung disease, metastatic lung disease, or pulmonary infections. Patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis whose condition worsens while receiving erlotinib have been observed to have an increased mortality compared to those without idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. In the event of acute onset of new or progressive, unexplained pulmonary symptoms/respiratory insufficiency such as dyspnea, cough, and fever, erlotinib therapy should be interrupted pending diagnostic evaluation. If interstitial lung disease is diagnosed, erlotinib should be discontinued and appropriate treatment instituted as necessary. Erlotinib has also been shown to increase the effects of radiation therapy.
 
Erlotinib exposure may be increased in patients with hepatic disease; erlotinib is eliminated by hepatic metabolism and biliary excretion. In patients with liver metastases and moderately to severely elevated liver enzymes, erlotinib pharmacokinetic values were similar to those of values from individuals without liver abnormalities. Erlotinib has been associated with elevated hepatic enzymes; therefore, periodic liver function (transaminases, bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase) testing is recommended. In addition, cases of hepatic failure and hepatorenal syndrome have been reported in patients treated with erlotinib monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy; some have been fatal. In a pharmacokinetic study of erlotinib in patients with moderate hepatic impairment associated with significant liver tumor burden, 10 out of 15 patients died on treatment or within 30 days of receiving their last dose of erlotinib. Six out of the 10 patients who died had baseline total bilirubin > 3 x ULN. Patients with hepatic impairment, or who are receiving concomitant hepatotoxic medications, should be closely monitored while on erlotinib. Periodic liver function testing (transaminases, bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase) is recommended; consider dose reductions and/or interruption in the setting of worsening liver function; interrupt erlotinib dosing if severe changes in liver function occur (total bilirubin > 3 x ULN and/or transaminases > 5 x ULN in the setting of normal pretreatment liver function; doubling of total bilirubin and/or tripling of transaminases in the setting of abnormal pretreatment liver function; see Dosage).
 
Erlotinib may cause symptoms consistent with ocular disease. Patients who develop an onset of new eye symptoms such as ocular pain should be evaluated and managed appropriately. Interrupt or discontinue erlotinib if patients present with acute or worsening ocular pain. Corneal perforation or ulceration have been reported in patients receiving erlotinib. Ocular disorders such as abnormal eyelash growth, keratoconjunctivitis, and keratitis are known risk factors for corneal perforation and ulceration.
 
Cases of GI perforation, including some fatal cases, have been reported with the use of erlotinib. Patients with a history of peptic ulcer disease or diverticulitis may be at an increased risk for development of GI perforation while receiving erlotinib. Use erlotinib with caution in these patients.
 
Erlotinib is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category D and may cause harm to an unborn child if administered to a woman who is pregnant. There are no adequate studies in pregnant women using erlotinib. If erlotinib is used during pregnancy or if a patient becomes pregnant while receiving this drug, she should be apprised of the potential harm to the fetus or the potential for the loss of the pregnancy. Females of child-bearing potential should be counseled regarding appropriate contraceptive options. Adequate contraception methods should be used during therapy, and for at least two weeks after completing therapy.
 
It is not known if erlotinib is excreted in human breast milk. Because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in the nursing infant, women should be advised against breast-feeding while receiving erlotinib therapy.
 
The safe and effective use of erlotinib in children have not been established.
 
Advise cigarette smokers to stop smoking while taking erlotinib; tobacco smoking reduces the effectiveness of this drug. Tobacco smoking has been shown to increase the plasma clearance of erlotinib by about 24% and to decrease erlotinib plasma trough concentrations by about 2-fold in current smokers as compared with values from former or never smokers. Further, erlotinib systemic exposure from time zero to infinity is about one-third the value obtained from either former smokers or never smokers. Sudden smoking cessation may result in a reduced clearance of erlotinib despite the initiation of nicotine replacement products, as the effect of tobacco on hepatic microsomal enzymes is not related to the nicotine component (see Drug Interactions). Determine a patient's smoking status before erlotinib initiation. Inform patients of the importance of communicating a change in their smoking status to their health care professional.
 
Administration of erlotinib to a patient with dehydration or hepatic impairment may lead to renal failure (see Adverse Reactions), especially in patients with preexisting renal disease including renal impairment or renal failure, medical conditions or medications that may lead to renal disease, or other predisposing conditions including advanced age (elderly). Stop erlotinib receipt and intensively rehydrate a patient with dehydration. Periodically monitor renal function and serum electrolytes in patients at risk of dehydration.

    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Erlotinib can cause fetal harm if used during pregnancy, based on its mechanism of action and data from animal studies. In rabbits, embryo-fetal lethality and abortion occurred when erlotinib was given during organogenesis at doses resulting in exposures approximately 3 times those achieved in humans at the recommended daily dose based on AUC. There was no increase in embryo-fetal lethality or abortion in rabbits or rats with erlotinib administration during organogenesis at exposures approximately equal to the human exposure at the recommended daily dose. No teratogenic effects were observed with erlotinib administration during organogenesis in rabbits at doses up to 3 times the human recommended dose based on plasma drug concentration and in rats up to 0.7 times the human recommended dose based on body surface area. Advise pregnant individuals of the potential fetal risk. The American Society of Clinical Oncology provides additional information on the use of certain medications for the management of cancer during pregnancy.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with erlotinib. Erlotinib can cause fetal harm. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception while taking erlotinib and for 1 month after the last dose.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding while taking erlotinib and for 2 weeks after the last dose. Erlotinib has the potential to cause serious side effects in the breastfed child, including interstitial lung disease, hepatotoxicity, bullous and exfoliative skin disorders, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia with thrombocytopenia, ocular disorders, and diarrhea. There are no data on the presence of erlotinib in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production.

Cimetidine: (Major) Avoid coadministration of erlotinib with cimetidine if possible due to altered plasma concentrations of erlotinib. If concomitant use is unavoidable, separate dosing is required, as the solubility of erlotinib is pH dependent, decreasing as the pH increases. Erlotinib must be taken 10 hours after the last dose of cimetidine and at least 2 hours before the next dose. Additionally, monitor for erlotinib-related adverse reactions; a dose reduction may be necessary for severe reactions. Coadministration of erlotinib with medications that increase the pH of the upper gastrointestinal tract may decrease the absorption of erlotinib. Erlotinib exposure was decreased by 33% and the Cmax by 54% when erlotinib was administered 2 hours after a single dose of an H2-antagonist. When administered at least 10 hours after an evening dose of an H2-antagonist and 2 hours before the morning dose, erlotinib exposure was decreased by 15% and Cmax by 17%. Increasing the dose of erlotinib is not likely to compensate for the loss of exposure. Erlotinib is also primarily metabolized by CYP3A4 and to a lesser extent by CYP1A2. Cimetidine is a weak CYP3A4 and CYP1A2 inhibitor.

Additionally, vaccines may be less effective if administered during a period of altered immunocompetence.

  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Of 259 patients with pancreatic cancer who received erlotinib 100 mg with gemcitabine, the most common adverse reactions were fatigue, rash, nausea, anorexia, and diarrhea. Patients treated with erlotinib and gemcitabine experienced rash (unspecified) (69%), diarrhea (48%), weight loss (39%), and stomatitis/oral ulceration (22%) more commonly than those treated with gemcitabine alone. Similar adverse reactions to erlotinib (150 mg) during clinical trials for NSCLC were noted in 485 patients: rash (unspecified) (75%), diarrhea (54%), anorexia (52%), fatigue (52%), dyspnea (41%), cough (33%), nausea/vomiting (33%/23%), and infection (24%). Generally, adverse events occur within the first month of treatment, are transient, and usually are mild-to-moderate in severity. Repeated doses of erlotinib 200 mg twice daily resulted in a higher incidence of these reactions. Grade 3/4 rash and diarrhea occurred in 9% and 6% of 485 patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) who received 150 mg daily, respectively. The median time to onset of rash was 8 days, and the median time to onset of diarrhea was 12 days. Treatment with loperamide may help control erlotinib-induced diarrhea. Whereas, rash often improves with continued treatment, will usually diminish or even resolve with temporary cessation of therapy, and may respond to a variety of topical therapies, including erythromycin ointment, as well as oral tetracycline antibiotics. The skin rash may be an indication of the drug's antitumor activity. In a phase II trial evaluating the use of erlotinib 150 mg/day orally as second line in patients with HER1/EGFR-expressing NSCLC, the development of a rash was an indicator for tumor response and improved survival. Development of bullous, blistering or exfoliative skin conditions have occurred. Cases suggestive of Stevens-Johnson syndrome and/or toxic epidermal necrolysis, which in some cases were fatal, have been reported. Interrupt or discontinue treatment if a patient develops a bullous, blistering, or exfoliative skin condition. In clinical trials, rash and diarrhea each resulted in study discontinuation in 1% of erlotinib-treated patients. Patients with poorly controlled diarrhea or skin adverse reactions may be successfully managed with a brief interruption in therapy or a dosage reduction. When dose reduction is necessary, the erlotinib dose should be reduced in 50 mg decrements. Other adverse events reported in patients who received erlotinib 150 mg as monotherapy during clinical trials included stomatitis/oral ulceration (17%), pruritus (13%), dry skin (xerosis) (12%), and abdominal pain (11%). In post-marketing use of erlotinib, alopecia, hirsutism, paronychia, nail discoloration, and brittle or loose nails have all been reported.
 
Ocular adverse reactions associated with erlotinib in clinical trials include ocular irritation, abnormal eyelash growth (including hypertrichosis, in-growing eyelashes, and excessive thickening), conjunctivitis (12%), and keratoconjunctivitis sicca (12%). In addition, there have been reports of NCI CTC grade 3 conjunctivitis and keratitis. Corneal ulcerations may also occur. Interrupt or discontinue erlotinib in patients who present with acute or worsening eye disorders such as ocular pain. After symptoms and eye changes have resolved, a decision should be made regarding restarting erlotinib therapy.
 
Pulmonary toxicity consisting of interstitial lung disease-like events, including fatalities, has been reported to occur among patients in clinical trials with an overall approximate incidence of 0.7%. Symptoms started from 5 days to more than 9 months (median 39 days) after initiating erlotinib therapy. Most of the cases from the lung cancer trials were associated with confounding or contributing factors such as concomitant/prior chemotherapy, prior radiotherapy, pre-existing parenchymal lung disease, metastatic lung disease, or pulmonary infections. Patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis whose condition worsens while receiving erlotinib have been observed to have an increased mortality compared to those without idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. In the event of acute onset of new or progressive, unexplained pulmonary symptoms such as dyspnea, cough, and fever, erlotinib therapy should be interrupted pending diagnostic evaluation. Reported diagnoses in patients suspected of having interstitial lung disease-like events included pneumonitis, radiation pneumonitis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, interstitial pneumonia, interstitial lung disease, bronchiolitis obliterans, pulmonary fibrosis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and pulmonary infiltration. If interstitial lung disease is diagnosed, erlotinib should be discontinued and appropriate treatment instituted as necessary.
 
Hepatic failure and hepatorenal syndrome have been reported in patients treated with erlotinib monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy. Some cases have been fatal, and confounding factors for severe hepatic dysfunction include pre-existing liver dysfunction from cirrhosis, viral hepatitis, hepatocellular carcinoma, hepatic metastases, or concomitant treatment with potentially hepatotoxic drugs. In a pharmacokinetic study of erlotinib in patients with moderate hepatic impairment associated with significant liver tumor burden, 10 out of 15 patients died on treatment or within 30 days of receiving their last dose of erlotinib. One patient died from hepatorenal syndrome, 1 patient died from rapidly progressing hepatic failure, and the remaining 8 died from progressive disease. Six out of the 10 patients who died had baseline total bilirubin > 3 x ULN. Hepatic failure was noted in patients enrolled in clinical trials and during post-marketing receipt of the drug, but an estimation of the frequency of the event is unavailable. Elevated hepatic enzymes including hyperbilirubinemia have been observed in patients receiving erlotinib during clinical trials. Elevations were mainly transient or associated with liver metastasis; asymptomatic increases in liver transaminases have been noted. Severe elevated hepatic enzymes (> 5 times the upper limit of normal) were not reported among patients who received erlotinib for NSCLC. Among 259 patients who received erlotinib and gemcitabine for pancreatic cancer, 10% had a grade 3 hyperbilirubinemia, and 13% had grade 3 ALT elevation (5—20 times the upper limit of normal). The number of patients with hyperbilirubinemia among patients who either received gemcitabine monotherapy or received gemcitabine and erlotinib was similar, but the number of patients with ALT elevations was greater among patients who received gemcitabine and erlotinib. Grade 3 ALT elevations occurred in 34 of 259 patients with combination therapy as compared with 23 of 256 patients with gemcitabine monotherapy. Patients with hepatic impairment, or who are receiving concomitant hepatotoxic medications, should be closely monitored while on erlotinib. Periodic liver function testing (transaminases, bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase) is recommended; consider dose reductions and/or interruption in the setting of worsening liver function; interrupt erlotinib dosing if severe changes in liver function occur (total bilirubin > 3 x ULN and/or transaminases > 5 x ULN in the setting of normal pretreatment liver function; doubling of total bilirubin and/or tripling of transaminases in the setting of abnormal pretreatment liver function; see Dosage).
 
Bleeding events including epistaxis and infrequent cases of GI bleeding (e.g., peptic ulcer bleeding, gastritis, gastroduodenal ulcers, hematemesis, hematochezia, melena, and hemorrhage from possible colitis) have been reported in patients who received erlotinib. Some patients took erlotinib with either warfarin or NSAIDs (see Interactions). GI perforation, which in some cases was fatal, has been reported in patients receiving erlotinib. Patients with a history of peptic ulceration or diverticular disease are at an increased risk, as are patients receiving concomitant anti-angiogenic agents, corticosteroids, NSAIDs, or taxane-based chemotherapy. Permanently discontinue erlotinib in patients who develop GI perforation. In the pancreatic carcinoma trial, 1 of 259 patients who got both erlotinib and gemcitabine had a fatal hemorrhagic stroke, 6 had a myocardial infarction or myocardial ischemia (angina), 6 had a cerebrovascular accident, 10 had deep venous thrombosis, and 2 had microangiopathic hemolytic anemia with thrombocytopenia. In contrast, the respective numbers for the 256 patients who received gemcitabine monotherapy were 0, 3, 0, 3 and 0. The overall incidence of grade 3 or 4 thrombotic events, including deep venous thrombosis, was similar in the two treatment arms: 11% for erlotinib plus gemcitabine and 9% for placebo plus gemcitabine.
 
Cases of acute renal failure (unspecified) or renal insufficiency, including fatalities, with or without hypokalemia have been reported. Some cases of renal failure were secondary to baseline hepatic impairment, while others were secondary to severe dehydration due to diarrhea, vomiting, and/or anorexia while others were confounded by concurrent chemotherapy use. Stop erlotinib receipt and intensively rehydrate a patient with dehydration. Periodically monitor renal function and serum electrolytes in patients at risk of dehydration.

Description
  Text
  Erlotinib is an oral, selective epidermal growth factor receptor-tyrosine kinase inhibitor (EGFR-TKI) that is FDA-approved for the treatment of metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in patients with EGFR exon 19 deletions or exon 21 (L858R) substitution mutations for first-line treatment as monotherapy, as maintenance therapy after completion of platinum-based chemotherapy, and as second- or greater line therapy after progression on at least one prior chemotherapy regimen. It is also approved in combination with gemcitabine for the first-line treatment of locally advanced, unresectable, or metastatic pancreatic cancer. Dermatologic reactions including rash are common; patients should avoid sun exposure and consider the use of alcohol-free emollient cream and sunscreen during treatment. Erlotinib was originally approved by the FDA in November 2004.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Erlotinib is a synthetic quinazolinamine that reversibly inhibits the kinase activity of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), preventing autophosphorylation of tyrosine residues associated with the receptor and thereby inhibiting further downstream signaling. EGFR is expressed on cell surfaces of both normal and cancer cells. In some tumor cells, signaling through this receptor plays a role in tumor cell survival and proliferation irrespective of EGFR mutation status. Erlotinib has a higher binding affinity for EGFR exon 19 deletion or exon 21 (L858R) substitution mutations compared to its affinity for the wild-type receptor; inhibition of other tyrosine kinase receptors by erlotinib has not been fully characterized.

    Other
    Description
    Tobacco Smoking
In a single-dose pharmacokinetic trial in healthy volunteers, cigarette smoking increased erlotinib clearance and decreased the AUC by 64% (95% CI, 46% to 76%) compared with former smokers and never-smokers. In a trial of patients with NSCLC, steady-state trough plasma concentrations were approximately 2-fold lower in current smokers than in former or never-smokers; erlotinib plasma clearance was increased by 24%. There was a dose-proportional increase in erlotinib exposure when the dose was increased from 150 mg to 300 mg in current smokers with NSCLC in another study.
  Text
  Erlotinib is administered orally. It is 93% protein bound to plasma albumin and alpha-1-acid glycoprotein (AAG). The apparent volume of distribution (Vd) is 232 liters. The median elimination half-life of erlotinib when given as second- or third-line monotherapy is 36.2 hours; the time to reach steady state plasma concentration is 7 to 8 days. Erlotinib is metabolized primarily by CYP3A4 and to a lesser extent by CYP1A2 and the extrahepatic isoform CYP1A1 in vitro. Excretion is predominantly (83%) via the feces, with only 1% of a dose recovered as intact parent drug; renal elimination of erlotinib accounts for 8% of the administered dose (0.3% unchanged).
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes: CYP1A1, CYP1A2, CYP3A4
Erlotinib is predominantly metabolized by CYP3A4 and to a lesser extent by CYP1A2 and the extrahepatic isoform CYP1A1. Coadministration with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducers is expected to affect erlotinib exposure. Cigarette smoking (a moderate CYP1A2 inducer) also increases erlotinib clearance compared to former smokers or never-smokers.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Baseline Hepatic Impairment
No dosage adjustment is necessary.
 
Treatment-Related Hepatotoxicity
WITHOUT baseline hepatic impairment
Total bilirubin more than 3 times the upper limit of normal (ULN) or AST/ALT more than 5 times ULN: Hold erlotinib therapy. Resume erlotinib at a reduced dose (by 50 mg decrements) when liver function tests resolve to baseline or less than or equal to grade 1. Discontinue erlotinib if resolution or significant improvement does not occur within 3 weeks.
WITH baseline hepatic impairment or biliary obstruction
Total bilirubin 2 times baseline or AST/ALT 3 times baseline: Hold erlotinib therapy. Resume erlotinib at a reduced dose (by 50 mg decrements) when liver function tests resolve to baseline or less than or equal to grade 1. Discontinue erlotinib if resolution or significant improvement does not occur within 3 weeks.
  Renal Impairment
  Baseline Renal Impairment
No dosage adjustments are necessary.
 
Treatment-Induced Nephrotoxicity
Grade 3 or 4 renal impairment: Hold erlotinib therapy. When nephrotoxicity has resolved to baseline or less than or equal to grade 1, therapy may be resumed at a reduced dose (by 50 mg decrements). Alternatively, consider discontinuation of erlotinib.
