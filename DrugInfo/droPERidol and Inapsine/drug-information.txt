Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 2.5 mg IM/IV as an initial dose for most indications; dosage may be carefully titrated but should not exceed maximum recommendations per indication.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  NOTE: Due to the serious risk of proarrhythmic events, the use of droperidol is not recommended for any indication other than for the treatment of perioperative nausea and vomiting in patients for whom other treatments are ineffective or inappropriate.
 
Droperidol is contraindicated in patients with a hypersensitivity to droperidol or any of its inactive ingredients. Use droperidol with caution in patients with a history of phenothiazine hypersensitivity because of the drug's structural similarities to phenothiazines.
 
Droperidol is contraindicated in patients with known or suspected QT prolongation, including patients with congenital long QT syndrome. Cases of QT prolongation and/or torsade de pointes have been reported in patients receiving droperidol at doses at or below recommended doses. Some cases have occurred in patients with no known risk factors for QT prolongation, and some cases have been fatal. Droperidol should be administered with extreme caution to patients at risk for development of QT prolongation (e.g., alcoholism, cardiac arrhythmias including bradycardia, congestive heart failure, or diuretic therapy), or receiving other drugs that may prolong the QT interval (see Drug Interactions). Avoid electrolyte imbalance, especially hypokalemia or hypomagnesemia, during droperidol therapy. All patients should undergo a 12-lead ECG prior to droperidol administration to determine if a prolonged QT interval (i.e., QTc interval > 440 msec for males or 450 msec for females) is present. If QT prolongation is present, droperidol should not be given. In patients treated with droperidol, ECG monitoring should be performed prior to treatment and continued for 2—3 hours after completing droperidol to monitor for arrhythmias. Droperidol is not recommended in the treatment of alcohol withdrawal or in other clinical situations (e.g., agitation) where large high doses are likely to be required.
 
Use droperidol with caution in patients with cardiac disease because they may be unable to compensate for the drug's hypotensive effects. If hypotension occurs, the possibility of hypovolemia should be considered and managed appropriately. Fluids and other countermeasures to manage hypotension should be available. Repositioning the patient should be considered if operating conditions allow. When used with forms of conduction anesthesia that cause sympathetic blockade, such as epidural anesthesia or spinal anesthesia, droperidol is more likely to cause hypotension. It should be noted that in epidural and spinal anesthesia, tilting the patient into a head-down position may result in a higher than desired level of anesthesia, as well as impaired venous return to the heart. Care should be taken in moving and positioning patients because of the risk of orthostatic hypotension. If fluids and other measures do not correct hypotension, then administration of pressor agents, other than epinephrine (see Drug Interactions), should be considered. Droperidol should not be used during procedures designed to accurately ascertain the pulmonary artery pressure because droperidol can decrease pulmonary artery pressure.
 
Droperidol should be administered with extreme caution to patients being treated with MAOI therapy (see Drug Interactions).
 
Droperidol should be used with caution in patients with hepatic disease, renal impairment, or renal failure because higher droperidol levels are likely, and there is an increased risk of serious adverse reactions.
 
Use droperidol with caution in patients with diagnosed or suspected pheochromocytoma; severe hypertension and tachycardia have been observed after the administration of droperidol.
 
Droperidol should be used with caution in elderly and debilitated patients because they are at an increased risk of developing adverse reactions, including hypotension and cardiac arrhythmias. The initial dose of droperidol should be reduced in these patients and the effect of the initial dose should be considered in determining incremental doses. Titrate dose upward with caution.
 
Droperidol is classified as an FDA pregnancy risk category C drug. Droperidol is not teratogenic in animals. However, when administered intravenously at 4.4 times the upper human dose, droperidol has been shown to cause a slight increase in mortality of newborn rats. No adequate, well-controlled studies have been conducted in pregnant women. There are, however, several reports describing the use of droperidol alone or in combination with other products during pregnancy. Droperidol has been used as a sedative during pregnancy in several reports with no serious maternal or fetal adverse events. Other reports describe the use of droperidol in the treatment of hyperemesis gravidarum. In a study explaining the use of droperidol and diphenhydramine in 28 hospitalized women with hyperemesis gravidum, patients receiving droperidol had statistically significant shorter durations of hospitalization, fewer readmissions, and lower average daily nausea and vomiting scores compared to controls. Although limited evidence is available, data from animal and human reports suggest droperidol is an effective antiemetic for the treatment of severe nausea and vomiting of pregnancy and its use does not represent a significant risk to the fetus. There is insufficient data to support the use of droperidol during labor and obstetric delivery and its use in this manner is not recommended by the manufacturer.
 
It is not known whether droperidol is excreted in human breast milk; because of its low molecular weight (about 379), excretion into human milk is expected. Use caution when administering droperidol in women who are breast-feeding. The safety of droperidol in children < 2 years of age has not been established.
 
Use droperidol with caution in patients with a seizure disorder, a history of seizures, or who are taking anticonvulsants because butyrophenones are known to decrease the seizure threshold. When EEG is used for postoperative monitoring, it may be found the EEG pattern returns to normal slowly.
 
Droperidol can precipitate or exacerbate Parkinson's disease and thus, should be avoided in patients with Parkinson's disease. Droperidol is a dopamine D2 receptor antagonist (see Mechanism of Action). The dopamine blockade from droperidol may worsen preexisting Parkinson's disease.

    General Information
    Droperidol is contraindicated in people with baseline QT prolongation and congenital long QT syndrome. Avoid use in people who have conditions that may increase the risk of QT prolongation or torsade de pointes, including bradycardia, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, geriatric adults, females, structural abnormalities that interfere with electrical conduction (e.g., cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease), alcohol use disorder, or in those who have other additional risk factors for QT prolongation or torsade de pointes. The use of other medications that have been associated with QT prolongation or torsade de pointes may further increase risk. Droperidol should be used with extreme caution in any clinically significant cardiac disease. Patients should undergo a 12-lead ECG prior to administration of droperidol to determine if a prolonged QT interval (i.e., QTc greater than 440 msec for males or 450 msec for females) is present. For patients in whom the potential benefit of droperidol treatment is felt to outweigh the risks of potentially serious arrhythmias, ECG monitoring should be performed prior to treatment and continued for 2 to 3 hours after completing treatment to monitor for arrhythmias.
    Headline
    epidural anesthesia, spinal anesthesia
    General Information
    Droperidol use may cause hypotension. Vital signs and ECG should be monitored routinely. Certain forms of conduction anesthesia, such as spinal anesthesia and epidural anesthesia, can cause peripheral vasodilation and hypotension because of sympathetic blockade. Therefore, when droperidol is used to supplement these forms of anesthesia, the anesthetist should be familiar with the physiological alterations involved, and be prepared to manage them. If hypotension occurs, manage with appropriate parenteral fluid therapy, repositioning of the patient if appropriate, and other countermeasures. The administration of pressor agents other than epinephrine should be considered. Epinephrine may paradoxically decrease the blood pressure in patients treated with droperidol due to the alpha-adrenergic blocking action of droperidol. Since droperidol may decrease pulmonary arterial pressure, this fact should be considered by those who conduct diagnostic or surgical procedures where interpretation of pulmonary arterial pressure measurements might determine final management of the patient.

According to the revised 2001 labeling for droperidol, any drug known to have potential to prolong the QT interval should not be coadministered with droperidol.
Lofexidine: (Major) Avoid coadministration of lofexidine and droperidol due to the potential for additive QT prolongation and torsade de pointes (TdP). If coadministration cannot be avoided, use extreme caution and monitor ECG; initiate droperidol at a low dose and increase the dose as needed to achieve the desired effect. Additionally, monitor for excessive hypotension and sedation during coadministration as lofexidine can potentiate the effects of CNS depressants. Lofexidine prolongs the QT interval. In addition, there are postmarketing reports of TdP. Droperidol administration is associated with an established risk for QT prolongation and TdP.     General Information
    A potentially fatal symptom complex referred to as neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) has occurred in association with administration of dopamine antagonists, like droperidol. NMS is characterized by hyperpyrexia, severe extrapyramidal dysfunction, alterations in consciousness, altered mental status, and autonomic instability (tachycardia, low or high blood pressure, diaphoresis). Risk factors for recurrent NMS include a personal history of NMS, increasing age, and certain medical co-morbidities (e.g., electrolyte imbalances, dehydration). Droperidol should be immediately discontinued and appropriate supportive therapy initiated as soon as symptoms of NMS are discovered.

Description
  Text
  Droperidol is a butyrophenone derivative structurally similar to haloperidol. Droperidol has more potent antiemetic properties, while haloperidol is the more potent antipsychotic. Droperidol carries an approved indication for reducing the incidence of nausea and vomiting associated with surgical and diagnostic procedures. Due to the serious risk of proarrhythmic events, droperidol carries a black box warning describing the risk of QT prolongation and torsade de pointes and is not recommended for any indication other than the treatment of perioperative nausea and vomiting in patients for whom other treatments are ineffective or inappropriate. Nevertheless, it has also been used for nausea and vomiting induced by anesthetics or emetogenic antineoplastic medications such as cisplatin. Droperidol has also been used as an inducing agent and as an adjuvant to general anesthesia. Droperidol is used off-label for the management of severe agitation in adult patients in the emergency department both as monotherapy and in combination with midazolam. In clinical studies, the combination of droperidol with midazolam resulted in more rapid sedation and required fewer repeat doses than other antipsychotics or benzodiazepines. However, the benefits of treatment must be carefully weighed against the risks of QT prolongation and torsade de pointes.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Droperidol is a butyrophenone neuroleptic. Similar to haloperidol, droperidol antagonizes multiple receptor sites in the CNS including serotonin, GABA, norepinephrine, and especially, dopamine. There is evidence that butyrophenones antagonize dopamine-mediated neurotransmission at the synapse as well as block postsynaptic dopamine receptor sites. The antiemetic activity of droperidol is most likely due to blockade of dopamine receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone of the brain. In addition, droperidol has peripheral alpha-adrenergic antagonistic activity leading to vasodilation and reduction of the pressor effect of epinephrine. It can produce hypotension, decreased peripheral vascular resistance, and may decrease pulmonary arterial pressure, particularly if it is abnormally high. Droperidol may reduce the incidence of epinephrine-induced arrhythmias, but it has been associated with prolongation of the QTc interval and serious arrhythmias including torsade de pointes. Droperidol delays the recharging of potassium channels, thereby blocking the rapid component of the delayed rectifier potassium current, within minutes of a dose at the upper limit of the dosage range.
