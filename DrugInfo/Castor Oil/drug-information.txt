    Headline
    abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting
    General Information
    People should consult with their health care team before oral castor oil laxative use if they have abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting, or have had a sudden change in bowel habits that persists over a period of 2 weeks. During nonprescription (OTC) use, advise the individual to discontinue castor oil and consult the care team if the product fails to produce a bowel movement, if a laxative is used for more than 1 week, or if rectal bleeding occurs, as these could be signs of a serious condition.
    Headline
    elective induction of labor, labor, pregnancy
    General Information
    Castor oil should not be used during pregnancy as administration can induce uterine contractions which may lead to adverse consequences (e.g., uterine rupture, fetal distress) or prematurely induce labor. Oral, rectal, and topical use (e.g., castor oil packs) must be avoided. Castor oil should not be used for elective induction of labor, there are not enough data to show the effects of castor oil on ripening the cervix or inducing labor or compare its safety and efficacy to other methods of induction; published studies have included small numbers of pregnancies and have lacked proper methodological quality.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use castor oil with caution during breast-feeding. It is unknown if castor oil is distributed into breast milk. Use alternative, safer laxatives where systemic absorption is known and/or infant exposure is negligible. Medications that do not have reports of adverse infant effects during lactation with occasional use postpartum include psyllium, bisacodyl, docusate, senna concentrate or sennosides, and magnesium hydroxide.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  Adverse Reactions
    Headline
    abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, pelvic pain, pruritus ani, steatorrhea, vomiting
    General Information
    Castor oil causes gastrointestinal adverse effects including diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain (e.g., discomfort, cramps). Excessive GI irritation and purgation have, and may even be expected to, occur at therapeutic doses. Perianal irritation (pruritus ani), rectal bleeding (GI bleeding), steatorrhea, and, rarely, pelvic congestion (pelvic pain) have been reported. Prolonged use of stimulant laxatives is not recommended as it can result in a cathartic or laxative dependence also known as "cathartic colon"; loss of normal bowel function or motility, chronic constipation, and even structural damage to the myenteric plexus can be seen.

Description
  Text
  Castor oil is an oral stimulant laxative obtained from the seeds of Ricinus communis; during the extraction process, ricin, a toxic substance in the raw seeds, does not partition into the castor oil. Nonprescription castor oil products are marketed for the relief of occasional constipation and the drug has been used for bowel preparation prior to medical procedures. However, because of the intensity and severity of adverse reactions associated with use, other laxatives and bowel preparations are preferred. Because castor oil may be associated with uterine contractions, use should be avoided in pregnancy. There are reports of use of castor oil to induce labor at term in pregnancy. However, safety and efficacy for labor induction cannot be confirmed and use for this purpose is not recommended; studies published to date have included small numbers of women and are considered of poor methodological quality. Castor oil was initially marketed for use before 1962, which is when Congress passed the amended Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act requiring that the FDA establish both safety and efficacy for all subsequently approved drugs. Castor oil has not been formally evaluated for safety and efficacy by the FDA.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Castor oil is an oral stimulant laxative with severe purgative actions. It is converted to ricinoleic acid in the small intestine and this active moiety produces a laxative effect. Although the exact mechanism of action is unknown, it is thought to have a direct action on the intestinal mucosa, stimulate the myenteric plexus, and alter water and electrolyte secretion. Loose stools are produced usually within 2—6 hours.
