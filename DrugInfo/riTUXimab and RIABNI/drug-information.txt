Drug Class: Rituximab is a CD20-directed cytolytic antibody indicated to treat non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), chronic lymphocytic leukemia, moderately to severely active rheumatoid arthritis (RA), granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), microscopic polyangiitis (MPA), and pemphigus vulgaris.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: 500 mg/m2/dose IV or 1,000 mg/dose IV.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): chills, cytokine release syndrome, fatigue, fever, flushing, hyperhidrosis, night sweats, peripheral edema, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ards), bronchiolitis obliterans, bronchospasm, cough, dyspnea, epistaxis, hypoxia, pneumonitis, rhinitis, sneezing, throat irritation, angina, heart failure, hypertension, hypotension, myocardial infarction, supraventricular tachycardia (svt), ventricular fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, anemia, aplastic anemia, hemolytic anemia

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Severe, sometimes fatal, mucocutaneous reactions such as paraneoplastic pemphigus, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, lichenoid dermatitis, vesiculobullous dermatitis, exfoliative dermatitis, and toxic epidermal necrolysis have been reported in association with rituximab.           Adults
          Dosage Description
          375 mg/m2/dose IV once weekly for 4 or 8 doses. Patients who progress after the initial treatment may be retreated with 4 additional doses of 375 mg/m2/dose IV once weekly. A dose of 375 mg/m2/dose IV once weekly for 4 doses produced an overall response rate (ORR) of 48%. Response rates are higher in patients with International Working Formulation (IWF) B, C, and D histologic subtypes as compared to IWF A subtypes (58% vs. 12%). Patients with smaller lesions (i.e., less than 5 cm) and those previously treated with autologous bone marrow transplant have response rates of 55% and 78%, respectively. In patients who progressed after the initial treatment, the retreatment schedule produced an ORR of 57% with a median duration of response of 13.4 months. There is limited data regarding more than 2 courses. In patients with bulky disease (single lesion greater than 10 cm), who were excluded from the previous study, rituximab 375 mg/m2/dose IV once weekly for 4 doses resulted in an ORR of 36% (3% complete response, 33% partial response) with a median response duration of 6.9 months.

          Adults
          Dosage Description
          375 mg/m2/dose IV on day 1 of each cycle of chemotherapy for up to 8 infusions.  Rituximab has been studied in combination with first-line therapy consisting of cyclophosphamide 750 mg/m2/dose IV on day 1, vincristine 1.4 mg/m2/dose (Max: 2 mg/dose) IV on day 1, and prednisone 40 mg/m2/day PO on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 (R-CVP); cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP); cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, etoposide, prednisone, and interferon alfa-2a (R-CHVP+I); and mitoxantrone, chlorambucil, and prednisolone (R-MCP) in randomized, clinical trials. R-CVP was compared with CVP (repeated every 21 days for up to 8 cycles) in 361 previously untreated patients with stage III or IV follicular lymphoma in a randomized, phase III study. In a preplanned interim analysis (after 189 events), time to progression (TTP) was significantly improved in patients who received R-CVP compared with CVP alone and this study was terminated.   At a median follow-up time of 53 months, the median TTP was 34 months in the R-CVP arm compared with 15 months in the CVP arm (p less than 0.0001). Additionally, the 4-year overall survival rates were significantly improved with R-CVP (83% vs. 77%; p = 0.029). Grade 3 or 4 neutropenia occurred more often with R-CVP compared with CVP (24% vs. 14%).

          Dosage Description
          250 mg/m2/dose IV for 2 doses as part of the Y-90-ibritumomab therapeutic regimen. The Y-90-ibritumomab therapeutic regimen consists of 2 rituximab infusions given on day 1 and on day 7, 8, or 9 plus one Y-90-ibritumomab dose given within 4 hours after the second rituximab infusion. Immediately stop the rituximab infusion for serious infusion reactions and discontinue the Y-90-ibritumomab therapeutic regimen. In patients with previously untreated follicular NHL who achieved a partial or complete response to first-line chemotherapy, the dose of Y-90-ibritumomab is 0.4 millicuries (mCi)/kg/dose (14.8 megabecquerels (MBq)/kg/dose) IV over 10 minutes. In patients with relapsed or refractory low-grade or follicular NHL who have a normal platelet count (150,000 cells/mm3 or greater), the dose of Y-90-ibritumomab is 0.4 mCi/kg/dose IV over 10 minutes. In patients with relapsed or refractory low-grade or follicular NHL who have a platelet count of 100,000 to 149,000 cells/mm3, the dose of Y-90-ibritumomab is 0.3 mCi/kg/dose (11.1 MBq/kg/dose) IV over 10 minutes. Y-90-ibritumomab should not be administered in patients with relapsed or refractory low-grade or follicular NHL who have a platelet count less than 100,000 cells/mm3. Use the patient's actual body weight to calculate the Y-90-ibritumomab dose; do not exceed 32 mCi (1,184 MBq). Immediately stop the infusion and restart in another limb if signs or symptoms of extravasation occur.

          Dosage Description
          375 mg/m2/dose IV on day 1 of each cycle of chemotherapy for up to 8 infusions.    Rituximab has been studied in combination with 6 cycles of cyclophosphamide 750 mg/m2/dose IV on day 1, doxorubicin 50 mg/m2/dose IV on day 1, vincristine 2 mg IV on day 1, and prednisone 100 mg/day PO on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 repeated every 21 days (CHOP-21) or CHOP-like chemotherapy in 824 adult patients less than 60 years of age with previously untreated, good prognosis, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma in a large, multinational, randomized, clinical trial. At a median follow-up of 72 months, the 6-year EFS (74.3% vs. 55.8%; hazard ratio = 0.4; 95% CI, 0.32 to 0.62; p less than 0.0001), progression-free survival (80.2% vs. 63.9%; p less than 0.0001), and overall survival (90.1% vs. 80%; p = 0.0004) rates were significantly higher with rituximab plus CHOP-like chemotherapy compared with CHOP-like chemotherapy alone.
          Heading
          Adults and Elderly patients 60 years and older
          Dosage Description
          375 mg/m2/dose IV on day 1 of each cycle of chemotherapy for up to 8 infusions. Rituximab has been studied in combination with cyclophosphamide 750 mg/m2/dose IV on day 1, doxorubicin 50 mg/m2/dose IV on day 1, vincristine 1.4 mg/m2/dose (Max: 2 mg/dose) IV on day 1, and prednisone 40 or 100 mg/m2/day PO on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 (R-CHOP) repeated every 21 days for up to 8 cycles in patients 60 years of age or older with previously untreated diffuse large B-cell lymphoma in randomized, clinical trials. In another randomized trial, the median progression-free survival (PFS) (4.8 vs. 1.2 years; p less than 0.0001) and OS (8.4 vs. 3.5 years; p less than 0.0001) times were significantly improved with R-CHOP compared with CHOP in 399 patients aged 60 to 75 years. The 10-year PFS rates were 36.5% and 20.1% in the R-CHOP and CHOP arms, respectively, and the 10-year OS rates were 43.5% and 27.6%, respectively.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Severe, sometimes fatal, mucocutaneous reactions such as paraneoplastic pemphigus, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, lichenoid dermatitis, vesiculobullous dermatitis, exfoliative dermatitis, and toxic epidermal necrolysis have been reported in association with rituximab. Reaction onset has varied from 1—13 weeks after rituximab exposure. Discontinue rituximab in patients who have a severe mucocutaneous reaction. The safety of rituximab readministration has not been determined.
 
Severe and sometimes fatal infusion-related reactions occur with rituximab; deaths within 24 hours of rituximab infusion have been reported (see Adverse Reactions). In reported cases, the following factors were more frequently associated with fatal outcomes: females, pulmonary infiltrates, and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) or mantle cell lymphoma. Approximately 80% of fatal infusion reactions occurred in association with the first infusion of rituximab. Fatal reactions followed an infusion reaction complex, which included hypoxia, pulmonary infiltrates, acute respiratory distress syndrome, myocardial infarction, ventricular fibrillation, or cardiogenic shock. All patients should receive diphenhydramine, acetaminophen, and possibly corticosteroids as premedication for rituximab infusions. The rituximab infusion should be interrupted for severe reactions and supportive care measures instituted as indicated (e.g., intravenous fluids, vasopressors, oxygen, bronchodilators, diphenhydramine, and acetaminophen). In most cases, the infusion may be resumed at a 50% reduction in rate once the symptoms have completely resolved. Patients requiring close monitoring during the first and all subsequent infusions include those with preexisting cardiac disease, pulmonary disease, or respiratory insufficiency, those with prior clinically significant cardiopulmonary adverse events, and those with high numbers of circulating malignant cells (>= 25,000/mm3) with or without evidence of high tumor burden. Patients with preexisting cardiac arrhythmias and angina have had recurrences during rituximab therapy; monitor patients closely during the infusion and the immediate post-infusion period. Those patients who develop cardiac arrhythmias or angina during rituximab infusion should have the infusion stopped and undergo cardiac monitoring during and after subsequent infusions. Severe hypotension has occurred during rituximab infusions. Patients with underlying hypotension may be at increased risk for severe reactions. Patients being treated for hypertension should be instructed not to take their antihypertensive medication 12 hours before rituximab infusions.
 
Rituximab is a genetically engineered chimeric murine/human monoclonal IgG1 kappa antibody directed against the CD20 antigen and may be inappropriate for use by patients with known IgE-mediated murine protein hypersensitivity. Patients who develop human anti-murine antibody (HAMA) or human anti-chimeric antibody (HACA) titers may have allergic or hypersensitivity reactions when treated with rituximab or other murine or chimeric monoclonal antibodies. In addition, rituximab has been associated with hypersensitivity reactions (non-IgE mediated reactions) that may respond to adjustments in the infusion rate and medical management. Hypotension, acute bronchospasm, and angioedema have occurred during rituximab infusions (see Adverse Reactions). For severe hypersensitivity reactions, the rituximab infusion may be interrupted and then resumed at a 50% reduction in rate when symptoms completely resolve. Treatment with diphenhydramine and acetaminophen is recommended; additional treatment with bronchodilators and intravenous fluids may be indicated. In most cases, patients who have experienced non-life-threatening hypersensitivity reactions have been able to complete a full course of therapy. Medications for hypersensitivity reactions should be available for immediate use during administration of rituximab.
 
Rituximab has been associated with severe renal toxicity and in some cases has resulted in fatalities (see Adverse Reactions). Discontinuation of rituximab should be considered in patients with rising serum creatinine or oliguria. Patients with underlying renal impairment or renal failure may not tolerate rituximab therapy. Renal toxicity has occurred in patients with high numbers of circulating malignant cells (> 25,000/mm3) or high tumor burden who experience tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) and in patients with non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (NHL) administered concomitant cisplatin therapy during clinical trials. Acute renal failure requiring dialysis with instances of fatal outcome has been reported in the setting of TLS after rituximab treatment of non-Hodgkin's lymphoma. The combination of cisplatin and rituximab is not an approved regimen and should only be used in the setting of a clinical trial with extreme caution. Prophylaxis of TLS, including aggressive hydration and allopurinol treatment, should be considered for patients at risk (e.g., those with high number of circulating malignant cell or high tumor burden). Hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, hypocalcemia, and decreased urine output may be indicative of rituximab-induced TLS. Correction of electrolyte imbalances, monitoring of renal function and fluid balance, and administration of supportive care, including dialysis, should be instituted as necessary. Following resolution of TLS, rituximab has been tolerated when re-administered in conjunction with TLS prophylaxis.
 
Rituximab is not recommended for the treatment of patients with a severe active infection. Additionally, severe viral infections, either new, reactivated, or exacerbated, have been reported in clinical studies and postmarketing reports.
 
Persons at high risk for hepatitis B viral infection should be screened before initiation of rituximab therapy. Hepatitis B virus (HBV) reactivation with fulminant hepatitis, hepatic failure, and death has been reported in some patients with hematologic malignancies treated with rituximab (see Adverse Reactions). The median time to diagnosis of hepatitis was approximately 4 months after the initiation of rituximab and approximately one month after the last dose. Carriers of hepatitis B should be closely monitored for clinical (e.g., jaundice) and laboratory signs of active hepatitis infection and for signs of hepatitis during and for up to several months following rituximab therapy. In patients who develop viral hepatitis, rituximab and chemotherapy should be discontinued and appropriate treatment initiated. There are insufficient data regarding the safety of resuming rituximab therapy in patients who develop hepatitis subsequent to HBV reactivation.
 
Rituximab may cause exacerbation or reactivation of latent JC virus, which can cause progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML). At least 2 patients receiving rituximab for the treatment of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and 23 patients receiving rituximab in combination with other immunosuppressants for the treatment of hematologic malignancies have developed PML, which can be fatal. Patients receiving rituximab should be instructed to notify their health care provider immediately if they notice any new or worsening neurological signs and symptoms such as ataxia, visual changes, or confusion. Similarly, health care providers treating patients with rituximab should be suspect of PML in any patient presenting with neurological symptoms. Discontinue rituximab and consider discontinuation or reduction of any concomitant chemotherapy or immunosuppressive therapy in patients who develop PML. Health care providers should report suspected PML or other serious adverse events to the MedWatch program.
 
Rituximab is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. The mutagenic potential of rituximab has not been assessed, and the potential effects of rituximab on fertility in males or females are unknown. Human IgG is known to cross the placental barrier, which could cause fetal B-cell depletion. Depletion of B cells has been noted in offspring of cynomolgus monkeys treated with rituximab; recovery of B cell counts and function occurred within 6 months of birth. In humans, B-cell lymphocytopenia generally lasting less than six months can occur in infants exposed to rituximab in-utero. Rituximab was detected postnatally in the serum of infants exposed in-utero. No teratogenic effects of rituximab have been noted in monkeys. Because animal reproduction studies are not always predictive of human response and because no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women exist, rituximab should only be used during pregnancy if the potential benefit to the mother justifies the potential risk to the fetus. In addition, individuals of childbearing potential should use effective contraceptive methods during treatment and for 12 months after rituximab therapy.
 
It is not known if rituximab is excreted in human milk. Rituximab is secreted in the milk of lactating cynomolgus monkeys, and IgG is excreted in human milk. However, published data suggest that antibodies in human breast milk do not enter the neonatal and infant circulations in substantial amounts. Weigh the unknown risks to the infant from oral ingestion of rituximab against the known benefits of breast-feeding. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
The safety and effectiveness of rituximab have not been established in children.
 
Although the adverse effect profile of rituximab was similar among older and younger patients with rheumatoid arthritis, elderly patients with diffuse large B-cell NHL were more likely to experience cardiac adverse events and serious pulmonary adverse events as compared with younger patients. Specifically, supraventricular arrhythmias, pneumonia, and pneumonitis were more common among older patients. No overall differences in efficacy of rituximab were noted between the elderly and younger adult patients with either diffuse large B-cell NHL or rheumatoid arthritis. Clinical studies of rituximab in previously untreated, low-grade or follicular, CD 20-positive, B-cell NHL and in relapsed or refractory, low-grade or follicular lymphoma did not include sufficient numbers of elderly patients to determine if they responded differently as compared with younger patients.
 
Vaccination, particularly with live viruses, during chemotherapy or radiation therapy should be avoided because the antibody response is suboptimal. When chemotherapy is being planned, vaccination should precede the initiation of chemotherapy by >= 2 weeks. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history. The effect of rituximab on immune responses after vaccination was assessed in a trial of patients with rheumatoid arthritis randomized to receive either methotrexate alone or in combination with rituximab. Responses to pneumococcal vaccination were lower in the rituximab/methotrexate arm than in the methotrexate alone arm (19% v. 61%). Responses to tetanus toxoid vaccination were similar between the two treatment arms (39% v. 42%). Rheumatoid arthritis patients should receive immunizations according to current guidelines; administer live vaccines at least 4 weeks prior to a course of rituximab.
 
Rituximab binds to all CD20-positive B lymphocytes; obtain complete blood counts (CBC) and platelet counts at regular intervals during rituximab therapy and more frequently in patients who develop cytopenias. The duration of cytopenias can extend months beyond the treatment period.

Interactions
Abatacept: (Major) Avoid the concomitant use of rituximab and abatacept; coadministration may result in additive immunosuppression and an increased risk of infection. There is insufficient experience to assess the safety and efficacy of abatacept administered concurrently with other biologic therapy, such as rituximab, and therefore such use is not recommended.
Adalimumab: (Major) Avoid the concomitant use of rituximab with adalimumab, a tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitor; coadministration may result in additive immunosuppression and an increased risk of infection. Higher rates of serious infection were observed in patients with rheumatoid arthritis treated with rituximab who received subsequent treatment with a TNF inhibitor.
Albuterol; Budesonide: (Moderate) Rituximab and corticosteroids are commonly used together; however, monitor the patient for immunosuppression and signs and symptoms of infection during combined chronic therapy.
Anakinra: (Major) Utilize caution with concomitant use of rituximab with other biologic agents, such as anakinra; coadministration may result in additive immunosuppression and an increased risk of infection. Limited data are available on the safety of the use of biologic agents in rheumatoid arthritis patients exhibiting peripheral B-cell depletion following treatment with rituximab. Monitor patients closely for signs or symptoms of infection.
Anifrolumab: (Major) Coadministration is not recommended. Anifrolumab has not been studied in combination with other biologic therapies including B-cell targeted therapies such as rituximab. Potential concerns with use of these drugs together include an increased susceptibility to immunosuppression and serious infections, some of which might be fatal.
Auranofin: (Moderate) The concomitant use of rituximab with other disease modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), including gold compounds, may result in additive immunosuppression and an increased risk of infection. According to treatment guidelines, thare no data that adequately address the safety and efficacy of gold compounds in combination with biologic therapies. Monitor patients closely for signs or symptoms of infection.
azaTHIOprine: (Moderate) The concomitant use of rituximab with other disease modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), such as azathioprine, may result in additive immunosuppression and an increased risk of infection. Monitor patients closely for signs or symptoms of infection.
Azelastine; Fluticasone: (Moderate) Rituximab and corticosteroids are commonly used together; however, monitor the patient for immunosuppression and signs and symptoms of infection during combined chronic therapy.

Baricitinib may be used as monotherapy or concomitantly with methotrexate or other nonbiologic DMARDs.

Therefore, belimumab use is not recommended in combination with biologic therapies. Potential concerns with use together include an increased susceptibility to immunosuppression and serious infections, some of which might be fatal.

  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Adverse reactions of any grade have been in 99% of patients receiving rituximab. Severe and life-threatening (grade 3 and 4) events were reported in 57% of patients.
 
Infusion reactions, which are sometimes severe and/or fatal, are associated with rituximab. During the first infusion of rituximab, about 80% of patients experience infusion-related reactions; the incidence decreases with subsequent infusions (30% with the fourth, 14% with the eighth). Infusion-related reactions generally occur within 30 minutes to 2 hours of the start of the first infusion. These reactions may reflect hypersensitivity reactions in some patients. Fever and chills/rigors are the most common infusion-related reactions, occurring in 53% and 33% of patients, respectively. Other infusion-related events include nausea/vomiting (23%/10%), asthenia (26%), pruritus (14%), rash (unspecified) (15%), headache (14%), angioedema (13%), pain (12%), rhinitis (12%), back pain (10%), dizziness (10%), hypotension (10%), myalgia (10%), throat irritation (9%), bronchospasm (8%), urticaria (8%), dyspnea (7%), hypertension (6%), flushing (5%). Severe reactions are associated with hypotension, angioedema, hypoxia, or bronchospasm. Patients with bulky disease had a higher incidence of grade 3 or 4 hypotension and dyspnea. A cytokine release syndrome resulting in severe pulmonary and cardiovascular infusion-related events has been reported within 24 hours of rituximab infusions and has resulted in fatal outcomes in some situations. These fatal reactions followed an infusion reaction complex that included hypoxia, pulmonary infiltrates, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), myocardial infarction, ventricular fibrillation, or cardiogenic shock. Approximately 80% of fatal infusion reactions occurred with the first infusion. This syndrome may also be associated with features of tumor lysis syndrome in patients with a high tumor burden. Acute bronchospasm and acute pneumonitis has been reported 1—4 weeks after the last rituximab infusion. Closely monitor patients with at least 25,000/mm3 of circulating malignant cells, patients with pre-existing cardiac or pulmonary conditions, and patients who experienced prior cardiopulmonary adverse reactions. Premedicate with an antihistamine and acetaminophen to prevent infusion-related fevers and chills. For patients with rheumatoid arthritis, methylprednisolone 100 mg IV or its equivalent is recommended 30 minutes prior to each infusion. If severe infusion-related reactions occur, stop the infusion and administer appropriate supportive and symptomatic care. Closely monitor patients, as symptoms may worsen over time or patients may initially improve then deteriorate later. If symptoms resolve, the infusion can be resumed at least one-half the previous rate. In most cases, severe reactions occur with the first infusion of rituximab and in patients with high tumor burden or pulmonary disease. These patients may tolerate subsequent infusions of rituximab.
 
In both clinical studies and post-marketing surveillance, there have been a limited number of reports of bronchiolitis obliterans presenting up to 6 months post-rituximab infusion and a limited number of reports of pneumonitis (including interstitial pneumonitis) presenting up to 3 months after rituximab therapy, some of which resulted in fatal outcomes. The safety of resumption or continued administration of rituximab in patients with pneumonitis or bronchiolitis obliterans is unknown.
 
Patients with preexisting cardiac arrhythmias and angina have had recurrences of these events during rituximab infusions. Arrhythmias reported include ventricular tachycardia, supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), trigeminy, and an irregular pulse rate. Angina and myocardial infarction have been reported following administration of rituximab. Rare, fatal heart failure with symptomatic onset weeks after rituximab has been reported. Patients who develop significant cardiopulmonary events should have rituximab discontinued.
 
Lymphopenia (48%) is the most common hematologic reaction to rituximab. Other hematologic adverse reactions include leukopenia (14%), neutropenia (14%), thrombocytopenia (12%), and anemia (8%). Severe (Grade 3 or 4) cytopenias were reported in 12% of patients treated including lymphopenia (40%), neutropenia (6%), leukopenia (4%), anemia (3%), and thrombocytopenia (2%). The median duration of lymphopenia was 14 days (range: 1—588 days) and of neutropenia was 13 days (range: 2—116 days); however, the duration of cytopenias can last well beyond the treatment period. Cases of hemolytic anemia, bone marrow aplasia, prolonged pancytopenia, and late-onset neutropenia (defined as occurring 40 days after the last dose of rituximab) have been reported in patients with hematologic malignancies. In reported cases of late-onset neutropenia, the median duration was 10 days (range 3—148 days). Documented recovery from neutropenia was described in about 50% of the cases; of those with documented recovery, approximately 50% received growth-factor support. More than 50% of the reported cases of delayed onset neutropenia occurred in patients who had undergone a prior autologous bone marrow transplantation. The incidence and severity of neutropenia and anemia is higher in patients with bulky disease (i.e., a single lesion >= 10 cm) than those with lesions = 25,000/mm3) or high tumor burden. Following complete resolution of TLS, rituximab has been well tolerated when re-administered in conjunction with prophylactic therapy (i.e., allopurinol and hydration). Discontinuation of rituximab therapy should be considered in patients who develop azotemia or oliguria.
 
Abdominal pain, GI obstruction, and GI perforation, in some cases leading to death, were observed in patients receiving rituximab in combination with chemotherapy for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). In post-marketing reports, which include both patients with low-grade or follicular non-Hodgkin's lymphoma and DLBCL, the average time to onset of symptoms was 6 days (range 1—77 days) in patients with documented GI perforation. Complaints of abdominal pain, especially early in the course of treatment, should prompt a thorough diagnostic evaluation and appropriate treatment. Overall, abdominal pain has been reported in 14% of patients treated with rituximab. The incidence of abdominal pain was high in patients with bulky disease (i.e., a single lesion >= 10 cm) than those with lesions = 1% but < 5% of patients regardless of causality include agitation, anorexia, arthritis, conjunctivitis, depression, dyspepsia, edema, hyperkinesis, hypertonia, hyperesthesia, hypoglycemia, injection site reaction (i.e., pain), insomnia, lacrimation disorder, malaise, nervousness, neuritis, neuropathy, paresthesias, somnolence, vertigo, and weight loss.
 
Immune/autoimmune events have been reported with rituximab including uveitis, optic neuritis, systemic vasculitis, pleuritis, lupus-like symptoms, serum sickness, polyarticular arthritis, and vasculitis with rash.
 
In 67 patients treated with rituximab who were evaluated for human anti-murine antibody formation (HAMA), none tested positive. One percent (4/356) of patients tested positive for human anti-chimeric antibody (HACA) and 3 patients had an objective clinical response. Nevertheless, patients who develop human anti-murine antibody (HAMA) or human anti-chimeric antibody (HACA) titers may have allergic or hypersensitivity reactions when treated with rituximab or other murine or chimeric monoclonal antibodies.

    General Information
    Patients with hematologic malignancies or autoimmune diseases who receive rituximab may develop potentially fatal progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML). Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy is an opportunistic viral infection of the brain caused by reactivated latent JC virus. Latent JC virus is present in approximately 40% to 80% of healthy adults; JC virus typically only causes PML in immunocompromised patients. Evaluation includes but is not limited to consultation with a neurologist and attainment of brain MRI and lumbar puncture. If PML develops, discontinue rituximab and consider reductions or discontinuation of any concomitant chemotherapy or immunosuppressive therapy. PML is usually fatal or leads to severe disability, and there are no known effective treatments.

Description
  Text
  Rituximab is a CD20-directed cytolytic antibody indicated to treat non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), chronic lymphocytic leukemia, moderately to severely active rheumatoid arthritis (RA), granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), microscopic polyangiitis (MPA), and pemphigus vulgaris. Rituximab is used in combination with methotrexate for RA, but use is usually limited to individuals with inadequate response to TNF blockers or other biologic immunomodulators or with relevant lymphoproliferative history. Rituximab is also used off-label to treat multiple sclerosis, chronic graft-versus-host disease, lupus nephritis, and various other malignancies and autoimmune disorders. Rituximab labeling has boxed warnings for infusion-related reactions, severe mucocutaneous reactions, a risk for hepatitis B virus reactivation, and a risk for progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy. Premedication is recommended before rituximab infusions due to the potential for infusion-related reactions. Rituximab was initially FDA-approved in 1997.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Rituximab is a monoclonal antibody that targets the CD20 antigen expressed on the surface of pre-B and mature B-lymphocytes. Upon binding to CD20, rituximab mediates B-cell lysis. Possible mechanisms of cell lysis include complement dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) and antibody dependent cell mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC). B cells are believed to play a role in the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and associated chronic synovitis. In this setting, B cells may be acting at multiple sites in the autoimmune/inflammatory process, including through production of rheumatoid factor (RF) and other autoantibodies, antigen presentation, T-cell activation, and/or proinflammatory cytokine production. Rituximab is associated with the release of cytokines, specifically tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFa) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which may be responsible for the infusion-related adverse reactions.

Emetic Risk
Minimal
Premedication
In all patients, administer premedication with acetaminophen and an antihistamine before each infusion of rituximab to attenuate infusion-related events.
Premedication with corticosteroids is given according to the disease/condition and specific regimen.
For oncology patients administered rituximab according to the 90-minute infusion rate: Administer the glucocorticoid component of the chemotherapy regimen prior to infusion.
For patients with rheumatoid arthritis, pemphigus vulgaris (PV), granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), or microscopic polyangiitis (MPA): Administer methylprednisolone 100 mg IV or its equivalent 30 minutes before each infusion. Corticosteroids are also given as part of the regimen for patients with GPA or MPA; follow the dosing regimen recommended.
Recommended Prophylaxis
Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) and herpes infection prophylaxis is recommended for certain patients receiving rituximab.
Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL): PCP and anti-herpetic viral prophylaxis is recommended during treatment and up to 12 months after treatment as appropriate.
GPA and MPA: PCP prophylaxis is recommended during treatment and for at least 6 months after the last rituximab infusion.
PV: Consider PCP prophylaxis during and after rituximab treatment.

Do not mix or dilute with other drugs.
Discard any unused portion left in the vial.
Storage: Store refrigerated (2 to 8 degrees C; 36 to 46 degrees F). No incompatibilities with polyvinylchloride or polyethylene bags have been observed.
Ruxience: Store under refrigeration for up to 24 hours after preparation if diluted with 5% Dextrose Injection or for up to 16 days after preparation if diluted with 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection.
Riabni: Store under refrigeration for up to 24 hours after preparation if diluted with 5% Dextrose Injection or for up to 7 days after preparation if diluted with 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection; protect from light during storage if diluted in 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection.
Rituxan and Truxima: Store infusion refrigerated for up to 24 hours. While the infusions have been shown to be stable for an additional 24 hours at room temperature, the solutions do not contain a preservative and thus should be stored under refrigeration.
Ruxience and Riabni: The allowable storage times differ based on diluent used.
IV Infusion Administration
First infusion:
Standard infusion: Administer intravenously at an initial rate of 50 mg/hour. If no hypersensitivity or infusion-related events occur, increase infusion rate in 50 mg/hour increments every 30 minutes, to a maximum of 400 mg/hour.
For pediatric patients with mature B-cell NHL or acute leukemia: Administer intravenously at an initial rate of 0.5 mg/kg/hour (maximum, 50 mg/hour). If no hypersensitivity or infusion-related events occur, increase infusion rate by 0.5 mg/kg/hour every 30 minutes, to a maximum of 400 mg/hour.
Subsequent infusions:
Standard infusion: Administer at an initial rate of 100 mg/hour. Increase by 100 mg/hour at 30-minute intervals to a maximum of 400 mg/hour, as tolerated.
For adult patients with previously untreated follicular NHL and DLBCL: In patients who did not experience a grade 3 or 4 infusion-related adverse event during cycle 1 and do not have clinically significant cardiovascular disease or a circulating lymphocyte count of 5,000 cells/mm3 or more, rituximab may be infused over 90 minutes in cycle 2 with a glucocorticoid-containing chemotherapy regimen. The glucocorticoid should be administered prior to the 90-minute infusion. Initiate rituximab at a rate of 20% of the total dose over the first 30 minutes and the remaining 80% of the total dose over 60 minutes. If tolerated, this infusion schedule may be given in subsequent cycles. In a safety analysis of 150 patients who received this rapid infusion schedule beginning in cycle 2 with corticosteroid-containing chemotherapy, no grade 3 or 4 infusion-related reactions were observed.
For pediatric patients with mature B-cell NHL or acute leukemia: Administer intravenously at an initial rate of 1 mg/kg/hour (maximum, 50 mg/hour). If no hypersensitivity or infusion-related events occur, increase infusion rate by 1 mg/kg/hour every 30 minutes, to a maximum of 400 mg/hour.
Monitor patients closely during each infusion.
