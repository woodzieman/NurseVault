Drug Class: Calcitriol, or 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol, is the active form of vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) and is available for oral, topical, and intravenous use.

Routes: oral, injection (IV/IM/SC), topical; Common adult doses: 200 g ointment/week topically. Individualize systemic dosage based on clinical parameters; most patients respond to 1 mcg/day PO or less for labeled usage, but for some off-label uses, up to 3 mcg/day has been reported.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, hypervitaminosis d

In an Overdose: Progressive hypercalcemia due to overdosage of vitamin D and its metabolites may be so severe as to require emergency attention.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Calcitriol is a potent vitamin D analog and should not be given to patients with hypercalcemia or hypervitaminosis D. Increased calcium levels may also occur with topical administration.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Calcitriol is a potent vitamin D analog and should not be given to patients with hypercalcemia or hypervitaminosis D. Increased calcium levels may also occur with topical administration. Do not cover with an occlusive dressing as this may enhance the absorption of calcitriol. Chronic hypercalcemia can lead to soft tissue calcification, nephrocalcinosis, and other toxicities. The serum calcium times phosphate (Ca x P) product should not exceed 70. Radiographic evaluation of suspect areas may be useful in the early detection of soft tissue calcification. Patients with renal failure may be at increased risk for vitamin D-induced hypercalcemia even with usual dosages. During titration of calcitriol dose, serum calcium levels should be monitored twice weekly. If hypercalcemia develops or if the calcium-phosphorus product (Ca x PO4) is greater than 70, discontinue calcitriol until these parameters normalize (see Dosage).Calcitriol topical ointment should not be applied to the face; avoid ocular exposure. Accidental exposure to unaffected areas of skin may lead to skin irritation and should be avoided.Patients must receive an adequate amount of calcium while taking calcitriol. Patients are advised to have a dietary intake of calcium of at least 600 mg/day. The US RDA for calcium in adults is 1000-1500 mg/day.Calcitriol should be used cautiously in patients with hyperphosphatemia due to the risk of metastatic calcification. Treatment with calcitriol may be initiated after stabilization of phosphorus levels.Calcitriol should be administered with caution to patients with cardiac disease, arteriosclerosis, or those receiving digoxin because hypercalcemia and/or cardiac arrhythmias can result.Clinical experience has not identified differences in responses between elderly and younger patients receiving calcitriol. However, dosage should be initiated conservatively, at the lower end of the adult dosage range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic and renal function in the elderly.Patients who are taking calcitriol should avoid dehydration. Adequate fluid intake should be maintained.Patients with sarcoidosis, and possibly other granulomatous diseases, may have increased sensitivity to the effects of vitamin D.Excessive exposure to natural or artificial sunlight (UV) exposure with topical application of calcitriol may increase the risk of skin tumor formation and should be avoided. It may also be prudent to limit or avoid the use of phototherapy or other photosensitizing agents.Although vitamin D analogs have different potencies, all have similar effects on the mother and fetus during pregnancy. Calcitriol injection and topical ointment are classified as FDA pregnancy category C. There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of calcitriol in pregnant women. Systemic absorption may occur after topical application, although it is not known what effect this may have on a fetus. Calcitriol should be used during pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus. Oral calcitriol administered at 17 to 36 mcg/day during pregnancy resulted in mild hypercalcemia in the neonate during first 2 days of life which returned to normal on the third day. Problems in humans from normal daily intake of vitamin D have not been documented; however, hypercalcemia during pregnancy has been associated with suppression of parathyroid hormone in the neonate, resulting in mental retardation and congenital aortic stenosis. The risks to benefits of untreated hypoparathyroidism or hypophosphatemia in the mother should be considered before using vitamin D analogs such as calcitriol.Systemically administered vitamin D and its metabolites, including calcitriol, are distributed in small amounts into breast milk. It is not known if calcitriol is excreted into breast milk after topical application. A committee of the American Academy of Pediatrics considers maternal consumption of vitamin D compatible with breast-feeding. However, serum calcium concentrations in the infant should be monitored when using vitamin D in a nursing mother.

    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    Use oral or intravenous calcitriol with caution in individuals with renal failure due to the risk of ectopic calcification. Calcitriol increases inorganic serum phosphate concentrations. Use a nonaluminum phosphate-binding agent and a low phosphate diet to control serum phosphorus concentrations in individuals receiving calcitriol who are undergoing dialysis.
    Headline
    hypercalcemia, hypervitaminosis D, immobility
    General Information
    Oral or intravenous calcitriol is contraindicated in individuals with hypercalcemia or evidence of hypervitaminosis D (vitamin D toxicity). An abrupt increase in calcium intake as a result of changes in diet (e.g., increased consumption of dairy products) or uncontrolled intake of calcium preparations may trigger hypercalcemia; estimate daily dietary calcium intake and adjust the intake when indicated. Immobility, such as that following surgery, increases the risk of hypercalcemia. Monitor serum calcium concentrations at least twice weekly during oral or intravenous calcitriol dose titration. The serum calcium times phosphate (Ca x P) product should not exceed 70.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    In general, use cautious dose selection for oral or intravenous calcitriol in geriatric adults, usually starting at the low end of the dosage range, reflecting the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function, and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data on the use of calcitriol during pregnancy have not identified a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, miscarriage, or adverse outcomes. Systemic calcitriol may be used for the management of hypoparathyroidism during pregnancy. Pregnancy is associated with variable changes in calcium homeostasis due to pregnancy-induced alterations in the production, metabolism, and excretion of calcium and calcitropic hormones. These changes occur in a heterogeneous manner; it is not possible to predict changes in the dose requirements of calcium and vitamin D analogues. Monitor serum calcium adjusted for albumin (due to intravascular volume changes during pregnancy), and phosphorus every 2 to 4 weeks and following dose changes within 4 to 7 days. The American Academy of Dermatology and National Psoriasis Foundation guidelines of care for psoriasis state that topical vitamin D analogues such as calcitriol may be used during pregnancy if the benefit outweighs the risk. In one patient taking oral calcitriol 17 to 36 mcg/day during pregnancy, the offspring manifested milk hypercalcemia in the first 2 days of life, which returns to normal on the third day. In animal studies, no teratogenic effects were observed in rats exposed to calcitriol at doses approximately 5 times the maximum recommended dose (MRHD) based on body surface area. External and skeletal abnormalities were observed in rabbits exposed to calcitriol at doses 2 to 6 times the MRHD, though this effect was inconsistent across litters.  In rabbits, calcitriol applied topically to 6.4% of the body surface area during organogenesis increased the incidence of post-implantation loss and minor skeletal abnormalities, possibly secondary to maternal toxicity. No effects on reproductive or fetal parameters were observed in rats exposed to topical calcitriol.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use calcitriol with caution during breast-feeding. Systemically administered vitamin D and its metabolites, including calcitriol, are present in human milk in small amounts. Calcitriol is 99.9% protein-bound; it is unlikely that calcitriol achieves significant concentrations in human milk. Limited data from case reports have not identified adverse effects in breastfed children. Breast-feeding may be continued in patients who require calcitriol for the management of hypoparathyroidism. However, lactation is associated with changes in calcium homeostasis due to postpartum and lactation-induced alterations in the production, metabolism, and excretion of calcium and calcitropic hormones. Monitor breast-feeding patients closely, as calcitriol requirements may decrease with lactation and increase with cessation of breast-feeding. These changes vary from patient to patient. It is not known if topical administration of calcitriol results in sufficient systemic absorption to produce detectable quantities in human milk. The American Academy of Dermatology and National Psoriasis Foundation guidelines of care for psoriasis state that topical vitamin D analogues such as calcitriol may be used during breast-feeding if the benefit outweighs the risk. To avoid direct exposure to the breastfed child, do not apply calcitriol ointment directly to the nipple or areola.

Description
  Text
  Calcitriol, or 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol, is the active form of vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) and is available for oral, topical, and intravenous use. Calcitriol is structurally similar to calcifediol; however, calcitriol has more than twice the vitamin D activity of calcifediol. Vitamin D occurs in two primary forms: cholecalciferol (vitamin D3), which is synthesized in the skin after exposure to ultraviolet light, and ergocalciferol (vitamin D2), which is produced from plant sterols and is the primary dietary source of vitamin D. Many vitamin D analogs are commercially available, but the responsiveness of various conditions differs based on the pharmacologic properties of each vitamin D analog. Calcitriol is preferable to calcifediol in the management of hypocalcemia in dialysis-dependent renal failure patients because calcifediol must be activated by healthy kidneys to be effective. Calcitriol is indicated for the treatment of secondary hyperparathyroidism and the resultant metabolic bone disease in chronic renal failure patients who are not receiving dialysis; calcitriol also has been used in hypoparathyroidism, familial hypophosphatemia, and vitamin D-dependent rickets. In addition, a topical formulation is used for the treatment of plaque psoriasis. Systemic calcitriol was approved by the FDA in 1978. Calcitriol ointment (Vectical) was approved by the FDA in January 2009.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  In the body, active vitamin D (1,25-(OH)2D3), also known as calcitriol, plays an important role in maintaining calcium balance and in the regulation of parathyroid hormone (PTH). Calcitriol promotes renal reabsorption of calcium, increases intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphorus, and increases calcium and phosphorus mobilization from bone to plasma. Calcitriol appears to promote intestinal absorption of calcium through binding to a specific receptor in the mucosal cytoplasm of the intestine. Subsequently, calcium is absorbed through formation of a calcium-binding protein. Some evidence suggests that calcitriol acts at the level of the cell nucleus to increase plasma calcium and phosphorus. Once plasma saturation of these electrolytes occurs, bone mineralization takes place. Calcitriol decreases PTH levels through several mechanisms. Calcitriol suppresses PTH, first, by a negative feedback mechanism due to increased calcium levels and, second, by suppressing the synthesis and release of PTH by inhibiting PTH gene transcription in specific target cells.
 
Calcitriol is synthesized from calcifediol in the kidneys. The synthesis of calcitriol is stimulated by PTH activating renal alpha-hydroxylase enzymes and low plasma phosphorus levels. Hypocalcemia stimulates the release of PTH. Patients with uremia are unable to synthesize calcitriol in response to hypocalcemia, which perpetuates the hypocalcemia and leads to secondary hyperparathyroidism. This may be a contributory factor to renal osteodystrophy, characteristic of renal failure. The beneficial effect of calcitriol in renal osteodystrophy is due to the correction of hypocalcemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism. It is not certain if calcitriol produces other independent beneficial effects.
 
The mechanism of topical calcitriol in the treatment of plaque psoriasis is unknown.

  Text
  Calcitriol is administered orally, topically, and by intravenous injection. Calcitriol has a duration of action of 3—5 days. Response to vitamin D can be monitored through serum calcium levels, which should be maintained at 9—10 mg/dL. This vitamin is widely distributed. Vitamin D is excreted into breast milk in small amounts.
 
Calcitriol is metabolized in the liver to hydroxy metabolites. When circulating concentrations of the metabolites are adequate, further hydroxylation in the kidneys occurs. Metabolism is influenced by parathyroid hormone, serum calcium and phosphate concentrations, and other hormones. The plasma half-life is 3—6 hours. Calcitriol and its metabolites are excreted primarily via the bile, with some renal elimination.
