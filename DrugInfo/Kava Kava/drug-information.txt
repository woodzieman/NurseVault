        Dosage Description
        40 to 70 mg kavalactones (130 to 250 mg of kava kava standardized to 30% kavalactones, or 100 mg kava kava standardized to 70% kavalactones) PO 2 to 3 times per day. In a 25-week, randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind study, 101 outpatients with anxiety of non-psychotic origin received kava kava 100 mg PO 3 times per day (extract WS1490; containing 70% kavalactones). At 8 weeks and the duration of the study, significant superiority of kava kava over placebo was demonstrated as determined by the Hamilton Anxiety Scale, the Clinical Global Impression Scale, and the Adjective Mood Scale. Several studies in Germany have noted positive effects on anxiety symptoms of non-mental origin. Of note are the small numbers of subjects included (usually less than 60 patients per study), and the relatively short durations (4 to 24 weeks), of these trials. Guidelines from the World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry (WFSBP) and Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) Taskforce state that kava did not show sufficient evidence for use in the treatment of generalized anxiety disorder, but note there may be some benefit to use for short-term management of anxiety symptoms. The American Herbal Products Association's Kava Committee, a trade group, recommends a Max: 300 mg/day PO of total kavalactones. The German Commission E recommends a more conservative daily dosage limit of 60 to 120 mg/day of kavalactones. Duration of treatment should be limited to no more than 3 months without health care professional oversight. People who are on prescribed anti-anxiety medications should not use Kava without health care professional guidance.

  Kava Kava has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. Products containing kava kava are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Caution should be used when patients are being treated for other psychiatric diseases (e.g., anxiety, psychosis, etc.) by a health care professional, particularly if they are on prescription medication for such problems. Kava kava could interfere with prescribed therapies. Kava kava is not recommended for the treatment of diagnosed psychiatric disorders of mental origin. The decision to self-treat anxiety or insomnia with kava kava should be weighed against the possibility that the problem could coexist with another medical condition (e.g., thyroid disease, neurologic disorders).Since kava kava may affect epileptic activity, patients with seizures should not use this product except on the advice of a health care professional. Kava kava has been reported to increase Parkinson's disease symptoms. Patients who are being treated for Parkinson's are recommended not to take kava kava supplements. Kava kava should be used with caution in patients who have pre-existance of any neurological disease.There is no experience with kava kava in the presence of renal disease, including renal impairment or renal failure. Kava kava should be used with caution in these populations.Avoid use of kava kava if hepatic disease is present. There are insufficient data on the safety of this herb in patients with hepatic impairment. Components of kava kava appear in part to be hepatically eliminated. If jaundice, hepatitis, cholestasis, or other clinical symptoms of hepatic disease occur in a patient taking this herb, the herb should be permanently discontinued (see Adverse Reactions).Caution is warranted in the elderly due to limited study of kava kava in this patient population at this time, and the greater likelyhood of the elderly to have concomitant diseases like renal disease or hepatic disease.Prior to undergoing surgery, the surgeon and anesthesiologist should be notified of the use of kava kava; the herb has the potential to increase the sedative effects of drugs commonly used during surgical procedures. Some herbal products have been reported to induce changes in heart rate or blood pressure during surgery, or intensify the effects of anesthetic agents. It may be wise to discontinue this herb 2—3 weeks prior to an elective procedure.

A 22-year old woman had a similar reaction to the same dose, and a 63-year old woman who had taken 150 mg of kava kava extract 3 times per day for 4 days to treat anxiety also complained of painful spasms. Description
  Text
  Kava kava is a bitter herb derived from the rhizome fibers of the Piper methysticum plant, a member of the black pepper family. In Fiji, the plant is referred to as "yaquona", in Hawaii it is known as "awa", and in the Aboriginal tribes is known by "grog". Aqueous extractions of kava kava have been used for over 3,000 years in the south Pacific as a natural relaxant, and as an important part of religious and ceremonial events. The extract of the kava root contains at least 7 major active kava pyrones, commonly known as kavalactones. It is believed that kava kava can promote feelings of satisfaction and relaxation without causing drowsiness or interfering with cognition. Kava kava is widely used in Europe for treatment of nervous anxiety, stress, and restlessness. Germany's Commission E has recognized kava kava as an approved herb for these purposes. Several studies in Germany have noted positive effects on anxiety symptoms of non-mental origin. Of note are the small numbers of subjects included (usually less than 60 patients per study), and the relatively short durations (4 to 24 weeks), of these trials. Guidelines from the World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry (WFSBP) and Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) Taskforce state that kava did not show sufficient evidence for use in the treatment of generalized anxiety disorder, but note there may be some benefit to use for short-term management of anxiety symptoms. However, use is cautioned in people with hepatic issues due to reports of rare but potentially fatal hepatotoxicity.  The Veterans Affairs/Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guidelines for chronic insomnia recommend against the use of kava due to a lack of improvement in sleep symptoms in clinical data and the increased risk of liver damage.
 
NOTE: In the U.S., herbal products and dietary supplements are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for dietary supplements as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals since supplements are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required for these products and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  The kava kava rhizome (root) contains at least 7 identified kavalactones, and many minor, as yet unidentified, entities. The known active kava constituents are kawain, dihydrokawain, methysticin, dihydromethysticin, 7,8-dihydroyagonin, and yangonin. Most of what is known about kavalactone psychopharmacology is from animal studies, and the exact mechanisms are still unclear. The limbic structures (e.g., hippocampus, amygdala) are the preferential sites of action for kava in the CNS. Kavalactones are thought to act on GABA receptors to produce anxiolytic and mild sedative activity. Several studies show mild effects of kava kava on GABA-A binding sites, independent of benzodiazepine receptors in vitro. This weak GABA receptor activity may not be the primary mechanism of anxiolytic action for kava kava. Based on recent animal studies, kava kava may also alter CNS serotonin (5-HT) activity at 5-HT type 1A receptors, in a mechanism similar to buspirone. Naloxone is unable to reverse kava kava induced sedation, ruling out opioid receptor activities. Inhibition of dopamine or norepinephrine may also play a role in the activity of kava kava.
 
Kava kava has several other pharmacologic actions in the CNS. Methysticin and kawain have been shown to have anticonvulsant properties. The anticonvulsant actions of kava kava appear to result from the ability to inhibit voltage dependent sodium and calcium channels in the hippocampus. Some kavalactones may even have neuroprotective effects. In animal models of focal cerebral ischemia, methysticin and dihydromethysticin have reduced the infarct area in rats. Based on other pharmacologic studies, it is postulated that kava kava neuroprotection may involve the antagonism of NMDA receptors. In addition, kava kava has been shown to have local anesthetic, analgesia, and central acting skeletal muscle relaxant activities similar to mephenesin in animals. Local anesthetic actions are evidenced by the oral numbness that occurs in humans after mastication of the kava root.
 
Kava kava has also been studied for non-CNS activity. Kawain appears to inhibit cyclooxygenase activity and thromboxane synthase activity, leading to decreased thromboxane A2 and reduced platelet aggregation. Whether antithrombotic activity is of clinical significance is unknown. Mild antifungal activity has also been elicited from kava extracts, and may account for its history of use in Polynesian culture as an anti-infective.

Pharmacokinetics
  Text
  Precise pharmacokinetic data are not yet available for kava kava or its constituents. Kavalactones are primarily found in the rhizome of the product, and the root is used in most herbal preparations. Potency of kava kava preparations, expressed as percentage of kavalactones, can vary substantially from manufacturer to manufacturer, and can be dependent on the variety of kava plant utilized. Traditional kava drinks or teas are suspensions of the lipid constituents of the masticated or pulverized roots in water. Mastication may release more of the kavalactones from the root via enzymatic release by saliva. These traditional infusions are made with cold water, and then filtered. Kava-root tablets, capsules, extracts, tinctures, powders, and dried roots are available in the US, and are prepared from the grating and grinding of the dried root product into a fine powder. Products may contain up to 70% kavalactones. Standardized and non-standardized products are available. Some products are not labeled with kavalactone content, and labeled contents may be unreliable.
 
After oral administration of an aqueous extract of kava kava, kavalactones cross the blood-brain barrier. Animal studies have shown that dihydrokawain and kawain attain maximum concentration in the brain within 5 minutes, and the other kavalactones within 45 minutes. In humans, urinary analysis via gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) has identified some of the metabolites of the kavalactones. Reduction and demethylation reactions appear to be important metabolic transformations. All active kavalactones are excreted unchanged in the urine. GC-MS techniques could be utilized for urine screening of recent kava consumption.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes:
In vitro data indicate that kava kava has a high potential for causing drug interactions through inhibition of multiple hepatic microsomal CYP450 enzymes. Kava Kava caused significant inhibition in vitro of the activities of CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, CYP3A4, and CYP4A9/11; the activities of CYP2A6, CYP2C8, and CYP2E1 were unaffected.

Administration
  General Information
  NOTE: Potency of kava kava products can vary substantially from manufacturer to manufacturer. Not all kava kava products are interchangeable. The use of standardized product from a reliable manufacturer is recommended.

    Oral Administration
    Description
    Kava kava is available in a variety of forms, including capsules, extracts, tablets, roots, and powder.
Take before meals or on an empty stomach. Food may reduce kavalactone absorption.
A kava beverage of 4 servings can be made by adding one-fourth (one-quarter) of a cup of the powdered kava root to a fine nylon mesh bag, then squeezing the bag repeatedly with a wine press or by hand as 1.5 liters of cold water is poured through the bag. The beverage will have an appearance similar to coffee with cream.

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Kava kava components undergo hepatic biotransformation; the herb should be avoided in those with hepatic impairment.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments are not available. A percentage of kavalactones are excreted unchanged in the urine, and caution is advised when using this herb in renal impairment.
