Drug Class: Dextromethorphan; bupropion is an oral combination product indicated for the treatment of major depressive disorder in adults and for agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer's disease.

Routes: oral; Common adult doses: 90 mg/day PO dextromethorphan; 210 mg/day PO bupropion.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): akinesia, aphasia, aseptic meningitis, coma, dizziness, drowsiness, dysarthria, dyskinesia, eeg changes, fatigue, headache, hypoesthesia, insomnia, neuropathic pain, paresthesias, pseudoparkinsonism, restlessness, tremor, colitis, gi bleeding, gi perforation, hepatitis, pancreatitis, peptic ulcer, agitation, anxiety, delirium, euphoria, hallucinations, hostility

In an Overdose: Serotonin syndrome rarely happens with single drug therapy, and more commonly is reported with interactions between multiple serotonergic drugs or accidental or intentional drug overdoses.

    Headline
    adolescents, children, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Antidepressants, including dextromethorphan; bupropion, can increase the risk of suicidal ideation and behaviors in pediatric patients. In a pooled analysis of placebo-controlled trials of antidepressants (n = 4,500 pediatrics and 77,000 adults), there was an increased risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors in children, adolescents and adults 24 years of age and younger receiving an antidepressant versus placebo, with considerable variation in the risk of suicidality among drugs. The difference in absolute risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors across different indications was highest in those with major depression. It is unknown whether the risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors in children, adolescents, and young adults extends to longer-term use (i.e., beyond 4 months). However, there is substantial evidence from placebo-controlled maintenance studies in adult patients with major depression that depression itself is a risk factor for suicidal thoughts and behaviors and that antidepressant therapy delays recurrence of depression. All patients should be monitored for symptom worsening or suicidality, especially during the initial few months of therapy and after dose changes. Caregivers and/or patients should immediately notify the care team of changes in behavior or suicidal ideation. A change to the treatment regimen or discontinuation of dextromethorphan; bupropion may be necessary in patients with emerging suicidality or worsening depression.
    Headline
    bipolar disorder
    General Information
    Activation of mania, hypomania, or a mixed episode may occur with medications used to treat depression, especially in people predisposed to bipolar disorder. A major depressive episode may be the initial presentation of bipolar disorder. Prior to initiating treatment with dextromethorphan; bupropion, people with depressive symptoms should be screened for risk factors for bipolar disorder, including a detailed personal psychiatric history and family history of bipolar disorder, suicide, and/or depression. If an individual taking bupropion develops symptoms consistent with mania or hypomania, the medication should be discontinued, and appropriate therapy should be initiated. Bupropion use has also been reported to cause neuropsychiatric reactions, such as delusions, hallucinations, psychosis, concentration disturbance, paranoia, confusion, and other behavioral changes. Some patients experiencing the adverse events had bipolar disorder. In most cases, these symptoms resolved after dose reduction or withdrawal of treatment. High doses of dextromethorphan have also been associated with psychosis, stupor, coma, and hyperexcitability. Since the risks of adverse neuropsychiatric reactions are dose-related, screen patients for use of other dextromethorphan- or bupropion-containing products prior to starting dextromethorphan; bupropion therapy. If concomitant use with other dextromethorphan- or bupropion-containing products is clinically warranted, closely monitor patients for neuropsychiatric changes and instruct patients to contact their care team if such reactions or changes occur.
    Headline
    anorexia nervosa, arteriovenous malformation, bulimia nervosa, CNS infection, CNS tumor, diabetes mellitus, head trauma, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hypoxia, seizure disorder, stroke, substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    Dextromethorphan; bupropion is contraindicated in people with a seizure disorder, a current or prior history of bulimia nervosa or anorexia nervosa, and in people undergoing abrupt discontinuation of alcohol, benzodiazepines (benzodiazepine withdrawal), barbiturates, or antiepileptic medications, as these patients may be at higher risk of seizures. Other factors that may increase the risk of seizures include severe head injury (head trauma); arteriovenous malformation; CNS infection or CNS tumor; severe stroke; metabolic disorders (e.g., hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, severe hepatic impairment, or hypoxia); a substance abuse disorder (e.g., use of illicit drugs or the abuse or misuse of prescription medications); or use of concomitant medications that lower seizure threshold (e.g., other bupropion products, antipsychotics, tricyclic antidepressants, theophylline, tramadol, or systemic corticosteroids). Additional predisposing risks include diabetes mellitus treated with oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin, use of anorectic medications, and excessive use of alcohol, benzodiazepines, sedative/hypnotics, or opiates. The risk of seizures with bupropion use is dose-related. Prior to starting dextromethorphan; bupropion, screen patients for use of other bupropion-containing products and, if possible, avoid coadministration. If concomitant use with other bupropion is clinically warranted, inform patients of the risk of seizures. Dextromethorphan; bupropion should be discontinued and not re-initiated in patients who experience a seizure during treatment.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class C, CYP2D6 poor metabolizer, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Dextromethorphan; bupropion has not been evaluated in people with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure and use is not recommended in these individuals. No dosage adjustment is required in people with mild or moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A or B). A reduced maximum dosage is recommended for people who are a known CYP2D6 poor metabolizer.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    The use of dextromethorphan; bupropion in people with severe renal impairment or renal failure has not been studied and should be avoided. A reduction in maximum dosage is recommended for individuals with moderate renal impairment (eGFR 30 to 59 mL/minute/1.73 m2). No dosage adjustments are needed for people with mild renal impairment.
    Headline
    cardiac disease, hypertension
    General Information
    Treatment with bupropion can result in elevated blood pressure and hypertension or may exacerbate pre-existing hypertension. Caution is advised when administering dextromethorphan; bupropion to people with pre-existing hypertension or in people who are sensitive to changes in blood pressure, such as those with cardiac disease. There are no controlled studies assessing the safety of bupropion in people with a recent history of myocardial infarction or unstable cardiac disease. The risk of hypertension may also be increased if dextromethorphan; bupropion is taken concomitantly with other drugs that increase dopaminergic or noradrenergic activity. Assess blood pressure prior to initiating dextromethorphan; bupropion and monitor blood pressure periodically throughout treatment.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available clinical data on the use of dextromethorphan; bupropion is insufficient to evaluate any drug-associated risks of major birth malformations, miscarriage, or other adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. Dextromethorphan; bupropion administered to pregnant mice during organogenesis at doses of 26 mg/57 mg, 34 mg/75 mg, and 68 mg/150 mg/kg/day, respectively, did not affect body weight, weight gain, food consumption, or pregnancy and did not produce gross pathologic findings or placental or fetal findings at any dose level. The no-effect level for reproductive organ findings in mice was 68 mg/150 mg/kg in both sexes, which is approximately 3.7 times and 3.5 times, respectively the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) for dextromethorphan; bupropion on a mg/m2 basis. Data from epidemiological studies including pregnant women exposed to bupropion in the first trimester indicate no increased risk of congenital malformations. In addition, no increased risk of cardiovascular malformations during first trimester exposure to bupropion has been observed. The rate of cardiovascular malformations following 675 exposures to bupropion in the first trimester was 1.3% versus a background rate of about 1%. Data collected from the United Healthcare database and the National Birth Defects Prevention Study (11,700 infants with cardiovascular malformations and 20,093 infants with non-cardiovascular malformations) did not show an overall increased risk from cardiovascular malformations after bupropion exposure during the first trimester. Study findings on bupropion exposure during the first trimester and risk for left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) are inconsistent and do not allow conclusions regarding a possible association. The United Healthcare database lacked sufficient power to evaluate this association; the NBDPS found increased risk for LVOTO, and the Slone Epidemiology case control study did not find increased risk for LVOTO. Study findings on bupropion exposure during the first trimester and risk for ventricular septal defect (VSD) are inconsistent and do not allow conclusions regarding a possible association. The Slone Epidemiology Study found an increased risk for VSD following first trimester maternal bupropion exposure but did not find increased risk for any other cardiovascular malformations studied (including LVOTO). The NBDPS and United Healthcare database study did not find an association between first trimester maternal bupropion exposure and VSD. For the findings of LVOTO and VSD, the studies were limited by the small number of exposed cases, inconsistent findings among studies, and the potential for chance findings from multiple comparisons in case control studies. No clear evidence of teratogenic activity was found in reproductive developmental studies conducted in rats and rabbits. However, in rabbits, slightly increased incidences of fetal malformations and skeletal variations were observed at doses approximately 2 times the MRHD and greater, while decreased fetal weights were seen at doses 5 times the MRHD and greater. Since dextromethorphan acts as a low affinity antagonist to the glutamate receptor subtype N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) in the CNS, there has been some concern about its safe use during pregnancy. Dextromethorphan exhibited adverse developmental effects in avian embryos; however, the avian study data have limited applicability to human gestation. Human surveillance data and retrospective studies have shown dextromethorphan to be relatively safe during the first trimester; a human epidemiologic study and a smaller controlled study have not demonstrated elevated risks of congenital malformations. In one controlled study, there were no cases of neural tube defects, and no differences in number of live births, spontaneous or elective abortions, stillbirths, or major or minor malformations among infants exposed to dextromethorphan during the first trimester and those who were not. The results suggested that use during pregnancy does not pose a risk to the fetus; however, due to the small sample size, an increased risk of rare malformations could not be ruled out. Dextromethorphan; quinidine administered to female rats during organogenesis, pregnancy, and lactation resulted in embryo-fetal deaths at the highest studied dose of 50 mg/100 mg/kg/day and reduced skeletal ossification, pup survival, and pup weight at all studied doses. Developmental delay was observed in offspring at the mid and high doses. An increased incidence of fetal malformations was observed following oral administration of dextromethorphan; quinidine to pregnant rabbits during organogenesis. Due to the lack of data specific to the dextromethorphan; bupropion combination and the risks noted in animal studies, dextromethorphan; bupropion is not recommended during pregnancy. If a female becomes pregnant while taking dextromethorphan; bupropion, treatment should be discontinued and the patient should be counseled about potential risks to the fetus. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant patients exposed to antidepressants, including dextromethorphan; bupropion. Information about the registry can be obtained at https://womensmentalhealth.org/research/pregnancyregistry/antidepressants/ or by calling 1-866-961-2388.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Advise patients to use caution when driving or operating machinery or performing other activities requiring coordination and concentration until they know how dextromethorphan; bupropion will affect them. Dextromethorphan; bupropion may cause dizziness. Patients at risk of falls, especially those with motor impairment affecting gait, balance issues, or a history of falls, should take extra precaution to avoid injury. Consumption of alcohol during treatment with dextromethorphan; bupropion should be minimized or avoided; bupropion may decrease alcohol tolerance, and concomitant use of alcohol with bupropion may increase the risk of neuropsychiatric events.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Due to the potential for neurotoxicity, breast-feeding should be avoided during dextromethorphan; bupropion treatment and for 5 days after the final dose. Data from published literature report the presence of bupropion and its metabolites in human milk; however, there are no data on the effects of bupropion or its metabolites on milk production. Limited data from postmarketing reports of bupropion use in breast-feeding patients have not identified a clear association of adverse events in the breastfed infant. Peak breast milk concentrations of bupropion and its metabolites are present within 2 to 4 hours after an oral dose. In a lactation study of 10 women, levels of bupropion and its active metabolites were measured in expressed milk. Average daily infant exposure to bupropion and its metabolites (assuming 150 mL/kg daily consumption) was 2% of the maternal weight-adjusted dose.  One case report describes a possible seizure in a breast-fed infant during maternal use of extended-release bupropion. In two other cases, no infant-related adverse events were noted during breast-feeding. Postmarketing reports have also described seizures in breast-fed infants, but the relationship of bupropion exposure and these seizures is not clear. It is not known whether dextromethorphan is present in human milk. The effects of dextromethorphan on the breast-fed infant or effects on milk production are also unknown. Neurotoxicity was observed in animal studies of juvenile rats that were treated with a combination of dextromethorphan/quinidine on postnatal day 7, which corresponds to the third trimester of gestation through the first few months of life and may extend through the first 3 years of life in humans.
    Headline
    closed-angle glaucoma, narrow iridocorneal angles
    General Information
    Caution is recommended when prescribing dextromethorphan; bupropion to people with closed-angle glaucoma. Avoid use in people with untreated anatomically narrowed angles. The pupillary dilation that can occur with antidepressants, including bupropion, may precipitate a closed-angle glaucoma attack in patients with anatomically narrow iridocorneal angles who do not have a patent iridectomy.
    Headline
    reproductive risk
    General Information
    Due to the potential reproductive risk with dextromethorphan; bupropion, use alternative therapy in patients who are planning to become pregnant. Based on animal studies, dextromethorphan; bupropion may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy. In addition, the separate effect of dextromethorphan on developmental toxicity at the recommended clinical dose is unclear.
    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    In clinical studies of dextromethorphan; bupropion, no overall differences in safety or effectiveness were observed between geriatric and younger adult subjects. However, the greater sensitivity of some older individuals cannot be ruled out. Dextromethorphan; bupropion may cause hyponatremia and SIADH, and geriatric adults may be particularly at risk of these effects. Closely monitor sodium concentrations during treatment in older adults. The U.S.Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates antidepressant use in long-term care facilities. When used to manage behavior, stabilize mood, or treat a psychiatric disorder, tapering as outlined in the OBRA guidelines should be attempted unless clinically contraindicated. Dosages and durations of treatment should align with prescribing labels, published literature recommendations, and expert guidelines.

Interactions
Abiraterone: (Moderate) Abiraterone inhbits CYP2D6 and dextromethorphan is a CYP2D6 substrate. Monitor for dextromethorphan-related side effects, such as drowsiness, nausea or vomiting, sweating, restlessness, or tremor. If dextromethorphan- related side effects occur, a dose reduction or discontinuation of dextromethorphan may be necessary. In an in vivo drug-drug interaction trial, the Cmax and AUC of the CYP2D6 substrate dextromethorphan were increased 2.8- and 2.9-fold, respectively when dextromethorphan 30 mg was given with abiraterone acetate 1,000 mg daily along with prednisone 5 mg twice daily. The AUC for dextrorphan, the active metabolite of dextromethorphan, increased approximately 1.3 fold.

Excessive use of psychostimulants, including caffeine, is associated with an increased seizure risk and may increase this risk during the concurrent use of bupropion. Carefully consider a patient's caffeine intake from all sources, including medicines. Monitor for irritability, tremor, increased blood pressure, insomnia and seizures. Many non-prescription medicines and weight loss aids may contain caffeine and patients should read labels carefully. Examples of foods and beverages containing caffeine include coffee, teas, colas, energy drinks, chocolate, and some herbal or dietary supplements. Patients should be advised to limit excessive caffeine intake during bupropion therapy.

    General Information
    Monitor all patients treated with an antidepressant for any indication for worsening of depression or the condition being treated and the emergence of suicidal behaviors or suicidal ideation, especially during the initial few months of drug therapy and after dosage changes. In a pooled analysis of placebo controlled trials of antidepressants (n = 4,500 pediatric patients and 77,000 adult patients), there was an increased risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors in patients 24 years of age and younger receiving an antidepressant versus placebo, with considerable variation in the risk of suicidality among drugs. Completed suicide, depression, homicidal ideation, suicide ideation, and suicide attempts have been reported with postmarketing use of bupropion. Patients, family members, and caregivers should be advised to monitor any patient taking dextromethorphan; bupropion for worsening of depressive symptoms and emergence of suicidal ideation or actions and report these to their healthcare team immediately.

    Headline
    serotonin syndrome
    General Information
    Dextromethorphan is associated with serotonergic effects. Excessive dosage due to higher than recommended doses or substance abuse (e.g., in combination with products containing narcotics or sympathomimetics) may result in additional adverse effects consistent with serotonin syndrome such as, confusion, excitement, nervousness, restlessness, irritability, nausea, vomiting, and dysarthria (slurred speech). In an ISMP safety report, bupropion was noted as 1 of 19 overall drugs and one of the 9 antidepressants having the strongest signals for serotonin syndrome with 18 cases reported over 1 year to the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS). Serotonin syndrome rarely happens with single drug therapy, and more commonly is reported with interactions between multiple serotonergic drugs or accidental or intentional drug overdoses. How bupropion might promote serotonergic excess is unclear, as bupropion is a relatively weak inhibitor of the neuronal reuptake of norepinephrine and dopamine, and does not inhibit the reuptake of serotonin. Bupropion does not inhibit monoamine oxidase. The manufacturers have not reported serotonin syndrome as a postmarketing event. Prior to initiating dextromethorphan; bupropion, patients should be screened for any use of other dextromethorphan-containing products. If concomitant use of dextromethorphan; bupropion with other serotonergic medications is necessary, patients should be counseled on the risks of serotonin syndrome and monitored for symptoms, which may include altered mental status, hypertension, restlessness, myoclonus, hyperthermia, hyperreflexia, diaphoresis, shivering, and tremor. Dextromethorphan; bupropion and/or the concomitant serotonergic drug should be discontinued immediately if these symptoms occur, and supportive symptomatic care should be initiated.

    laboratory test interference
    General Information
    Laboratory test interference may occur with dextromethorphan; bupropion use. False-positive urine immunoassay screening tests for amphetamines have been reported in patients taking bupropion. The false-positive result is due to lack of specificity of some screening tests. False-positive test results may result even following discontinuation of bupropion therapy. Description
  Text
  Dextromethorphan; bupropion is an oral combination product indicated for the treatment of major depressive disorder in adults and for agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer's disease. Dextromethorphan is a noncompetitive N-methyl D-aspartate (NDMA) receptor antagonist and sigma-1 receptor agonist and bupropion is an antidepressant from the aminoketone class and a CYP2D6 inhibitor. When used together, bupropion inhibits the metabolism of dextromethorphan, increasing serum levels and clinical effect. The combination was demonstrated to be effective for depression in a placebo-controlled trial that included adult patients who met DSM-5 criteria for major depressive disorder (MDD). Patients received 45 mg dextromethorphan/105 mg bupropion orally twice daily or placebo for 6 weeks. The primary outcome measure was change from baseline to week 6 in Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) total score. Study results indicate that dextromethorphan; bupropion was statistically significantly superior to placebo in improvement in depressive symptoms as measured by decrease in total MADRS score at week 6. Of note, the change in MADRS total score from baseline to week 1 and baseline to week 2 were statistically significant. This rapid onset of symptom improvement is different from other antidepressant medications on the market, which often take 4 to 6 weeks to take effect. In a second, 6-week study of adult patients with MDD, 45 mg dextromethorphan/105 mg bupropion orally twice daily was compared to 105 mg bupropion sustained-release orally twice daily. The primary outcome was change from baseline MADRS total score over the 6-week period. Outcomes from the study indicate that dextromethorphan contributes to the antidepressant efficacy of dextromethorphan; bupropion. In the treatment of agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer's disease, efficacy was established in two clinical trials of patients with a diagnosis of probable Alzheimer's disease (AD), agitation, and a Mini-Mental State Examination score of 10 to 24, with a total score of 4 or more on the agitation/aggression item of the Neuropsychiatric Inventory. In a 5-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, patients receiving dextromethorphan; bupropion demonstrated a statistically significantly superior improvement from baseline on the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory (CMAI) when compared to placebo. On a key secondary endpoint of response on the modified Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study-Clinical Global Impression of Change (mADCS-CGIC), a statistically significantly greater proportion of AD patients receiving dextromethorphan; bupropion were rated as at least minimally improved compared to placebo. In a long-term, double-blind, randomized withdrawal study of known responders to dextromethorphan; bupropion, patients who continued treatment had a statically significantly longer time to relapse of agitation symptoms than individuals switched to placebo. The most common side effects of dextromethorphan; bupropion include dizziness, drowsiness, headache, dry mouth, diarrhea, sexual dysfunction, and hyperhidrosis. Since bupropion may cause seizures, dextromethorphan; bupropion is contraindicated in people with a seizure disorder, a current or prior diagnosis of bulimia or anorexia nervosa, and those who are undergoing abrupt discontinuation of alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, and antiepileptic medications. Dextromethorphan; bupropion was FDA approved in 2022.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Dextromethorphan; bupropion is a combination of dextromethorphan, a non-competitive N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist and a sigma-1 receptor agonist, and bupropion, a relatively weak norepinephrine and dopamine neuronal reuptake inhibitor. When used together, bupropion increases plasma levels of dextromethorphan via competitive inhibition of cytochrome P450 2D6, which is the major metabolic pathway for dextromethorphan. The mechanism of dextromethorphan in the treatment of major depressive disorder is unclear.

    Description
    Following oral administration of dextromethorphan; bupropion, dextromethorphan exhibits non-linear pharmacokinetics at steady-state with greater than dose-proportional changes in AUC and Cmax for varying doses of dextromethorphan [60 mg to 120 mg (0.67 to 1.33 times the maximum recommended dose of dextromethorphan; bupropion)] and less than dose-proportional changes for varying doses of bupropion [150 mg to 300 mg (0.71 to 1.43 times the maximum recommended dose of dextromethorphan; bupropion)]. Steady-state plasma concentration of dextromethorphan and bupropion are achieved within 8 days with dextromethorphan; bupropion. Following administration of dextromethorphan; bupropion, the accumulation ratios for dextromethorphan at steady-state are 20 and 32, respectively, based on Cmax and AUC0-12, compared to 1.3 and 1.4, respectively, for dextromethorphan given without bupropion. The accumulation ratios for bupropion at steady-state are 1.1 and 1.5, respectively based on Cmax and AUC0-12. Median Tmax was 3 hours for dextromethorphan and 2 hours for bupropion following administration of dextromethorphan; bupropion extended-release tablets. Cmax of the hydroxybupropion metabolite occurred approximately 3 hours post-dose and was approximately 14 times the peak level of bupropion and AUC was almost 19 times that of bupropion. The Cmax of the erythrohydroxybupropion and threohydroxybupropion metabolites occurred approximately 4 hours post-dose and were approximately equal to and 5 times greater than the parent compound, respectively and AUC values were 1.2 and 7 times that of bupropion, respectively. When dextromethorphan; bupropion was taken with food, Cmax was unchanged for dextromethorphan and increased by 3% for bupropion, while the AUC0-12 was reduced by 14 % and increased by 6%, respectively. Dextromethorphan; bupropion may be taken with or without food.

  Text
  Dextromethorphan; bupropion extended-release tablets are administered orally. Dextromethorphan is primarily metabolized in the liver by CYP2D6 to dextrorphan. Bupropion is a CYP2D6 inhibitor, thereby increasing plasma concentrations of dextromethorphan.
Dextromethorphan: Plasma protein binding of dextromethorphan is 60 to 70%. In CYP2D6 extensive metabolizers, approximately 37 to 52% of the orally administered dose of dextromethorphan is recovered in the urine. Less than 2% of the administered dose is excreted as unchanged parent drug in the urine. In contrast, for CYP2D6 poor metabolizers approximately 45 to 83% of the administered dose is recovered in the urine. Approximately 26% of the administered dose is excreted as unchanged parent drug in the urine. Excretion of dextromethorphan is primarily by renal elimination of metabolites, and the mean elimination half-life was 22 hours when administered in combination with bupropion (a 3-fold increase from the elimination half-life of dextromethorphan when used as monotherapy).
Bupropion: Plasma protein binding of bupropion is 84%. Bupropion is metabolized by CYP2B6 and is a potent inhibitor of CYP2D6. Bupropion is extensively metabolized to 3 active metabolites; hydroxybupropion, which is formed via hydroxylation of the tert-butyl group of bupropion, and the amino-alcohol isomers, threohydroxybupropion, and erythrohydroxybupropion, which are formed via reduction of the carbonyl group. In vitro findings indicate that CYP2B6 is the principal isoenzyme involved in the formation of hydroxybupropion, while CYP450 enzymes are not involved in the formation of the other metabolites. Based on results of an antidepressant screening test in mice, hydroxybupropion appears to be one-half as potent as bupropion, while threohydroxybupropion and erythrohydroxybupropion are 5-fold less potent. The major urinary metabolite is formed from oxidation of the bupropion side chain, which results in the formation of a glycine conjugate of meta-chlorobenzoic acid. Following oral administration of 200 mg bupropion, 87% and 10% of the radioactive dose was recovered in the urine and feces, respectively; only 0.5% of the oral dose was excreted as unchanged bupropion.The mean elimination half-life of bupropion is 15 hours. Half-lives of hydroxybupropion, erythrohydroxybupropion, and threohydroxybupropion are 35 hours, 44 hours, and 33 hours, respectively.
 
Affected Cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2D6, CYP2B6, OCT2, P-glycoprotein (P-gp)
Dextromethorphan: Dextromethorphan is primarily metabolized by CYP2D6 isoenzyme and is a sensitive substrate; thus, clinically significant drug interactions are possible when coadministered with potent CYP2D6 inhibitors or inducers. Dextromethorphan is also a P-gp substrate. Dextromethorphan does not inhibit or induce CYP450 isoenzymes.
Bupropion: Bupropion undergoes extensive metabolism by CYP2B6; clinically significant drug interactions are possible with drugs that are metabolized by or are inhibitors or inducers of this isoenzyme. In vitro data indicate that bupropion and hydroxybupropion are inhibitors of CYP2D6. In vitro data has also shown that bupropion and its 3 metabolites are also inhibitors of the renal organic transporter OCT2 to a clinically significant extent; however, in vivo drug interaction studies have not found clinically significant drug-drug interactions with OCT-2 substrates.
