        Dosage Description
        Initially, 2.5 mg subcutaneously once weekly for 4 weeks. Then, titrate dosage in a step-wise manner over at least 5 weeks to reach 20 mg subcutaneously once daily. Recommended titration: 2.5 mg twice weekly for 1 week followed by 10 mg once weekly for 1 week, then 10 mg twice weekly for 1 week, then 10 mg given 4 times/week for 1 week, and then 10 mg once daily for 1 week. After titration, the recommended maintenance dose is 20 mg subcutaneously once daily for at least 24 weeks. The dose may be increased to 40 mg once daily in individuals who have received 20 mg/day for at least 24 weeks. In individuals who have not achieved a blood phenylalanine concentration of 600 micromol/L or less after at least 16 weeks on 40 mg/day, the dose may be increased to 60 mg once daily. Max: 60 mg/day. If there is an inadequate response after 16 weeks of 60 mg once daily, discontinue pegvaliase therapy. Based on individual tolerability, additional time may be required before each dosage escalation.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis
    General Information
    Pegvaliase is associated with a risk of serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis. Premedication with a histamine antagonist selective for H1- or H2-receptors and/or antipyretic prior to injection should be considered. Administer the initial dose of pegvaliase under the supervision of a health care provider equipped to manage anaphylaxis; monitor the individual for at least 60 minutes following the first administration. Anaphylaxis requires immediate treatment with epinephrine. Prior to the first pegvaliase dose, the treated individual should be prescribed epinephrine. Consider the risks and benefits of readministering the drug to individuals after a severe reaction. If the decision is made to readminister pegvaliase, administration should be performed under the supervision of a health care provider equipped to manage anaphylaxis and the individual should be closely monitored for a minimum of 60 minutes after administration. An adult observer may be helpful for individuals who may need assistance in recognizing and managing anaphylaxis during pegvaliase treatment. The individual and observer should be instructed on how to recognize the signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis, how to properly administer epinephrine, and to seek immediate medical care. If an adult observer is needed, the observer should be present during and for at least 60 minutes after pegvaliase administration.
    Headline
    adolescents, children
    General Information
    Frequent blood phenylalanine monitoring is recommended in children and adolescents. During titration and maintenance of pegvaliase treatment, individuals may experience blood phenylalanine concentrations below 30 micromol/L. Prolonged low blood phenylalanine levels have been associated with catabolism and endogenous protein breakdown, which has been associated with adverse developmental outcomes. For blood phenylalanine levels below 30 micromol/L, the dosage of pegvaliase may be reduced and/or dietary protein and phenylalanine intake may be modified to maintain blood phenylalanine levels within a clinically acceptable range and above 30 micromol/L.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use pegvaliase with caution during breast-feeding. There are no data on the presence of pegvaliase in human milk, its effects on the breastfed child, or its effects on milk production. In animal studies, pegvaliase was present in rat milk and administration of pegvaliase during lactation decreased pup weight and survival. However, systemic absorption of pegvaliase was not detected in the rat pups. Pegvaliase may cause low phenylalanine (Phe) concentrations in human milk. Monitor blood Phe concentrations in breast-feeding individuals treated with pegvaliase. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the patient's clinical need for treatment, and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from the medication or from the patient's underlying medical condition.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available data from pharmacovigilance and published literature on the use of pegvaliase during pregnancy have not identified a clear association with major birth defects, miscarriage, or other adverse outcomes. There are risks to the fetus associated with suboptimal control of phenylalanine (Phe) concentrations during pregnancy, such as increased risk of miscarriage, major birth defects, intrauterine fetal growth delay, and future intellectual disability. In animal studies, adverse effects occurred at exposures up to 5 times the maximum recommended daily dose based on body weight in rabbits and at exposures ranging from 2.8 to 13 times the human steady state area under the curve at the maximum recommended daily dose in rats. Observed adverse effects included skeletal variations (without malformation), fetal malformations involving the skeletal system, kidneys, lungs, and eyes, as well as embryo-fetal toxicity (increased resorption and reduced fetal weight), decreased litter size, decreased offspring survival during lactation, and delayed sexual maturation. These findings were accompanied by significant maternal toxicity, including marked reductions in weight gain and food consumption, and mortality. Monitor phenylalanine (Phe) concentrations closely during pregnancy. To reduce the risk of hyperphenylalaninemia-induced fetal effects, maintain blood Phe concentrations between 120 and 360 micromol/L during pregnancy and during the 3 months prior to conception. Maintain Phe concentrations above 30 micromol/L. A pregnancy registry monitors outcomes of pegvaliase exposure during pregnancy. Report exposure by calling 1-866-906-6100 if pegvaliase is administered during pregnancy or if pregnancy occurs during treatment or within 1 month after the last dose.

Interactions
Conjugated Estrogens; medroxyPROGESTERone: (Major) The use of medroxyprogesterone acetate suspension injection (a formulation containing PEG 3350) may increase the risk for serious hypersensitivity reactions and anaphylaxis when given with pegvaliase. Make sure any patient receiving pegvaliase has an emergency supply of epinephrine and knows how to use it in the case of a serious allergic reaction or anaphylaxis. In a single dose study of pegvaliase in adult patients with PKU, 2 patients receiving medroxyprogesterone acetate suspension injection (a formulation containing PEG 3350) experienced hypersensitivity reactions. One of the 2 patients experienced a hypersensitivity reaction on day 15 after a single pegvaliase dose of 0.67 mg within 15 minutes following medroxyprogesterone acetate injectable suspension, and subsequently experienced anaphylaxis on day 89 within 30 minutes after the next dose of medroxyprogesterone acetate injectable suspension. The other patient experienced a hypersensitivity reaction on day 40 after a single pegvaliase dosage of 0.08 mg within 10 minutes following medroxyprogesterone acetate injectable suspension. Both patients had high anti-PEG IgG antibody titers at or around the time of the hypersensitivity reactions. Most pegvaliase-treated patients developed anti-PEG IgM and IgG antibodies after treatment with the drug. The clinical effects of concomitant treatment with different PEGylated products is unknown. Monitor patients treated with pegvaliase and concomitantly with other PEGylated products for hypersensitivity reactions including anaphylaxis.

    General Information
    Pegvaliase can cause serious hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis, including anaphylactoid reactions, anaphylactic shock, and angioedema. In clinical trials, 29 of 285 patients (10%) experienced anaphylactic reactions. Anaphylaxis usually occurred within 1 hour after injection (81%; 34/42 episodes); however, delayed episodes occurred up to 48 hours post-dose. The exposure-adjusted rate of anaphylaxis was highest during the induction and titration phases and decreased in the maintenance phase. Most cases (69%) occurred within the first year, but events were reported up to 1,604 days (4.4 years) after treatment initiation. Symptoms included syncope, hypotension, hypoxia, dyspnea, wheezing, chest discomfort/chest tightness, tachycardia, angioedema, throat tightness, flushing, urticaria, pruritus, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Management included epinephrine, corticosteroids, antihistamines, and/or oxygen. Of those rechallenged, 29% had recurrence of anaphylaxis. All episodes resolved without sequelae. In premedicated pediatric patients (age 12 to 17 years), anaphylaxis was reported in 11% of individuals. Non-anaphylactic hypersensitivity reactions were reported in 72% (204/285) of patients, with rates highest during the induction and titration phases and decreased in the maintenance phase. Hypersensitivity reactions generally occurred more frequently in pegvaliase-treated individuals with higher anti-pegvaliase-pqpz antibody titers. In pediatric patients (age 12 to 17 years), non-anaphylactic hypersensitivity reactions were reported in 33% of patients. The exposure adjusted rate during the induction and titration phase was 1 episode/patient year and 1.4 episodes/patient year during the maintenance phase. Angioedema independent of anaphylaxis occurred in 8% (22/285; 45 episodes), more frequently during induction/titration; three patients discontinued treatment and all episodes resolved. Pruritus was reported in 20% of patients during induction/titration phase and 27% during the maintenance phase. Hypersensitivity reactions were reported in 153 of 285 (54%) patients treated with pegvaliase during the induction/titration phase and 145 of 225 (64%) patients during the maintenance phase. Hypersensitivity reaction reports included urticaria, anaphylaxis, rash (generalized), hypersensitivity, rash erythematous, maculopapular rash, rash pruritic, swelling face, contact dermatitis, swollen tongue, lip swelling, rash macular, pharyngeal edema, injection site hypersensitivity, eczema or atopic dermatitis, drug eruption, allergic dermatitis, tongue edema, palatal edema, mouth edema, multiple allergies, lip edema, eye edema, exfoliative rash, drug hypersensitivity, dermatitis acneiform (acneiform rash), allergic pruritus, mouth swelling, injection site rash, gingival swelling, face edema, eyelid edema (blepharedema), ocular swelling, dermatitis psoriaiform (psoriaform rash), dermatitis infected, allergic conjunctivitis, bronchospasm, angioedema, allergic sinusitis, and allergic-related coughing. Serum sickness was reported in 2% of patients and was more frequent during the induction/titration phase (0.04 episodes/patient-year) and decreased over time (less than 0.01 episodes/patient-year in the maintenance phase). The duration ranged from 1 to 8 days, and all cases resolved without sequelae. Five individuals continued treatment without a recurrence and 2 individuals discontinued treatment. The most common adverse reactions leading to treatment discontinuation were hypersensitivity reactions (6%) including anaphylaxis (3%) and angioedema (1%). Hypersensitivity reactions (9%) commonly led to dosage reduction and hypersensitivity reactions (14%) and anaphylaxis (4%) were among the most common causes of temporary drug interruption.

    erythema, hematoma, injection site reaction, paresthesias, skin necrosis
    General Information
    Subcutaneous administration of pegvaliase can cause injection site reactions including symptoms such as erythema, pruritus, pain, bruising, rash, swelling, urticaria, induration, hemorrhage, edema, mass, inflammation, nodule, discoloration, warmth, hematoma, irritation, vesicles, hypersensitivity, papule, discomfort, scar, paresthesias, hypertrophy, extravasation, dryness and scab. During clinical trials, injection site reactions occurred in 252 of 285 (88%) patients receiving pegvaliase during the induction/titration phase and 166 of 225 (74%) patients during the maintenance phase. Injection site reactions were reported as early as after the first pegvaliase dose and occurred at any time during treatment. Injection site reactions were most frequent during the induction/titration phase (21 episodes/patient-years) and decreased over time (3.3 episodes/patient-years in the maintenance phase). The mean duration of injection site reaction was 10 days (median: 2 days, range 1 to 1,612 days), and 8% of injection site reactions had a duration of at least 14 days. At the time of the last observation (up to 77 months of follow-up), 99% of injection site reactions were resolved. Three injection site reactions consistent with granulomatous skin lesions were reported (each reaction occurring in 1 patient). These included granulomatous dermatitis (occurred after 464 days of pegvaliase treatment; lasted 16 days), xanthogranuloma (occurred after 378 days of pegvaliase treatment; lasted 638 days) and necrobiosis lipoidica diabeticorum (occurred after 281 days of pegvaliase treatment and lasted 281 days). Xanthogranuloma was treated with a topical antihistamine, corticosteroid, and pegvaliase treatment was discontinued. Necrobiosis lipoidica diabeticorum was treated with steroid injections and complicated by Pseudomonas infection. All 3 injection site reactions resolved. One patient reported soft tissue infection (occurred after 196 days of pegvaliase treatment; lasted 8 days) associated with mesenteric panniculitis treated with antibiotics, which resulted in treatment discontinuation. During clinical trials, injection site reactions led to discontinuation of treatment in 1% of patients. Additionally, injection site reactions (4% of patients) were some of the most common reactions leading to dosage reduction and leading to temporary drug interruption. In pediatric patients (age 12 to 17 years), injection site reactions occurred in 86% of patients (31/36). Injection site infection, cellulitis, skin necrosis, and abscess were reported during postmarketing reports. Some cases required hospitalization, surgical debridement, intravenous antibiotics, and discontinuation of therapy.

    arthralgia, back pain, musculoskeletal pain
    General Information
    Arthralgia occurred in 86% of patients, including back pain, musculoskeletal pain, pain in extremity, and neck pain. During the induction/titration phase, 74% of patients and 68% of patients during the maintenance phase reported arthralgia. Arthralgia episodes were more frequent during the induction/titration phase (7.4 episodes/patient-year) and decreased over time (1.4 episodes/patient-year in the maintenance phase). Out of 285 patients, 44 (15%) had 1 episode of arthralgia, 32 (11%) had 2 episodes of arthralgia, 18 (6%) had 3 episodes of arthralgia, and 146 (51%) had 4 or more episodes of arthralgia. Arthralgia occurred as early as after the first dose of pegvaliase and anytime after treatment. The mean duration of arthralgia was 16 days (median 3 days, range 1 to 936 days); 19% of arthralgia episodes had a duration of at least 14 days. Severe arthralgia, defined as severe pain limiting self-care activities of daily living, was reported by 11 (4%) patients. Other joint-related signs and symptoms that were reported included joint swelling (24 patients; 8%), joint stiffness (22 patients; 8%), and musculoskeletal stiffness (20 patients; 7%). Arthralgia episodes were managed with medications (e.g., nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, glucocorticoids, and acetaminophen), dosage reduction (4% of episodes), temporary drug interruption (4% of episodes), or treatment discontinuation (0.6% of episodes). At the time of last observation (up to 77 months of follow-up), 97% of arthralgia episodes were reported as resolved. In pediatric patients (age 12 to 17 years), arthralgia (75%), myalgia (19%), and back pain (17%) were reported during a clinical trial.

    Headline
    antibody formation
    General Information
    As with all therapeutic proteins, there is potential for immunogenicity and antibody formation with pegvaliase. In clinical trials (including adults and some 16 to 17 year olds), 100% of individuals treated with pegvaliase developed anti-pegvaliase-pqpz antibodies; 91% seroconverted by Week 4. Mean anti-pegvaliase-pqpz antibody titers peaked 2 weeks after initiation and remained elevated throughout treatment (more than 3 years after initiation). Anti-phenylalanine ammonia lyase (anti-PAL) IgM antibodies were detected in 98% by 2 months (declining to 67% at 36 months). Anti-PAL IgG antibodies were detected in 99.6% of individuals by 4 months, with IgM and IgG titers peaking at approximately 3 and 6 months, respectively. Drug-induced anti-PEG IgM and IgG antibodies occurred in 98% of individuals (peaking at 1 to 3 months). In pediatric patients (age 12 to 17 years), 100% of individuals developed pegvaliase-pqpz antibodies, anti-PAL IgG and IgM, anti-PEG IgG and IgM. The overall time course of anti-drug antibody development was similar to that in adult patients; however, the anti-pegvaliase-pqpz antibody titers were generally higher in patients who were 12 to 17 years. Neutralizing antibodies (NAb) that are capable of inhibiting PAL enzyme activity were detected in 89% of individuals (peaking 16 to 20 weeks) and then remained present throughout treatment (more than 3 years after treatment initiation). In clinical trials, 27 of 29 individuals who experienced anaphylaxis were tested for anti-pegvaliase-pqpz IgE antibodies; 26 tested negative. One individual had a positive screening result, but there was not a sufficient sample to confirm IgE positivity. This patient tested negative for anti-pegvaliase-pqpz IgE at routine visits prior to and after the anaphylaxis episode. In pediatric patients, 3 individuals who experienced anaphylaxis were tested and all were negative for pegvaliase-specific IgE antibodies. Among 285 patients, 67 patients were tested for anti-PAL IgE antibodies and anti-pegvaliase IgE antibodies, 5 (8%) tested positive at least once for anti-PAL IgE antibodies, but negative for anti-pegvaliase-pqpz IgE antibodies. Higher antibody responses for all antibody analytes, including NAb, were associated with lower mean trough pegvaliase concentrations and with higher blood phenylalanine (Phe) concentrations. Hypersensitivity reactions occurred more frequently in patients with higher antibody titers for some, but not all antibody analytes. The effect of anti-drug antibodies on the safety of pegvaliase in pediatric patients has not been characterized.

Description
  Text
  Pegvaliase is a phenylalanine-metabolizing enzyme indicated to reduce blood phenylalanine concentrations in individuals with phenylketonuria (PKU) who have uncontrolled blood phenylalanine concentrations greater than 600 micromol/L on existing management, such as medical foods and phenylalanine-restricted diets. It works by converting phenylalanine to ammonia and trans-cinnamic acid. In the PRISM phase 3 studies, mean blood phenylalanine (Phe) decreased from 1,232.7 micromol per liter at baseline to 564.5 micromol per liter at 12 months and 311.4 micromol per liter at 24 months, representing reductions of 51.1 percent and 68.7 percent, respectively. Within 24 months, 68.4% of participants achieved blood Phe 600 micromol/L or less, 60.7% of participants achieved blood Phe 360 micromol/L or less, which is below the upper limit recommended in the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics PKU management guidelines, and 51.2% achieved blood Phe 120 micromol/L, below the upper limit of normal in the unaffected population. Improvements in neuropsychiatric outcomes were associated with Phe reduction and sustained with long term therapy. In pediatric patients aged 12 to 17 years, pegvaliase produced significantly greater reductions in blood Phe at Week 72 compared with diet alone, with a p value less than 0.0001. The most common side effects are arthralgia, injection-site reactions, hypersensitivity reactions, and headache. Anaphylaxis has been reported after administration of pegvaliase and may occur at any time during treatment. The initial dose must be administered under the supervision of a health care provider and closely observed for at least 60 minutes following injection. Anaphylaxis occurred most frequently during upward titration of the dose within the first year of treatment. There is a boxed warning regarding this risk of anaphylaxis and the product is only available via a Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) called the Palynziq REMS Program. Pegvaliase was FDA-approved in 2018.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Phenylketonuria (PKU) is caused by deficiency or reduced activity of the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH), which metabolizes phenylalanine (Phe). PAH deficiency leads to elevated Phe concentrations, which are toxic to the brain. Pegvaliase, a PEGylated phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) enzyme, converts Phe to ammonia and trans-cinnamic acid. It substitutes for the deficient PAH enzyme activity in patients with PKU and reduces blood phenylalanine concentrations.
 
Pharmacodynamics
In individuals with PKU, pegvaliase treatment is associated with increases in ammonia and trans-cinnamic acid concentrations relative to baseline. The time course of these increases and the exposure-response relationships for safety have not been fully characterized.

    Pegvaliase exhibits high inter-patient and intra-patient variability due to the heterogeneity of the immune response in adult patients with phenylketonuria (PKU).     Administration Details
      Name
      Subcutaneous Administration
      Description
      Preparation for injections and required monitoring
Administer the initial dose of pegvaliase or any readministration after an anaphylaxis episode subcutaneously under the supervision of a health care provider who is equipped to manage anaphylaxis. The individual should be closely observed for at least 60 minutes after administration.
For hypersensitivity reactions, consider premedication with a histamine antagonist selective for H1- or H2-receptors and/or an antipyretic prior to administration, based upon individual tolerability.
Prior to the first dose, the individual should be prescribed epinephrine. An adult observer may be helpful for individuals who may need assistance in recognizing and managing anaphylaxis during pegvaliase treatment. The patient and observer should be instructed on how to recognize the signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis, how to properly administer epinephrine, and to seek immediate medical care after its use. The observer should be present during and for at least 60 minutes after each pegvaliase administration.
Prior to allowing self-injection after the first dose, confirm patient competency with self-administration.
 
Subcutaneous Administration
Prior to administering a dose, check to ensure the right prefilled syringe(s) needed for the dose are chosen.
Allow the syringe(s) to sit at room temperature in the carton for at least 30 minutes. Do not warm by other means.
Do not shake.
Wash hands and prepare the skin with an alcohol swab.
Open the container and inspect the syringe. It is normal to see air bubbles in the pre-filled syringe. Do not try to expel them.
Pull the needle cap off.
Pinch up the skin around the chosen injection site.
Subcutaneous injections can be made into the front middle of thighs and the abdomen at least 5 centimeters (2 inches) away from the navel. If a caregiver is administering the injection, the top of buttocks and the back of the upper arms are also appropriate injection sites.
If more than 1 injection is needed for a single dose, the injection sites should be at least 5 centimeters (2 inches) away from each other; the second injection site can be on the same part of the body or a different part of the body.
Do not inject into moles, scars, birthmarks, bruises, rashes, or areas where the skin is hard, tender, red, damaged, burned, inflamed, or tattooed. Check the site for signs of injection site infection (i.e. redness, swelling, or tenderness). Do not administer into the affected area until the infection has resolved.
Rotate administration sites with each injection.
Use a quick motion to fully insert the needle into the pinched skin at a 45- to 90-degree angle.
Use thumb to push in the plunger slowly and steadily to inject all the medicine.
Slowly move thumb up to release the plunger. The needle will automatically be covered by the prefilled syringe.
Discard any unused pegvaliase; the prefilled syringes are preservative-free and single-dose only. Dispose in a puncture resistant container, such as an FDA-cleared sharps disposal container immediately after use.
Storage: Syringes may be stored at room temperature in the original carton between 20 degrees C to 25 degrees C (68 degrees F to 77 degrees F) for 30 days. Protect from light. Once removed from the refrigerator, do not return.
Missed doses: If a dose is missed, administer the next dose as scheduled. Do not administer extra or double doses.
