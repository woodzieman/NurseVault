        Adults
        Dosage Description
        60 micrograms (mcg)/kg or 1.8 mg/m2 orally once daily. Typically, the daily dose does not exceed 4 mg; however, the usual dosage range for remission induction is 4 to 8 mg/day. Hold therapy when the total leukocyte count drops to approximately 15,000 cells/mcL. Discontinue therapy before the leukocyte count falls into the normal range; the leucocyte count may continue to fall for more than 1 month after stopping therapy. During remission, busulfan therapy may be resumed at the remission induction dose when the total leukocyte count is approximately 50,000 cells/mcL. If remission is less than 3 months, maintenance therapy at 1 to 3 mg daily may be started to keep hematologic disease under control and prevent rapid relapse.

          Dosage Description
          1 mg/kg orally every 6 hours for 4 days (total of 16 doses) in combination with cyclophosphamide (60 mg/kg/day IV for 2 days). Treatment with oral busulfan followed by cyclophosphamide (BuCY) compared with cyclophosphamide plus total body irradiation (CY/TBI) prior to allogeneic bone marrow transplant (alloBMT) resulted in similar 3-year (61% vs. 64%) and 7-year (59% vs. 56%) leukemia-free survival rates in a subgroup of 69 patients with AML in a randomized controlled study. However, the incidence of veno-occlusive disease, hemorrhagic cystitis, chronic graft versus host disease (GVHD), and death from GVHD at 7 years was significantly higher in the BuCY arm. In another randomized study, estimated overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) rates were significantly worse when BuCY was given as a conditioning regimen prior to alloBMT compared with CY/TBI in 101 patients with AML in first remission. In a long-term follow-up report of this study (median follow-up time, 10.8 years; range, 9.5 to 12.7 years), the 10-year OS rates were 43% and 59% (p = 0.04) and the 10-year DFS rates were 35% and 55% (p = 0.02) in the BuCY and CY/TBI arms, respectively. Patients in both studies received cyclosporine and methotrexate as posttransplant immunosuppression. In a long-term pooled analysis (mean follow-up greater than 7 years) that evaluated 172 patients with AML from these randomized studies, treatment with BuCY prior to alloBMT led to nonsignificantly worse 10-year OS (51% vs. 63%) and DFS (47% vs. 57%) rates compared with CY/TBI. In this analysis, the 5-year cumulative incidence of extensive chronic GVHD and 7-year cumulative incidence of cataracts, pulmonary complications, and avascular osteonecrosis were not significantly different in AML patients who received BuCY or CY/TBI; alopecia occurred significantly more often with BuCY.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Patients receiving busulfan require close monitoring of blood cell counts due to busulfan-induced bone marrow suppression, including neutropenia and thrombocytopenia. Due to the severe myelotoxicity of busulfan, oral busulfan should only be used once the diagnosis of chronic myelogenous leukemia has been confirmed and the intravenous formulation should be administered under the supervision of a qualified physician who is experienced in allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, the use of cancer chemotherapeutic drugs, and the management of patients with severe pancytopenia. Because the maximum effect of busulfan-induced myelosuppression may be delayed and prolonged, dosages should be reduced at the first sign of hematopoietic toxicity; a bone marrow biopsy should be performed if bone marrow status is uncertain. As busulfan-induced bone marrow suppression is dose related and delayed in onset, failure to discontinue the drug soon enough may result in increased toxicity; recovery without growth factor support may also be delayed (range 1 month to 2 years). Therefore, blood, platelet, and growth factor support may be necessary following busulfan therapy. Patients with compromised bone marrow reserve due to previous treatment or radiotherapy may be more susceptible to the myelosuppressive effects of busulfan and should be closely monitored. Active infections should be treated prior to busulfan therapy, when possible. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with myelosuppressive therapy. Patients should be counseled to promptly report the development of fever, sore throat, signs of local infection, bleeding, or symptoms of anemia. Any of these symptoms may indicate busulfan-induced toxicity; however, they may also indicate transformation of chronic myelogenous leukemia to the acute blastic phase.Intramuscular injections should not be administered to patients with platelet counts = 3 cycles of chemotherapy, prior stem cell transplantation, or have preexisting hepatic disease have an increased risk of developing hepatic veno-occlusive disease following high-dose busulfan therapy. In addition, high busulfan AUC values (>1,500 micro-mol•min/L) may be associated with the development of hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD). Regular monitoring of serum transaminases, alkaline phosphatase, and bilirubin is recommended for early detection of busulfan-induced hepatotoxicity. A reduced incidence of hepatic VOD and other regimen-related toxicities have been observed in patients treated with high-dose busulfan and cyclophosphamide when the first dose of cyclophosphamide has been delayed for > 24 hours after the last dose of busulfan.The effectiveness of high-dose busulfan therapy has not been specifically studied in children or infants. A open-label uncontrolled study has examined the pharmacokinetics of high-dose busulfan as part of a conditioning regimen prior to hematopoietic stem cell transplant in pediatric patients aged 5 months to 16 years. Intravenous dosage guidelines for children have been suggested by this study; however, no efficacy data are available (see Dosage).Generalized seizures can occur in patients who receive high-dose busulfan for bone marrow transplantation. Patients with a history of seizures or head trauma may be at increased risk. Prophylactic anticonvulsant therapy with phenytoin should be started prior to high-dose busulfan therapy and continued during therapy to decrease the incidence of seizures. Clonazepam as a continuous infusion (0.1 mg/kg/day) has been used in children.Busulfan is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category D and is well known to cause fetal abnormalities. Females of childbearing age should be counseled against becoming pregnant while receiving busulfan and regarding appropriate contraceptive options. However, several reports have been published of women delivering normal infants after taking busulfan during pregnancy. There have been reports of small infants being born after the mothers received busulfan during pregnancy, in particular the third trimester. One case reports an infant with mild anemia and neutropenia at birth after busulfan was administered to the mother from the eighth week of pregnancy to term.Patients who wish to have children should be counseled regarding the possibility of infertility and alternatives for contraception following busulfan therapy. Fertility and normal menses may return after stopping busulfan treatment; however, infertility may be permanent following busulfan therapy.It is unknown if busulfan is excreted in breast milk. Breast-feeding is not recommended during busulfan therapy because of the potential risks to the infant.The World Health Organization has concluded that there is a causal relationship between busulfan exposure and the development of a secondary malignancy. Busulfan has been associated with the development secondary malignancies, including acute myeloid leukemia and malignant tumors, within 5—8 years of chronic oral busulfan therapy. All patients should be counseled regarding the risk of secondary malignancies associated with busulfan therapy and long-term follow-up may be required.Caution is recommended in the preparation, handling and administration of busulfan solution to avoid accidental exposure. If skin or ocular exposure occurs, skin and eyes should be thoroughly rinsed. The use of protective gowns, gloves and goggles during preparation and administration is recommended.Hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, hypocalcemia, and decreased urine output may be indicative of busulfan-induced tumor lysis syndrome (TLS). Appropriate measures (e.g. aggressive hydration and allopurinol) must be taken to prevent severe electrolyte imbalances and renal toxicity during and following chemotherapy administration in patients with large chemosensitive tumors.Vaccination during chemotherapy or radiation therapy should be avoided because the antibody response is suboptimal. When chemotherapy is being planned, vaccination should precede the initiation of chemotherapy by >= 2 weeks. The administration of live vaccines to immunocompromised patients should be avoided. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    bone marrow suppression, infection, radiation therapy, serious adverse effects
    General Information
    Severe and prolonged bone marrow suppression/pancytopenia including anemia, granulocytopenia, and thrombocytopenia occurs with intravenous (IV) and oral busulfan therapy. Serious infection and bleeding may occur. Patients must receive a hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation after IV busulfan therapy to prevent potentially fatal complications due to severe myelosuppression. Monitor complete blood counts (CBC) including differential and platelet counts daily during IV busulfan treatment and until engraftment occurs. Administer antibiotics and blood cell and platelet support as indicated. Oral busulfan may cause serious adverse effects including bone marrow hypoplasia. Use oral busulfan with caution in patients with compromised bone marrow reserve including patients who received prior radiation therapy or chemotherapy. Monitor CBC including differential and platelet counts weekly during oral busulfan treatment. Reduce or discontinue oral busulfan therapy for myelosuppression. Perform a bone marrow examination if the bone marrow status is uncertain. Bone morrow recovery following oral busulfan is potentially reversible but may take from 1 month to 2 years to occur.
    Headline
    head trauma, seizure disorder
    General Information
    Use busulfan with caution in patients with a history of seizure disorder, head trauma, or in patients who are receiving other drugs that may cause seizures. Seizures have been reported in patients receiving high-dose oral or intravenous (IV) busulfan. Start prophylactic therapy with anticonvulsant agents (e.g., benzodiazepines, phenytoin, valproic acid, levetiracetam) prior to IV busulfan therapy.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Busulfan may cause fetal harm if it is administered during pregnancy, based on animal data and its mechanism of action. Advise pregnant people of the potential fetal risk. In a single case report, malformation was reported following busulfan exposure during pregnancy. In this case, the patient received X-ray therapy early in the first trimester, mercaptopurine until the third month, and then busulfan until delivery. In animal reproduction studies, busulfan was teratogenic when administered during organogenesis. Additionally, the injectable busulfan formulation is dissolved in N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMA), a solvent. DMA may also cause fetal harm. Developmental anomalies (e.g., anasarca, cleft palate, vertebral anomalies, rib anomalies, and serious anomalies of the vessels of the heart) were observed in rats when DMA doses approximately 40% of the daily human dose were administered during organogenesis.
    Headline
    people who can cause pregnancy in others, people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant and people who can cause pregnancy in others about the reproductive risk associated with busulfan. Busulfan can cause fetal harm. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception during treatment with busulfan and for 6 months after the last dose. For those that can cause pregnancy in others, contraception should be used during busulfan treatment and for 3 months after the last dose. Busulfan therapy may damage spermatozoa and testicular tissue in individuals, leading to possible genetic fetal abnormalities. Busulfan may cause male and female infertility. Amenorrhea and ovarian suppression have been commonly reported in premenopausal individuals undergoing chronic, low-dose busulfan therapy for chronic myelogenous leukemia. Busulfan may cause temporary or permanent infertility in prepubertal individuals and in people who may become pregnant, who are treated with high-dose busulfan in the conditioning regimen prior to allogeneic stem cell transplantation. Additionally, sterility, azoospermia, and testicular atrophy have been reported in individuals who received busulfan.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise individuals to avoid breast-feeding while taking busulfan, due to the potential for serious adverse reactions in the breast-fed child. There are no data on the presence of busulfan in human milk.

  General Information
  Hematologic toxicity including severe neutropenia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia is the dose-limiting toxicity of busulfan. The incidence of severe pancytopenia following high-dose busulfan therapy is 100%. A few hypersensitive patients have experienced sudden onset of irreversible marrow toxicity. Aplastic anemia has occurred rarely, usually following long-term conventional dosing or high-dose therapy. Pancytopenia caused by busulfan is more prolonged than that induced by other alkylating agents and may last as long as 2 years. Patients who become pancytopenic should receive aggressive medical support including antibiotics, colony-stimulating factors, and blood product transfusions throughout the period of pancytopenia. In the treatment of chronic myeloid diseases, busulfan should be discontinued prior to the leukocyte count reaching the normal range, at approximately 15,000/mm3, to avoid severe myelosuppression. Following high-dose therapy, recovery of neutrophil counts to >= 500/mm3 occurred at median 13 days when prophylactic G-CSF was administered. Fever, chills, and infection are common in patients with prolonged bone marrow suppression; sepsis may also occur.Nausea/vomiting are usually not severe with busulfan at doses used to treat chronic myelogenous leukemia. Stomatitis and xerostomia (dry mouth) have been reported during conventional dose busulfan therapy. Higher doses used in stem cell transplant preparative regimens are moderately emetogenic and require antiemetic therapy; mild to moderate nausea and vomiting occurred in 92% and 95% of patients, respectively, during high-dose clinical trials; nausea was severe in 7% of patients. Severe stomatitis (26%), esophagitis (2%), diarrhea (75%, severe 5%), constipation (38%), dyspepsia (44%), hematemesis (2%), pancreatitis (2%), rectal discomfort (24%), and anorexia (mild/moderate 64%, severe 21%) occur when busulfan is used in high doses for stem cell transplantation. Symptoms have been reported to occur within 8 months to 10 years after the initiation of therapy; the average onset is within 4 years of busulfan therapy. Clinical manifestations include gradual onset of a persistent nonproductive cough, rales, fever, and shortness of breath; however, the onset can be acute. Biopsy reveals bronchopulmonary dysplasia and interstitial pulmonary fibrosis, similar to radiation-induced pneumonitis. Pulmonary function studies have revealed diminished diffusion capacity and decreased pulmonary compliance. There is no specific therapy for this complication; most patients die within 6 months of diagnosis. Busulfan therapy should be stopped if pulmonary toxicity develops. Occasionally, this syndrome has resolved following discontinuation of therapy and corticosteroid administration. High-dose intravenous busulfan therapy has been associated with mild or moderate dyspnea in 25% (severe 2%) of patients and hyperventilation in 5% of patients. During clinical trials of the high-dose intravenous therapy, 3 patients (5%) developed documented alveolar hemorrhage; all required ventilatory support and all died. An increased local cutaneous reaction has been observed in patients receiving radiotherapy soon after busulfan. Other cutaneous reactions reported to busulfan therapy include urticaria, erythema multiforme, erythema nodosum, alopecia, porphyria cutanea tarda, excessive dry skin (xerosis) and skin fragility with anhidrosis. Approximately 10% of patients treated long-term with busulfan will develop posterior subcapsular cataracts. Other ocular adverse reactions reported include corneal thinning and lens changes.Cardiac tamponade, with fatal outcome in some cases, has been reported in a small number of pediatric thalassemia patients treated with high-dose oral busulfan and cyclophosphamide as part of a bone marrow transplant preparative regimen. Pain (abdominal) and vomiting preceded the tamponade in most patients. One case of endocardial fibrosis has been reported in a 79-year old woman who received a total busulfan dose of 7200 mg over a 9-year period for the management of chronic myeloid leukemia. At autopsy, she was found to have endocardial fibrosis of the left ventricle in addition to interstitial pulmonary fibrosis. The amount of DMA administered during intravenous busulfan therapy is less than the that which caused these effects in other trials. Description
  Text
  Busulfan (1,4-butanediol dimethanesulfonate) is a bifunctional alkylating agent. The IV formulation is indicated in combination with cyclophosphamide as a conditioning regimen prior to allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation in patients with chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML). The oral tablet formulation is indicated for the palliative treatment of CML. It is less effective in patients with Philadelphia chromosome (Ph)-negative CML and in young children with Ph-negative disease. It is not effective for the treatment of blast-phase CML, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, or acute leukemias.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Busulfan is a member of the alkyl alkane sufonates class of alkylating agents. In aqueous solutions, busulfan hydrolyzes to release the methansulfate groups. This produces reactive carbonium ions that can alkylate DNA and other proteins resulting cytotoxicity. Busulfan reacts more readily with thiol groups of amino acids and proteins than nitrogen mustards. Busulfan exhibits SN2-alkylating kinetics which means the alkylating reaction is dependent upon the concentrations of both busulfan and the target compound. Busulfan binds to DNA at the N-7 position of guanine. Small amounts DNA-DNA interstrand crosslinking have been detected with busulfan while extensive DNA-protein crosslinking occur which may lead to interference with DNA replication, transcription of RNA, and nucleic acid function. Busulfan's activity is seen mostly in myeloid cells and in hematopoietic stem cells at high doses rather than lymphoid cells. This can lead to prolonged bone marrow aplasia in some patients. The reason for this specificity is unknown.
 
Resistance to busulfan occurs through similar mechanisms as other alkylating agents. Possible mechanisms of resistance include decreased drug uptake by malignant cells, increased cellular inactivation, increased inactivation of alkylating intermediates and increased DNA repair mechanisms.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    The gastrointestinal absorption of busulfan is very good. In a study that compared a single 2-mg IV and oral dose of busulfan, the mean bioavailability was 80% +/- 20% in patients aged 13 to 60 years (n = 8). Busulfan appears to have linear kinetics over the dose range of 2 to 6 mg. Following the administration of oral busulfan 2 mg on day 1, 4 mg on day 2, and 6 mg on day 3, the mean dose-normalized (to a 2-mg dose) AUC and Cmax values were approximately 130 ng x hour/mL and 30 ng/mL, respectively, in adult patients (n = 5). The mean dose-normalized (to a 4-mg dose) AUC, Cmax, and Tmax values were 269 +/- 62 ng x hour/mL, 68.2 +/- 24.4 ng/mL, and 0.9 hours, respectively, following the administration of a single dose of busulfan (range, 4 to 8 mg) in 12 patients.

    Intravenous Route
    Description
    The AUC, Cmax, and clearance (normalized to actual body weight) values were 1,167 micromolar x min, 1,222 ng/mL, and 2.52 mL/min/kg, respectively, in a pharmacokinetic analysis of 59 patients who received busulfan 0.8 mg/kg IV every 6 hours for 16 doses plus cyclophosphamide prior to an allogeneic stem-cell transplantation.     Renal Impairment
    Description
    The impact of hemodialysis on busulfan clearance has been evaluated in one patient with chronic renal failure undergoing autologous stem cell transplantation.     Name
    Pediatrics
    Description
    Pediatric patients (ages 2 months to 3.6 years) were found to have lower peak busulfan concentrations, faster elimination, and lower AUC values than adults. Changes in bioavailability, either due to changes in absorption, first pass elimination, or in actual volume of distribution may account for these changes in busulfan pharmacokinetics. The lower incidence of adverse reactions and higher transplant failure rates seen in children versus adults receiving high-dose busulfan therapy may be due to these differences. The absolute bioavailability of oral busulfan is lower in pediatric patients compared with adult patients. In a study that compared a single 2-mg IV and oral dose of busulfan, the mean bioavailability was 68% +/- 31% in patients aged 1.5 to 6 years who received a single 2-mg IV and oral dose of busulfan (n = 8). The estimated clearance and volume of distribution were 3.37 mL/min/kg and 0.64 L/kg, respectively, in pediatric patients aged 5 months to 16 years with malignant hematologic (n =15) or nonmalignant (n = 9) diseases who received 16 doses of busulfan IV plus cyclophosphamide prior to a stem-cell transplantation. The target AUC value of 900 to 1,350 micromolar x min was achieved with busulfan IV therapy at dose 1 in 71% of patients; between doses 9 and 13, the target AUC value was reached in 91% of patients.
  Text
  Busulfan is administered orally and intravenously (IV). It is 32% bound to plasma proteins and 47% bound to red blood cells. Busulfan is a small, highly lipophilic molecule that easily crosses the blood brain barrier; it achieves concentrations in the cerebrospinal fluid approximately equal to those in plasma. The terminal elimination half-life was approximately 2.6 hours in adult patients who received oral busulfan (dose range, 2 to 8 mg). Busulfan is predominantly metabolized by conjugation with glutathione, both spontaneously and via glutathione S-transferase (GST) catalysis. The conjugate undergoes extensive oxidative metabolism in the liver. Busulfan is metabolized in the liver and via enzymatic degradation, forming at least 12 metabolites. Metabolites including tetrahydrothiophene, tetrahydrothiophene 12-oxide, sulfolane, and 3- hydroxysulfolane are inactive. The elimination of busulfan appears to be independent of renal function; less than 2% of the administered dose is excreted in the urine unchanged within 24 hours. Following the administration of oral or IV radiolabeled busulfan, 25% to 60% of the radiation was recovered in the urine within 48 hours after administration; minimal amounts were recovered in the feces.
