Drug Class: Pneumococcal 23-valent polysaccharide vaccine, PPSV23 helps protect against disease caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, which may include acute otitis media, sinusitis, pneumonia, bacteremia, sepsis, or meningitis.

Routes: intramuscular; Common adult doses: 0.5 mL/dose IM or subcutaneously.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): erythema, injection site reaction, peripheral edema, pruritus, skin necrosis, ecchymosis, hemolytic anemia, leukocytosis, lymphadenopathy, purpura, thrombocytopenia, arthralgia, back pain, myalgia, anaphylactoid reactions, angioedema, serum sickness, urticaria, asthenia, fatigue, malaise

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Do not give the pneumococcal vaccines via intravenous administration. Prior to administration, health care personnel should inform the patient, parent, guardian, or responsible adult of the vaccine's benefits and risks.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Do not give the pneumococcal vaccines via intravenous administration. Prior to administration, health care personnel should inform the patient, parent, guardian, or responsible adult of the vaccine's benefits and risks. This should include the provision of the vaccine information statement from the manufacturer. The responsible adult should report any adverse reaction following vaccine administration to the health care provider. The US Department of Health and Human Services has established a Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) to accept all reports of suspected adverse events after the administration of any vaccine. This includes, but is not limited to, the reporting of events required by the National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act of 1986. The toll-free number for VAERS is (800) 822—7967.
 
Pneumococcal vaccine is an FDA pregnancy risk category C drug, although no adequate human studies have been done in pregnant women. Use of the vaccine during the first trimester is not recommended unless the potential benefits to the mother outweigh the risks to the fetus.
 
Use with caution in women who are breast-feeding because it is not known if the pneumococcal vaccine is excreted in milk. Theoretically, it should be harmless, and no adverse reactions in nursing mothers have been reported.
 
The efficacy of pneumococcal 23-valent vaccine is reduced in children under 5 years, and it is not recommended in children under 2 years of age. The heptavalent vaccine, Prevnar(TM), is safe and effective for routine pneumococcal prophylaxis in healthy children and infants under the age of 2 years (see Dosage).
 
Pneumococcal vaccine can cause fever, so it should not be given to patients with fever of unknown etiology.
 
Use pneumococcal vaccine with caution in patients with idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) because pneumococcal vaccination has been associated with relapse of this condition in previously stabilized patients.
 
Use pneumococcal vaccine cautiously in patients with severe cardiac disease or pulmonary disease because anaphylactoid reactions may not be well tolerated in these individuals.
 
Pneumococcal vaccine should be given at least 2 weeks before elective splenectomy, organ transplant, or immunosuppressive or chemotherapy. Patients with severe immunosuppression can have a suboptimal antibody response to vaccination. The formerly-available 12 valent pneumococcal vaccine has been found to be less immunogenic in patients with Hodgkin's disease who have received intensive chemo- or radiotherapy. Furthermore, administration of the 12 valent vaccine has been shown to decrease preexisting antibody levels to other strains of pneumococcus. Becuase of this, the 23 valent pneumococcal vaccine is not indicated in Hodgkin's disease patients who have received intensive chemotherapy and/or radiation therapy.
 
In a study of 30 elderly patients, antibody response to pneumococcal vaccine was lower in the 15 subjects with preexisting vitamin B12 deficiency than in the other 15 subjects. Although the effect of vitamin B12 supplementation was not assessed in this study and therefore no recommendation can be made regarding the need for supplementation prior to vaccination, clinicians should be alert to the possibility of impaired antibody response in these patients.
 
Patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection generally have lower vaccine-induced antibody levels than healthy individuals. The antibody levels are proportional to the severity of the HIV infection (i.e., CD4+ counts) and are lowest in those with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS). Pneumococcal vaccination should be offered to patients with HIV infection with CD4+ counts >= 200 cells/mm3, if they have not received this vaccine during the previous 5 years. Vaccination should be offered to those with CD4+ counts 200 cells/mm3 on HAART. The recommendation to vaccinate is increasingly pertinent because of the rising incidence of infections with drug-resistant strains of S. pnuemoniae. Some authorities are concerned that immunizations might stimulate HIV replication, but this concern may be less relevant in the setting of HAART. However, because of the theoretical concern that increases in HIV plasma RNA following vaccination during pregnancy might increase the risk of perinatal transmission of HIV, providers may wish to defer vaccincation for HIV-positive pregnant patients until after HAART is initiated.

    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    Deferral of pneumococcal vaccine administration may be appropriate for a patient with a moderate or severe acute illness such as infection with or without fever. The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices recommends that vaccinations be delayed during the course of a moderate or severe acute febrile illness and administered after the acute phase of illness has resolved. All vaccines can be administered to persons with minor illnesses such as diarrhea, mild upper-respiratory infection with or without low-grade fever, or other low-grade febrile illness. Vaccinate persons with moderate or severe febrile illness as soon as they have recovered from the acute phase of the illness.
    Headline
    immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP)
    General Information
    Use pneumococcal vaccines with caution in patients with immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) because pneumococcal vaccination has been associated with relapse of this condition in previously stabilized patients. Monitor patients for relapse after vaccination.
    Headline
    cardiac disease, cranial cerebrospinal fluid leak, pulmonary disease
    General Information
    Use pneumococcal vaccines cautiously in individuals with severe cardiac disease or pulmonary disease because systemic reactions may pose significant risks in these patient populations. Pneumococcal meningitis may not be prevented with pneumococcal immunization in individuals with a cranial cerebrospinal fluid leak.
    Headline
    immunosuppression
    General Information
    Patients with significant immunosuppression may not have an adequate antibody response to the pneumococcal vaccines. Immunosuppressed persons may include individuals with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection; severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID); hypogammaglobulinemia; agammaglobulinemia; altered immune states due to generalized neoplastic disease; or an immune system compromised by radiation therapy or drug therapy (e.g., chemotherapy or corticosteroid therapy with greater than physiologic doses). Short-term (less than 2 weeks) corticosteroid therapy or intra-articular, bursal, or tendon injections with corticosteroids should not be immunosuppressive. Vaccine effectiveness may be reduced when PPSV23 is administered during periods of altered immunocompetence. If elective splenectomy or immunosuppressive therapy is planned, complete necessary pneumococcal immunization at least 2 weeks before surgery or therapy initiation. Pneumococcal vaccine, PPSV23 may be offered to individuals with a CD4 count less than 200 cells/mm3; however, PPSV23 should preferably be deferred until the CD4 count increases to above 200 cells/mm3 to optimize vaccine response.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Available human data on the use of pneumococcal vaccine, polyvalent during pregnancy are insufficient to establish the presence or absence of vaccine-associated risk. According to the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), administration of inactivated vaccines during pregnancy has not resulted in adverse fetal effects. The ACIP recommends vaccination during pregnancy when the likelihood of disease exposure is high, when potential infection would cause harm to the pregnant person or fetus, and when the vaccine is unlikely to cause harm. Vaccination during pregnancy should be considered when the potential benefits outweigh the potential risks, including the likelihood of disease exposure and the potential consequences of pneumococcal infection.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Pneumococcal vaccine is compatible with breast-feeding. In general, inactivated vaccines are not expected to pose a risk to lactating individuals or to the breastfed child. Breast-feeding does not appear to adversely affect immunization, and limited data suggest it may enhance the immune response to certain vaccine antigens.

Interactions
Acetaminophen: (Moderate) Concomitant administration of antipyretics, such as acetaminophen, may decrease an individual's immunological response to the pneumococcal vaccine. A post-marketing study conducted in Poland using a non-US vaccination schedule (2, 3, 4, and 12 months of age) evaluated the impact of prophylactic oral acetaminophen on antibody responses to Prevnar 13. Data show that acetaminophen, given at the time of vaccination and then dosed at 6 to 8 hour intervals for 3 doses on a scheduled basis, reduced the antibody response to some serotypes after the third dose of Prevnar 13 when compared to the antibody responses of infants who only received antipyretics 'as needed' for treatment. However, reduced antibody responses were not observed after the fourth dose of Prevnar 13 with prophylactic acetaminophen.

Data show that acetaminophen, given at the time of vaccination and then dosed at 6 to 8 hour intervals for 3 doses on a scheduled basis, reduced the antibody response to some serotypes after the third dose of Prevnar 13 when compared to the antibody responses of infants who only received antipyretics 'as needed' for treatment. However, reduced antibody responses were not observed after the fourth dose of Prevnar 13 with prophylactic acetaminophen.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The most frequent adverse reaction of pneumococcal vaccine administration in patients of any age is an injection site reaction, which usually is of minor consequence. Patients may experience erythema and warmth at the injection site. The other most common adverse events in young children include fever, irritability, drowsiness, insomnia or restless sleep, mild nausea/vomiting and decreased appetite (anorexia) in the days following vaccination. In addition, malaise, headache, and weakness have been reported. In clinical trials of infants and children, the vaccine was simultaneously administered with other vaccines, including DTP and Hib immunizations.While seizures were reported in 8 patients receiving Prevnar™ in clinical trials; these patients had received concomitant DTP- or DTaP-containing vaccines. Seizures were described in 393 reports to the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System. About a third of patients had symptoms within 24 hours and about a fourth of the seizures were defined as febrile seizures. Of the 393 seizure cases, 57 occurred after receipt of only Prevnar™.Pneumococcal vaccination has been associated with relapse of idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) in previously stabilized patients. A report of a patient that developed a platelet count less than 150,000 mm3, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, and aplastic anemia 1 week after her first dose of Prevnar™ given alone has been received through the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS). Over the first 2 years after approval of Prevnar™, 78 reports of possible thrombocytopenia (e.g., petechiae, purpura, or ecchymosis) were received through VAERS. Most patients developed symptoms 1—35 days after immunization. Twelve patients had a documented platelet count < 20,000 mm3, but at least 8 patients had vaccines, medications, or viral or bacterial infections as potential causes.Paresthesias and acute radiculoneuropathy, such as Guillain-Barre syndrome, have occurred after pneumococcal vaccination, but such reactions are rare, and a causal relationship cannot be established.Over the first 2 years after approval of Prevnar™, 6 reports of serum sickness were received through the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System. Description
  Text
  Pneumococcal 23-valent polysaccharide vaccine, PPSV23 helps protect against disease caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, which may include acute otitis media, sinusitis, pneumonia, bacteremia, sepsis, or meningitis. Pneumococcal vaccination remains an important preventive strategy because some S. pneumoniae isolates are resistant to 1 or more antibiotics, and resistance can lead to treatment failures. Pneumococcal vaccines include pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) and pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine. PCVs contain pneumococcal capsular polysaccharides conjugated to a carrier protein, whereas PPSV23 contains nonconjugated purified capsular polysaccharides from 23 Streptococcus pneumoniae serotypes. PPSV23 is not approved for use in individuals younger than 2 years because individuals in this age group do not develop an effective immune response to the capsular types contained in the polysaccharide vaccine. Antibody responses to most pneumococcal capsular types are generally low or inconsistent in individuals younger than 2 years. For guidance on determining which pneumococcal vaccine an individual needs and when, please refer to the PneumoRecs VaxAdvisor app or download at https://www.cdc.gov/pneumococcal/hcp/vaccine-recommendations/app.html. PPSV23 was FDA-approved in 1983.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Pneumococcal 23-valent polysaccharide vaccine, PPSV23 contains purified capsular polysaccharides from 23 Streptococcus pneumoniae serotypes: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6B, 7F, 8, 9N, 9V, 10A, 11A, 12F, 14, 15B, 17F, 18C, 19F, 19A, 20, 22F, 23F, and 33F. PPSV23 is a non-conjugated polysaccharide vaccine; therefore, unlike pneumococcal conjugate vaccines, the pneumococcal polysaccharides are not bound to a carrier protein such as the non-toxic diphtheria CRM197 protein. It induces type-specific antibodies that enhance opsonization, phagocytosis, and killing of pneumococci by leukocytes and other phagocytic cells. The antibody concentrations that correlate with protection against pneumococcal disease have not been clearly defined. It will not prevent disease caused by capsular types not included in the vaccine.

Pharmacokinetics
  Text
  Pneumococcal 23-valent polysaccharide vaccine, PPSV23 is administered intramuscularly or subcutaneously. Distribution, metabolism, and excretion have not been well-defined. The antibody concentrations that correlate with protection against pneumococcal disease have not been clearly defined, and immune response to the vaccine may not be sufficient to prevent pneumococcal infection in all individuals.
 
Immune response to PPSV23 usually occurs within 2 to 3 weeks after vaccination, and more than 80% of healthy adults develop antibodies against the serotypes contained in the vaccine. Older adults and persons with some chronic illnesses or immunodeficiency may not respond as well. Elevated antibody concentrations persist for at least 5 years in healthy adults but may decline more quickly in persons with certain underlying illnesses.

Administration
  General Information
  For storage information, see the specific product information within the How Supplied section.
Verify immunization status in the state or local immunization information system in addition to the health care electronic record. If this vaccine has been previously given, question the patient, parent, or guardian about any symptoms or signs of an adverse reaction.
Advise individuals on the benefits and risks of the vaccine. Provide a copy of the Vaccine Information Statement (VIS).
Record manufacturer, lot number, administration date, and the name and address of the person administering the vaccine.
Ensure supportive care, including epinephrine 1 mg/mL injection, is readily accessible in the event of a clinically significant hypersensitivity reaction.
Complete a Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) report if adverse events have been identified. The VAERS toll-free information line is 1-800-822-7967 and email address is info@vaers.org.
