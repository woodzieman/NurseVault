Drug Class: Mineral oil is an oral and rectal lubricant laxative indicated for the treatment of occasional constipation or fecal impaction.

Routes: topical, rectal, oral, ophthalmic; Common adult doses: 90 mL/day PO or 1 rectal enema/day PR for no longer than 1 week.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): dizziness, fecal incontinence, fecal urgency, skin irritation, syncope, weakness

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Patients who require the use of laxative products, including mineral oil, for more than one week should consult their health care provider.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  Contraindications And Precautions
    Headline
    Drug Interactions
    General Information
    The coadministration of certain medications may lead to harm and require avoidance or therapy modification; review all drug interactions prior to concomitant use of other medications.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    Treatment-Related Restrictions of Use
    General Information
    Patients who require the use of laxative products, including mineral oil, for more than one week should consult their health care provider. Frequent or prolonged use may result in a dependence on laxatives.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of mineral oil in pregnant women; mineral oil has not been formally evaluated by the FDA in regard to safety and efficacy, and there are no animal studies. Thus, it is not known whether this drug will cause fetal harm when administered to a pregnant woman or affect reproduction capacity. The safest first-line treatments to use for constipation during pregnancy are those that are not absorbed systemically (e.g., fiber, bulk-forming laxatives) in order to minimize drug exposure to the fetus. Expert opinions generally regard the use of mineral oil in pregnancy as potentially unsafe; use only when benefit to the mother outweighs potential fetal risk. Mineral oil should generally be avoided during pregnancy because it can impair maternal fat-soluble vitamin absorption (e.g., vitamin K), potentially leading to neonatal coagulopathy and hemorrhage.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use mineral oil with caution in breast-feeding patients. It is unknown if mineral oil is excreted in human milk when used as a laxative. Because mineral oil has poor oral absorption, it is unlikely to pass into breast milk in significant amounts or cause infant adverse effects with short-term use. Rectal formulations would further limit absorption and are compatible with breast-feeding. Use alternative, safer laxatives where systemic absorption is known and/or infant exposure is negligible. Medications that do not have reports of adverse infant effects during lactation with occasional use postpartum include psyllium, bisacodyl, docusate, senna concentrate or sennosides, and magnesium hydroxide.     One small, observational study showed mineral oil use by the breast-feeding parent had no effect infant bowel patterns. Repeated and chronic use of mineral oil should be avoided because it may cause a deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) which in turn may affect maternal nutrition and breast milk nutritional quality. The general exposure of humans to mineral oils (e.g., from food products, drugs, and cosmetics) and the toxicologic effects may be concerning; the long-term effects are still unknown. Many cosmetic skin care products contain mineral oils and paraffins. These should not be applied to the breast or nipples prior to nursing as the infant may ingest the components which may cause unintended chemical exposure or changes in bowel habits.
    Headline
    abdominal pain, nausea, rectal bleeding, vomiting
    General Information
    Do not use mineral oil laxative products in patients with abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, a sudden change in bowel habits persisting for more than 2 weeks, or rectal bleeding.
    Headline
    dysphagia, geriatric
    General Information
    Do not use the oral microemulsion formulation of mineral oil in patients who are bedridden or who have difficulty swallowing (dysphagia). According to the Beers Criteria, orally administered mineral oil is considered a potentially inappropriate medication (PIM) for use in geriatric adults and should be avoided due to the potential for aspiration or other adverse effects and the availability of safer alternatives.
    Headline
    renal failure
    General Information
    Mineral oil oral solution products should be avoided in patients with renal failure.
    Headline
    infants, neonates
    General Information
    The Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) list includes mineral oil as a potentially inappropriate medication for use in neonates and infants and use should be avoided. If avoidance is not possible, use mineral oil with caution and appropriate monitoring.

Interactions
Acetaminophen; HYDROcodone: (Moderate) Concurrent use of hydrocodone with strong laxatives that rapidly increase gastrointestinal motility, such as mineral oil, may decrease hydrocodone absorption. Closely monitor patients for changing analgesic requirements or adverse events.
Alendronate; Cholecalciferol: (Moderate) Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins is reported to be decreased with prolonged oral administration of mineral oil. However, despite warnings in various texts, there is little direct evidence that the interaction is of practical/clinical importance with limited use as directed. It may be prudent for those taking dietary supplements of Vitamin A, D, E, or K to separate administration by 1 hour before or 4 hours after a mineral oil oral dosage to help limit absorption interactions. Theoretically, the effect on fat-soluble vitamin absorption may more likely occur with prolonged or chronic administration of mineral oil.
Atropine; Difenoxin: (Moderate) Diphenoxylate can decrease GI motility. Drugs used to treat constipation, such as laxatives, would counteract the effect of antidiarrheals. In general, it would be illogical to concurrently administer these drugs at the same time. If an antidiarrheal medication is needed, it would be wise to temporarily discontinue use of agents with laxative effects.
Bumetanide: (Moderate) Loop diuretics may increase the risk of hypokalemia especially in patients receiving prolonged therapy with laxatives. Monitor serum potassium levels to determine the need for potassium supplementation and/or alteration in drug therapy.
Calcifediol: (Moderate) Separate administration of oral vitamin D analogs by 1 hour before or 4 to 6 hours after mineral oil to limit effects on absorption and availability of the vitamin D analog. Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins may be decreased with concomitant administration of mineral oil. The bioavailability of orally administered vitamin D analogs may also be decreased.

    Headline
    pneumonitis
    General Information
    Rarely, a lipoid pneumonitis is caused by inhalation or aspiration of fat-containing substances like mineral oils, etc. The best offense against this rare, but potentially serious, adverse effect is avoiding administration of mineral oil orally before bedtime or lying down, and also avoiding its use in patients at risk for aspiration. However, cases have been reported in children and adults. It can present as an delayed onset, chronic respiratory illness simulating interstitial lung disease with cough and difficulty breathing, or it may present as an acute respiratory illness with sudden onset dyspnea. Radiological findings are diverse but may appear similar to ARDS; CT scans are sometimes helpful. Pathologically, the pneumonitis is due to a foreign body reaction. In suspected cases, diagnosis may be confirmed by demonstrating the presence of lipid-laden macrophages in sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, or fine needle aspiration cytology/biopsy from the lung lesion. Treatment requires discontinuation of mineral oil use, supportive therapy, repeated bronchoalveolar lavage, and corticosteroids.

Description
  Text
  Mineral oil is an oral and rectal lubricant laxative indicated for the treatment of occasional constipation or fecal impaction. The oral formulation when taken by mouth encourages bowel movement by coating the bowel and the stool mass with a waterproof film, keeping water in the stool and allowing the stool to remain soft, and its passage is made easier. Mineral oil is formed from purified distillates from petroleum products according to standards of the United States Pharmacopeia. Mineral oil retards colonic absorption of fecal water and softens the stool. This is the only laxative recommended for fecal impaction. Mineral oil can also be used topically to treat and prevent dry skin and minor skin irritations including diaper rash. Mineral oil is an unapproved marketed drug that has not been formally evaluated for safety and efficacy by the FDA.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mineral oil is an oral and rectal lubricant laxative. Orally, its onset of action is usually 6 to 8 hours, and it works in the colon. Mineral oil retards colonic absorption of fecal water and softens the stool. Rectally, mineral oral works locally to lubricate and soften the stool. A bowel movement is usually produced in 2 to 15 minutes after rectal enema of mineral oil. Mineral oil sits on the surface of the skin and in spaces between cells and provides a hydrophobic barrier. This barrier prevents trans-epidermal water loss to trap water in the skin. Overall this leads to greater hydration, flexibility, and softness of the stratum corneum.
