    Hypersensitivity
    General Information

    Headline
    family history of obsessive-compulsive disorder, impulse control disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, substance abuse disorder
    General Information
    Similar to other atypical antipsychotic medications with partial dopamine agonist activity, people who are at higher risk for impulse-control problems (e.g., people with obsessive-compulsive disorder or a family history of obsessive-compulsive disorder, impulse control disorder, bipolar disorder, impulsive personality, substance abuse disorder, or other addictive behaviors) should be closely monitored for new or worsening uncontrollable urges when receiving brexpiprazole. Postmarketing reports and pharmacovigilance data suggest that patients taking brexpiprazole can experience intense compulsive urges, particularly for gambling, and the inability to control these urges. Less frequently reported impulse control symptoms include eating or binge-eating, shopping, and sexual actions. In some cases, these uncontrollable urges stop when the medication is discontinued or the dose is reduced. Inform patients and caregivers of the risk of impulse control symptoms. Because patients may not recognize these behavioral changes as abnormal, health care providers should specifically ask patients about any new or increasing urges or compulsions during brexpiprazole treatment. Impulse control symptoms can also be associated with the underlying disorder that is being treated. Consider a reduction in dose or discontinuation of the medication if such urges develop during use of brexpiprazole, as they may result in harm to the patient or others.
    Headline
    diabetes mellitus, obesity
    General Information
    Brexpiprazole, like other atypical antipsychotic agents, may cause metabolic changes. Metabolic changes are of particular concern in people with pre-existing risk factors, such as diabetes mellitus and/or obesity. Weight gain, increased blood sugar, and worsening or new-onset diabetes may occur during therapy. While all medications in the atypical antipsychotic class have been shown to produce some metabolic changes, each has its own specific risk profile. Metabolic changes may increase cardiovascular or cerebrovascular risk over time. Hyperglycemia, sometimes associated with diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar coma, or death, has been reported in people treated with atypical antipsychotics. Monitor weight and for symptoms of hyperglycemia during treatment. Regularly monitor patients with established diabetes mellitus for worsening of glucose control during atypical antipsychotic treatment. In those with risk factors for diabetes mellitus (e.g., obese patients, family history of diabetes), perform fasting blood glucose testing at the beginning of treatment. In patients who develop symptoms of hyperglycemia during treatment, order fasting blood glucose testing. In some cases, hyperglycemia has resolved when the antipsychotic is discontinued. However, some patients require continuation of antidiabetic agents despite discontinuation of the suspect drug.

    Headline
    adolescents, children, suicidal ideation
    General Information
    Safety and efficacy have not been established for brexpiprazole in pediatric patients younger than 13 years of age. Because brexpiprazole is approved for the adjunct treatment of major depression in adults, a boxed warning in the product label describes the risk of suicidality and suicidal ideation in children, adolescents, and young adults receiving antidepressants. In a pooled analysis of placebo-controlled trials of antidepressants (n = 4,500 pediatrics and 77,000 adults), there was an increased risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors in patients 24 years of age and younger receiving an antidepressant versus placebo, with considerable variation in the risk of suicidality among drugs. The difference in absolute risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviors across different indications was highest in those with major depression. The need for an antidepressant in children, adolescents, or young adults for any use must be weighed against the risk of suicidality; it is unknown if this risk extends to long-term use. All patients should be monitored for symptom worsening or suicidality, especially at treatment initiation or after dose changes. Caregivers and/or patients should immediately notify the prescriber of changes in behavior or suicidal ideation. A change to the treatment regimen or discontinuation of brexpiprazole may be necessary in patients with emerging suicidality or worsening depression. Brexpiprazole has also been studied in the pediatric population for the treatment of irritability associated with autism spectrum disorder in pediatric patients. However, the safety and effectiveness of brexpiprazole in this population was not established during these clinical trials. In an 8-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled, flexible-dose clinical study of 119 patients aged 5 to 17 years who received brexpiprazole for the treatment of irritability associated with autism spectrum disorder, the patients who received brexpiprazole were more likely to experience adverse effects than the placebo arm and side effects occurred at higher rates than those reported in adult studies of brexpiprazole. These adverse effects were also reported in the 26-week open-label extension of the trial, with weight changes leading some pediatric subjects to drop out of the trial. The Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) list includes brexpiprazole as a potentially inappropriate medication for use in children and adolescents and use should be avoided. If avoidance is not possible, use brexpiprazole with caution and appropriate monitoring.
    Headline
    cardiovascular disease, hypovolemia
    General Information
    Brexpiprazole should be used with caution in patients with known cardiovascular disease (e.g., history of myocardial infarction or ischemic heart disease, heart failure or conduction abnormalities) or conditions which would predispose patients to hypotension (dehydration, hypovolemia, and treatment with antihypertensive medications). Brexpiprazole can cause orthostatic hypotension and, in rare cases, syncope; risks may be increased in patients with underlying risk factors. Orthostatic hypotension from antipsychotic use may lead to falls with the potential for fractures and other injuries; a fall risk assessment should be completed and reassessed as needed in patients with conditions, diseases, or concurrent medication use that could exacerbate orthostasis.
    Headline
    activities requiring coordination and concentration, driving or operating machinery
    General Information
    Brexpiprazole may cause somnolence, postural hypotension, or motor and sensory instability, which may lead to falls and, consequently, fractures or other injuries. For people with diseases, conditions, or medications that could exacerbate these effects, complete fall risk assessments when initiating antipsychotic treatment and periodically during long-term antipsychotic therapy. Brexpiprazole has the potential to cause somnolence and impair cognitive and motor skills, and brexpiprazole recipients should be advised to use caution when driving or operating machinery or performing other activities requiring coordination and concentration until they are reasonably certain that brexpiprazole does not affect them adversely. Given the primary CNS effects of brexpiprazole, use caution during coadministration with other CNS depressants and alcohol. Ethanol ingestion may further impair cognitive and motor skills and patients should be advised to avoid use of alcoholic beverages when taking brexpiprazole.
    Headline
    leukopenia
    General Information
    Brexpiprazole should be used cautiously in people with a history of leukopenia. Hematologic effects including leukopenia, neutropenia, and agranulocytosis have been associated with antipsychotic use. A history of drug-induced leukopenia or neutropenia or pre-existing low white blood cell (WBC) count may increase the likelihood of developing hematologic effects during treatment with an antipsychotic medication. Patients with a history of clinically significant low WBC count or drug-induced leukopenia/neutropenia should have frequent complete blood count (CBC) assessments during the first few months of treatment. Consider discontinuation of brexpiprazole if a clinically significant decline in WBC occurs in the absence of an identifiable cause. Patients with clinically significant neutropenia should be closely monitored for fever and infection, and appropriate medical intervention should be instituted if necessary. Brexpiprazole should be discontinued in patients with severe neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count less than 1,000/mm3), with ongoing medical care until the symptoms resolve.
    Headline
    seizures
    General Information
    Brexpiprazole should be used cautiously in patients with seizures, a history of seizure disorder, or with conditions that potentially lower the seizure threshold. Conditions that lower the seizure threshold may be more prevalent in a population of 65 years or older. Like other antipsychotic drugs, brexpiprazole may cause seizures.
    Headline
    conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature
    General Information
    Atypical antipsychotics, including brexpiprazole, may disrupt the body's ability to reduce core body temperature. Patients receiving brexpiprazole should be advised of conditions contributing to an elevated core body temperature (e.g., strenuous exercise, exposure to extreme heat, dehydration, and coadministration of anticholinergic medications).
    Headline
    Parkinson disease
    General Information
    Avoid use of brexpiprazole in people with Parkinson disease unless the benefit clearly outweighs the risk of worsening of motor symptoms. Atypical antipsychotics can worsen the motor symptoms of Parkinson disease and can also lead to the development of extrapyramidal symptoms.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class B, Child-Pugh class C, CYP2D6 poor metabolizer, hepatic failure
    General Information
    The maximum recommended dosage in people with moderate to severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B or Child-Pugh class C) or hepatic failure is lower than those with mild hepatic impairment and those with normal hepatic function. People with moderate to severe hepatic impairment have higher brexpiprazole exposures than people with normal hepatic function, which may increase the risk for adverse reactions. Dosage adjustment is also recommended in people who are known CYP2D6 poor metabolizers, as these individuals have higher brexpiprazole concentrations than normal metabolizers of CYP2D6.
    Headline
    renal failure, renal impairment
    General Information
    A lower maximum dosage is recommended for people with moderate to severe renal impairment, including patients with renal failure or end-stage renal disease (ESRD). People with renal impairment (CrCl less than 60 mL/minute) experience greater brexpiprazole exposure than individuals with mild renal impairment or normal renal function.
    Headline
    dementia with psychotic disturbance, geriatric
    General Information
    Brexpiprazole is approved for treatment of agitation in people with Alzheimer disease and should be given routinely, not on an "as needed" basis. Brexpiprazole is not indicated for treating dementia with psychotic disturbance without agitation. Antipsychotics, including brexpiprazole, pose a risk of increased mortality when used for dementia with psychotic disturbance in geriatric adults. Most of the deaths are cardiovascular (e.g., heart failure, sudden death) or infectious (e.g., pneumonia) in nature. A higher incidence of stroke and transient ischemic attack, including fatal stroke, has also been reported. Initiate brexpiprazole at the lower end of the dosage range and titrate carefully in the older adult.  The Beers Criteria label antipsychotics as potentially inappropriate medications for geriatric adults, except for specific indications like agitation in Alzheimer disease, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder adjunct therapy, Parkinson disease psychosis, or short-term use as part of an antiemetic regimen during chemotherapy. Avoid use in delirium, dementia, or non-psychotic Parkinson disease. Reserve antipsychotic use for when non-pharmacologic strategies have failed, are not possible, or the patient poses a danger to self or others. Due to the potential for hyponatremia and SIADH, monitor sodium levels. The U.S. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) regulates antipsychotic use in long-term care facilities and requires documented clinical justification. Taper antipsychotics unless clinically contraindicated, following guidelines for indications, dosages, and duration. OBRA limits "as needed" use to 14 days in long-term care facilities, with ongoing use requiring evaluation by the prescriber.

    dysphagia, increased risk for aspiration
    General Information
    Use brexpiprazole with caution in people with dysphagia or those at increased risk for aspiration. Antipsychotic drugs have been associated with esophageal dysmotility and aspiration of gastric contents.
    Headline
    breast cancer, increased risk for breast cancer
    General Information
    Brexpiprazole can cause hyperprolactinemia, likely due to central dopamine D2 antagonism; the elevation persists during chronic administration. In animal studies, an increase in pituitary gland and mammary gland neoplasias have been observed during administration of some antipsychotics and may be mediated by prolactin. Although the clinical significance of these findings is unknown, some human breast cancers may be prolactin-dependent; therefore, brexpiprazole should be used cautiously in those who have breast cancer or an increased risk for breast cancer, such as those with genetic markers or a personal or family history. Published epidemiologic studies have shown inconsistent results when exploring the potential association between hyperprolactinemia and breast cancer.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Use brexpiprazole during pregnancy only when the benefits outweigh the potential risks. There are no data regarding the use of brexpiprazole in human pregnancy to inform drug-associated risks. In animal reproduction studies, no teratogenicity was observed with oral brexpiprazole given to pregnant rats and rabbits during organogenesis at doses up to 73 and 146 times, respectively, of maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) of 4 mg/day on a mg/m2 basis. However, when pregnant rats were administered brexpiprazole during the period of organogenesis through lactation, the number of perinatal deaths of pups was increased at 73 times the MRHD. There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in pregnant patients exposed to brexpiprazole; information about the registry can be obtained at womensmentalhealth.org/clinical-and-research-programs/pregnancyregistry or by calling 1-866-961-2388. It is not known if antipsychotics, through their effect on prolactin, would affect labor or delivery.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Consider pregnancy testing in people who may become pregnant prior to initiation of an antipsychotic, as there are limited controlled data regarding use of these agents during gestation. Ideally, health care providers should use pre-conceptual planning to help such individuals taking atypical antipsychotics balance the risk for illness relapse with the potential reproductive risk of continuing medication while pregnant.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Use brexpiprazole with caution during breast-feeding. Lactation studies to assess the presence of brexpiprazole in human milk have not been conducted. Brexpiprazole is present in rat milk. The development and health benefits of breast-feeding should be considered along with the clinical need for brexpiprazole and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed infant from the drug or from the underlying maternal condition. Antipsychotics may cause hyperprolactinemia and galactorrhea to varying degrees, and thus may interfere with proper lactation. Due to individual variability in response to antipsychotics, it may be prudent to continue the existing regimen if ongoing treatment is deemed necessary during breast-feeding. However, other agents may be preferred, especially while breast-feeding a newborn or preterm infant. Alternate medications for consideration include atypical agents such as olanzapine or quetiapine. Data during breast-feeding are limited. Regardless of the antipsychotic used, the breastfed infant should be closely monitored for excessive drowsiness, lethargy, and developmental delays. Combination treatment with antipsychotics may increase the risk of these adverse events. 46875
    Headline
    neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero
    General Information
    Neonates and infants exposed to this medication in utero during the third trimester of pregnancy are at risk for extrapyramidal and/or withdrawal symptoms following delivery. There have been reports of agitation, hypertonia, hypotonia, tremor, somnolence, respiratory distress and feeding disorders in these neonates. These complications have varied in severity and have ranged from self-limited to those requiring intensive care unit support and prolonged hospitalization. Neonates exhibiting signs or symptoms of extrapyramidal effects or withdrawal should be carefully monitored. The knowledge about long-term neurobehavioral effects in offspring is limited for all antipsychotic agents and requires further investigation.

Abiraterone: (Moderate) Because brexpiprazole is partially metabolized by CYP2D6, patients should be carefully monitored for brexpiprazole-related adverse reactions during concurrent use of a moderate CYP2D6 inhibitor such as abiraterone. If abiraterone is used in combination with brexpiprazole and a moderate or strong CYP3A4 inhibitor, the brexpiprazole dose should be reduced to one-quarter (25%) of the usual dose and the patient should be carefully monitored for brexpiprazole-related adverse reactions.     General Information

    General Information

In a 2 year, open-label study of pediatric patients with schizophrenia, 2.5% of subjects with normal fasting glucose at baseline experienced a shift to high glucose concentrations while taking brexpiprazole. Although it is possible that individuals with schizophrenia may have an increased risk of diabetes mellitus, epidemiological studies suggest an increased risk of hyperglycemia-related adverse effects during atypical antipsychotic use. Atypical antipsychotics may have effects on glucose metabolism that are independent of their effect on weight gain. In one study, subjects taking atypical antipsychotics (e.g., clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine) were 9% more likely to have a new diagnosis of diabetes mellitus than subjects taking older therapies. While a causal relationship has not been determined, temporal associations suggest that atypical antipsychotic therapy may precipitate or unmask diabetes mellitus in some individuals; glucose levels normalize in most individuals after discontinuation of the drug. A positive re-challenge has been demonstrated in some of these cases. The possibility of impaired glucose tolerance should be considered in individuals who develop symptoms of hyperglycemia or diabetes, such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weakness. Dosage reduction, if clinically possible, may improve glycemic control. Hypoglycemia has occurred infrequently with antipsychotic use.

    General Information
    Brexpiprazole may rarely cause orthostatic hypotension. Adverse reactions associated with orthostatic hypotension can include dizziness, sinus tachycardia, and rarely, syncope.     Headline
    hyperthermia, hypothermia
    General Information
    Antipsychotic agents, including brexpiprazole, can disrupt the patient's ability to reduce core body temperature, which may result in hyperthermia. Patients at particular risk for hyperthermia include those who exercise strenuously, are exposed to extreme heat, are dehydrated, or are receiving concomitant anticholinergic medications. A less frequently described alteration in thermoregulatory processes reported with both conventional and atypical antipsychotics is hypothermia. Thermoregulation is multi-factorial; however, the dopaminergic system appears to have a primary role, and serotonin may also have modulatory activity (5-HT2a receptors). Most cases of hypothermia associated with antipsychotics have occurred in conjunction with other potential precipitating factors such as hypothyroidism, sepsis, organic brain injury, or environmental temperature.     Headline
    neuroleptic malignant syndrome
    General Information
    A potentially fatal symptom complex referred to as neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) has been associated with administration of antipsychotic drugs, including brexpiprazole. NMS is characterized by hyperpyrexia, muscle rigidity, altered mental status, and autonomic instability (e.g., irregular pulse or blood pressure, tachycardia, diaphoresis, cardiac dysrhythmia). Increased serum creatine phosphokinase (CPK), rhabdomyolysis, and related acute renal failure also have occurred. In trials of brexpiprazole as monotherapy for schizophrenia, increased CPK occurred in 2% to 4% of patients receiving brexpiprazole. NMS has been reported during postmarketing use of brexiprazole. The cause of NMS is not completely understood; however, dopamine receptor blockade is one of the mechanisms by which NMS is thought to occur. A primary risk factor for developing NMS appears to be the initiation or increase in dose of an antipsychotic. High potency and depot antipsychotics carry the greatest risk. Environmental risk factors include conditions that inhibit heat dissipation such as an elevated ambient room temperature, prolonged heat exposure, the use of patient restraints, or dehydration. NMS occurs more frequently in young adults, which is most likely the result of age of first exposure rather than an age-related risk. NMS occurs more frequently in men, which is thought to be related to the higher likelihood of male versus female exposure to the causative agent. Risk factors for recurrent NMS include a personal history of NMS, increasing age, and certain medical co-morbidities (e.g., electrolyte imbalances, dehydration). Brexpiprazole should be immediately discontinued and appropriate supportive therapy initiated as soon as symptoms of NMS are discovered.

    Headline
    tardive dyskinesia
    General Information
    Tardive dyskinesia (TD) is a syndrome of potentially irreversible, involuntary, dyskinetic movements that may develop during treatment with antipsychotic drugs. Involuntary orofacial movements (tongue, mouth, jaw, eyelids, or face) or choreoathetoid movements in the extremities may occur. Factors associated with an increased risk of TD include an age above 55 years, female gender, white or African ethnicity, presence of a mood disorder, intellectual disability, CNS injury, prior or current akathisia, significant parkinsonism, or acute dystonic reaction. The rate of TD in adults treated with a first generation antipsychotic appears to be at least 3 times that observed with second generation antipsychotics like brexpiprazole. It is believed that the likelihood of developing TD increases with prolonged treatment and cumulative doses. Less commonly, it can also develop after short periods of time and with low dosages or may go undetected until the medication is stopped. The use of antipsychotics may mask signs and symptoms of TD; however, TD may also remit partially or completely if the antipsychotic is withdrawn. The effect that symptomatic suppression has upon the long-term course of TD is unknown. In people who require chronic treatment with brexpiprazole, the lowest possible dose and the shortest duration of treatment should be used. Monitor brexpiprazole recipients routinely (at 3 to 6-month intervals) for movement disorders using standardized assessments (e.g., AIMS, DISCUS). If signs or symptoms of TD develop, consider discontinuation of brexpiprazole therapy.

Description
  Text
  Brexpiprazole is in an atypical antipsychotic that belongs to the drug subclass known as 'dopamine system stabilizers' (DSSs). Similar to aripiprazole, brexpiprazole is a partial dopamine agonist, which is in contrast to other antipsychotic agents that are full dopamine antagonists. Atypical antipsychotics are the standard of care for schizophrenia and related disorders, and except for clozapine, are first-line treatment options for the management of psychosis. Brexpiprazole is indicated for the treatment of schizophrenia in adult and adolescent patients 13 years and older. In clinical trials for schizophrenia in adults and adolescents, brexpiprazole was superior to placebo in the primary efficacy endpoint of change from baseline to Week 6 in the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) total score, which measures positive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia, and general psychopathology. Brexpiprazole was also superior to placebo in a long-term maintenance trial assessing the safety and efficacy of brexpiprazole in delaying the time to relapse in adults with schizophrenia. Brexpiprazole is also used as adjunctive therapy to antidepressants in adults with major depressive disorder (MDD); brexpiprazole was superior to placebo in change from baseline to Week 6 in the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), a 10-item clinician-related scale used to assess the degree of depressive symptomatology. Brexpiprazole is also indicated for agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer's disease (AD). The primary efficacy endpoint in the 2 preapproval agitation studies was the change from baseline in the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory total (CMAI) score at Week 12 including 1) aggressive behavior (e.g., screaming, throwing things, cursing/verbal aggression, kicking, pushing scratching, hurting self or others); 2) physically non-aggressive behavior (e.g., repetitive mannerisms, general restlessness, pacing); and 3) verbally agitated behavior (e.g., complaining, repetitive questions, constant requests for attention). At total doses of 2 or 3 mg per day, the total CMAI scores were significantly decreased with brexpiprazole treatment vs. placebo in both studies at 12 weeks; a 1 mg/day dosage was not significantly effective vs. placebo in these trials. The FDA-approved indication is specifically for treating agitation due to Alzheimer's disease. It is not known if brexpiprazole is effective for other Behavioral and Psychological Symptoms of Dementia (BPSD) symptoms, such as psychosis, or for other dementia types. In a systematic review of 20 randomized controlled trials that included 6,374 individuals and examined use of 5 common second-generation antipsychotics, brexpiprazole was found to be the most efficacious in the treatment of both agitation and aggressive behaviors, an additional BPSD symptom that can affect caregiver burden and healthcare costs. As with all atypical antipsychotics, the label includes a boxed warning regarding an increased mortality risk in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis. Product labels for all antidepressant treatments contain a boxed warning related to an increased risk of suicidality in children, adolescents, and young adults during the initial stages of therapy, whether treating depression or other psychiatric conditions. Brexpiprazole was initially FDA approved in 2015.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Although the exact mechanism of action is unknown, brexpiprazole may exert its therapeutic effects through a combination of partial agonist activity at dopaminergic D2 receptors and serotonergic 5-HT1A receptors, and antagonist activity at serotonergic 5-HT2A receptors. Brexpiprazole is also a partial agonist at dopaminergic D3 receptors and an antagonist at a1A, a1B, a1D, and a2C alpha receptors, as well as 5-HT2B, 5-HT7, histamine H1, and muscarinic M1 receptors. Brexpiprazole has a high affinity for 5-HT1A, 5-HT2A, D2, D3, a1B, and a2C receptors, and a moderate affinity for H1 receptors. Actions at muscarinic, alpha-1, and histamine receptors likely explain some adverse effects of antipsychotics such as orthostatic hypotension and somnolence.
 
At a dose 3 times the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) for the treatment of schizophrenia and 4 times the MRHD for adjunctive therapy to antidepressants for the treatment of major depressive disorder or agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer's disease, brexpiprazole does not result in a clinically relevant prolongation of the QTc interval.

  Text
  Brexpiprazole is administered orally. Brexpiprazole has a high volume of distribution and is highly protein bound in plasma (greater than 99%) to serum albumin and alpha1-acid glycoprotein, and its protein binding is not affected by renal or hepatic impairment. Both in vitro and in vivo data indicate that the metabolism of brexpiprazole is primarily mediated by CYP3A4 and CYP2D6. After single and multiple doses, brexpiprazole and its major metabolite, DM-3411, are the predominant drug moieties in the systemic circulation. At steady-state, DM-3411 represents 23% to 48% of brexpiprazole exposure (AUC) in plasma. DM-3411 does not contribute to the therapeutic effects of brexpiprazole. The half-lives of brexpiprazole and DM-3411 are 91 hours and 86 hours, respectively. Approximately 25% of a single oral dose is recovered in the urine and 46% is excreted in feces. Less than 1% of a dose is excreted as unchanged brexpiprazole in the urine and approximately 14% of a dose is excreted unchanged in the feces.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and drug transporters: CYP2D6, CYP3A4
Based on in vitro metabolism studies of brexpiprazole evaluating CYP1A1, CYP1A2, CYP2A6, CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, CYP2E1, and CYP3A4, the metabolism of brexpiprazole is primarily mediated by CYP3A4 and CYP2D6. Because CYP2D6 is one of the primary metabolic pathways of brexpiprazole, one-half of the brexiprazole dose is recommended in known CYP2D6 poor metabolizers. One-half of the dose is recommended during concurrent use of a strong CYP3A4 or CYP2D6 inhibitor, and one-quarter of the dose is recommended during combined use of a moderate to strong CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 inhibitor with brexpiprazole. If the co-administered drug is discontinued, adjust the brexpiprazole dose to its original level. A doubling of the brexpiprazole dose over 1 to 2 weeks is recommended during concurrent use of a strong CYP3A4 inducer. If the co-administered CYP3A4 inducer is discontinued, reduce the brexpiprazole dose to the original level over 1 to 2 weeks. One-quarter of the brexpiprazole dose is recommended in CYP2D6 poor metabolizers who are receiving a moderate to strong CYP3A4 inhibitor. In vitro data indicate that brexpiprazole is not a substrate of efflux transporters such as P-gp or BCRP and there is no clinically relevant inhibition of CYP450 isoenzymes. No dosage adjustment of brexpiprazole is required when administered concomitantly with CYP2B6 inhibitors (e.g., ticlopidine) or gastric pH modifiers (e.g., omeprazole). Additionally, no dosage adjustment for substrates of CYP2D6 (e.g., dextromethorphan), CYP3A4 (e.g., lovastatin), CYP2B6 (e.g., bupropion), BCRP (e.g., rosuvastatin), or P-gp (e.g., fexofenadine) is required when administered concurrently with brexpiprazole.
