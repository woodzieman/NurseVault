Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Strontium-89 should be used with caution in patients with bone marrow suppression due to previous myelosuppressive chemotherapy or radiation therapy. To avoid severe thrombocytopenia and/or leukopenia, patients should have minimum platelet counts of > 65,000/mm3 and white blood cell counts > 2,500/mm3. Complete blood counts should be monitored at least every other week for 8 weeks following strontium-89 administration. Patients with extensive bone marrow infiltration by tumor may be at increased risk of prolonged leukopenia and thrombocytopenia following strontium-89 injection.
 
Special consideration of the risks versus benefits of strontium-89 therapy should be given to patients with underlying coagulopathy disorders such as chronic disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). These patients may be at increased risk of adverse effects due to strontium-89-induced thrombocytopenia.
 
Intramuscular injections should be avoided in patients with platelet counts < 50,000/mm3 who are receiving strontium-89. IM injections can cause bleeding, bruising, or hematomas in patients with thrombocytopenia.
 
Since pain is the most important clinical symptom, patients with impending spinal cord compression or pathologic fractures should receive strontium-89 with caution. The benefit of strontium-89 therapy in patients with < 2 months projected survival is minimal.
 
Strontium-89 is a pregnancy category D agent. Radiopharmaceuticals are generally not recommended for therapeutic use during pregnancy due to the risk of radiation exposure to the fetus. Strontium-89 would be expected to cause adverse effects in the fetus. In cases where the pregnancy status is uncertain, a pregnancy test should be administered prior to beginning therapy with strontium-89.
 
Since strontium-89 acts as a calcium analog, it is expected to be excreted into breast milk. It is recommended to stop breast-feeding after treatment with strontium-89 therapy due to the risk of radiation exposure to the infant.
 
There have been no studies examining the safety and efficacy of strontium-89 in children. However, there are cases describing the use of strontium in a small number of children.
 
Patients with urinary incontinence may require bladder catheterization for the first week after strontium-89 injection to decrease radiation contamination of clothing, bedding, and the surrounding environment.
 
Strontium-89 is primarily excreted by the kidney. Patients with renal impairment may have decreased elimination of strontium-89. These increased levels may lead to increased availability of strontium-89 to the bone leading increased retention in some patients. This may result in variable clinical responses and toxicities in patients when similar doses are given.

Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  The most significant adverse reaction associated with strontium-89 is dose-dependent myelosuppression, which is manifested primarily as thrombocytopenia and leukopenia. Decreases in baseline platelet counts of 20—40% may occur. Platelet nadir occurs 4—6 weeks following strontium-89 injection and recovery occurs within 6—8 weeks. Leukopenia is not as severe. Severe, prolonged myelosuppression resulting in neutropenia, sepsis, and death has been reported in one patient following strontium-89 therapy for metastatic prostate cancer.Approximately 10—20% of patients may develop a flare reaction consisting of bone pain, chills, fever, and flushing shortly after administration of strontium-89. These symptoms usually resolve within 48 hours and may be predictive of a positive response.Secondary malignancy has been reported in patients with prostate cancer treated with strontium-89. Acute myelogenous leukemia has been reported in 2 patients 17 and 26 months following administration of strontium-89. Cytogenetic testing of one patient's bone marrow revealed monosomy 7, which is commonly seen in acute leukemias secondary to chemotherapy or radiation.

Description
  Text
  Description: Strontium-89 ((89)Sr) chloride is a parenteral radiopharmaceutical agent. It is used as palliative therapy to relieve bone pain in patients with painful skeletal metastases. Strontium-89 emits pure beta radiation. It differs from other therapeutic radioisotopes due to its long half-life and lack of gamma emission. Due to the long half-life and extended retention in bone (> 100 days), low doses (3—4 mCi) of strontium-89 produce effective analgesia. In clinical studies, 75—80% of patients with bone pain due to metastatic prostate cancer have significant reduction in their pain following strontium-89 administration. The use of strontium-89 as an adjuvant to external beam radiation in patients with hormone-refractory, metastatic prostate cancer resulted in significant improvements in pain control, delayed the progression of symptoms and the onset of pain at new metastatic sites and reduced the requirement for additional palliative radiation therapy. In addition, significant decreases in prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and alkaline phosphatase levels were noted, which may indicate that strontium-89 affects disease progression. Strontium-89 has also been used in metastatic breast and lung cancers with moderate decrease in pain. Although strontium-89 is typically used to palliate osteoblastic metastatic lesions, case reports suggest decreases in bone pain in patients with multiple myeloma whose lesions demonstrate osteoblastic as well as osteolytic characteristics. Use of this agent should be restricted to physicians qualified in the safe use and handling of radiopharmaceuticals. The FDA approved strontium-89 chloride on June 18, 1993.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Mechanism of Action: Strontium-89 chloride is a radioisotope. It emits pure beta radiation with maximum beta energy of 1.5 MeV and a tissue penetration of 8 mm. Strontium-89 is taken up in bone by exchanging with the calcium component of hydroxyapatite. Since many patients with bone metastasis have multiple areas of involvement, systemic radioisotopes deliver radiation to all these areas over an extended period and at a relatively low dose-rate. Radioisotopes are less toxic, more easily administered, and more effective at subclinical sites of bone disease than other delivery methods of radiation. The mechanism by which strontium-89 and other radioisotopes produce their analgesic effects is not understood. As patients may experience significant pain relief within 24—48 hours, the initial analgesic benefit is probably not due to the effect of strontium-89 on the cancer. Potential mechanisms include reduction in lymphocyte-associated cytokines or alterations in the functions of osteoclasts and osteoblasts. Strontium-89 may be incorporated into cancer cells and damage DNA causing cell death. Radioisotopes are most effective in treating osteoblastic lesions.

Pharmacokinetics
  Special Populations
    Name
    Renal Impairment
    Description
    In patients with renal impairment, the clearance of strontium-89 is impaired. This results in an increased retention of strontium-89. The ratio between metastatic and normal bone is not affected; however, variable clinical responses may be seen.
  Text
  Strontium-89 is administered intravenously. Strontium mimics calcium in humans. It is rapidly taken up into the bone matrix and is excreted through the kidneys. Strontium-89 decays by beta emission to yttrium-89 and contains no (90)-Sr/(90)-Y contamination. The amount of strontium-89 retained in the body is dependent upon the amount of metastatic involvement of the bone. Patients with small metastatic burdens eliminate approximately 80% of strontium-89 within 100 days. Patients with diffuse metastatic involvement eliminate only 10—20% of the strontium-89 within 100 days. Strontium-89 is concentrated at metastatic sites within the bone at a ratio of 10:1 to normal bone. Metastatic bone retains strontium-89 longer than normal bone. The ratio of absorbed radiation dose at metastatic sites compared with normal bone increases with time. Strontium-89 has a physical half-life of 50.5 days in metastatic bone and a half-life of 14 days in normal bone. The average radiation dose delivered to the tumor is 23 cGy/MBq (range 6—61 cGy/MBq).
