Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Females of childbearing age should avoid becoming pregnant while taking imatinib (FDA pregnancy risk category D). Sexually active female patients taking imatinib should use adequate contraception. In animal studies, imatinib was teratogenic when given during organogenesis at doses approximately equal to the clinical dose of 800 mg/day. Teratogenic effects included exencephaly or encephalocele, absent/reduced frontal lobes, and absent parietal lobes. Total fetal loss was noted in all animals. In animal studies using doses approximately one-half the maximum human dose of 800 mg/day based on body surface area, significant post-implantation loss as evidenced by either early fetal resorption or stillbirths, nonviable pups, and early pup mortality between postpartum days 0 and 4. Fetal loss was not seen in doses equivalent of about one-third of 800 mg/day. Animals exposed to a maternal dose equivalent to about one-third the maximum human dose of 800 mg/day in utero and during lactation demonstrated decreased body weight, and although fertility was not affected, fetal loss was seen when these male and female animals were mated. No adequate and well-controlled studies have been done in pregnant women. If imatinib is used during pregnancy or if a patient becomes pregnant while taking imatinib, the woman should be apprised of the potential risks to the fetus.
 
According to the manufacturer, imatinib or breast-feeding should be discontinued because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in nursing infants from imatinib. Imatinib and its active metabolite are excreted into human milk. Based on data from three breastfeeding women taking imatinib, the milk:plasma ratio is roughly 0.5 for imatinib and about 0.9 for the active metabolite. Considering the combined concentration of imatinib and its active metabolite, a breastfed infant could receive up to 10% of the maternal therapeutic dose based on body weight. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition. If a breast-feeding infant experiences an adverse effect related to a maternally ingested drug, healthcare providers are encouraged to report the adverse effect to the FDA.
 
Dose adjustments are necessary in patients receiving imatinib with moderate and severe renal impairment (see Dosage, patients with renal impairment). Patients with mild and moderate renal impairment exhibited a 1.5—2 fold increase in mean exposure to imatinib in a pharmacokinetic study of 59 patients with varying degrees of renal impairment. Two patients with severe renal impairment were dosed at 100 mg/day and exhibited drug exposure similar to patients with normal renal function dosed at 400 mg/day.
 
Severe congestive heart failure and left ventricular dysfunction have been reported with imatinib (see Adverse Reactions). Most of the patients with reported cardiac events were known to have co-morbidities and risk factors for heart problems, including advanced age and previous history of cardiac disease. Patients with cardiac disease or risk factors for heart failure should be monitored carefully, and any patient with signs or symptoms consistent with cardiac failure should be evaluated and treated. Imatinib is often associated with edema and occasionally serious fluid retention; the risk increases with higher doses, history of cardiac disease, and in elderly patients. Patients with underlying cardiac disease (e.g., heart failure or ventricular dysfunction) or renal disease may not tolerate fluid changes associated with imatinib. Patients should be weighed and monitored regularly for signs of peripheral edema. An unexpected rapid weight gain should be carefully investigated and appropriate treatment provided.
 
Bone marrow suppression often occurs during imatinib therapy and is dependent upon the stage of the underlying disease. Complete blood counts should be performed at least weekly for the first month, biweekly the second month, and periodically thereafter, as clinically indicated. Dosage adjustments for neutropenia and thrombocytopenia are recommended (see Dosage). In patients with chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), the occurrence of cytopenias is dependent upon the stage of disease and is more frequent in patients with accelerated or blast crisis than in patients with chronic phase CML. Patients should be closely monitored for any signs or symptoms of bleeding, especially GI bleeding. Gastrointestinal tumor sites may have been the source of GI bleeds in patients treated with imatinib (see Adverse Reactions). Patients with an active infection should be treated prior to receiving imatinib when possible. Opportunistic infection, including viral infection and fungal infection, may occur in some patients due to severe immunosuppression. Patients with a history of varicella zoster, other herpes infection (e.g., herpes simplex), or other viral infection are at risk for reactivation of the infection when treated with chemotherapy. Patients should immediately report any symptoms of severe myelosuppression such as fever, sore throat, or abnormal bleeding.
 
Intramuscular injections should not be given to patients with a platelet count = 2 weeks. Those undergoing chemotherapy should not be exposed to others who have recently received the oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV). Measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination is not contraindicated for the close contacts, including health care professionals, of immunocompromised patients. Passive immunoprophylaxis with immune globulins may be indicated for immunocompromised persons instead of, or in addition to, vaccination. When exposed to a vaccine-preventable disease such as measles, severely immunocompromised children should be considered susceptible regardless of their vaccination history.
 
The effect of imatinib on male fertility and spermatogenesis is unknown. Discus the possible infertility risk with any male patient. In rats, fertility was not affected, although lower testes and epididymal weights and a reduced number of motile sperm were observed in the high-dose males rats.

    diabetes mellitus, hypertension, renal impairment
    General Information
    Use imatinib with caution in patients with a history of renal impairment. Nephrotoxicity including a decline in renal function has been reported with imatinib therapy. Patients with pre-existing renal impairment, cardiac failure, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension are at increased risk for developing nephrotoxicity. Use imatinib with caution in patients with severe renal impairment. Dose reductions are necessary in patients with moderate (CrCl, 20 to 39 mL/min) or severe (CrCl, less than 20 mL/min) renal impairment.
    Headline
    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components.
    Headline
    bleeding disorder
    General Information
    Use imatinib with caution in patients with a bleeding disorder due to an increased risk of bleeding in these patients. Hemorrhage including GI hemorrhage has been reported with imatinib treatment. Gastrointestinal tumor sites may have been the source of GI bleeds in patients treated with imatinib.
    Headline
    cardiac disease, renal failure
    General Information
    Use imatinib with caution in patients with cardiac disease, risk factors for heart failure, or a history of renal failure. Severe congestive heart failure and left ventricular dysfunction have been reported in patients who received imatinib in clinical trials; patients with advanced age, a prior history of cardiac disease or other co-morbidities may be at increased risk.
    Headline
    Child-Pugh class C, hepatic failure
    General Information
    Use imatinib with caution in patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C), including hepatic failure. Severe hepatotoxicity may occur with imatinib; fatal cases of acute liver failure and liver injury requiring liver transplants have been reported. Treatment interruption and/or imatinib dosage adjustments are recommended depending on the severity of hepatotoxicity.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Imatinib may cause fetal harm when administered during pregnancy. Postmarketing reports describe spontaneous abortions and congenital anomalies associated with in utero exposure. In animal studies, prenatal exposure to imatinib in rats resulted in teratogenic effects and increased congenital abnormalities at doses equivalent to the maximum recommended human dose of 800 mg/day (based on body surface area). Pregnant patients should be advised of the potential risk to the fetus. The American Society of Clinical Oncology provides additional information on the use of certain medications for the management of cancer during pregnancy.
    Headline
    people who may become pregnant, reproductive risk
    General Information
    Counsel people who may become pregnant about the reproductive risk associated with imatinib. Imatinib can cause fetal harm. Verify pregnancy status prior to treatment initiation. Advise people who may become pregnant to use effective contraception (methods that result in less than 1% pregnancy rates) while taking imatinib and for 14 days after the last dose.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Advise patients to avoid breast-feeding during imatinib therapy and for 1 month after the final dose. Imatinib may cause serious adverse effects in a breastfed child. Both imatinib and its active metabolite are present in human milk. In data from three lactating individuals receiving imatinib, the milk-to-plasma ratio was approximately 0.5 for imatinib and 0.9 for the active metabolite. Based on the combined concentrations, a breastfed child could receive up to 10% of the patient's weight-adjusted therapeutic dose.

Interactions
Abemaciclib: (Moderate) Monitor for an increase in abemaciclib-related adverse reactions if coadministration with imatinib is necessary; consider reducing the dose of abemaciclib in 50-mg decrements if toxicities occur. Discontinue abemaciclib for patients unable to tolerate 50 mg twice daily. Abemaciclib is a CYP3A4 substrate and imatinib is a moderate CYP3A4 inhibitor. Coadministration with other moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors is predicted to increase the relative potency adjusted unbound AUC of abemaciclib plus its active metabolites (M2, M18, and M20) by approximately 1.6- to 2.4-fold.
Acalabrutinib: (Major) Decrease the acalabrutinib dose to 100 mg PO once daily if coadministered with imatinib. Coadministration may result in increased acalabrutinib exposure and toxicity (e.g., infection, bleeding, and atrial arrhythmias). Acalabrutinib is a CYP3A4 substrate; imatinib is a moderate CYP3A4 inhibitor. In physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) simulations, the Cmax and AUC values of acalabrutinib were increased by 2- to almost 3-fold when acalabrutinib was coadministered with moderate CYP3A inhibitors. Additionally, acalabrutinib may increase imatinib exposure and increase the risk of imatinib toxicity. Acalabrutinib is a substrate and inhibitor of the breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) transporter in vitro; it may inhibit intestinal BCRP. Imatinib is a BCRP substrate.
Acetaminophen: (Major) Imatinib, STI-571 may affect the metabolism of acetaminophen. In vitro, imatinib was found to inhibit acetaminophen O-glucuronidation at therapeutic levels.   Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Some of the reported adverse reactions during clinical trials of imatinib in patients with chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) may be due to the underlying disease.
 
The most frequently reported adverse reactions with imatinib include nausea/vomiting (63—73%/22—58%). Nausea and vomiting were the most commonly reported adverse events among children who got imatinib. Imatinib is sometimes associated with GI irritation; administration with food and a full glass of water may help minimize this problem.
 
The most frequently reported adverse reactions with imatinib include diarrhea (42—60%) and abdominal pain (29—37%). Imatinib is sometimes associated with GI irritation; administration with food and a full glass of water may help minimize this problem.
 
The most frequently reported adverse reactions with imatinib include edema (54—76%), muscle cramps (28—62%), fatigue (30—48%), and skin rash (unspecified) (26—48%). Periorbital edema or peripheral edema in the lower extremities was most frequently reported and managed with diuretics, other supportive measures, or by decreasing the dose of imatinib (see Dosage). Adverse events due to local or general fluid retention included pleural effusion, anasarca, ascites, pericardial effusion, pulmonary edema, and rapid weight gain with or without superficial edema. The frequency of severe superficial edema in patients with chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) was 1.5—6%, and the frequency of severe fluid retention (e.g., pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, pulmonary edema, and ascites) was 1.3—6%. Severe superficial edema and severe fluid retention (e.g., pleural effusion, pulmonary edema, and ascites) were reported in 1—6% of patients taking imatinib for GIST. Grade 3/4 pleural effusions or ascites was observed in 2% of GIST patients. Dyspnea has been reported in 12—16% of patients with CML during clinical trials, and was classified as severe in 4—5% of patients. These events were dose-related and more common in the blast crisis and accelerated phase CML studies (doses >= 600 mg/day) and in the elderly.
 
Serious or life-threatening events have been reported. Two cases of cerebral edema, one of which was fatal, have also been described as a possible complication of imatinib. During post-marketing surveillance, increased intracranial pressure, cerebral edema, cardiac tamponade, and papilledema have been reported. Peripheral edema was not reported in children receiving imatinib, while an increased incidence of peripheral edema was experienced by individuals >= 65 years of age. A patient with blast crisis died with pleural effusions, congestive heart failure, and renal failure (unspecified), which were attributed to imatinib therapy. In a case report, 10 patients with no previous history of cardiac disease developed significant congestive heart failure with left ventricular dysfunction during imatinib therapy. All 10 patients had normal left ventricular function prior to imatinib therapy (ejection fraction 56 +/- 7%), and then presented after a mean of 7.2 months (range 1—14 months) of treatment with heart failure, including significant volume overload and symptoms corresponding to a New York Heart Association (NYHA) heart failure class of 3—4 (mean NYHA class 3.5 +/- 0.5). Ejection fraction at the time of presentation was 25 +/- 8% and was associated with mild ventricular dilation. Myocardial biopsies from 2 patients indicated abnormalities consistent with toxin-induced myopathies. Patients were managed with ACE-inhibitors and other treatment measures for heart failure. The authors did further studies in mice and in vitro to ascertain the cause of imatinib-induced cardiotoxicity. They concluded that mitochondrial dysfunction and the consequent energy rundown were crucial factors in the mechanism of imatinib cardiotoxicity. The authors recommend patients be closely monitored during imatinib therapy for signs of congestive heart failure. In an international randomized phase 3 trial of 1106 patients with newly diagnosed chronic phase CML, severe cardiac failure and left ventricular dysfunction was observed in 0.7% of patients taking imatinib compared to 0.9% of patients taking interferon + cytarabine. Cases of cardiogenic shock/left ventricular dysfunction have been associated with the initiation of imatinib therapy in patients with hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) and cardiac involvement. Temporary cessation of imatinib and administration of systemic steroids and circulatory support measures reportedly reversed cardiogenic shock/left ventricular dysfunction. In patients with HES or chronic eosinophilic leukemia and in patients with myelodysplastic/ myeloproliferative diseases or aggressive systemic mastocytosis associated with high eosinophil concentrations, consider obtaining an echocardiogram and determining serum troponin concentrations. If either the echocardiogram result or the serum troponin concentration is abnormal, consider the prophylactic use of systemic steroids (1—2 mg/kg) for 1—2 weeks concomitantly with imatinib at the initiation of therapy. Evaluate and treat any patient with signs or symptoms consistent with cardiac failure.
 
Bleeding was reported in 18% of gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) patients. During clinical trials for GIST, 7 patients (5%) experienced severe GI bleeding episodes: 3 had GI bleeds, 3 had intra-tumoral bleeding, and 1 had both. Gastrointestinal tumor sites may have been the source of the GI bleeds. In phase 3 GIST studies, 211 patients (12.9%) reported grade 3—4 hemorrhage at any site. In newly diagnosed CML trials, 18.9% of patients reported bleeding with 1.8% of patients reporting grade 3—4 hemorrhage. In other CML trials, including trial of patients in accelerated or blastic phase or those following interferon failure in chronic phase, the incidence of bleeding ranged from 30—53%, including intracranial bleeding (2—9%) and GI bleeding (2—8%). Rare reports, including fatalities, of GI perforation have been received.
 
Cytopenias including leukopenia, neutropenia, pancytopenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia were reported in all imatinib studies, with a higher frequency at doses >= 750 mg/day and in patients with more advanced CML. In patients with newly diagnosed CML, these cytopenias were less frequent than in other CML patients. The frequency of grade 3 or 4 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia was 2- and 3-fold higher in CML patients with blast crisis and accelerated phase compared to those in chronic phase. Neutropenia lasted between 2—3 weeks, and the median duration of thrombocytopenia was 3—4 weeks. In pediatric patients with CML, the most frequent toxicities observed were grade 3 or 4 cytopenias including neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia. The cytopenias generally occur within the first several months of therapy. Interruption of therapy or dose reduction of imatinib may be necessary if these side effects occur (see Dosage). Complete blood counts should be performed weekly for the first month of therapy, biweekly for the second month, and periodically thereafter as clinically indicated (for example, every 2—3 months).
 
Bullous rash/vesicular rash reactions, including erythema multiforme and Stevens-Johnson syndrome, have been reported during treatment with imatinib. In some case reports, a recurrent dermatologic reaction was observed upon rechallenge. Several post-marketing reports from outside the US have described cases in which patients tolerated the reintroduction of imatinib therapy after the resolution or improvement of the reaction. In these cases, imatinib was resumed at a lower dose than that at which the reaction occurred and some patients also received concurrent treatment with antihistamines or corticosteroids. Acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis has been reported during imatinib therapy (estimated frequency 1—10%).
 
The following adverse reactions, regardless of relationship to imatinib, were reported in >= 10% of a specific patient population (incidence varies depending upon disease state and status) during any clinical trial: anxiety, chest pain (unspecified), dizziness, musculoskeletal pain, back pain, headache, insomnia, dysgeusia (taste alteration), arthralgia, dyspepsia, myalgia, fever, cough, anorexia, constipation, flatulence, pharyngitis, night sweats, diaphoresis, increased lacrimation, pruritus, epistaxis, upper respiratory tract infection (including influenza, pharyngitis, and sinusitis), hypokalemia, petechiae, pneumonia, and asthenia/weakness. The following less common adverse reactions (estimated 1—10%) have been reported during clinical trials with imatinib: abdominal distention, alopecia, blurred vision, conjunctivitis, gastrointestinal reflux, joint swelling, paresthesias, stomatitis, and xerosis (dry skin).
 
Severe elevated hepatic enzymes including hyperbilirubinemia (jaundice) occurred in 5% of CML patients receiving imatinib and were usually managed with dose reduction or interruption of therapy; the median duration of these episodes was approximately 1 week. Treatment was discontinued permanently because of liver laboratory abnormalities in < 1% of patients. However, one patient, who was taking acetaminophen regularly for fever, died of acute hepatic failure. When imatinib is combined with chemotherapy, transaminase elevations and hyperbilirubinemia has been observed. Additionally, cases of acute hepatic failure have been reported. In the GIST trial, grade 3 or 4 ALT elevations were observed in 6.8% of patients. Bilirubin elevation was observed in 2.7% of patients. Interruption of therapy or dose reduction of imatinib may be necessary if these side effects occur (see Dosage). Liver function (transaminases, bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase) should be monitored before initiation of treatment and monthly, or as clinically indicated.
 
Clastogenesis (chromosomal aberrations) was observed in an in vitro mammalian cell assay for genotoxic effects for imatinib in the presence of metabolic activation. Two intermediates of the manufacturing process, which are also present in the final product, are positive for mutagenesis in the Ames assay. In a study of fertility, male rats dosed for 70 days prior to mating, testicular and epididymal weights and percent motile sperm were decreased at doses of 65 mg/kg, approximately three-fourths the maximum clinical dose of 800 mg/day, based on body surface area; this effect was not seen at doses <= 20 mg/kg (one-fourth the maximum human dose of 800 mg/day). When females rats were dosed 14 days prior to mating and through gestational day 6, there was no effect on mating or the number of pregnant females. Females of childbearing age should avoid becoming pregnant while taking imatinib. In animal studies, imatinib induced teratogenesis at doses approximately equal to the clinical human dose of 800 mg/day. Teratogenic effects included exencephaly or encephalocele, absent/reduced frontal lobes, and absent parietal lobes. Total fetal loss was noted in all animals. No adequate and well-controlled studies have been done in human pregnancy.
 
Cases of clinical hypothyroidism have occurred in thyroidectomy patients undergoing levothyroxine replacement during treatment with imatinib. TSH levels should be carefully monitored in these patients.

Description
  Text
  Imatinib is an oral BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitor. It is indicated for the treatment of adults and pediatric patients with newly diagnosed chronic-phase Philadelphia chromosome-positive (Ph+) chronic myeloid leukemia (CML); chronic-phase, accelerated-phase, or myeloid or lymphoid blast-phase Ph+ CML after failure of interferon-alfa therapy; relapsed or refractory Ph+ acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL); myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative diseases associated with platelet-derived growth factor receptor; aggressive systemic mastocytosis without the D816V c-Kit mutation or with c-Kit mutational status unknown; and hypereosinophilic syndrome and/or chronic eosinophilic leukemia. It is also indicated for the treatment of unresectable, recurrent and/or metastatic dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans; Kit (CD117)-positive unresectable and/or metastatic malignant gastrointestinal stromal tumors; and in pediatric patients 1 year of age and older with newly diagnosed Ph+ ALL in combination with chemotherapy. Severe myelosuppression, congestive heart failure, and hepatotoxicity have been reported. 63991

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Imatinib is a protein tyrosine kinase inhibitor. Inhibition of the BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase results in inhibition of proliferation and induces apoptosis in BCR-ABL positive cell lines and in fresh leukemic cells from Philadelphia chromosome positive chronic myeloid leukemia. In vivo, imatinib inhibits tumor growth of BCR-ABL transfected murine myeloid cells as well as BCR-ABL positive leukemia lines derived from CML patients in blast crisis. It is also an inhibitor of the receptor tyrosine kinases for platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and stem cell factor (SCF), c-Kit, and inhibits PDGF- and SCF-mediated cellular events. In vitro, imatinib inhibits proliferation and induces apoptosis in gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) cells, which express an activating c-Kit mutation.
Chronic myelogenous leukemia is characterized by a genetic abnormality, the Philadelphia chromosome (Ph), resulting from the reciprocal translocation between the long arms of chromosomes 9 and 22. The resultant fusion protein, BCR-ABL, is a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that is responsible for the development of CML. Imatinib causes growth arrest or apoptosis in cells that express BCR-ABL but does not affect normal cells. Resistance to imatinib and subsequent disease relapse seem to be associated with a failure to maintain effective inhibition of BCR-ABL activity. Resistance has been reported due to BCR-ABL gene amplification or alteration of the amino acids of BCR-ABL resulting in high levels of kinase activity even in the presence of imatinib.

    Description
    Imatinib is well absorbed following oral administration; the mean absolute bioavailability is 98%. Peak plasma concentrations are reached within 2 to 4 hours of imatinib dosing.   Text
  Imatinib is administered orally. It is approximately 95% bound to plasma proteins, primarily albumin and alpha 1-acid glycoprotein, at clinically relevant concentrations. Imatinib is metabolized via CYP450 isoenzymes, primarily CYP3A4. Its major active metabolite, N-demethylated piperazine derivative (CGP74588), has similar potency as the parent drug. In healthy volunteers, the elimination half-lives of imatinib CGP74588 were approximately 18 and 40 hours, respectively. In a 50-year-old patient, the clearance of imatinib is predicted to be 8 L/hour in patients weighing 50 kg and 14 L/hour in patients weighing 100 kg; this represents an inter-patient variability of about 40%. Following an oral 14C-labeled dose of imatinib, approximately 81% of the dose (feces, 68%; urine, 13%) was eliminated within 7 days. Unchanged imatinib accounted for 25% of the dose (feces, 20%; urine, 5%) with the remainder excreted as metabolites.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 isoenzymes and transporters: CYP3A4, CYP1A1, CYP2D6, CYP2D9, CYP2C19, BCRP, BSEP, MATE1, MATE2-K, OATP1A2, OCT1, OCT2, P-gp
Imatinib is primarily metabolized via CYP3A4; it is metabolized via CYP1A2, CYP2D6, CYP2C9, and CYP2C19 to a lesser extent. Avoid the concomitant use of strong CYP3A4 inducers; consider at least a 50% dose increase if coadministration is unavoidable. Use imatinib cautiously in combination with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors. In vitro, imatinib inhibits CYP2C9 and CYP2D6. It is a substrate of BCRP, P-gp, OATP1A2, and OCT1; it also inhibits BCRP, BSEP, P-gp, MATE1, MATE2-K, OCT1, and OCT2. In drug interaction studies, imatinib was a moderate inhibitor of CYP3A4 and a weak inhibitor of CYP2D6; use caution if imatinib is coadministered with CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 substrates that have a narrow therapeutic index.
