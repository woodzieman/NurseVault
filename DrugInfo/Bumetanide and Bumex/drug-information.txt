Drug Class: Bumetanide is a sulfonamide-derived oral, parenteral, and intranasal loop diuretic indicated for the management of edema associated with congestive heart failure, hepatic and renal disease, including nephrotic syndrome.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC), oral, nasal; Common adult doses: 10 mg/day PO, IV, or IM; 2 mg/day intranasally.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): arthralgia, diaphoresis, dizziness, fatigue, headache, muscle cramps, musculoskeletal pain, vertigo, weakness, hypocalcemia, hypochloremia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hyponatremia, hypophosphatemia, metabolic alkalosis, dehydration, hypotension, hypovolemia, orthostatic hypotension, polyuria, syncope, azotemia, interstitial nephritis, oliguria, proteinuria, renal failure (unspecified), pruritus, rash (unspecified), stevens-johnson syndrome

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: Bumetanide is contraindicated in patients with known bumetanide hypersensitivity. Because cross-sensitivity with furosemide has rarely been observed, bumetanide can be substituted for furosemide in patients allergic to furosemide.

Contraindications And Precautions
  Headline
  Contraindications/Warnings/Precautions
  General Information
  Bumetanide is contraindicated in patients with known bumetanide hypersensitivity. Because cross-sensitivity with furosemide has rarely been observed, bumetanide can be substituted for furosemide in patients allergic to furosemide. The risk of an allergic reaction after administration of a loop diuretic in a patient with sulfonamide hypersensitivity or thiazide diuretic hypersensitivity appears to be very low. Although bumetanide is a sulfonamide derivative, sulfonamide cross-sensitivity has been rarely documented. A case report, published in 1987, documents an anaphylactic reaction to IV furosemide in a patient who was subsequently skin-tested with furosemide, bumetanide, ethacrynic acid, chlorothiazide, and sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim. A positive reaction was elicited to all except ethacrynic acid. This case documents hypersensitivity to both furosemide and bumetanide in a patient with sulfonamide hypersensitivity. Prior to this, neither the FDA nor the manufacturer of furosemide (Lasix®) had received any reports of cross-sensitivity between furosemide and sulfonamide antibiotics. Bumetanide does not contain the N4-aromatic amine or the N1-substituent which are present in sulfonamide antibiotics. Non-arylamine sulfonamide derivatives, such as loop diuretics, have been proposed to have a lower risk of allergic reactions in patients with sulfonamide allergy, presumably due to lack of an arylamine group at the N4 position (a proposed structural site of action for sulfonamide allergy). One large retrospective cohort study has reported that in patients with the presence of an allergic reaction after exposure to a sulfonamide antibiotic, 9.9% had an allergic reaction after receiving a non-antibiotic sulfonamide derivative, while in patients who lacked an allergic reaction after sulfonamide antibiotic exposure, 1.6% had an allergic reaction after administration of a non-antibiotic sulfonamide derivative (adjusted odds ratio 2.8; 95% CI, 2.1—3.7). A causal relationship between sulfonamide hypersensitivity and allergic reactions with non-arylamine sulfonamide derivatives has not been definitively established and remains controversial. In general, patients with a documented sulfonamide allergy are considered to be predisposed for development of allergic drug reactions.
 
Bumetanide is a potent diuretic and can cause electrolyte imbalance. Patients should be carefully monitored; dosage adjustments may be necessary. Because of this, bumetanide is contraindicated in patients with severe electrolyte imbalance until the condition is improved or corrected. Significant hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, hypochloremia, and/or hypomagnesemia should be corrected before administration. Loop diuretics may induce metabolic alkalosis associated with hypokalemia and hypochloremia; this acid/base imbalance is effectively treated with potassium chloride replacement.
 
Bumetanide is contraindicated in patients in hepatic encephalopathy (hepatic coma) until the condition is improved or corrected. Bumetanide-induced fluctuations in serum electrolyte concentrations can occur rapidly and precipitate serious hepatic effects in susceptible patients, including hepatic encephalopathy and/or coma. Bumetanide should be used with caution in patients with significant hepatic disease such as cirrhosis with ascites.
 
Blood and/or urine glucose levels should be monitored closely in patients with diabetes mellitus receiving bumetanide because loop diuretics can impair glucose tolerance resulting in hyperglycemia.
 
Bumetanide can cause dehydration. Patients should be careful monitored; dosage adjustments may be necessary. Because of this, bumetanide is contraindicated in any patient with anuria. Bumetanide should be used with caution in patients with severe renal disease such as severe renal impairment or renal failure. Bumetanide-induced hypovolemia can precipitate oliguria and azotemia in these patients. Although bumetanide can be used to induce diuresis in renal insufficiency, any marked increase in blood urea nitrogen or serum creatinine, or the development of oliguria during therapy of patients with progressive renal disease, is an indication for discontinuation of treatment with bumetanide. Renal failure may reduce drug clearance and warrant the use of higher doses with extended dosing intervals. Bumetanide may be less effective in patients with renal failure and higher doses may be required. Delayed excretion of bumetanide in patients with renal failure may increase the risk of toxicity (e.g., ototoxicity).
 
Patients with pre-existing hypovolemia or hypotension should have their condition corrected before bumetanide is initiated. Orthostatic hypotension may occur during treatment with loop diuretics. Excessive hypotension can result in syncope. The antihypertensive effects of diuretics may be enhanced in patients predisposed for orthostatic hypotension, including the post-sympathectomy patient. Greater sensitivity to the hypotensive and diuretic effects of bumetanide is possible in elderly patients.
 
Since loop diuretics can reduce the clearance of uric acid, patients with gout or hyperuricemia can have exacerbations of their disease.
 
Patients with ventricular arrhythmias, heart failure, potassium-losing nephropathy, aldosterone excess, or diarrhea should be monitored closely since bumetanide- induced hypokalemia can exacerbate these conditions.
 
Excessive diuresis with bumetanide should be avoided in patients with acute myocardial infarction due to the risk of precipitating shock.
 
High doses and accumulation of bumetanide may cause ototoxicity. Bumetanide should be used with caution in patients with hearing impairment or tinnitus. Adverse otic effects occur more frequently in patients who are also receiving other ototoxic agents or who have severe renal impairment.
 
Bumetanide has been reported to cause pancreatitis. It should be used with caution in patients with a history of pancreatitis.
 
Bumetanide is classified as FDA pregnancy risk category C. The safety of bumetanide administration during pregnancy has not been established, so the drug should be administered to pregnant women only when absolutely necessary. It is important to note that diuretics are generally not recommended for the treatment of pregnancy-induced hypertension (eclampsia, preeclampsia) since they do not alter the course of toxemia and may exacerbate maternal hypovolemia associated with this condition.
 
It is not known whether bumetanide is excreted in human milk. The manufacturer states that breast-feeding generally should not be undertaken during bumetanide therapy since bumetanide may be excreted in human milk. In addition, diuretics such as bumetanide may suppress lactation.
 
Safety and efficacy of bumetanide has not been established in adolescents and children under age 18 years. In vitro studies using pooled sera from critically ill neonates have shown bumetanide to be a potent displacer of bilirubin (see Pharmacokinetics). The administration of bumetanide could present a particular concern if given to critically ill or neonates with jaundice who are at risk for kernicterus.

    Hypersensitivity
    General Information
    This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Because cross-sensitivity with furosemide has rarely been observed, bumetanide can be substituted for furosemide in patients allergic to furosemide. According to the manufacturer, individuals with a sulfonamide hypersensitivity may show a hypersensitivity to bumetanide. However, while bumetanide is a sulfonamide derivative, sulfonamide cross-sensitivity has rarely been documented. Sulfonamide antibiotics contain the N4-aromatic amine (arylamine) and the N1-aromatic heterocyclic ring which are believed to be involved in the development of IgE-hypersensitivity reactions; bumetanide does not contain either. A relationship between sulfonamide hypersensitivity and allergic reactions with non-arylamine sulfonamide derivatives has not been definitively established and remains controversial. It has been theorized that individuals with a documented sulfonamide allergy may have a predisposition for the development of allergic drug reactions.

    General Information
    Bumetanide is contraindicated in individuals with severe electrolyte imbalance until the condition is improved or corrected. Bumetanide may cause electrolyte imbalances; thus, correct any significant electrolyte imbalances, such as hyponatremia, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, hypochloremia, and/or hypomagnesemia, before administration. Monitor individuals taking bumetanide for signs of electrolyte or fluid imbalance and periodically monitor serum electrolytes.

Therefore, use bumetanide with caution in individuals with hepatic cirrhosis and ascites and consider initiating oral or injectable bumetanide in the hospital with small doses and careful monitoring of electrolytes and clinical status. Individuals with hepatic cirrhosis and ascites have a higher risk of developing hypokalemia.

Excessive doses or too frequent administration may lead to profound water loss, electrolyte depletion, dehydration, reduction in blood volume, and circulatory collapse. Carefully monitor patients and adjust dose as necessary.     Headline
    preeclampsia, pregnancy
    General Information
    There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of bumetanide during human pregnancy. Investigational and marketing experience have not indicated any evidence of adverse effects on the fetus; however, these data do not rule out the possibility of harmful effects. Bumetanide has been shown to be nonteratogenic, but it has a slight embryocidal effect in rats when given in doses of 3,400 times the maximum human therapeutic dose (MRHD) and in rabbits at doses of 3.4 times the MRHD. Moderate growth retardation and increased incidence of delayed ossification of sternebrae were observed in rats at oral doses of 100 mg/kg/day (3,400 times the MRHD); these adverse effects were not observed at doses of 30 mg/kg/day (1,000 times the MRHD). Diuretics are generally considered second-line agents for the treatment of hypertension during pregnancy. Theoretically, there is concern about the potential for diuretics to cause intravascular volume depletion, which could cause fetal growth restriction or oligohydramnios. However, this concern has not been supported by the literature. Closely monitor the pregnant person's blood pressure, heart rate, and volume status and adjust their pharmacologic treatment as necessary to avoid hypotension and placental hypoperfusion. Diuretics should be generally avoided in preeclampsia, as plasma volume is reduced.

    Headline
    geriatric
    General Information
    Greater sensitivity to the usual doses of bumetanide may occur in geriatric patients and care should be taken in initial dose selection. In general, initial doses should be at the low end of the dosage range to reflect the greater frequency of decreased hepatic, renal, or cardiac function and of concomitant disease or other drug therapy. Excessive doses or too frequent administration in geriatric adults may result in profound water loss, dehydration, electrolyte depletion, reduction in blood volume and circulatory collapse with the possibility of vascular thrombosis and embolism. Geriatric adults may have a higher risk of orthostatic hypotension with bumetanide therapy. Monitor renal function and serum electrolytes along with signs and symptoms of fluid and electrolyte imbalances. According to the Beers Criteria, diuretics are considered potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) in geriatric adults and should be used with caution due to the potential for causing or exacerbating SIADH or hyponatremia. Sodium levels should be closely monitored when starting or changing dosages of diuretics in older adults.

Ototoxicity is best documented with the loop diuretics ethacrynic acid and furosemide, but may also occur with either bumetanide or torsemide. The exact mechanism by which furosemide or other loop diuretics produce ototoxicity is unknown. Usually, reports indicate that furosemide ototoxicity is associated with rapid injection, severe renal impairment, higher than recommended dosages or infusion rates, or concomitant therapy with aminoglycoside antibiotics, ethacrynic acid, or other ototoxic drugs. Adverse Reactions
  Headline
  Adverse Reactions
  General Information
  Muscle cramps (1.1%), dizziness (1.1%), low blood pressure (0.8%), and headache (0.6%) are the most frequent adverse effects (possibly or probably drug-related) reported during bumetanide therapy. Less frequent clinical adverse reactions to bumetanide include impaired hearing (0.5%), pruritus (0.4%), electrocardiogram changes (0.4%), weakness (0.2%), urticaria (0.2%), abdominal pain (0.2%), arthralgia (0.2%), musculoskeletal pain (0.2%), rash (unspecified) (0.2%), and nausea/vomiting (0.2% vomiting). Other adverse reactions, which have each occurred in approximately 0.1% of patients treated with bumetanide include vertigo, chest pain, fatigue, diaphoresis, hyperventilation, xerostomia, dyspepsia, asterixis, nipple tenderness, and diarrhea.
 
Electrolyte abnormalities reported during bumetanide therapy have included hypochloremia (14.9%), hypokalemia (14 .7%), and hyponatremia (9.2%). Bumetanide may also increase urinary calcium and phosphate excretion with resultant hypocalcemia and/or hypophosphatemia. Other loop diuretics have been shown to increase the urinary excretion of magnesium; this may result in hypomagnesemia. Patients receiving bumetanide should be informed of the signs and symptoms of electrolyte imbalances (e.g., lassitude, mental confusion, fatigue, faintness, dizziness, muscle cramps, headache, paresthesia, thirst, anorexia, nausea, or vomiting) and report these signs immediately. Hypokalemia is a particular concern with bumetanide administration, and supplemental therapy with potassium chloride or potassium-sparing diuretics may be necessary. Loop diuretics may also induce metabolic alkalosis associated with hypokalemia and hypochloremia; this acid/base imbalance is effectively treated with potassium chloride replacement. Bumetanide-induced metabolic alkalosis can be exacerbated in patients with other conditions that cause potassium loss including vomiting, diarrhea, paracentesis, GI drainage, and/or excessive sweating.
 
Hypotension has been reported in 0.8% of bumetanide-treated patients. Hypovolemia and dehydration can occur during bumetanide therapy due to polyuria and excessive electrolyte losses. The risk of such events is increased when large doses are administered or when the drug is given patients with a restricted sodium intake. The resultant dehydration and hypovolemia can cause orthostatic hypotension and hemoconcentration, which could lead to circulatory collapse, syncope, or thromboembolic events, particularly in chronic cardiac patients or the elderly.
 
Renal failure (unspecified), increased serum creatinine, and azotemia (increased blood urea nitrogen) have been reported in 0.5%, 7.4%, and 10.6% of bumetanide-treated patients, respectively. Abrupt decreases in glomerular filtration rates and renal blood flow have occurred in patients receiving bumetanide. Reversible azotemia, and/or increased serum creatinine have occurred in dehydrated patients. Bumetanide-induced renal failure (e.g., interstitial nephritis) occurs rarely. Increases in urinary protein (0.3%) have also been observed during bumetanide therapy. Although bumetanide can be used to induce diuresis in renal insufficiency, any marked increase in blood urea nitrogen or serum creatinine, or the development of oliguria during therapy of patients with progressive renal disease, is an indication for discontinuation of treatment with bumetanide.
 
Bumetanide-induced fluctuations in serum electrolyte concentrations can occur rapidly, and may precipitate hepatic encephalopathy and coma in susceptible patients with preexisting hepatic disease. Encephalopathy has been reported in 0.6% of bumetanide-treated patients with preexisting liver disease. Hyperaldosteronism, secondary to cirrhosis or nephrosis, can also predispose patients to developing potassium depletion when bumetanide is administered. Diuresis induced by bumetanide may also rarely be accompanied by changes in LDH (1%), total serum bilirubin (0.8%), alkaline phosphatase (0.4%), and hepatic enzymes (approximately 0.6% SGOT or SGPT).
 
Hyperuricemia has been reported in about 18.4% of tested patients who were receiving bumetanide. Bumetanide occasionally can cause transient elevations of uric acid concentrations. Bumetanide therapy has reportedly resulted in reversible hyperuricemia, which is usually asymptomatic.
 
Impaired hearing and ear discomfort have been reported in 0.5% and 0.1% of bumetanide-treated patients, respectively. Ototoxicity, manifested as tinnitus, or reversible or permanent hearing loss rarely occurs during bumetanide therapy and is usually associated with rapid administration of large doses of the drug. Adverse otic effects occur more frequently in patients who are also receiving other ototoxic agents or who have severe renal impairment.
 
Impotence (erectile dysfunction) such as difficulty maintaining an erection and premature ejaculation have been reported in approximately 0.1% of patients treated with bumetanide.
 
Bumetanide has been associated with hyperglycemia (6.6%), possibly due to potassium depletion. Glycosuria may occur; increases in urinary glucose has been reported in 0.7% of bumetanide-treated patients.
 
Diuretics, particularly thiazide and loop diuretics, have been shown to cause hypercholesterolemia, hypertriglyceridemia, as well as increased plasma concentrations of LDL. Some studies have suggested that these effects may decrease or cease with long-term therapy, and are not clinically important.
 
Rarely, adverse hematologic effects have been reported during bumetanide therapy and include thrombocytopenia (0.2%) and leukopenia. Deviations in hemoglobin (0.8%), prothrombin time (0.8%), hematocrit (0.6%), WBC (0.3%), and differential counts (0.1%) have also been observed during bumetanide therapy. No causal relationship to the drug has been established. There have been rare spontaneous reports of thrombocytopenia from post-marketing experience.

Patients receiving bumetanide should be informed of the signs and symptoms of electrolyte imbalances (e.g., lassitude, mental confusion, fatigue, faintness, dizziness, muscle cramps, headache, paresthesia, thirst, anorexia, nausea, or vomiting) and report these signs immediately. Hypokalemia is a particular concern with bumetanide administration, and supplemental therapy with potassium chloride or potassium-sparing diuretics may be necessary. Loop diuretics may also induce metabolic alkalosis associated with hypokalemia and hypochloremia; this acid/base imbalance is effectively treated with potassium chloride replacement. Bumetanide-induced metabolic alkalosis can be exacerbated in patients with other conditions that cause potassium loss including vomiting, diarrhea, paracentesis, GI drainage, and/or excessive sweating.

Description
  Text
  Bumetanide is a sulfonamide-derived oral, parenteral, and intranasal loop diuretic indicated for the management of edema associated with congestive heart failure, hepatic and renal disease, including nephrotic syndrome. Although structurally related to furosemide, bumetanide has a diuretic potency of approximately 40 times more than furosemide. Bumetanide was FDA-approved in 1983.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Bumetanide is a loop diuretic that inhibits sodium and chloride resorption by competing with chloride for the Na+/K+/2Cl- co-transporter in the ascending limb of the loop of Henle. Bumetanide may also act on the proximal tubule, as phosphate reabsorption occurs primarily in the proximal tubule and phosphaturia occurs with bumetanide therapy. A profound diuresis results from the increased urinary excretion of sodium, chloride, potassium, and hydrogen ions. Additionally, bumetanide causes increased urinary excretion of calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate, ammonium, and phosphate. The diuresis caused by bumetanide can lead to increased aldosterone production, resulting in increased sodium resorption and increased potassium and hydrogen excretion. Excessive loss of these electrolytes can lead to metabolic alkalosis.
 
Bumetanide substantially increases renal blood flow by dilating the renal vasculature. Initially, diuretics lower blood pressure by causing hypovolemia (decreased plasma and extracellular fluid), temporarily increasing the glomerular filtration rate and decreasing cardiac output. Cardiac output eventually returns to normal, but a reduction in peripheral resistance is maintained, resulting in lower blood pressure.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Oral Route
    Description
    Bumetanide has an oral bioavailability of 80% to 100%. Diuresis generally begins 30 to 60 minutes following oral administration. Peak effects occur within 1 to 2 hours following oral administration with diuresis largely complete within 4 hours; with higher doses, the diuretic action lasts for 4 to 6 hours. Food appears to lower and delay the time to reach the peak plasma concentration; however, food did not have a statistically significant effect on bumetanide bioavailability.

  Special Populations
    Name
    Pediatrics
    Description
    Children and Adolescents
Limited date are available describing the pharmacokinetics of bumetanide in older pediatric patients. The mean total clearance, renal clearance, and elimination half-life of bumetanide in 9 critically ill patients (3 months to 25 years of age) after a single dose of 0.1 mg/kg IV were 3.9 mL/kg/minute, 1.1 mL/kg/minute, and 1.65 hours, respectively.
 
Infants
A pharmacokinetic study in 53 critically ill neonates and infants (4 days to 6 months of age) showed postnatal age to be a significant determinant of bumetanide clearance and half-life, with both renal and nonrenal clearance increasing with increasing age and half-life decreasing over the first month of life then plateauing. In a population of neonates and infants receiving bumetanide for volume overload, mean serum clearance rates were 2.17 mL/kg/minute in patients younger than 2 months of age and 3.8 mL/kg/minute in patients 2 to 6 months of age. Mean serum half-life of bumetanide was 2.5 hours and 1.5 hours in patients younger than 2 months of age and those 2 to 6 months of age, respectively. The pharmacodynamic effects of bumetanide doses ranging from 0.005 to 0.1 mg/kg have been studied in 56 infants aged 4 days to 6 months. In these infants, urine flow rates increased at least 2-fold in all patients, peaking at 1 hour in most patients, and returning to or falling below baseline by 6 hours. Maximal increases in electrolyte excretion were seen within 3 hours, which correlated with an increased rate of bumetanide excretion during this time period. Pharmacologic response was best correlated with bumetanide excretion rate and was not necessarily dose-related in these infants.
 
Neonates
In neonatal patients, bumetanide elimination is considerably slower than that of adults, possibly because of immature renal and hepatobiliary function in neonates. The mean elimination half-life decreases from approximately 6 hours at birth to 2.4 hours at 1 month of age. A pharmacokinetic study in 53 critically ill neonates and infants (4 days to 6 months of age) showed postnatal age to be a significant determinant of bumetanide clearance and half-life, with both renal and nonrenal clearance increasing with increasing age and half-life decreasing over the first month of life then plateauing. According to the manufacturer, small pharmacokinetic studies of intravenous bumetanide in preterm and full term neonates with respiratory disorders have reported an apparent half-life of approximately 6 hours, with a range up to 15 hours and a serum clearance ranging from 0.2 to 1.1 mL/kg/minute (compared to a mean half-life and serum clearance of 1.07 hours and 1.84 mL/70 kg/minute, respectively, in adults). In a population of neonates and infants receiving bumetanide for volume overload, mean serum clearance rates and mean serum half-life were 2.17 mL/kg/minute and 2.5 hours in patients younger than 2 months of age, respectively. The degree of protein binding of bumetanide in cord sera from healthy neonates was approximately 97%, suggesting the potential for bilirubin displacement. A study using pooled sera from critically ill neonates found that bumetanide at concentrations of 0.5 to 50 mcg/mL, but not 0.25 mcg/mL, caused a linear increase in unbound bilirubin concentrations.
    Name
    Geriatric
    Description
    In a study of geriatric individuals aged 65 to 73 years (n = 10), total bumetanide clearance was significantly lower (1.8 +/- 0.3 mL/min per kg) when compared to younger individuals (2.9 +/- 0.2 mL/min per kg) following a single oral bumetanide dose of 0.5 mg. Maximum plasma concentrations were higher in geriatric individuals (16.9 +/- 1.8 ng/mL) compared with younger individuals (10.3 +/- 1.5 ng/mL). The increase in urine flow rate and total sodium and potassium excretion was lower in geriatric subjects compared to younger subjects; potassium excretion and fractional sodium excretion were similar between the two age groups. Nonrenal clearance, bioavailability, and volume of distribution were not significantly different between the two groups.
  Text
  Bumetanide is administered orally, intranasally, intramuscularly, or intravenously. The duration of action is 2.5 to 6 hours. Bumetanide is 94% to 96% plasma protein-bound and has a volume of distribution of 25 liters. Bumetanide is metabolized in both the kidneys and the liver with 81% of a dose excreted in the urine (45% unchanged), and 2% undergoes biliary excretion. Urinary and biliary metabolites are formed by oxidation of the N-butyl side chain. The half-life of bumetanide is 1 to 1.5 hours for both oral and parental products and 3 hours for the intranasal dosage form.
 
Affected cytochrome P450 (CYP) isoenzymes and drug transporters: none

Dosage Adjustment Guidelines
  Hepatic Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in hepatic impairment are not available; start at the lower end of the dosage range and titrate carefully to the desired response. Use bumetanide with caution in individuals with hepatic disease, since minor alterations of fluid and electrolyte balance may precipitate hepatic coma.
  Renal Impairment
  Specific guidelines for dosage adjustments in renal impairment are not available;higher doses with extended dosage intervals may be required in individuals with renal failure. Bumetanide is contraindicated in individuals with anuria. Use bumetanide with caution in individuals with severe renal disease. Consider withholding or discontinuing therapy in individuals who develop a clinically significant decrease in renal function while on bumetanide therapy.
