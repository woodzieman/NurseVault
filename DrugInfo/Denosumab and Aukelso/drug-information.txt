Drug Class: Denosumab is a highly specific monoclonal antibody that binds to the human receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-beta ligand (RANKL), sometimes called a RANK ligand inhibitor.

Routes: injection (IV/IM/SC); Common adult doses: Treatment and prevention of osteoporosis (Prolia, Jubbonti, Ospomyv, Stoboclo, Conexxence, Bildyos, Bosaya, Enoby, or Boncresa): 60 mg/dose subcutaneously.

Common Side Effects (excluding routine GI upset): cystitis, infection, pharyngitis, dental pain, oral ulceration, osteonecrosis, hypercalcemia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatemia, muscle cramps, paresthesias, tetany, arthralgia, back pain, bone pain, chills, fever, flushing, headache, musculoskeletal pain, myalgia, neuropathic pain, gastroesophageal reflux, asthenia, fatigue, peripheral edema

In an Overdose: No overdose/antidote information is listed in this file.

Key Nursing Considerations: Contraindications/precautions: This medication is contraindicated in patients with a history of hypersensitivity to it or any of its components. Ensure proper product selection.

Recommendations for adult calcium and vitamin D intake from the Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation (BHOF) to promote general bone health:
BHOF recommends a target daily intake of 1,200 mg of elemental calcium for females older than 50 years and males older than 70 years. Target daily elemental calcium intake for males 70 years or younger or females 50 years and younger is 1,000 mg.

    Headline
    hypocalcemia, hypoparathyroidism, malabsorption syndrome, metabolic bone disease, parathyroid surgery, renal failure, renal impairment, renal osteodystrophy, small bowel resection, thyroid surgery, vitamin D deficiency
    General Information
    Denosumab has been associated with cases of severe, sometimes fatal, hypocalcemia; use is contraindicated with pre-existing hypocalcemia. Correct pre-existing hypocalcemia before initiation of therapy. Individuals with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) including severe renal impairment (eGFR less than 30 mL/minute/1.73 m2) or renal failure, particularly those on dialysis, may be at an increased risk for developing severe hypocalcemia with serious outcomes, including hospitalization and death; consider the risk/benefit profile of denosumab treatment for osteoporosis (Prolia and Prolia biosimilars) in these patients. As a result, a boxed warning was placed in the Prolia and Prolia biosimilars labeling to help reduce the risks of severe hypocalcemia and assist providers in appropriate patient selection and to ensure adequate monitoring. An increased risk of hypocalcemia has also been observed in clinical trials of Xgeva and its biosimilars in patients with increasing renal dysfunction, most commonly with severe dysfunction (CrCl less than 30 mL/minute and/or on dialysis), and with inadequate/no calcium supplementation. Patients with In individuals without advanced CKD who are predisposed to hypocalcemia or disturbances of mineral metabolism (e.g., hypoparathyroidism, thyroid surgery, parathyroid surgery, malabsorption syndromes, small bowel resection, or treatment with other calcium-lowering medications), monitor serum calcium and mineral concentrations (phosphorus and magnesium) 10 to 14 days after Prolia or any Prolia biosimilar injection. The presence of underlying CKD-mineral bone disorder (CKD-MBD, renal osteodystrophy) markedly increases the risk of hypocalcemia. Concomitant use of calcimimetic medications may also worsen hypocalcemia risk. To minimize the risk of hypocalcemia in these patients, evaluate for the presence of CKD-MBD with intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH), serum calcium, 25(OH) vitamin D, and 1,25(OH)2 vitamin D before choosing treatment with Prolia or a Prolia biosimilar. Also consider assessing bone turnover status (serum markers of bone turnover or bone biopsy) to evaluate for underlying metabolic bone disease. Monitor serum calcium weekly for the first month after Prolia or Prolia biosimilar administration and monthly thereafter. All individuals with advanced CKD (including those who are dialysis-dependent) should be educated about the symptoms of hypocalcemia and the importance of maintaining serum calcium levels with adequate calcium and activated vitamin D supplementation. The use of Prolia or its biosimilars in these individuals should be supervised by a health care provider who is experienced in diagnosis and management of CKD-MBD.  More frequent monitoring is highly recommended in people with advanced renal disease receiving dialysis and may also be necessary when denosumab is administered with other drugs that can lower calcium concentrations. In all denosumab recipients treated for osteoporosis, ensure adequate intake of calcium and vitamin D. Osteoporotic individuals with vitamin D deficiency may require a higher dose of vitamin D supplementation during denosumab treatment and correction of the deficiency before initiating denosumab is optimal. For patients receiving the Xgeva formulation or its biosimilars, calcium concentrations should be monitored throughout therapy, especially in the first weeks of initiating therapy, and vitamin D, calcium, and magnesium should be supplemented as necessary. Instruct denosumab-treated individuals to seek prompt medical care if signs or symptoms of hypocalcemia develop.
    Headline
    immunosuppression
    General Information
    Patients on concomitant immunosuppressant agents or with impaired immune systems (immunosuppression) may be at increased risk for serious infections when receiving denosumab for the treatment of osteoporosis. Consider the benefit-risk profile in such patients before treating with denosumab. In patients who develop serious infections while on Prolia or any of its biosimilars, prescribers should assess the need for continued denosumab therapy. Serious infections leading to hospitalization were reported more often in individuals treated with denosumab for osteoporosis than in the placebo group. Serious infections as well as infections of the abdomen, urinary tract, ear, and endocarditis were reported more frequently in the denosumab group vs. placebo during clinical trials. Overall, the incidence of infections was similar between the treatment group and placebo.
    Headline
    anemia, coagulopathy, dental disease, dental work, ill-fitting dentures, malignancy, planned procedure, poor oral hygiene
    General Information
    Osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ) has been reported in individuals receiving denosumab in clinical trials of both osteoporotic and oncology patients. ONJ is typically associated with dental procedures, such as tooth extraction, and local infection with delayed healing; however, cases have appeared spontaneously. If patients require invasive dental procedures, the clinical judgment of the treating physician and/or oral surgeon should guide the management plan for each patient. A dental examination with appropriate preventive dentistry and correction of dental complications prior to initiating denosumab therapy is recommended for patients with risk factors for ONJ such as invasive dental procedures and dental work (e.g., tooth extraction, dental implants, oral surgery), malignancy or diagnosis of cancer, concomitant corticosteroid therapy, concomitant chemotherapy such as treatment with angiogenesis inhibitors, poor oral hygiene, and comorbid conditions such as periodontal and/or other pre-existing dental disease, anemia, coagulopathy, infection, and ill-fitting dentures. Good oral hygiene practices should be maintained during treatment. The risk of ONJ may increase with duration of exposure to denosumab. For people requiring a planned procedure, such as invasive dental procedures, clinical judgment of the treating physician and/or oral surgeon should guide the management plan of each individual based on benefit/risk assessment. People who develop ONJ while on denosumab therapy should receive care by a dentist or an oral surgeon. In these people, extensive dental surgery to treat ONJ may exacerbate the condition.
    Headline
    contraception requirements, pregnancy testing, reproductive risk
    General Information
    All females treated with any denosumab formulation should avoid becoming pregnant while taking denosumab and should be informed of the potential reproductive risk should pregnancy occur. Pregnancy testing should occur to verify the pregnancy status prior to initiating denosumab treatment. Discuss contraception requirements with the patient. Females of reproductive potential who receive any denosumab formulation should be instructed to use highly effective contraception during therapy, for at least 5 months after the last dose, and to contact their health care provider if pregnancy occurs or if the patient suspects pregnancy within 5 months of taking the last dose. In a study of 12 healthy male volunteers, denosumab was present in seminal fluid at a concentration approximately 2% of serum exposure. After vaginal intercourse, the amount of drug delivered to a female partner would result in exposures approximately 11,000 times lower than that seen after the recommended dose. Male condom use is not necessary as it is unlikely the partner or fetus would be exposed to pharmacologically relevant concentrations of denosumab via seminal fluid.
    Headline
    breast-feeding
    General Information
    Denosumab is not recommended for use during breast-feeding. Either the drug or breast-feeding should be discontinued because of the potential for serious adverse reactions in the breastfed child, taking into account the importance of the drug to the treated patient. It is not known if denosumab is excreted into human milk. Maternal antibodies are known to be present in human milk; however, data are limited regarding use of denosumab during lactation. The drug is a protein and would likely be digested in the gastrointestinal tract of the breastfed infant, but exposure and effects on the nursing infant are not known. Exposure of pediatric patients to denosumab is not recommended as it may impair bone growth and inhibit tooth eruption. Additionally, patients receiving treatment during pregnancy may have impaired lactation postpartum due to drug-induced interruption of mammary gland development; animal studies have demonstrated conflicting results regarding altered maturation of mammary glands. Consider the benefits of breast-feeding, the risk of infant drug exposure, and the risk of an untreated or inadequately treated condition.
    Headline
    pregnancy
    General Information
    Denosumab is available as 18 products; Prolia, Jubbonti, Ospomyv, Stoboclo, Xgeva, Xbryk, Wyost, Osenvelt, Bomyntra, Conexxence, Bildyos, Bilprevda, Bosaya, Aukelso, Enoby, Xtrenbo, Boncresa, and Oziltus. Prolia, Jubbonti, Ospomyv, Stoboclo, Conexxence, Bildyos, Bosaya, Enoby, or Boncresa use is contraindicated during pregnancy. Xgeva, Xbryk, Wyost, Osenvelt, Bomyntra, Bilprevda, Auxelso, Xtrenbo, or Oziltus should be avoided during pregnancy. Denosumab can cause fetal harm when given during pregnancy based on findings in animals. The risk for adverse fetal effects may be greater during the second and third trimesters. Monoclonal antibodies cross the placenta linearly, with the greatest amount transferred during the third trimester. If pregnancy occurs during therapy, discontinue treatment; inform the individual of the potential risk to the fetus. Animal studies conducted in cynomolgus monkeys and mice genetically engineered to lack RANKL demonstrated fetal harm. In cynomolgus monkeys dosed throughout gestation at a dose 25-fold higher than the recommended human dose, stillbirths, fetal loss, and postnatal mortality occurred. Other effects included the absence of peripheral (axillary, inguinal, mandibular, and mesenteric) lymph nodes, reduced bone strength, abnormal bone growth, decreased neonatal growth, decreased hematopoiesis, tooth malalignment, and dental dysplasia. Measurable blood concentrations of denosumab, at 22% to 621% of maternal concentrations, were detected at birth through 1 month of age. The effects of denosumab on bone quality and strength returned to normal during observation through 6 months of age. Tooth eruption was normal; however, dental dysplasia was still present. Small mandibular and mesenteric lymph nodes were present with axillary and inguinal nodes still missing. Mineralization in multiple tissues also occurred in 1 recovery animal. No maternal harm occurred prior to labor and adverse effects during labor were infrequent. In mice, fetal lymph node development was also lacking and postnatal impairment of bone and tooth development occurred.
    Headline
    adolescents, children, infants, neonates
    General Information
    Denosumab is available in many products. The safety and efficacy of the Prolia, Jubbonti, Ospomyv, Stoboclo, Conexxence, Bildyos, Bosaya, Enoby, or Boncresa products have not been established in adolescents, children, infants or neonates. Xgeva, Xbryk, Wyost, Osenvelt, Bomyntra, Bilprevda, Aukelso, Xtrenbo, or Oziltus should only be used in pediatric individuals with selected conditions and with close clinical monitoring. Xgeva, Xbryk, Wyost, Osenvelt, Bomyntra, Bilprevda, Aukelso, Xtrenbo, or Oziltus are recommended for use in the treatment of giant cell tumor of the bone (GCTB) only in skeletally mature adolescents; safety and efficacy have not been established in pediatric patients who are not skeletally mature.  In an analysis that looked at the efficacy of denosumab in 10 adolescents with GCTB, skeletal maturity was defined as having at least 1 mature long bone (e.g., closed epiphyseal growth plate of the humerus) and a body weight of at least 45 kg. Clinically significant hypercalcemia requiring hospitalization and complicated with acute renal injury has been reported in denosumab-treated pediatric individuals with GCTB with growing skeletons and in pediatric individuals with osteogenesis imperfecta.  Hypercalcemia has been reported within the first year after treatment discontinuation. Monitor individuals for signs and symptoms of hypercalcemia and treat them appropriately. After treatment is discontinued, monitor individuals for signs and symptoms of hypercalcemia, assess serum calcium periodically, and reassess calcium and vitamin D supplementation requirements. Denosumab is a monoclonal antibody that inhibits RANKL and thus affects osteoclast activity and bone remodeling; use in pediatric individuals may impair bone growth and inhibit tooth eruption. Denosumab administered to adolescent primates at doses of up to 50-times the human equivalent dose resulted in the development of abnormal growth plates consistent with denosumab-activity.

    General Information
    Osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ) has been reported with denosumab therapy. Perform a dental examination with appropriate preventive dentistry prior to treatment in people with risk factors for ONJ such as invasive dental procedures (e.g. tooth extraction, dental implants, oral surgery), diagnosis of cancer, concomitant therapies (e.g. chemotherapy, corticosteroids angiogenesis inhibitors), poor oral hygiene, and co-morbid disorders (e.g. periodontal and/or other pre-existing dental disease, anemia, coagulopathy, infection, ill-fitting dentures). Symptoms of ONJ include jaw pain, osteomyelitis, osteitis, bone erosion, tooth or periodontal infection, dental pain (toothache), gingival ulceration (oral ulceration), or gingival erosion. Of subjects who developed ONJ, 79% had a history of tooth extraction, poor oral hygiene, or use of a dental appliance. Two trials included an open-label extension phase in which subjects were offered denosumab 120 mg every 4 weeks (median overall exposure 14.9 months; range 0.1 to 67.2 months). The person-year adjusted incidence of confirmed ONJ was 1.1% during the first year, 3.7% in the second, and 4.6% per year thereafter. The median time to ONJ was 20.6 months (range 4 to 53 months). The combined person-year adjusted incidence of confirmed ONJ was 0.2% during the first year of treatment, 1.5% in the second, 1.8% in the third, 2.1% in the fourth, 1.4% in the fifth, and 1.5% per year thereafter. Toothache (dental pain) occurred in 10% or more of subjects treated with denosumab (Xgeva and Xgeva biosimilars). Advise individuals to maintain good oral hygiene, to inform all dental health care providers of denosumab therapy prior to dental work/procedures, and to report persistent pain and/or slow healing of the mouth or jaw following dental work/procedures. If ONJ develops or is suspected, individuals should be evaluated by a dentist or oral surgeon; extensive dental surgery may exacerbate ONJ.

    General Information
    Electrolyte imbalances have been reported with denosumab therapy. Severe symptomatic hypocalcemia resulting in hospitalization, life-threatening events, and fatal cases has been reported in postmarketing surveillance of denosumab. Correct hypocalcemia prior to starting denosumab therapy. Results from a long-term safety study have suggested an increased risk of hypocalcemia with denosumab use in people with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) and osteoporosis. In addition, an internal FDA study showed a substantial risk of severe and symptomatic hypocalcemia resulting in hospitalization and death in people on dialysis who were treated with denosumab for osteoporosis, resulting in the addition of a boxed warning for the risk of hypocalcemia. Consider the benefit-risk profile when administering denosumab to individuals with severe renal impairment or with renal failure receiving dialysis due to the potential for severe hypocalcemia. Frequent clinical monitoring of calcium concentrations is highly recommended in these individuals. Advise people on dialysis to seek immediate medical care if they experience symptoms of hypocalcemia. At the month 1 visit, hypocalcemia (serum calcium less than 8.4 mg/dL) was reported in 2.4% of subjects who received denosumab (Prolia and Prolia biosimilars) in a randomized, double-blind study in men with prostate cancer receiving androgen deprivation therapy. Additionally, hypocalcemia (defined as serum calcium concentration less than 8.5 mg/dL) occurred in 1.7% of postmenopausal women with osteoporosis who received denosumab (Prolia and Prolia biosimilars); calcium nadirs occurred at approximately day 10 in subjects with normal renal function. In a study of postmenopausal women with osteoporosis, the mean percent change in serum calcium from baseline to day 10 after denosumab (Prolia and Prolia biosimilars) administration was -5.5% in women with a CrCl less than 30 mL/minute and -3.1% in women with a CrCl or 30 mL/minute or greater. In a separate trial (n = 33), grade 3 or higher electrolyte abnormalities reported by subjects with hypercalcemia of malignancy included hypomagnesemia (3%), hypokalemia (3%), and hypophosphatemia (76%). In some postmarketing reports, hypocalcemia persisted for weeks to months, required frequent monitoring, and intravenous and/or calcium replacement, with or without vitamin D. Severe hypocalcemia resulting in hospitalization, life-threatening events, and fatal cases have been reported. The presence of underlying CKD-mineral bone disorder (CKD-MBD, renal osteodystrophy) markedly increases the risk of hypocalcemia. Concomitant use of calcimimetic medications may also worsen hypocalcemia risk. To minimize the risk of hypocalcemia in these individuals, evaluate for the presence of chronic kidney disease mineral and bone disorder with intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH), serum calcium, 25(OH) vitamin D, and 1,25(OH)2 vitamin D prior to deciding on denosumab (Prolia and Prolia biosimilars) treatment. Also consider assessing bone turnover status (serum markers of bone turnover or bone biopsy) to evaluate the underlying bone disease that may be present. Monitor serum calcium weekly for the first month after denousmab (Prolia and Prolia biosimilars) administration and monthly thereafter. For all individuals receiving denosumab, monitor calcium, and other electrolytes (e.g., phosphorus, magnesium); advise individuals to report signs and symptoms of hypocalcemia (e.g., muscle cramps/spasms, twitching, tetany, paresthesias or numbness of fingertips, toes, or perioral area). Adequately supplement individuals with calcium and vitamin D and magnesium. Assess serum calcium periodically, reevaluate the individual's supplementation requirements and manage individuals clinically as appropriate. Clinically significant hypercalcemia requiring hospitalization and complicated with acute renal injury has been reported in denosumab-treated subjects with GCTB with growing skeletons and pediatric patients with osteogenesis imperfecta. Denosumab is only recommended for treatment of skeletally mature adolescents with GCTB. Hypercalcemia was reported in the first year after discontinuation of treatment. After treatment is discontinued, monitor individuals for signs and symptoms of hypercalcemia (e.g., nausea, vomiting, headache, and decreased alertness); assess serum calcium periodically, reevaluate the individual's calcium and vitamin D supplementation requirements and manage individuals clinically as appropriate.

    General Information
    Bone fractures developing along the femoral shaft (below the lesser trochanter to above the supracondylar flare) have been reported by denosumab recipients, some of whom were receiving concurrent glucocorticoid therapy. Many individuals experience a dull, aching thigh pain weeks to months before the complete fracture occurs, thus, health care teams are advised to rule out an incomplete femur fracture in any individual presenting with thigh or groin pain. Consider treatment interruptions based on the risk-to-benefit assessment of the individual. The risk of bone fractures, including multiple vertebral fracture, increases following denosumab (Prolia and Prolia biosimilars) discontinuation in individuals treated for osteoporosis. During clinical trials of women with postmenopausal osteoporosis, 6% of women who discontinued denosumab developed new vertebral fractures and 3% developed multiple new vertebral fractures. New vertebral fractures occurred as early as 7 months (average 19 months) after the last denosumab dose. Prior vertebral fractures was a predictor of multiple vertebral fractures after denosumab discontinuation. If denosumab treatment is discontinued, consider transitioning to alternative antiresorptive therapy. The incidence of atypical femoral fracture in subjects with giant cell tumor of bone (GCTB) treated with denosumab (Xgeva and Xgeva biosimilars) during clinical trials was 0.9% (n = 548). During a 5-year long term follow up study, the combined incidence of confirmed atypical femoral fracture was 1.3% of subjects receiving denosumab (Xgeva and Xgeva biosimilars).

Description
  Text
  Denosumab is a highly specific monoclonal antibody that binds to the human receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-beta ligand (RANKL), sometimes called a RANK ligand inhibitor. Indication-specific denosumab products are available, and it is essential that clinicians choose the right product for use. Prolia, Jubbonti, Ospomyv, Stoboclo, Conexxence, Bildyos, Bosaya, Enoby, and Boncresa are used for osteoporosis treatment and prevention in selected adults at high risk for fracture. Denosumab reduces the risk for both vertebral and nonvertebral fractures, including hip fracture, with efficacy similar to the bisphosphonates. Denosumab is considered a first or second-line option to bisphosphonate therapy, particularly in individuals who have contraindications to or experience adverse effects of bisphosphonates. Unlike anabolic agents, there is no restriction on the duration of use of denosumab. Adherence and costs, including the need for sequential treatment if denosumab is discontinued, must also be considered when individualizing treatment. Xgeva, Xbryk, Wyost, Osenvelt, Bomyntra, Bilprevda, Aukelso, Xtrenbo, and Oziltus are used for prevention of skeletal-related events in individuals with multiple myeloma or with bone metastases due to solid tumors, for the treatment of refractory hypercalcemia of malignancy, and for adults and skeletally-mature adolescents with giant cell tumor of bone that is unresectable or where surgical resection is likely to result in severe morbidity. Denosumab osteoporosis formulations have a boxed warning in the product labels for the risk of severe hypocalcemia in individuals with advanced chronic kidney disease. Consider the benefit-risk profile when administering denosumab for any indication to individuals with severe renal impairment or with renal failure receiving dialysis. Frequent clinical monitoring of calcium concentrations is highly recommended in these individuals. Advise individuals on dialysis to seek immediate medical care if they experience symptoms of hypocalcemia. Denosumab was initially FDA-approved in June 2010.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Denosumab neutralizes the biologic activity of receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL) by binding to it and blocking its interaction with cell surface receptors known as receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa-beta (RANK). Endogenous RANKL, a transmembrane or soluble protein, is expressed on the surface of both precursor and mature osteoclasts and is essential for their formation, function, and survival. RANKL inhibition by denosumab (Prolia, Jubbonti, Ospomyv, Stoboclo, Conexxence, Bildyos, Bosaya, Enoby, and Boncresa) decreases RANKL activation of RANK receptors on the surface of osteoclasts resulting in a down regulation of osteoclast activity and thus a reduction of bone resorption; an increase in bone mass and strength in both cortical and trabecular bone is seen. By blocking RANKL from activating RANK, denosumab (Xgeva, Xbryk, Wyost, Osenvelt, Bomyntra, Bilprevda, Aukelso, Xtrenbo, and Oziltus) inhibits the increased osteoclast activity that leads to bone pathology in solid tumor with osseous metastases. Additionally, denosumab prevents osteolysis and tumor growth in giant cell tumors of the bone containing stromal cells that express RANKL and osteoclast-like giant cells that express the RANK receptor.

Pharmacokinetics
  Route Specific Pharmacokinetics
    Name
    Subcutaneous Route
    Description
    The bioavailability after subcutaneous administration was 62% in one pharmacokinetic trial. After a single 60-mg subcutaneous dose to fasted, healthy adults (n = 73, age range: 18 to 64 years), a maximum serum concentration of 6.75 (+/- 1.89) mcg/mL was achieved at a median time of 10 days (range: 3 to 21 days). The mean AUC(0 to 16 weeks) of denosumab was 316 mcg x day/mL (SD = 101 mcg x day/mL). Multiple subcutaneous doses of 60 mg given every 6 months did not result in denosumab accumulation or otherwise affect denosumab pharmacokinetics. Doses above 60 mg displayed approximately dose-proportional increases in drug exposure. Up to a 2.8-fold accumulation in serum denosumab has been noted with doses of 120 mg of denosumab administered subcutaneously every 4 weeks to adult cancer patients with bone metastases; steady-state was achieved by 6 months. Following the administration of denosumab 120 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks with 2 additional 120 mg doses given on day 8 and 15 of the first month of therapy, steady-state levels were achieved in 3 months.

      Description
      Denosumab should be administered by a health care team.

Administration using Prolia, Jubbonti, Ospomyv, Stoboclo, Bildyos, Bosaya, or Boncresa prefilled syringes
Leave the green needle safety guard (Prolia), transparent safety guard (Boncresa), and safety guard wings (Jubbonti) in their original position until after dosage administration; sliding the guard or touching the safety guard wings prior to administration will prevent injection.
Remove and discard the needle cap immediately prior to dose administration.
Administer the full contents of the denosumab prefilled syringe subcutaneously in the upper arm, the upper thigh, or the abdomen.
Immediately following injection, point the needle away from people and gently slide the green safety guard over the needle (Prolia) or release the plunger until the needle is covered by the safety guard (Jubbonti, Ospomyv, Stoboclo, Bildyos, Bosaya, or Boncresa).
Discard all used supplies as appropriate.
 
Administration using Conexxence, Bomyntra, or Enoby prefilled syringes
Inject subcutaneously into the upper arm, the upper thigh, or the abdomen.
Remove and discard the needle cap immediately prior to dose administration. Do not twist or bend the needle cap or hold prefilled syringe by the plunger rod.
Pinch the skin and insert the needle at a 45 to 90-degree angle.
Push the plunger with slow and constant pressure until it cannot be pressed further. A "click" or "snap" may be heard or felt.
Keeping the prefilled syringe at the injection site, slowly release the plunger and lift the syringe off the skin. The clear needle guard will automatically cover the needle.
Discard all used supplies as appropriate.
 
Administration using single-use vials (Xgeva, Xbryk, Wyost, Osenvelt, Bomyntra, Bildyos, Bilprevda, Aukelso, Xtrenbo, or Oziltus)
Use a 27-gauge needle to withdraw and inject the denosumab dose.
Administer subcutaneously in the upper arm, upper thigh, or abdomen.
Do not re-insert the needle into the vial. Discard any unused medication and all used supplies as appropriate.
