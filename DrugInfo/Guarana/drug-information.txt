Although only small amounts are secreted in breast-milk, caffeine can accumulate in the neonate if maternal ingestion is moderate to high. Higher caffeine intake (> 500 mg/day) by a nursing mother may cause irritability or poor sleeping patterns in the infant who is breast-feeding. Caffeine containing products, such as guarana, should be used cautiously during lactation due to their high caffeine contents. Mothers who are breast-feeding infants who are prescribed caffeine for apnea should avoid additional caffeine and caffeine-containing products.Prior to undergoing surgery, the surgeon should be notified of the use of guarana. In many cases, it is unknown what effects herbal medicines may have on surgical procedures, and since guarana acts as a stimulant it may induce changes in heart rate or blood pressure during surgery. Guarana has been noted to inhibit platelet aggregation, which could increase the risk for bleeding in patients undergoing surgical procedures. These changes in platelet aggregation could also increase risk of bleeding in patients who are receiving anticoagulant therapy; concomitant use should be avoided.Guarana's effects are mainly due to the active constituent, caffeine. Caffeine is a central nervous system stimulant and, therefore, guarana should be used cautiously in patients with anxiety disorders and/or panic disorder because it can aggravate these conditions. Patients suffering from insomnia should not consume guarana, especially within 6 hours of bedtime because it can exacerbate insomnia. In overdoses, caffeine has been associated with seizures, therefore, guarana should be avoided in patients with a seizure disorder.Because of the presence of caffeine and other xanthenes, guarana should be used cautiously in patients with cardiac disease. Guarana has been noted to increase heart rate and the caffeine constituent can also stimulate the force of contraction. Guarana may increase left ventricular output and stroke volume. Patients who have angina or a history of cardiac arrhythmias should not receive guarana. Guarana should not be taken in the first few days—weeks after a myocardial infarction. Patients with hypertension should avoid the use of guarana.Because the clearance of guaranas' active constituent, caffeine, is delayed in patients with hepatic impairment or hepatic disease, the use of guarana should generally be avoided in such patients. Also, renal impairment or renal failure may delay caffeine clearance and guarana use should be avoided in these patients.Because caffeine can either raise or decrease blood sugar, patients with diabetes mellitus should use guarana with caution.The stimulatory effects of caffeine can be augmented in hyperthyroidism. Therefore, avoid the use of guarana in patients with thyroid disease, especially hyperthyroidism.Patients with peptic ulcer disease should avoid the use of guarana because caffeine has been shown to stimulate gastric secretions, aggravating this condition.Tobacco smoking (cigarettes) has been shown to increase the clearance of caffeine and theophylline (found in guarana). Also, passive smoke exposure may cause an increase in methylxanthine clearance. Tobacco smoke contains hydrocarbons that induce hepatic CYP450 microsomal enzymes. Because the effect on hepatic microsomal enzymes is not related to the nicotine component of tobacco, sudden smoking cessation may result in a reduced clearance of caffeine or other methylxanthines, despite the initiation of nicotine replacement. Guarana dosage may need to be reduced at the cessation of smoking.

Description
  Text
  Guarana is a botanical dietary supplement whose main active constituent is caffeine. Guarana is the common name of a climbing, shrubby vine found in Venezuela and the northern parts of Brazil, Paullina Cupana. A dried paste consisting of the ground seed kernel of the berries that grow on this plant is also referred to as guarana. Guarana contains 3% to 8% caffeine, as well as theophylline, theobromine, tannic acid, and saponins. Methylxanthines, particularly caffeine, are the primary components of guarana that provide pharmacologic activity. Historically, guarana has been used to relieve headaches, cramps and fevers, as an aphrodisiac, and to prevent malaria and dysentery. The tannic acid present in guarana is an astringent and may account for its use as a digestive tonic. Guarana is available in dietary supplements as a single entity or in combinations with other herbal ingredients, and is found in some foods and beverages as an additive. Guarana is also the name of a popular South American soft drink, which is also made using the ground seeds of the Paullina Cupana plant. Guarana is mainly used as a CNS stimulant to promote wakefulness and as an aid in weight loss, although controlled data supporting efficacy for weight loss are lacking. Guarana has been used in combination with ephedra, ma huang; however this combination should be avoided, due to the potential for potentially dangerous cardiovascular side effects.
 
In the U.S., herbs and other dietary supplements are marketed under the Dietary Supplement and Health Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA). Consequently, scientific data supporting claimed benefit(s) are not always available for these supplements as they are for traditional pharmaceuticals as these products are not regulated as drugs. Consumers should also note that rigid quality control standards are not required and substantial variability can occur in both the potency and the purity of these products. Monographs on dietary supplements are included when reliable clinical data are available. The information presented is condensed from the best clinical data we could find.

Mechanism Of Action
  Text
  Although the exact mechanism of guarana's action is unknown, most of its effects are attributed to its high caffeine content. The seed paste contains 3—8% caffeine. The following, which briefly describes the actions of caffeine and theophylline, is expected to also be true of guarana, as guarana's constituents are caffeine and, to much a lesser extent, theophylline. Caffeine is a mild, direct stimulant at all levels of the CNS and also stimulates the heart and cardiovascular system. The related xanthine, theophylline, shares these properties. Both caffeine and theophylline are CNS stimulants, with theophylline exerting more dramatic effects than caffeine at higher concentrations. The xanthines stimulate the medullary respiratory center and relax bronchial smooth muscle. Caffeine stimulates voluntary muscle and gastric acid secretion, increases renal blood flow, and is a mild diuretic.
 
Because caffeine helps suppress appetite, guarana is touted as useful as a weight-loss aid. Guarana is often stated to increase the basal metabolic rate with a subsequent increased energy expenditure (thermogenesis) and lipolysis. Unpublished studies report that the combination of guarana and ephedra induces a slight increase in metabolic rate over a 24-hour period and greater weight loss compared to placebo, when used for 8 weeks. However, ephedra may exhibit these actions when given alone. It is unknown if guarana itself exhibits thermogenic effects or if guarana acts to enhance the effects of ephedra. Anectdotal reports state that guarana may enhance athletic endurance, perhaps by an antioxidant effect or reducing a buildup of lactic acid in the muscles.
 
Aqueous extracts of guarana have been shown to have antiplatelet activity in both rabbits and humans. Although the exact mechanism of action is unknown, it is thought to be associated with the inhibition of thromboxane synthesis.

  Text
  Guarana is administered orally. The caffeine present in guarana is distributed rapidly to all body tissues and readily crosses the blood-brain and placental barriers. The tannins are thought to slow the body's absorption and metabolism of the active constituents of the plant, so that caffeine-related effects are not experienced as quickly as those from other dosage forms of caffeine. This also may provide for a longer duration of effect from guarana versus caffeine. Caffeine is distributed into breast milk. No further pharmacokinetic information is available.
